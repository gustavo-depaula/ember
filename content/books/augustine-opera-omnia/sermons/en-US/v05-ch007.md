# Sermon 154

**1. 1.** Yesterday's reading from the Epistle of the holy apostle Paul, you who were present at the sermon, heard; today's recited reading follows it. That difficult and perilous passage is still under treatment, which, with the help of our Lord—so far as you help me with devout affection toward him—we have undertaken to expound and untangle according to the strength he deigns to give. Let your Charity grant me patience, so that if, on account of the obscurity of the matters, I have a difficult discourse, I may at least have an easy voice. For if both are difficult, there is much labor; and would that the labor be not in vain. But that our labor may profit, let your hearing be patient. That the Apostle does not blame the Law, we satisfied our hearers yesterday, as I think. For there he says, *What, then, shall we say? Is the law sin? Far from it! But I did not know sin except through the law. For I would not have known coveting, unless the law said, You shall not covet. But sin, taking occasion through the commandment, worked in me every kind of coveting. For without the law sin was dead*—that is, it lies hidden, does not appear. *But I was alive once without the law; but when the commandment came, sin revived. But I died, and the commandment that was unto life was found by me* (for what is so pertinent to life as *You shall not covet*?) *to be unto death. For sin, taking occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me*[^1]; it terrified coveting, did not extinguish it; terrified, did not suppress it; it made fear of punishment, not love of righteousness. *And so*, he says, *the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did that which is good, then, become death to me? Far from it*[^2]! For the law is not death, but sin is death. What, then, from the occasion of the commandment? *But sin, that it might appear as sin* (for it was hidden when it was said to be dead) *worked death in me through what is good; that*, transgression being added, *the sinner*, or sin, *might become beyond measure through the commandment*; because transgression would not be added to sin if there were no commandment. For in another place the same Apostle says openly, *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^3]. What, then? Why do we doubt that the law was given for this purpose, that man might find himself out? For when God did not forbid evil, man lay hidden from himself; he did not find out his own feeble strength except when he received the law of prohibition. He found himself out, then; he found himself out among evils. Where does he flee from himself? For wherever he flees from himself, he follows himself. And what does it profit him to have knowledge of himself, once found out, when conscience wounds him?

**2. 2.** He speaks, then, in this reading too, which has been recited today, the one who has found himself out. *We know*, he says, *that the law is spiritual; but I am carnal, sold under sin. For what I do, I do not know. For I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*[^4]. Here it is asked with great care who is to be understood—whether the Apostle himself who was speaking, or whether he transfigured some other person in himself, whom he was touching in himself, as he said in a certain place, *But all these things I transfigured in myself and Apollos for your sake, that in us you might learn*[^5]. If, then, the Apostle is speaking (which no one doubts), and when he says, *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*, speaks not of another but of himself; what are we to understand, my brothers? Did the apostle Paul, for instance, not wish to commit adultery, and yet commit adultery? Did he not wish to be greedy, and yet was greedy? But who of us would dare clothe himself in such blasphemy as to think this of the Apostle? Perhaps, then, it is some other person; perhaps it is you, or you, or that man, or I. If, then, it is one of us, let us hear it as though about ourselves, and let us not be angry but correct ourselves. But if it is he himself—for perhaps it is he himself too—let us not so understand what he said, *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*, as though he wished to be chaste, and was an adulterer; or wished to be merciful, and was cruel; or wished to be devout, and was ungodly. Let us not take it in this sense: *For I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*.

**3. 3.** But in what sense? I wish not to covet, and I covet. What did the law say? *You shall not covet*[^6]. The man heard the law, recognized the fault: he declared war, he found captivity. But perhaps some man, not the Apostle. What, then, do we say, my brothers? Did the Apostle have no coveting in his flesh which he would have wished not to have, to which, nevertheless, as it existed, tickled, suggested, solicited, blazed up, tempted, he did not consent? I tell your Charity: if we believe that the Apostle had no infirmity of coveting at all against which he struggled, we believe a great deal of him; and would that it were so. For we ought not to envy the apostles, but to imitate the apostles. Yet, dearly beloved, I hear the Apostle himself confessing that he had not yet reached such a perfection of righteousness as we believe to be in the angels; whose angelic equality we hope for, if we reach what we will. For what else does the Lord promise us in the resurrection, where he says, *In the resurrection of the dead they will neither marry nor be given in marriage; for they will not begin to die, but will be equal to the angels of God*[^7]?

**3. 4.** So someone says: And how do you know that the apostle Paul did not yet have the righteousness and perfection of an angel? I do no injury to the Apostle, I believe none but the Apostle, I seek no other witness; I do not hear the one who suspects, I do not care for the one who praises too much. Tell me, holy Apostle, about yourself, where no one doubts that you are speaking of yourself. For where you said, *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*[^8], there are those who say that you transfigured some other person in yourself—one laboring, failing, conquered, captive. Tell me about yourself, where no one doubts that you are speaking of yourself. *Brothers*, says the Apostle, *I do not reckon that I have laid hold of it*. And what do you do? *But one thing: forgetting what is behind, stretching out to what is ahead, according to my intention*, he says—not according to perfection; *according to my intention I press on toward the palm of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus*. Above he had already said, *Not that I have already received it, or am already perfect*[^9]. Still it is objected and said: The Apostle said these things because he had not yet reached immortality, not because he had not yet reached the perfection of righteousness. So he was already as righteous as the angels are, but not yet immortal as the angels are. "Just so," they say, "just so it is exactly." You just now said: He was as righteous as the angels are, but not yet immortal as the angels are. So he already held the perfection of righteousness, but by pressing on toward the heavenly palm he was seeking immortality.

**4. 5.** Tell us, holy Apostle, some other, plainer passage—not where you seek immortality, but where you confess infirmity. And here it is now whispered, now objected. I seem to myself to hear the thoughts of certain people, and here it is said to me: It is true, I know what you are going to say: he confesses infirmity, but of the flesh, not of the mind; he confesses infirmity, but of the body, not of the soul; but in the soul there is perfect righteousness, not in the body. For who does not know that the Apostle was indeed frail in body, mortal in body, as he says, *We have this treasure in earthen vessels*[^10]? What, then, have you to do with the earthen vessel? Say something about the treasure. If he had anything less, if there was something that could be added to his gold of righteousness, let us find it. Let us hear the man himself, lest we be thought injurious. *And lest by the greatness of my revelations*, the Apostle says, *in the greatness of my revelations I be exalted*. Surely here you recognize the Apostle as having a greatness of revelations, and fearing the precipice of pride. So that you may know that even the Apostle himself, who wished to save others, was still being cured; that you may know that he too was still being cured; if you value his honor highly, hear what the physician applied to him for his swelling; hear not me, hear him. Hear him confessing, that you may perceive him teaching. Hear: *And lest by the greatness of my revelations I be exalted*. Behold, can I now say to the apostle Paul: Lest you be exalted, holy Apostle? You—lest you be exalted, must it still be guarded against? You—lest you be exalted, must it still be feared? You—lest you be exalted, must medicine still be sought for this infirmity?

**5. 6.** "What do you say to me?" he says. And you—hear what I am; and do not be high-minded, but fear[^11]. Hear how the little lamb enters where the ram is so endangered. *Lest by the greatness*, he says, *of my revelations I be exalted, there was given to me a sting of my flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet me*[^12]. What swelling did he fear, who received so biting a poultice? Now then say that there was as much righteousness in him as there is in the holy angels. Or does perhaps even a holy angel in heaven, lest he be exalted, receive a sting, an angel of Satan, by whom he may be buffeted? Far be it to suspect this of the holy angels. We are human beings; let us recognize the holy apostles as human beings—chosen vessels, but still frail; still sojourning in this flesh, not yet triumphing in the heavenly homeland. Therefore, since he asked the Lord three times that this sting be taken from him, and was not heard according to his will, because he was heard for his health[^13], perhaps he speaks not unfittingly where he says, *But we know that the law is spiritual; but I am carnal*[^14].

**5. 7.** The Apostle, then, is carnal—he who said to others, *You who are spiritual, instruct such a one in a spirit of gentleness*—he addresses others as spiritual, and is himself carnal? But what did he say even to those spiritual ones, because they were not yet in heavenly and angelic perfection, were not yet in the security of that homeland, but dwelt in the anxiety of this pilgrimage? What did he say to them? Certainly he called them spiritual: *You*, he says, *who are spiritual, instruct such a one in a spirit of gentleness, looking to yourself, lest you too be tempted*[^15]. Behold, the one he has already called spiritual, he feared for him the frailty of temptation, by which the spiritual one could be tempted, if not from the mind, surely from the flesh. For he is spiritual, because he lives according to the spirit; but he is still in part carnal, in the mortal part: the same one is spiritual and the same one carnal. Behold the spiritual: *I serve the law of God with the mind*. Behold the carnal: *But the law of sin with the flesh*[^16]. The same one, then, is both spiritual and carnal? The very same, plainly; as long as he lives here, so it is.

**6. 8.** Do not wonder, whoever you are, whoever yields and consents to carnal cravings—you who either think them good, for the satisfying of lust to the full, or at least already see them to be evil in such a way that you nevertheless consent by yielding to them, and follow where they lead, and perpetrate the evils they suggest: you are wholly carnal. You, you, whoever you are who are such, are wholly carnal. But if you indeed covet what the law forbids when it says, *You shall not covet*; yet keep another thing which the law likewise commands, *Do not go after your desires*[^17]; you are spiritual in mind, carnal in flesh. For it is one thing not to covet, another not to go after your desires. Not to covet belongs to the wholly perfect; not to go after your desires belongs to the one fighting, wrestling, laboring. Where the battle rages, why is victory despaired of? When will there be victory? When death will be swallowed up in victory. For then it will be the voice of one triumphing, not the sweat of one fighting. What will that voice of one triumphing be, when this corruptible will have put on incorruption, and this mortal will have put on immortality? You see the victor, hear the one exulting, await the one triumphing. *Then shall come to pass the saying that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your struggle? Where, O death, is your sting*[^18]? *Where is it*? Behold, it was, and is not. *Where, O death, is your struggle*? Behold the struggle of death: *I do not do what I will*. Behold the struggle of death: *We know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal*. If, then, the Apostle speaks of himself; if I say it, I do not affirm it; if the Apostle says it: *We know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal*; for in mind spiritual, in body carnal: when wholly spiritual? When *the animal body is sown, the spiritual body will rise*[^19]. Now, then, while the struggle of death rages, *I do not do what I will*: in part spiritual, in part carnal; in the better part spiritual, in the lower part carnal. Still I contend, I have not yet conquered; it is a great thing for me not to be conquered. *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*. What do you do? I covet. Even though I do not consent to the coveting, even though I do not go after my desires; yet I still covet: and surely even in that part I am present.

**7. 9.** For it is not that I am in the mind and another is in the flesh. But what? *So then it is I myself*: because I am in the mind, I am in the flesh. For there are not two contrary natures, but from both there is one man; because there is one God, by whom man was made. *So then it is I myself*, I myself, *serve the law of God with the mind, but the law of sin with the flesh*[^20]. With the mind I do not consent to the law of sin; yet I would not wish there to be any law of sin in my members. Because, then, I would not wish it, and yet it is there, *I do not do what I will*: because I covet, and I do not will it, *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*. What do I hate? To covet. I hate to covet, and yet I do it with the flesh, not with the mind: *What I hate, that I do*.

**7. 10.** *But if I do what I do not will, I agree with the law, that it is good*[^21]. What is this: *If I do what I do not will, I agree with the law, that it is good*? You would agree with the law if you did what it willed: you do what the law hates; how do you agree with the law? Precisely: *If I do what I do not will, I agree with the law, that it is good*. How? Because the law commands, *You shall not covet*. What do I will? Not to covet? By willing what the law wills, *I agree with the law, that it is good*. If the law said, *You shall not covet*, and I willed to covet, I would not agree with the law, and would stand wholly apart from it by that perversity of will. For if, the law saying, *You shall not covet*, I willed to covet, I do not agree with the law of God. What now? What do you say, O law? *You shall not covet*. And I do not will to covet, I too do not will it; what you do not will, I do not will; therefore I agree, because what you do not will, I do not will. My infirmity does not fulfill the law; but my will praises the law. Therefore, if I do what I do not will, I agree with the law for this reason: because I do not will what it does not will, not because I do what I do not will. For the very doing is coveting, not consenting to the coveting: lest anyone now seek for himself in the Apostle an example of sinning, and give a bad example. *I do not do what I will*. For what does the law say? *You shall not covet*. And I do not will to covet, and yet I covet; although I do not give assent to my coveting, although I do not go after it. For I resist, I turn away my mind, I refuse it arms, I hold back my members; and yet what I do not will happens in me. What the law does not will, I do not will with the law; what it does not will, I do not will: therefore I agree with the law.

**8. 11.** But because I am in the flesh, I am in the mind; but I am more in the mind than in the flesh. For because I am in the mind, I am in that which rules; for the mind rules, the flesh is ruled; and I am more in that by which I rule than in that in which I am ruled. Because, then, I am more in the mind: *But now it is no longer I that work it. But now*, what is it? *But now*, already redeemed, I who was before sold under sin, having now received the grace of the Savior, so that with the mind I delight in the law of God, *it is not I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me. For I know that there does not dwell in me*. Again, then, *in me*; hear what follows: *that is, in my flesh, anything good. For to will lies near at hand for me. I know*. What do you know? *That there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, anything good*[^22]. A while before you had said, *What I do, I do not know*[^23]. If you do not know, how do you know? Now you say, *I do not know*; now you say, *I know*; how I am to understand this, I do not know. Or is this what I understand? For where he says, *What I do, I do not know*, he said, "I do not know" in the sense of: I do not approve, I do not accept, it does not please me, I do not consent, I do not praise. For Christ too will not be ignorant of those to whom he is going to say, *I do not know you*[^24]. I understand this too, plainly: *For what I do, I do not know*; because what I do not do, I do not know. *For it is not I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*. Therefore I do not know it: because it is not I that do it, just as it was said of the Lord, *Him who knew no sin*[^25]. What is, *knew no sin*? Did he then not know what he convicted? did he not know what he punished? If, then, he did not know what he punished, he punished unjustly. But because he punished justly, he knew what he punished. And yet he knew no sin, because he had committed no sin. *For what I do, I do not know: for I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do. But if I do what I do not will, I agree with the law, that it is good. But now*, grace having been received, *it is no longer I that work it*; the mind is free, the flesh captive. *It is not I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me. For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, anything good*.

**9. 12.** *For to will lies near at hand for me, but to accomplish the good does not*[^26]. *To will lies near at hand, to accomplish does not*. He did not say, to do; but, *to accomplish*. For you do not do nothing. Coveting rebels, and you do not consent; another's wife delights you, and you do not assent, you turn away your mind, you enter into the inner chamber of the mind. You see coveting making a din outside, you pronounce sentence against it, cleansing your conscience. I do not will, you say, I do not do it. Suppose it delights me, I do not do it, I have something else in which to delight. *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man*[^27]. Why do you, my flesh, make an uproar? Why do you tumultuously suggest foolish delights, temporal, fleeting, vain, harmful, and tell them to me as it were chattering? *The unjust have told me of delights*. From there comes this coveting too. It tells me of delights, *but not like your law, O Lord*[^28]. *For I delight in the law of God*: not from myself, but from the grace of God. You, coveting, make an uproar in the flesh, you do not subject the mind to yourself. *In God I will hope; I will not fear what the flesh may do to me*[^29]. It is against me—that is, with the mind not consenting—that the flesh makes its uproar. *In God*, he says, *I will hope; I will not fear what the flesh may do to me*. As neither another's, so neither my own. Does he, then, who does these things within himself do nothing? He does much: it is a great thing that he does, but yet he does not accomplish it. For what is it to accomplish? *Where, O death, is your struggle*[^30]? Therefore, *to will lies near at hand for me, but to accomplish the good does not*.

**9. 13.** *For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I do not will, that I do*. And he repeats: *But if I do what I do not will*—that is, I covet—*it is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me. I find, then, a law for me wishing to do good*[^31]. I find the law good; the law is good, the law is something good. How do I prove it? Because I wish to fulfill it. *I find, then, a law for me wishing to do good; because evil lies near at hand for me*. And this *for me*. For my flesh is not another's, nor is the flesh of another substance, nor is the flesh from another principle, nor is the soul from God and the flesh from the race of darkness. Far from it! Sickness fights against health. The half-alive man lies in the way, is still being cured, all his sicknesses are healed[^32]. *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do. But if I do what I do not will, I find, then, a law for me wishing to do good, because evil lies near at hand for me*. What evil?

**10. 14.** *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man. I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*[^33]; captive, but from the flesh; captive, but in part. For the mind fights against, and delights in, the law of God. For so we ought to understand it, if the Apostle is speaking of himself. If, then, the mind does not consent to sin as it tickles, suggests, flatters; if the mind does not consent, since it has within other delights of its own, in no way to be compared with the delights of the flesh: if, then, it does not consent, and there is in me something dead and something alive, death still contends, but the living mind does not consent. Is that death not in you? Does that which is dead not pertain to you? Still there is for you a struggle. What, then, is to be hoped for from this?

**10. 15.** *Wretched man that I am*: even if not in mind, yet in flesh a wretched man. For man is not in the mind, and not man in the flesh. For who ever hated his own flesh[^34]? *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death*[^35]? What is this, brothers? It is as though he wishes to be without a body. Why do you hasten? If your intention is only this, to be without a body; death will come at some time, and the last day that arrives will, without doubt, free you from this body of death. Why do you groan as over a great matter? Why do you say, *Who will free me*? You speak as a mortal, you speak as one about to die. The separation of the mind from the flesh will come at some time: because of the brevity of life it is never far off; because of daily mischances, when it will be, you do not know. Whether, then, you hasten or delay, all human life is short: why do you groan as over a great matter, and say, *Who will free me from the body of this death*?

**11. 16.** And he adds, *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^36]. Will pagans, then, who do not have the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, not die? Will they not at some time, on the last day, be loosed from the flesh? Will they not on that day be freed from this body of death? Why do you wish to ascribe it as a great thing to the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, that you will be freed from this body of death? The Apostle answers you, if we have grasped his meaning—rather, since with the Lord's help we have without doubt grasped it; the Apostle answers you and says: I know what I am saying. You say that pagans are freed from this body of death, because the last day of this life will come, and they will be loosed for a time from this body of death. There will come a day too, *when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice; and those who have done good will come forth to the resurrection of life*; behold, freed from this body of death. *Those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment*[^37]: behold, they have returned to this body of death. This body of death returns to the ungodly man, nor will he ever be loosed from it. Then there will not be eternal life, but eternal death, because eternal punishment.

**12. 17.** But you, O Christian, pray as much as you can, cry out and say, *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death*? It is answered to you: You are made secure, not concerning yourself, but concerning your Lord; you are made secure concerning your pledge. Hope, with Christ, for the kingdom of Christ; you already hold the pledge, the blood of Christ. Say, say, *Who will free me from the body of this death*? that it may be answered to you, *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^38]. For you will not be freed from this body of death in such a way that you do not have this body. You will have it, but no longer of this death. It will be the same, and it will not be the same. It will be the same, because it will be the same flesh; it will not be the same, because it will not be mortal. Thus, thus you will be freed from the body of this death, so that this mortal will put on immortality, and this corruptible will put on incorruption[^39]. By whom? Through whom? *The grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord. For through one man came death, and through one man the resurrection of the dead. As in Adam all die*; hence comes your groaning. *In Adam all die*: hence comes your groaning, hence your contending with death, hence this body of death. *But as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^40]. Made alive, having received an immortal body, where you may say, *Where, O death, is your struggle*[^41]?, you will be freed from this body of death; yet not by your own power, but by *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
