# Sermon 1: A Treatise Against the Manichees

On What Is Written:

"In the Beginning God Made Heaven and Earth"

And: "in the Beginning Was the Word"

**1.** Whoever remembers his debt and the apostolic saying in which it is written, *Owe no one anything, except to love one another*[^1], ought to compel himself to pay it. And indeed, however great the roar with which the terror of tax collectors bears down upon debtors, love demands far more vehemently, for it takes away from the demand the weight of fear and lays on a greater weight of shame. I remember that I promised your Charity that a reply against the foolish and ruinous slanders of the Manichees, by which they lie in wait against the Old Testament, would not be wanting on our part, so far as the Lord deigns to grant. Take heed, therefore, and see the serpent's snares, and withdraw your necks from them and set them under the yoke of Christ. For they dare to hold out deceits of this kind to the unwary, saying that the Scriptures of the New and the Old Testament are opposed to each other, so that the two cannot be held together by one faith. And striving to convince people that the very openings of the book of Genesis and of the Gospel according to John are at enmity with each other, they set them, as it were, face to face in conflict.

**2.** They say: Moses says, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^2], and does not name the Son through whom all things were made, whereas John says, *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^3]. Is this a contradiction, or are they rather in contradiction with themselves, who have chosen to find fault, in their blindness, with what they do not understand rather than to seek it out in piety? For what will they say when I have replied that the very "beginning" is the Son of God, in whom Genesis says that God made heaven and earth? Or will I perhaps be unable to prove this, when I know that witnesses from the very New Testament, to which they are subject whether they will or no with the neck of their pride broken, are at hand for me? For the Lord says to the unbelieving Jews, *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote of me*[^4]. Why then should I not understand the Lord himself as the beginning in which God the Father made heaven and earth? For *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^5] Moses certainly wrote, and that he wrote of the Lord is confirmed by the Lord's own voice. Or is he perhaps not himself also the beginning? Nor will there be any need to doubt this, since the Gospel speaks, where the Jews, having asked the Lord who he was, he himself answered, *The beginning, who also speak to you*[^6]. Behold the beginning in which God made heaven and earth. Heaven, therefore, and earth God made in the Son, through whom all things were made and without whom nothing was made, so that, the Gospel also being in agreement with Genesis, we may hold our inheritance according to the accord of both Testaments, and leave the quarrelsome slanders to the disinherited heretics.

**3.** Now it ought in no way to disturb your prudence that, although John the Evangelist did not say "All things were made in him" but *All things were made through him*[^7], we do not read in Genesis "Through the beginning God made heaven and earth" but *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^8]. For the Apostle says, *That he might make known to us the mystery of his will according to his good pleasure, which he set forth in him in the dispensation of the fullness of times, to restore all things in Christ, those that are in heaven and those that are on earth, in him*[^9]. Therefore, just as here you hear what he says, *in him*, in such a way that you understand also "through him," so in what John says, *All things through him*, you are compelled to understand also "in him." And just as here the understanding is not taken from me by which I understand that all things were made in him, since I read *through him*, so in Genesis, when I read that heaven and earth were made in him, who forbids me to understand also "through him"? Unless perhaps the Manichees transfer the dispute from the two Testaments and set it up among the most blessed witnesses of the New Testament, that is, between Paul and John, because the one says *in him* and the other *through him*. But just as we do not believe that Paul and John are opposed to each other, so too we compel them to confess the agreement of Moses and Paul.

**4.** And since, just as these two agree with each other, so also John agrees with both, because he said *through him* in such a way as not to forbid "in him" being understood, all the divine writings stand at peace among themselves. But as it usually happens that, when we gaze at passing clouds through the darkness of night, our sight is so confused by their murk that the stars seem to us to be running in the opposite direction, so these heretics, because in the cloud of their error they find no peace, think rather that divine Scripture is quarreling.

**5.** Perhaps they may say that *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^10] was not said of the Word of God. Grant that this is so: let what is written, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*, be understood as said not of the beginning who is the only Son of God, but of the beginning of time—not because time already existed before there was any creation (for no one would say that even time is coeternal with God, who is the founder of times), but so that time began together with heaven and earth. If, then, anyone understands it thus, recognizing at least the distance between creature and Creator, lest he say that what God made is coeternal with God who made it, certainly even in that word the number of persons will shine forth, where it is said, *Let us make man to our image and likeness*[^11], and, *God made man to the image of God*[^12]. And yet, even if it did not appear, and the Trinity were intimated to those who understand under the name of unity, the beginning of the Gospel ought not on that account to have seemed to the prudent to contradict the beginning of Genesis. For it could seem so only to the imprudent. For we have countless examples of such ways of speaking in the Scriptures. The Lord himself says, in speaking, *But I say to you, do not swear at all, neither by heaven, for it is God's throne, nor by the earth, for it is the footstool of his feet*[^13]. Will they perhaps, because he does not name himself there, deny that Christ sits in heaven? Likewise the Apostle says, *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and how inscrutable his ways! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that it should be repaid to him? For from him and through him and in him are all things. To him be glory forever and ever*[^14]. Here too no express mention of the Son is made. The Apostle says there is one God and Lord, from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things. Why then did these people choose Moses to set against John the Evangelist, but were unwilling to set the apostle Paul against him? Clearly because they wished to persuade unlearned people that the two Testaments are contrary, so as to use one as a witness and reject the other. For this is what their error professes. For if there were another whose equally insane fury tried to show to the unlearned that the New Testament itself was contrary to itself, what else would he do but set forth Paul and John, just as these set forth Moses and John, as enemies quarreling with each other? But just as the purest and truest faith commends the agreement of Paul and John, and in what blessed Paul says, *From him and through him and in him are all things*[^15], teaches us to understand not only the Father but also the Son and the Holy Spirit, so, looking at the peace of Moses and John, in what Moses said, *In the beginning God made heaven and earth*[^16], if he takes "beginning" as the beginning of time, he recognizes in the word *God* nothing else than the unity of the Trinity, or he unhesitatingly embraces as the "beginning" in which God made heaven and earth the Son himself. There are many other passages that we could call to mind according to these rules of the speech of the divine Scriptures. But lest we burden your Holiness's memory, it will suffice to have recalled these. We exhort you to seek out the rest for yourselves, or to take note when the Scriptures are read, and to consider and discuss them among yourselves in harmony.
