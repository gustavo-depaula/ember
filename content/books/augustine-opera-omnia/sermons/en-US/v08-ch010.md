# Sermon 281: On the Heavenly Birthday of the Martyrs Perpetua and Felicity

**1.** Among their fellow martyrs, both the merit and the name of Perpetua and Felicity, God’s holy servants, shine out and stand foremost. For where the sex is weaker, the crown is more glorious. Surely a manly spirit accomplished something greater in these women, since feminine frailty did not fail beneath so heavy a burden. They had clung well to the one husband to whom the one Church is presented as a chaste virgin. They had clung well, I say, to that husband from whom they drew the strength to resist the devil, so that women might overthrow the enemy who through a woman had overthrown a man. He who became weak for them showed himself unconquered in them. To reap them, he filled them with strength; to sow them, he emptied himself. He led them to these honors and praises, having endured insults and accusations for their sake. He made women die with manly courage and faithfulness, having mercifully deigned to be born of a woman for their sake.

**2.** It delights the devout mind to behold such a spectacle as blessed Perpetua related had been revealed to her about herself: she had become a man and fought with the devil. For in that contest she too was running toward perfect manhood, toward the measure of the age of Christ’s fullness. Fittingly, that ancient and crafty enemy, to leave no stratagem untried, having deceived a man through a woman, now tried to overcome her through a man, because he perceived a woman contending manfully with him. He did not put forward her husband, lest she, whose heavenly thoughts already made her home in heaven, grow stronger through shame at any suspicion of fleshly desire. Instead, he armed her father with deceptive words, so that the devout soul which would not soften at the prompting of pleasure might be broken by the force of filial affection. Then holy Perpetua answered her father with such restraint that she neither violated the commandment requiring honor for parents nor yielded to the tricks employed by the enemy behind him. Defeated on every side, the enemy had that same father beaten with a rod, so that, having disregarded his words, she might at least feel pain at his blows. She did indeed grieve at the injury to her aged father, and she preserved her affection for the man to whom she had refused her assent. For she hated his folly, not his nature; his unbelief, not her own parentage. With all the greater glory, then, she bravely resisted the harmful persuasion of a father so dearly loved that she could not see him beaten without sorrow. Thus that grief took nothing away from the strength of her courage and added something to the praise of her martyrdom. For *to those who love* *God, all things work together for good*.

**3.** Felicity, for her part, was pregnant even in prison. In giving birth she bore witness to her female condition with a woman’s cries. Eve’s punishment was not absent, but Mary’s grace was present. What the woman owed was being exacted; he whom the Virgin had borne was helping her. At last the child was born, mature though the month was premature. For God brought it about that the burden of the womb was laid down before its proper time, so that the honor of martyrdom would not be postponed beyond its proper time. God brought it about, I say, that the child was delivered before the appointed day, so that Felicity might nevertheless be restored as was fitting to so great a company. Had she been absent, it might have seemed that the martyrs lacked not only a companion but even the martyrs’ own reward. For the two women’s names expressed what is the gift of them all. Why do the martyrs endure everything, except to glory in perpetual felicity? Thus these women were named for that to which all are called. And so, although the company in that contest was large, the names of these two expressed the everlasting life of them all and marked the feast of them all.
