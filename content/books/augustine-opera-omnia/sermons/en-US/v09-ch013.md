# Sermon 312: On the Feast of Cyprian the Martyr

**1.** The solemnity of so gracious and joyful a day, and the festivity, so happy and pleasant, of the crown of so great a Martyr, demands the sermon I owe. But his prayers carry so great a burden with me, that if I render anything less than is owed, may he not despise me as I speak to you, but refresh all of you by praying for you. I will indeed do what I am certain is most welcome to him, that I praise him in the Lord, while concerning him I praise the Lord. For he was gentle, even when he sustained the perils of this turbulent and stormy life in various trials, and that man well knew how to sing to God with a truthful heart: *Let the meek hear, and be glad*[^1]. And now, having left the land of the dying, blessed he possesses the land of the living. For he was of those of whom it was said: *Blessed are the meek, for they will possess the land*[^2]. But what land, except that of which it is said to God: *You are my hope, my portion in the land of the living*[^3]? Or if the land of the living is nothing but the body of those who rise, taken from earth and changed into heavenly glory; he is no longer groaning in the weakness of this mortality, for whom to remain in the flesh was not best, but necessary for our sake; but loosed and freed from the bond both of debt and of chains, he awaits at rest with Christ the redemption of his body. For he who was not conquered by the trial of his living flesh is secure about the restoration of it when buried.

**2.** So let his soul be praised in the Lord, that the meek may hear and be glad. Let the good soul be praised in the Lord, in whose possession it becomes good, by whose inspiration it thrives, by whose illumination it shines, by whose forming it is beautiful, by whose filling it is fruitful. For when he deserts it, it once tossed about dead, dark, deformed, barren, before it had believed in Christ. For what did eloquence profit him, a pagan, by which as by a precious cup he both drank and proffered deadly errors? But when the kindness and humanity of our God the Savior shone forth, he cleansed him, believing in him, from worldly desires, and made him a vessel for honor, useful to his house, prepared for every good work. Nor did he, like an ungrateful man, keep silent about these things. For far be it that, knowing God, he should not glorify him as God: but he gave thanks, not impiously swallowing back his former things which he had vomited, but piously recalling the things he had changed. For writing to his friend, that he too from darkness, which is what was in him, might become light in the Lord: *I,* he says, *when I lay in darkness and in blind night, and when in the surge of a tossing world I wavered, unsteady and doubtful, straying with wandering steps, ignorant of my own life, a stranger to truth and light.* And a little after: *For as I myself,* he says, *was held entangled in the many errors of my former life, from which I did not believe I could be freed; so I was compliant to the vices clinging to me, and, in despair of better things, I cherished my evils as though now my own and homebred.*

**3.** Behold what sort of Cyprian Christ found: behold what sort of soul that uprooter and planter approached to strike and to heal. For not in vain does he say: *I will kill, and I will make alive; I will strike, and I will heal*[^4]; nor in vain, as a figure of things to come, was it said to Jeremiah: *Behold, I have set you today over the nations and kingdoms, to uproot, and to dig out, and to destroy, and to rebuild, and to plant*[^5]. So the uprooter and planter approached that soul; and he overturned the old Cyprian, and, having laid himself there as the foundation, he built the new Cyprian upon himself, and made the true Cyprian out of himself. For the Church says to Christ: *My beloved is a cluster of cyprus to me*[^6]. So when he was made a Christian by Christ, then truly he was also made Cyprian from cyprus. For he became the good fragrance of Christ in every place, as the apostle Paul says: he who was himself destroyed as a persecutor, and built up as a preacher. *We are,* he says, *the good fragrance of Christ to God in every place, both among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing: to some indeed a fragrance of life unto life, but to others a fragrance of death unto death. And who is sufficient for these things?*[^7] For some lived by imitating Cyprian: others perished by envying Cyprian.

**4.** To him be praise, to him glory, who, by justifying through faith the soul of his servant, rescued it from the impious, and made it his sword, that is, a twice-sharpened blade; so that through that tongue the folly of the Gentiles, laid bare, might be struck; through which before, covered and veiled, it seemed beautiful to the prudent; and so that the instrument of so noble an eloquence, by which unworthy ornaments were being made for the ruinous teachings of demons, might be turned to the building up of the Church, at whose growth those teachings would collapse; and so that the trumpet of so great a voice, which used to whet the contests of forensic lies, might rouse devoted martyrs warring for Christ and glorying in him, to lay low the devil by the precious deaths of the saints. Among whom was Cyprian himself, who by his godly and holy eloquence—no longer raising fabulous smoke, but radiant with the light of the Lord—was kindled; who by dying lived, judged he overcame the judge, struck he conquered the adversary, and killed he killed death. For he who in the school of human perversity had taught both his own and others' tongues to speak lies, so that what the adversary objected might be denied with cunning deceit, had now in another school learned by confessing to avoid the adversary. For where the enemy turned the name of Christ into a charge, there Christ turned the punishment into praise.

**5.** And if anyone still perhaps asks who has conquered, to pass over the heavenly kingdom of the saints, which unbelievers refuse to believe because they cannot see it; now in this earth, in this life, in houses, in fields, in cities, in the whole world, behold there are fervent praises of the martyrs: where are the raging accusations of the impious? Behold how the memorials of the slain are honored, now let those men show the idols of the demons. What will they do to them in judging, who by dying overturned their temples? How will he condemn their proud deceits by the splendor of his soldiers rising again, who extinguished their smoking altars by the blood of the dying?

**6.** Among these legions of Christ, the most blessed Cyprian, a teacher of glorious battles and himself a glorious warrior, so taught what he was to do, and did what he had taught; so that both in the words of the teacher the mind of the martyr might be known beforehand, and in the mind of the sufferer the words of the teacher might be recognized. For he was not like those of whom the Lord says: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do: for they say and do not do*[^8]. This man, because he believed, spoke; because he spoke, he suffered. So he taught in his life what he did; and he did in his death what he taught. To him be praise, to him glory, to the Lord our God, the king of the ages, the creator and re-creator of men, who enriched the Church of this city with such a bishop, and consecrated the spaciousness of this place with so holy a body. To him be praise, to him glory, who deigned to predestine that man among his saints before the ages, to create him among men at the opportune time, to call him wandering, to cleanse him filthy, to form him believing, to teach him obedient, to rule him teaching, to help him fighting, to crown him conquering. To him be praise, to him glory, who made such a man, in whom he especially showed his Church against how great evils, and before how great goods, love is to be set, and how no love belongs to a Christian by whom the unity of Christ is not kept. This unity he so loved, that for love's sake he did not spare the bad, and for peace's sake he tolerated the bad; both free in saying what he himself felt, and peaceable in hearing what he knew his brothers felt. Deservedly in the Catholic Church he merited the height of so great an honor, whose most harmonious bond he preserved with such humility. Wherefore, dearly beloved, having paid, according to my powers, the sermon owed to so gracious a festivity, I exhort your love and devotion that we pass this day honorably and soberly, and that we offer to the day on which the most blessed Cyprian suffered, that which he loved so that he might suffer it.
