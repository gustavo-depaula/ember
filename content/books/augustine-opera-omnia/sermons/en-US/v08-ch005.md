# Sermon 277: On the Feast of the Martyr Vincent

(*in Which There Is Discussion of the Spiritual Body After the Resurrection, and Whether God Can Be Seen with the Eyes of Such a Body*).

[Preached in the Restored Basilica]

**1. 1.** We have awaited the martyr contending with the eyes of faith, and we have loved the whole man, beautiful invisibly. For of what beauty he had a spirit, whose very corpse was unconquered! He confessed the Lord while living: he overcame the enemy even when dead. What do we think, brothers, that the providence and counsel of the almighty Creator, when it bestowed this honor even on his dead body, granted something to the martyr? For what? If he had not been buried, was the Creator ignorant of the source from which he would raise him up? To the martyr both the crown was preserved in victory and eternal life in the resurrection. But concerning his body, the memory of consolation was bestowed on the Church. So in many cases God, by a certain condescension, bestows from his servants upon his servants, granting what profits more the one to whom it is given than the one concerning whom it is given. So God fed holy Elijah by a bird: yet the mercy and omnipotence of God had not failed, that it might always feed him so. Yet he is sent to be fed to a widow[^1]: not because there was no way for the servant of God to be nourished, but so that the faithful widow might merit a blessing. He bestowed, then, the bodies of the saints on his churches for memorials of prayers, not for the glories of the martyrs. For they have their glory whole and entire with their Creator. Nor do they fear anything for their body, since there is nothing for them to fear. Rather, if they spare the body, they harm the body. But if through faith they do not spare the body, they benefit the body.

**2. 2.** Behold, attend to this, and question your faith. If Saint Vincent, out of dread of the torments, had denied Christ, he would have seemed to spare his body: yet by a certain mortal condition he would have been freed from his body. What would he do in the resurrection, when he was cast headlong into eternal fire? Denying Christ, he is denied by Christ. *Whoever denies me*, he says, *before men, I will deny him before my Father who is in heaven*[^2]. Behold, he might deny, those torturers might rest, and with the soul wounded the body would be sound; nay, with the soul slain, the body would live: what would the brief life of the body profit one who is dead forever? The day the Lord recalls would come, *when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice, and will come forth*[^3]: but with a great difference. All will come forth, but not all will come forth to the same thing. All will rise, but not all will be changed. *For those who have done good,* he says, *to the resurrection of life; but those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment*[^4]. When he says: *All who are in the tombs,* he manifestly declares the resurrection of bodies. But when you hear "judgment," do not flatter yourself as if it were a temporal judgment; "judgment" is put here for eternal punishment. According to this it is said: *But he who does not believe is already judged*[^5]. This difference, then, will separate the just from the unjust, the faithful from the unbelievers, the confessors from the deniers, the lovers of perishing life from the lovers of eternal life; that distinction will separate them. *And the just will go into eternal life, but the impious into eternal fire*[^6]. There they will be tormented with the body, who spared the body. For by fearing the torments of the body, they spared the body; and by sparing the body, they denied Christ; and by denying Christ, they deferred eternal punishments even for the body. If they deferred them, did they take them away?

**3. 3.** Therefore the martyrs of Christ prudently did not despise their bodies. That is a perverse and worldly philosophy, of those who do not believe in the resurrection of bodies. For they seem to themselves to be, as it were, great despisers of the body, because they suppose that they hold these very bodies as prisons, into which they think the souls have been thrust down, for what they sinned elsewhere beforehand. But our God made both body and spirit; and he is the creator of both, and the re-creator of both; the establisher of both, and also the restorer of both. The martyrs, then, did not despise the flesh as an enemy, nor persecute it. *For no one ever hated his own flesh*[^7]. Rather they took thought for it, when they seemed to neglect it: when, faithfully persisting in it, they endured temporal torments, they were also obtaining eternal glory for the flesh itself.

**4. 4.** But what will be the glory of this flesh in the resurrection—who can set it forth in words? No one of us has yet experienced it by possessing it. Now we bear burdensome flesh; because it is needy, because it is weak, because it is mortal, because it is corruptible. *For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul*[^8]. But do not fear this in the resurrection. *This corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^9]. What is now a burden will be an honor; what is now a load will then be a relief. For it will have no weight, such that you feel you have a body. See, dearly beloved: when our body is sound—even this fragile and mortal body—when it is regulated by the balance of its parts, when nothing in it quarrels against another; when heat does not overcome and oppress cold, when the warmth is not extinguished by an abundance of cold and, while it struggles, does not afflict; when dryness does not soak up moisture, nor moisture flood and oppress; but all the things of which it is composed are held in balance by a harmonious joining among themselves, this is what is called health. And health of the body is, to put it briefly, the concord of the things of which it is composed. This health, then—that is, the concord of the members and the humors—in a corruptible thing, in a needy and weak thing, in a thing that can still hunger and thirst, grow weary from standing, be refreshed by sitting, again grow weary from sitting, fail from hunger, be refreshed by eating; that cannot relieve past failings except by beginning others: for whatever else you take for refreshment when weary is the beginning of another weariness, since in the very thing you have taken to relieve yourself, if you persist, you will be wearied by that too: in this weak and corruptible body, then, what is health itself, of whatever kind? For this, which in mortal and corruptible flesh is called health, is in no way to be compared with the health of the angels, equality with whom is promised to us in the resurrection[^10].

**5. 4.** Yet this health, such as it is, as I said, has something of delight—what a desirable good it is to all? How great a thing the poor man has, when he has this alone; how great a thing the rich man does not have, when this alone he does not have? Why does he boast of his wealth? The fever does not dread a silver bed: it does not fear the pomp of the rich man, it does not fear the darts of the warrior.

**5. 5.** What then is this very health, which the martyrs rightly despised, because in the same flesh they hoped for another? Yet because we have not yet experienced that one, let us somehow form a conjecture of it from this one which we know. What is health? If you say to me, What is it to see? as far as the body is concerned, I will answer you perhaps: to perceive shapes and colors. If you say to me, What is it to hear? I will answer: to perceive sounds. If you say, What is it to smell? I will answer: to perceive odors. What is it to touch? To perceive things hard or soft, hot or cold, rough and smooth, heavy or light. What is health? To perceive nothing. But even these very senses in us are cheapened by comparison with others. You see keenly: an eagle perhaps sees more keenly than you. You hear keenly: there are little creatures that hear more keenly. You smell keenly: you do not surpass a keen-scented dog. You judge flavors keenly by tasting: there are animals that distinguish untried herbs, and do not touch what is harmful. For you, however keenly you distinguish your food, unwittingly rush into poison. You perceive keenly by touch: how many birds sense the coming summer, and change their places; sense the winter threatening, and migrate to warmer places? What you sense when it comes, they sense before it comes. And this very thing which I have praised in health—the stone senses nothing, the tree senses nothing, the corpse senses nothing.

**6. 6.** For did that governor Dacian sense nothing in his heart, when he raged against a corpse that sensed nothing? For what was he now doing to one who sensed nothing, who could be overcome even by one who sensed? Yet he did whatever he could, he did it in anger. But the one who now suffered nothing openly was being crowned in secret. For he held to the saying of his Lord: who, when he wished to make us secure about those who kill the body: *Do not*, he says, *fear those who kill the body, and after that have nothing more they can do*[^11]. How is it that afterward they have nothing more they can do, when that madman did so much to Vincent's body? But what did he do to Vincent, who did nothing even to him while he could feel? Therefore, not to feel nothing as the stone feels nothing, as the tree feels nothing, as the corpse feels nothing; but to live in the body and to feel nothing of its burden, this is to be sound. And yet, however sound a man may be in this life, he feels even the weight of a sound body. Even a sound body that is corrupted—that is, corruptible—weighs down the soul. It weighs down the soul, that is, it does not obey the soul at the beck of its every wish. It obeys in many things: it moves the hands to work, the feet to walk, the tongue to speak, the eyes to see; it directs the hearing to perceive sounds: in all these things the body complies. The desire to change place feels a burden, feels a weight: the body is not moved with such ease to arrive where it desires. Someone established in a body desires to see a friend established in a body; he knows him to be far off, many stages lie between; in mind he has already gone ahead, but when he arrives in body, then he feels what a burden he carries. The weight of the flesh could not obey the will with the speed he had presumed: it could not be carried off with the speed he wished, with which the mind carries it. It is slow and burdensome.

**7. 7.** Do we suppose that the body itself has anything by which the swiftness of the body may be proved? Shall we name the feet? What is slower? They are the very things that arrive, and they scarcely follow our desires, and only with effortful toil do they arrive. But make someone as swift as certain animals are, with whom our swiftness is not to be compared; make someone as swift as the birds are: he does not arrive with the stroke with which he wished. The birds fly a long while in migrating, and sometimes, wearied, they settle on the masts of ships. So, even if we could fly like birds, we would still be slow in comparison with the desire to arrive. But when the body will be spiritual, of which it is said: *The body is sown an animal body, it will rise a spiritual body*[^12]; how great will be the ease there, how great the swiftness, how great the obedience to the one who wills? In nothing weight, in nothing need, in nothing weariness, in nothing resistance and reluctance from any other quarter.

**8. 8.** What was that body like, which the Lord passed through closed barriers? Attend, I beg, whether I may, with the Lord's help, by whatever words, either satisfy your expectation or not fall far short of it. An occasion has arisen for us to discuss something about the spiritual body from the passion of the martyr, in whom we thus saw and marveled that the body was despised amid torments. For we said that, by not sparing the body, he was taking thought for that same body: lest perhaps, by fleeing temporal punishments and by denying Christ, he should consign that same body to everlasting punishments and most atrocious torments. Wishing therefore from this to exhort you, and myself, to despise present things and hope for future ones: *For in this dwelling we groan, being burdened*[^13], and yet we do not wish to die, and we dread to be stripped of our burden; *for we do not wish to be unclothed, but to be clothed over, that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life*[^14]: on this occasion, then, I have undertaken to say something to you about the spiritual body, and I thought I should first commend this very health of this fragile and corruptible body, so that from it we might discover something great. We discover in health itself that we feel nothing. For indeed we have many things within, in our inward parts; who of us would know them except by seeing them in mangled bodies? Our inward parts, our inner organs, which are called the intestines—how do we know of them? And then it is well, when we do not feel them. For when we do not feel them, then we are sound. You say to someone, Watch your stomach. He answers you, What is a stomach? Happy ignorance: he does not know where he has what he always has sound. If he did not have it sound, he would feel it: if he felt it, he would not know it for his own good.

**9. 9.** But although, with the body's health praised, we have come to the swiftness of motion, we find ourselves somehow leaden. How great is the swiftness of the heavenly bodies? Do you wish to know how great it is? You gaze at the sun, and it seems to you as though it does not move, and yet it moves. Perhaps you say: It moves, but more slowly. Do you wish to know with how great a swiftness it moves? Do you wish to gather by reason what you do not perceive by sight? If anyone were to run by post-horses on a straight road from the east to the west on this earth, in how many days would he arrive? By whatever swiftness of horses he were carried, how many stages would he make? The span of the journey from the beginning of the east to the end of the west, the sun, which seems to you to stand still, completes in one day, and in one night returns to the rising. I will not say—because it is obscure, and difficult to persuade, and perhaps uncertain—how much vaster the heavenly spaces are than the earthly. When, then, we see so great a swiftness of the heavenly bodies, which seem to us, when we attend to them, not to move; to what swiftness can we compare the angelic body? They have been present, too, and when they wished, they offered themselves to be seen and touched. Abraham washed the feet of angels[^15]. He not only washed those bodies, but even handled them. They appeared as they wished, when they wished, to whom they wished. They feel no difficulty, no slowness at all. But we do not see them running, we do not see them migrating from place to place, that we might know them departing from the eyes of men: when they wished, they arrived. There is no source, then, from which we may furnish even from these a most evident example of this swiftness. Let us pass over things unknown, and let us not dare to presume anything rashly about things we have not experienced.

**10. 10.** In this very body which we bear, I find something whose ineffable swiftness I may marvel at. What is this? The ray of our eye, by which we touch whatever we behold. For what you see, you touch with the ray of your eye. If you wish to see farther, and some body is interposed, the ray strikes against the body set before it, and is not allowed to pass through to what you desire to see; and you say to the obstacle, Get out, you stand in my way. You wish to see a column, a man stands in the middle, he hinders your gaze. Your ray has been sent out, but it reaches as far as the man, it is not permitted to reach as far as the column: it runs into something else, it is not allowed through. Behold, the one who stood in your way has departed from the middle, your gaze reaches where it wished. Now examine, and if you can, find and answer, whether this gaze, this ray of your eye, reaches more quickly to what is near, more slowly to what is far. You saw a man set nearby, you saw him with such a delay, you stretched the ray of your eye toward him with such a delay, you reached him with the ray of your eye with such a delay, as the delay with which you reach that column too which you wished to see, and could not because the man was interposed: not the man more quickly, and the column more slowly; and behold, the man was nearby, and the column farther off. If you wished to walk, you would arrive at the man more quickly than at the column: but because you wished to see, you arrived at the man just as quickly as at the column. This is nothing about the column and the man. Cast your eyes farther still: you see a wall far off. Send them farther: you reach the sun. How great is the distance between you and the sun? Who could measure those intervals? Who, by any sharpness of thought, could estimate how far the sun is from you? And yet, as soon as you have opened your eye, behold, you are here, your ray is there. As soon as you wished to see, you arrived by seeing. You did not seek out engines to rely on, nor ladders to climb, nor ropes to lift yourself, nor wings to fly. To have opened the eye is to have arrived.

**11. 11.** What then is this swiftness? How great is it? What does it mean? It belongs to our body, it is sent forth from our flesh. We have rays, and we do not marvel. We use them by seeing; we are astonished by considering them. You find nothing with which you may compare this swiftness, as far as bodily swiftness is concerned. Rightly did the apostle Paul compare to this swiftness the ease of the resurrection, saying: *In the twinkling of an eye*[^16]. The twinkling of an eye is not in the closing and opening of the eyelids: for this is done more slowly than it is seen. You raise the eyelid more slowly than you direct the ray. Your ray reaches heaven more quickly than the raised eyelid reaches the brow. You see what the twinkling of an eye is: you see what ease the Apostle has given to the resurrection of bodies. How slowly were these things created and formed? Let us recall the delays of conception, and in the very wombs of mothers the seeds of little ones coalescing; the limbs formed through a course of time, on fixed days, over many months, until what was created and formed within is brought forth into the light. Then with what delay it grows, with what delay boyhood gives way to adolescence, adolescence to youth, youth to old age, and old age to death for all. There comes also another delay: a fresh corpse is seen whole, then dissolves into rottenness; and for that very dissolution times are needed, until it melts away into decay, and dries into ashes: and from the very beginnings of the womb to the final ashes of the tomb, how great a delay lies between? How many days? What spans of time? It comes to rising again: in the twinkling of an eye it is restored.

**12. 12.** Attend then, brothers, and compare the things that are to be compared with the things with which they are to be compared. This flesh is moved more quickly in walking than it was formed, than it was nourished, than it grew, than it reached the bearing of youth and attained its full age and stature: it is moved more quickly in walking than this happens in it. And further, the resurrection will happen in the twinkling of an eye: how great will be the swiftness of moving, if so great could be the swiftness of rising? Bodies have been mangled by raging men: though the limbs of the dead be scattered through the whole world, though their ashes be dispersed through the whole earth; from the whole so vast bosom, all that has been scattered is restored in the twinkling of an eye. We marvel at the excessive—and, were we not to experience it, incredible—swiftness of our rays, which are poured forth from the eyes: more marvelous is the spiritual ease in the body that is to be. In the twinkling of an eye indeed it will rise: but our Lord, what the ray of our eye cannot do, passed his body even through closed barriers. After the resurrection, when his disciples were gathered in one place, he suddenly appeared, the doors being shut[^17]. Where we cannot see, he was able even to enter. Let no one say, This indeed the Lord's body could do; will mine then also be able to? And here receive full assurance from the Spirit, who was speaking through the Apostle. For it is said of the Lord himself: *Who will refashion the body of our lowliness, conformed to the body of his glory*[^18].

**13. 13.** Concerning such a body, then, concerning so great an ease, so great a swiftness, so great a soundness of this body, let human frailty dare to define nothing rashly and presumptuously. What we will be, we will know when we have come to be it. Before we are it, let us not be rash, lest we not be it. Human curiosity sometimes asks and says to itself: Do you suppose that through that spiritual body we will see God? It can indeed be answered at once: God is not seen in a place, God is not seen part by part, God is not seen as diffused through spaces and separated by intervals. Although he fills heaven and earth, he is not therefore half in heaven, half on earth. For this air, if it fills heaven and earth, the part of it that is in heaven is not on earth. And whatever the water fills, it fills indeed the space by which it is contained; but half of it is in half the space, half in the other half, the whole in the whole. God is not anything like this. Do not doubt this at all, that God is not a body. To be diffused through spaces, to be contained by places, to have halves, thirds, quarters, wholes—this is proper to bodies. God is nothing such; because everywhere God is whole: not half elsewhere, and elsewhere established with the other half; but everywhere whole. He fills heaven and earth: but he is whole in heaven, whole on earth. *In the beginning was the Word*[^19]. So that you may hear this same thing even of the Son himself, because the Son too with the Father is one God: not equal in mass, but in divinity. *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God: this was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^20]. And a little later: *And the light shines in the darkness*[^21]. This only-begotten one, remaining whole with the Father, shines whole in the darkness, whole in heaven, whole on earth, whole in the Virgin, whole in the infant; not at alternating times, as if by migrating from place to place. For you too are whole in your house, and whole in church: but when in church, not in your house; when in your house, not in church. Not so, then, is he whole in heaven, whole on earth, whole in the Virgin, whole in the infant (to mention nothing else), as if by migrating from heaven to earth, from earth to the Virgin, from the Virgin to the infant, but everywhere whole at once. For he is not poured out like water, nor, like earth, separated and transferred by effort. When he is whole on earth, he does not abandon heaven: but even when he fills heaven, he does not withdraw from earth. For *he reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^22].

**14. 14.** If, then, a substance that is not seen in a place could be seen by the bodily eyes even then, when the body will be spiritual; if they could see it by some hidden power, by some power untried and wholly unknown, and grasped by no estimation—if they could, let them. For we see with our eyes, we do not envy our own eyes. Only let us not try to bring God to a place, let us not try to enclose God in a place, let us not try to diffuse God through the spaces of places as though by some mass; let us not dare this, let us not think it. Let the substance of the divinity remain in its own proper dignity. Let us at least, as far as we can, be changed for the better, and not change God for the worse. Especially because we find nothing defined about this in Scripture, or have not yet found it. For neither do I dare to presume that there is not in it something that could be found. Either it is not there, or it is hidden, or it is hidden from me. If anything can be found by anyone on either side of the question, I gladly accept it, and unless I have given thanks for being instructed, I will be ungrateful—not to the man who speaks, but to him who teaches through the man. But God forbid that the giver of grace should permit me to be ungrateful. This only I say, that the eyes which through intervals of places see what they see—that is, that there is a space between the one seeing and that which is seen; for otherwise these eyes do not see; for if you remove something very far off from them, they do not see it, because the rays do not reach things set far away; but if you bring something closer to them, unless there be some interval between the eyes of the one seeing and the body that is seen, it cannot be seen at all; for if, by bringing it closer, you touch the very eyes by which something is seen, the vision is lost when the space is lost: this, then, I say, that such eyes, which see whatever they see only through intervals and spaces of places, can neither see God now, nor will they be able to then, because he is not in a place. Either, then, it will be something else that they can see, which cannot be seen in a place: or, if there remains in them the inability to see except in a place, they will not see him who is not in a place.

**15. 15.** But until this is more diligently inquired into concerning the spiritual body—something to be either understood or rightly believed—let us hold that the body will rise again, let us hold that the form of our body will be this future one, which Christ either displayed or in secret promised. Let us hold that the body will be spiritual, not animal, as it now is. For it is evidently expressed, and cannot be contradicted: *The body is sown an animal body, it will rise a spiritual body*[^23]. Let us hold that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, in their proper nature, in their proper substance, are equally and alike invisible, since equally and alike we believe them immortal, equally and alike we believe them incorruptible. And in one place the Apostle put all these things together: *To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, incorruptible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen*[^24]. God alone, Father and Son and Holy Spirit, immortal, invisible, incorruptible: not invisible now, and visible afterward; because not incorruptible now, and corruptible afterward. As immortal always, as incorruptible always, so also invisible always. If invisibility is changed, it is to be feared that immortality is changed. I think it is for this reason that the Apostle himself put "invisible" in the middle, between "immortal" and "incorruptible." About which there could be doubt, he fortified it on both sides, lest it could be overturned.

**16. 15.** Let us hold this unwavering confession. To stumble in regard to a creature and to stumble in regard to the Creator are not the same. Let us by all means inquire by discussion into the qualities of creatures, and if we are deceived in something, let us walk in that to which we have come. For if we think otherwise in anything, this too God will reveal to us[^25]. On which point we discoursed at greater length yesterday. *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^26]. Let us press on by every means to cleanse our hearts, let us be vigilant with every effort; let us obtain by every prayer, as much as we can, that we may cleanse our heart. And if we are thinking about the things that are outside: *Cleanse*, he says, *what is within, and what is without will be clean*[^27].

**16. 16.** Perhaps to someone the testimony about the flesh may seem as clear as that about the heart: because it is written: *All flesh shall see the salvation of God*[^28]. We have most evident testimony about the heart. *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* We have it also about the flesh: *All flesh shall see the salvation of God.* Now who would doubt that the vision of God is promised to the flesh, were it not that it gives one pause, what is the salvation of God? Indeed, because it does not give one pause—for we are not in doubt—the salvation of God is Christ the Lord. Therefore, if our Lord Jesus Christ were seen in his divinity alone, no one would doubt that the flesh would see the substance of God; because: *All flesh shall see the salvation of God.* But because our Lord Jesus Christ can be seen with the eyes of the heart, pure, perfect, full of God, as far as concerns his divinity; yet he was also seen in the body, according to what is written: *After this he was seen on earth, and conversed with men*[^29]: how do I know in what sense it was said: *All flesh shall see the salvation of God?* That it will see Christ, has been said: let no one doubt.

**17. 16.** But whether it will see the Lord Christ in the body, or as *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^30]—this is what is in doubt, this is what is asked. Do not press me with one testimony; I confess it at once: *All flesh shall see the salvation of God*[^31]. They say that this means, All flesh shall see the Christ of God. But Christ was also seen in the flesh—not indeed in mortal flesh, if that spiritual change is still to be called flesh; because he himself too, after the resurrection, said thus to those who saw and touched him: *Handle, and see, that a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see that I have*[^32]. He will be seen in that way too: not only has he been seen, but he will also be seen. And perhaps then will be more perfectly fulfilled what was said, *all flesh.* For now the flesh has seen him, but not all flesh: but then, when he comes in judgment with his angels to judge the living and the dead, when all who are in the tombs hear his voice and have come forth, some to the resurrection of life, others to the resurrection of judgment[^33], they will see that very form which he deigned to take up for us, not only the just but also the wicked, some on the right, others on the left; because even those who killed him *will see him whom they pierced*[^34]. *All flesh, then, shall see the salvation of God.* Body through body: because in a true body he will come to judge. But to those set on the right and sent into the kingdom of heaven, he will show himself in the way in which he was already seen in the body: and yet he was saying: *He who loves me will be loved by my Father; and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him*[^35]. This the impious Jew will not see. *For the impious one shall be taken away, that he may not see the glory of God*[^36].

**18. 17.** The just Simeon saw him both with his heart, because he recognized the infant; and he saw him with his eyes, because he carried the infant. Seeing him in both ways, acknowledging the Son of God, and embracing the one begotten of the Virgin: *Now you let your servant depart, O Lord, in peace, because my eyes have seen your salvation*[^37]. See what he said. For he was held until he should see with his eyes too the one whom he discerned by faith. He received the little body, he embraced the body; by seeing the body, that is, by discerning the Lord in the flesh, he said: *My eyes have seen your salvation.* How do you know that all flesh does not see the salvation of God in this way? And lest we despair that he will come to judgment in that form which he took up for us, not in that in which he always remained equal to the Father, let us hear from this point too the voice of the angels. Before the eyes of his disciples, as he was being carried up into heaven, and they were attending, and were following with their gaze the one whom they desired in heart, they heard from the angels: *Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking into heaven? This Jesus, who has been taken up from you, will so come as you have seen him going into heaven*[^38]. So, then, so will he come, as he departed into heaven. He will come visibly to judgment; because he departed visibly into heaven. For if he departed visibly and will come invisibly, how will he *so come?* But if he will so come, then he will come visibly; and *all flesh shall see the salvation of God*[^39].

**19. 18.** I have not said this (remember, as far as you can, that we learn in order to discover, until we have discovered, the things we do not yet know; but what we know, we have no need to learn, but rather, with the Lord's help, to teach): I have not said this, then, because I have denied that the flesh will see; but because clearer testimonies are to be sought, if perhaps they can be found. For this one that has been brought forward, you see what sort it is. For it tells more in our favor, or rather in favor of the truth itself, or in favor of those who contend as if for certain that the flesh will in no way at all see God, not even in the resurrection of the dead. We do not contend about this, but by repeating it we remind the sharp and impress it upon the slow. Although we may be tedious to many, we say it nonetheless. God is not seen in a place, because he is not a body; because he is everywhere whole, because he is not less in a part and greater in a part. Let us hold this most firmly. But if that flesh receives so great a change that through it can be seen what is not seen in a place, let it be so by all means. But it must be inquired from what source this is taught. And if it is not yet taught, let it not yet be denied; but at least let it be held in doubt. Yet in such a way that it not be doubted that the flesh will rise again, that the spiritual body will come to be from the animal body, that this corruptible and this mortal will put on immortality and incorruption: that *in that to which we have come, we may walk*[^40]. Certainly, if by inquiring too much we somewhere go astray, let us at least go astray in regard to the creature, not in regard to the Creator. Let each one strive, as much as he can, to turn his body into spirit, provided only that he does not turn God into a body.
