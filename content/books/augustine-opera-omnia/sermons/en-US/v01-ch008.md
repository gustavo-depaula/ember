# Sermon 7: On the Bush in Which There Was a Flame

And the Bush Was Not Consumed

A Sermon Delivered

During the Lenten Fast

**1.** When the divine reading was read, the great miracle that had made Moses, the servant of God, most attentive, we too awaited in our hearts, and we ourselves became attentive to how the fire appeared in the bush and the bush was not burned. Then we noted that holy Scripture first said that an angel of the Lord appeared to Moses in the bush. Then Moses was speaking not as though with an angel, but as though with the Lord. Third, we noted that, when Moses asked the name of God, that he might have what to say to the sons of Israel when they asked what was the name of the God who had sent him to them, he answered, *I am who am*. And, commending this not in passing but by repetition, he added, saying, *This you shall say to the sons of Israel: He who is has sent me to you*[^1]. Finally, having now declared his name, he still added and said, *This you shall say to them: The Lord God of your fathers, the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is my name forever*[^2]. Of these things, what the Lord grants, hear. They are indeed great and like volumes of the mysteries of God, which, if we should try to unroll fittingly and sufficiently, neither strength nor time would suffice.

**2.** What, then, we can say briefly, was not in vain, not idly, not without the signification of some secret. There was a flame in the bush, and the bush was not burned. For the bush is a certain kind of thorn, nor could anything be set to its praise which the earth brought forth for the sinner. For at the first it was said to sinning man, *The earth shall bring forth for you thorns and thistles*[^3]. Nor ought we to think that the very fact that the bush was not burned, that is, was not caught by the flame, is something good. For if the flame signifies something good, in which an angel appeared or the Lord—whence also, when the Holy Spirit came, there appeared to them divided tongues as of fire—we ought to be caught by this fire, and not, on account of hardness, to be unburned. The bush that was not burned signified the people who resisted God. The bush, then, to which Moses was sent, signified the thorny people of the Jews. And therefore the bush was not burned, because the hardness of the Jews, as I said, resisted the law of God. For if that thorny people were not signified, Christ would not have been crowned with thorns by it.

**3.** But that the same one who was speaking to Moses is called both an angel of the Lord and the Lord, is a great question, and ought not to have a rash affirmer, but a cautious inquirer. There are two opinions that can be put forward on this, of which, whichever be true, both are according to the faith. When I said which of them is true, I said this: which of them he meant who wrote it. For when we, inquiring into the Scriptures, perceive something that the writer perhaps did not mean, we nevertheless ought not to perceive what would shrink from the rule of faith, from the rule of truth, from the rule of piety. So I propose both opinions. There may perhaps be even a third that escapes me. But of these two proposed, choose whichever you wish. Some say that he was called both the angel of the Lord and the Lord, because he was Christ, of whom the Prophet plainly says that he is the *angel of great counsel*[^4]. For "angel" is a name of office, not of nature. For he is called in Greek "angel" who in Latin is called "messenger." "Messenger," then, is a name of action: by acting, that is, by announcing something, he is called a messenger. Who would deny that Christ announced to us the kingdom of heaven? Then an angel, that is, a messenger, is sent by him who through him announces something. And who would deny that Christ was sent? He who so often says, *I came not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me*[^5], is himself properly sent. For that pool of Siloam too *is interpreted Sent*[^6]. Therefore he whose eyes he anointed with clay, he ordered to wash his face there. For no one's eye is opened, except his who is cleansed by Christ. Therefore the same is angel, the same is Lord.

**4.** But here something else occurs to be guarded against. For there are not lacking heretics who say that the natures of the Father and the Son differ and are discordant, and that they are not of one and the same substance. But the Catholic faith believes that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God, a Trinity of one substance, inseparably, equally, not confused by mixture, not separated by distinction. Those, then, who labor to persuade people that the Son is not of the same substance of which the Father is, argue from this, that the Son was seen by the fathers. "The Father," they say, "was not seen; but the invisible and the visible are a different nature. And therefore," they say, "of the Father it was said, *Whom no man has seen, nor can see*[^7]; so that he who was seen not only by Moses but also by Abraham, not only by Abraham but also by Adam himself and the other fathers, is believed or understood to be not God the Father, but rather the Son, a creature." The Catholic faith does not say this. But what does it say? God the Father, God the Son; the Father unchangeable, the Son unchangeable; the Father eternal, the Son coeternal; the Father invisible, the Son invisible. For if you say the Father is invisible, the Son visible, you have distinguished, rather indeed separated, the substances. How have you found grace, you who have lost faith? This question, then, is resolved thus: God the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit is by his proper nature invisible. But he appeared when he willed, to whom he willed. Not as he is, but as he willed, he whom all things serve. For if your soul, since it is invisible in your body, in order to appear utters a voice, and the voice in which your soul appears when you speak is not the substance of your soul—the one is one thing, the voice is another, and yet it appears even in a thing which it itself is not—so too God, if he appeared in fire, is not fire; if he appeared in smoke, is not smoke; if he appeared in sound, is not sound. These are not God, but they indicate God. This being held, we securely believe that he who appeared to Moses could be called both the Son and the Lord and the angel of the Lord.

**5.** But those who think that he was truly an angel of the Lord, not Christ but an angel sent, an account must be demanded of them why he was called the Lord. For just as from those who say that he was Christ an account is demanded why he was called an angel, so from those who say he was an angel an account is demanded why he was called the Lord. But those who say he was Christ—I have already mentioned how they get out of it, why he was called an angel; because the Prophet plainly called the Lord Christ the angel of great counsel. Those, then, who say he was an angel, must answer why he was called the Lord. And they too answer: Just as in the Scriptures the Prophet speaks and it is said that the Lord speaks, not because the Lord is the prophet, but because the Lord is in the prophet, so too when the Lord deigns to speak through an angel, as through an Apostle, as through a Prophet, rightly he is called both an angel on account of himself and the Lord on account of the indwelling God. For certainly Paul was a man and Christ was God, and yet the Apostle himself says, *Do you wish to receive a proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^8]. The Prophet too said, *I will hear what the Lord God speaks in me*[^9]. He who speaks in a man, speaks also in an angel. Therefore the angel of the Lord appeared to Moses, and it is said to him, *Lord*, and he says, *I am who am*. It is the voice of the indweller, not of the temple.

**6.** For if he was Christ on the ground that, though called an angel, he was one, what shall we do when three appeared to Abraham? What do we say here? Three appeared, and Abraham, as though speaking to one, says, *Lord*[^10]. What do we say? Why three? Was it the Trinity itself? Why, then, Lord? Because the Trinity is one Lord, not three Lords, and the Trinity is one God, not three gods; one substance, three persons. For the Father is not the Son, nor is the Son the Father, nor is the Holy Spirit either the Father or the Son. But the Father is none but the Son's; the Son is none but the Father's; the Spirit is both the Father's and the Son's. Although in those three some say that one there was preeminent, whom Abraham called Lord, when he had appeared with two, as Christ with his angels. But what do we make of it, that when two were sent to Sodom, appearing to Lot, Abraham's brother, he too recognizes the divinity in them, and, though he sees two, calls them Lord? The one calls Lord in three, the other calls Lord in two. Let us not, then, separate the Trinity and make a duality in Sodom; I think we better understand that our fathers recognized the Lord in the angels, understood the indweller in his dwelling, and gave glory not to those bearing him but to the one seated within. This opinion is confirmed not only by the Epistle written to the Hebrews, where it is said, *For if the word spoken through angels was made firm*[^11] (for he was speaking of the Old Testament, he commended that there angels spoke but God was honored in his angels, and through the angels the inner indweller was heard), but also in the Acts of the Apostles Stephen says, arguing and rebuking the Jews, *Stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears—stiff-necked*, the thorns not burned—*you have always resisted the Holy Spirit*[^12]. Therefore the bush was not burned, because the Spirit of the flame was resisted by the thorns of sins. *You have always resisted the Holy Spirit. Which of the Prophets did your fathers not kill?*[^13]. And whence it follows, *You who received the law by the edicts of angels and did not keep it*[^14]. If he said "angels" and not *of angels*, there would not be lacking those who would say, "It is Christ," because he is called the *angel of great counsel*. Christ an angel—but is Christ angels? The apostle Paul too says that the seed of Abraham was dispensed from the Old Testament to the New. And how was it dispensed? *Ordained,* he says, *through angels by the hand of a Mediator*[^15].

**7.** Now then, an angel, and in the angel the Lord, said to Moses asking his name, *I am who am. This you shall say to the sons of Israel: He who is has sent me to you*[^16]. "To be" is a name of unchangeableness. For all things that are changed cease to be what they were and begin to be what they were not. To be, is. True being, sincere being, genuine being, none has but he who is not changed. He has true being to whom it is said, *You shall change them and they shall be changed, but you are the same*[^17]. What is, *I am who am*, except, I am eternal? What is, *I am who am*, except, I am he who cannot be changed? No creature, not heaven, not earth, not Angel, not Virtue, not Throne, not Dominions, not Powers. Since, then, this is the name of eternity, it is more that he deigned to have the name of mercy: *I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob*[^18]. That in himself, this toward us. For if he willed to be only what he is in himself, what would we be? If Moses understood—rather, because Moses understood, when it was said to him, *I am who am; he who is has sent me to you*—he believed this to be much for men, he saw this to be much distant from men. For whoever has worthily understood this which is and truly is, and has been breathed upon by some light of the truest essence, even briefly as by a flash, sees himself far beneath, far most remote, far most unlike, as he says, *I said in my ecstasy*[^19]. For with his mind taken up he saw something—I know not what—that was more toward him. And this was what was true. *I said,* he says, *in my ecstasy*. What? *I am cast away from before your eyes*[^20]. Since, then, Moses saw himself far unequal to that which was said, not that which was seen, and as it were less capable, whence, inflamed with that very desire of seeing what is, he said to the God with whom he spoke, *Show me yourself*[^21]. As though, then, far unlike that excellence of essence, he despaired, God raises him in his despair, since he saw him afraid, as though saying: Since I said, *I am who am*, and, *He who is has sent me*, you understood what it is to be, and you despaired of grasping it. Raise your hope: *I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and of Jacob*. I am so what I am, I am so being itself, that I would not fail men. If in any way we can seek God and search out him who is, and indeed not *placed far from each one of us: For in him we live and move and are*[^22]; let us therefore praise his essence ineffably and love his mercy.
