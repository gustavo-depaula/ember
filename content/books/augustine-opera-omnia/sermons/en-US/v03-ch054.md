# Sermon 88: On the Words of the Gospel, Matthew 20:30-34: Where It Tells of the Two Blind Men Sitting Beside the Road and Crying Out, "Lord, Have Mercy on Us, Son of David"

**1. 1.** Your Holiness well knows, along with us, that our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ is the physician of our eternal salvation; and that he took on the infirmity of our nature for this purpose, that our infirmity might not be everlasting. For he assumed a mortal body, in which to slay death. *And although he was crucified out of our weakness,* as the Apostle says; *yet he lives by the power of God*[^1]. These too are the words of the same Apostle: *And because he now dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^2]. These things, then, are well known to your faith. At the same time, this too follows, that we should know that all the miracles he did bodily are of avail for our admonition, that we may receive from him what is not going to pass away nor have an end. He gave eyes back to the blind, which death was at any rate someday going to close; he raised up Lazarus, who was to die again. And whatever he did for the health of bodies, he did not do that they might be everlasting—though in the end he is going to give everlasting health even to the body itself. But because those things that were not seen were not believed, through these temporal things that were seen he built up faith toward those that were not seen.

**2. 2.** Let no one, therefore, brothers, say that our Lord Jesus Christ does not do these things now, and on this account prefer the former times to the present times of the Church. For in a certain place the same Lord prefers, to those who see and therefore believe, those who do not see and yet believe[^3]. For at that time the weakness of his disciples wavered so far that, though they saw him already risen, they thought he must even be touched, that they might believe. It did not suffice their eyes that they saw, unless their hands were also applied to his members, and the scars of the recent wounds were touched: so that that disciple who doubted, suddenly, when the scars were touched and recognized, cried out: *My Lord and my God*[^4]! The scars revealed him who had healed all wounds in others. Could not the Lord have risen without scars? But he knew the wounds in the disciples' heart, and to heal them he kept the scars on his body. And what did the Lord say to that one now confessing and saying, *My Lord and my God? Because you have seen me,* he says, *you have believed: blessed are those who do not see, and believe*[^5]. Whom did he mean, brothers, but us? Not because us alone, but also those after us. For after a little time, after he withdrew from mortal eyes that faith might be strengthened in hearts, whoever believed, believed without seeing, and their faith had great merit; and to attain this faith they applied only a devout heart, not also a touching hand.

**3. 3.** These things, then, the Lord did, to invite us to faith. This faith now is fervent in the Church, spread throughout the whole world. And now he works greater healings, on account of which he did not disdain then to display those lesser ones. For as the mind is better than the body, so also the health of the mind is better than the health of the body. Now blind flesh does not open its eyes by a miracle of the Lord; and a blind heart opens its eyes to the word of the Lord. Now a mortal corpse does not rise again; but a soul rises again, which lay dead in a living corpse. Now the deaf ears of the body are not opened; but how many have the ears of the heart closed, which nevertheless open at the penetrating word of God, so that those believe who did not believe, and those live well who lived badly, and those obey who did not obey? And we say, That man has believed; and we marvel, when we hear of those whom once we knew to be hard. Why, then, do you now marvel at one believing, innocent, serving God, except that you behold seeing one whom you knew to be blind; you behold living one whom you knew to be dead; you behold hearing one whom you knew to be deaf? For see the dead in another way, of whom the Lord said to a certain man who was therefore delaying to follow the Lord because he wanted to bury his father: *Let,* he said, *the dead bury their own dead*[^6]. Surely the buriers of the dead are not dead in body, for if they were this, they could not bury dead bodies. Yet he calls them dead: where, if not within, in the soul? For just as, even visibly, the master of a house often lies dead in his house, intact and sound, so in an intact body many have a dead soul within: and these the Apostle thus rouses: *Rise, you who sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ will enlighten you*[^7]. He himself enlightens the blinded who rouses the dead. For by his voice through the Apostle it is cried to the dead man: *Rise, you who sleep.* The blind man will be enlightened by the light when he has risen. But how many deaf men did the Lord behold before his eyes, when he said: *He who has ears to hear, let him hear*[^8]. For who stood before him without the ears of the body? What other ears, then, was he requiring but those of the inner man?

**4. 4.** Likewise, what sort of eyes was he seeking, when he was speaking certainly to those who saw, but who saw through the eyes of the flesh? For when Philip said to him: *Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^9]: he understood this well, that the Father, once shown, could suffice; but to one for whom the equal of the Father did not suffice, how would the Father suffice? But why did he not suffice? Because he was not seen. Why was he not seen? Because the eye was not yet healthy, by which he could be seen. For this which was seen in the Lord's flesh by these eyes was seen not only by the disciples who honored him, but also by the Jews who crucified him. He, then, who wanted to be seen in another way, was requiring other eyes. And therefore he answered thus to the one saying, *Show us the Father, and it is enough for us: So long a time I am with you, and you have not known me? Philip, he who has seen me, has seen the Father also*[^10]. And that he might meanwhile heal the eyes of faith, he is first admonished according to faith, that he might be able to come to sight. And lest Philip should think that God must be conceived as he saw the Lord Jesus Christ in the body, he immediately added: *Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me*[^11]? He had said a moment before: *He who has seen me, has seen the Father also.* But Philip did not yet have a healthy eye, by which he might see the Father; and therefore not even that by which he might see the Son himself coequal to the Father. So he took up his mind's gaze, still wounded and not able to look upon so great a light, to be healed and strengthened with the medicines and poultices of faith, and said: *Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me?* He, then, who cannot yet see what the Lord is going to show, let him not seek to see first what he believes; but let him first believe, that the eye may be healed by which he sees. For only the form of a servant was displayed to servile eyes: because he who did not consider it robbery to be equal to God, if by those whom he wanted to heal he could already be seen as equal to God, would not have needed to empty himself and take the form of a servant[^12]. But because there was no means by which God might be seen, and there was a means by which man might be seen; he who was God was made man, that the thing that was seen might heal that by which he was not seen. For he himself says in another place: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^13]. Philip could surely have answered and said, Lord, behold I see you: is the Father such as is this which I see, since you said: *He who has seen me, has seen the Father also?* Before Philip answered this, or perhaps before he thought it, when the Lord had said: *He who has seen me, has seen the Father also,* he at once subjoined: *Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father in me?* For with that eye he could not yet see either the Father or the Son equal to the Father: but that the eye might be healed for seeing, it had to be anointed for believing. Therefore, before you see what you cannot see, believe what you do not yet see. Walk by faith, that you may come to sight. Sight will not gladden in the homeland the one whom faith does not console on the way. For the Apostle says thus: *As long as we are in the body, we are away from the Lord*[^14]. And he at once adds why we are still away, although we have already believed: *By faith,* he says, *we walk, not by sight*[^15].

**5. 5.** Our whole work in this life, then, brothers, is to heal the eye of the heart, by which God is seen. For this the sacrosanct mysteries are celebrated; for this the word of God is preached; for this the Church's moral exhortations are given—that is, those pertaining to the correcting of morals, to the amending of carnal desires, to renouncing this world not by voice only, but by a changed life; for this the divine and holy Letters do whatever they do: that that inner part may be cleansed of the thing that hinders us from the sight of God. For as the eye, made to see this temporal light—and, though heavenly, yet corporeal and visible not only to men but even to the basest animals (for it was made for this, to see this light)—yet, if something is thrown in, or rushes in, by which it is troubled, is shut off from this light; and although they surround it with their presence, it nevertheless turns itself away and is absent: and it is not only made absent by its own disturbance from the present light, but the very light to see which it was made is even painful to it: so also the eye of the heart, disturbed and wounded, turns itself away from the light of righteousness, and neither dares to contemplate it nor is able.

**6. 6.** What troubles the eye of the heart? Desire, greed, iniquity, worldly concupiscence troubles, closes, blinds the eye of the heart. And yet how a physician is sought when the eye of the flesh is troubled, how it is not deferred that it be opened and cleansed, that it be healed so that this light may be seen! There is running about, no one rests, no one delays, if even a straw falls into the eye. The sun, certainly, which we want to see with healthy eyes, God made. He who made it is surely much more shining: nor is it light of this kind that pertains to the eye of the mind. That light is eternal wisdom. But God made you, O man, to his own image. Would he give you a means by which to see the sun he made, and not give you a means by which to see him who made you, since he made you to his own image? He gave this too: he gave you both. But you love these outer eyes much, you neglect that inner one much: you carry it worn down and wounded. It is a pain to you if your maker wills to show himself: it is a pain to your eye, before it is cured and healed. For in paradise too Adam sinned, and hid himself from the face of God. When, then, he had a healthy heart of pure conscience, he rejoiced at the presence of God: but after that eye was wounded by sin, he began to dread the divine light, he fled into the darkness and into the thicket of trees, fleeing the truth, seeking the shadows.

**7-8. 7.** Therefore, my brothers, since we too were born from there, and, as the Apostle says: *In Adam all die*[^16]—for all of us were once two men—if we were unwilling to obey the physician so as not to fall sick, let us obey, that we may be freed from sickness. The physician gave us precepts while we were healthy; the physician gave us precepts, that we might not need a physician. *The healthy,* he says, *do not need a physician, but the sick*[^17]. Healthy, we despised the precepts, and by experience we felt into how great a ruin of ours we had despised that precept. We have now begun to be sick, we toil, we are on the bed of infirmity: but let us not despair. For because we could not come to the physician, he himself deigned to come to us. He did not despise the wounded man, despised by the healthy. He did not cease to give other precepts to the languishing man, who refused to keep the first ones lest he languish: as though saying: Surely you have felt by experience that I spoke truly when I said: Do not touch this. Be healed, then, at last, and live again. Behold, I bear your infirmity: drink the bitter cup. For you made for yourself those precepts of mine, which, given to a healthy man, were so sweet, so laborious. They were despised, you began to toil: you cannot be healed unless you drink the bitter cup, the cup of temptations, with which this life abounds, the cup of tribulations, straits, sufferings. Drink, he says, drink, that you may live. And lest the languishing man answer him, I cannot, I cannot bear it, I will not drink: the physician, healthy, drank first, that the sick man might not hesitate to drink. For what is bitter in such a cup that he did not drink? If insult: he heard first, when he was expelling demons: *He has a demon*[^18], and that *in Beelzebub he casts out demons*[^19]. Whence, that he might console the sick, he said: *If they called the head of the household Beelzebub, how much more his household members*[^20]? If pains are bitter: he was bound and scourged and crucified. If death is bitter: he even died. If the manner of death is dreaded by weakness: nothing in that time was more ignominious than the death of the cross. For not without reason did the Apostle, commending his obedience, add, saying: *Made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross*[^21].

**9. 8.** But because he was going to honor his faithful at the end of this age, he first honored the cross in this age; so that the princes of the earth, believing in him, should forbid any of the guilty to be crucified: and what the persecuting Jews procured for the Lord with great insult, his servants now bear with great confidence, and even kings on their forehead. It is now apparent of what kind a death the Lord deigned to undergo for us: as the Apostle says: *Made a curse for us*[^22]. And when the blindness of the Jews insulted him hanging there, he could certainly have come down from the cross, who, if he had not willed it, would not have been on the cross: but it was a greater thing to rise from the tomb than to come down from the cross. These things, then, the Lord, doing divine things and suffering human things, admonishes us by bodily miracles and bodily patience, that we may believe and be healed for beholding those invisible things that the eye of the flesh does not know. Doing this, then, he cured these blind men, of whom now the Gospel was read out. And in curing them, see what he admonished in the inner sick man.

**10. 9.** Attend to the outcome of the deed itself and the order of things. Those two blind men sitting by the road cried out, as the Lord passed by, that he might have mercy on them. But by the crowd that was with the Lord they were checked, that they might not cry out. And do not think this was left without a mystery. But they overcame the crowd that checked them by a most persevering cry, that their voice might reach the ears of the Lord: as though he had not already anticipated their thoughts. The two blind men, then, cried out, to be heard by the Lord, and could not be suppressed by the crowds. The Lord was passing by, and they were crying out. The Lord stood still, and they were healed. For *Jesus stood still, and called them, and said: What do you want me to do for you? But they said: That our eyes be opened*[^23]. For their faith the Lord acted, and restored their eyes. If we have now understood the inner sick man, the inner deaf man, the inner dead man, let us seek there also the inner blind man. The eyes of the heart are closed: Jesus passes by, that we may cry out. What is it, Jesus passes by? Jesus does temporal things. What is it, Jesus passes by? Jesus does transitory things. Attend and see how many of his deeds have passed by. He was born of the Virgin Mary: is he always being born? As an infant he was suckled: does he always nurse? Through the ages he ran up to young manhood: does he always grow bodily? Boyhood succeeded infancy, adolescence boyhood, youth adolescence, as it passed and gave way. Even the very miracles he did have passed by: they are read and believed. For because such things were written that they might be read, they were passing by as they were being done. Finally, not to linger over many things, he was crucified: does he always hang on the cross? He was buried, he rose again, he ascended into heaven: now he dies no more, and *death will no longer have dominion over him*[^24]: and his divinity always abides, and the immortality even of his body will now never fail. But still all those things that were done by him temporally have passed by; and they are written to be read, and preached to be believed. In all those things, then, Jesus passed by.

**11. 10.** What are the two blind men beside the road, but two peoples, to heal whom Jesus came? Let us show these two peoples in the holy Scriptures. It is written in the Gospel: *I have other sheep that are not of this fold; and them too I must bring, that there may be one flock and one shepherd*[^25]. Who, then, are the two peoples? One of the Jews, and the other of the gentiles. *I am not sent,* he says, *except to the sheep that have perished of the house of Israel*[^26]. To whom did he say this? To the disciples: when that Canaanite woman was crying out, who confessed herself to be a dog, that she might deserve the crumbs from the masters' table. And because she deserved it, the two are now shown to whom he had come: namely the Jewish people, of whom he says: *I am not sent except to the sheep that have perished of the house of Israel:* and the people of the gentiles, whose type this woman bore forth, whom he had at first rejected, saying: *It is not good to throw the children's bread to the dogs*[^27]: and to whom, when she said: *Yes, Lord, for even the dogs eat of the crumbs that fall from their masters' table,* he had answered: *O woman, great is your faith; be it done to you as you wish*[^28]. From there too was that centurion, of whom the same Lord says: *Amen I say to you, I have not found such great faith in Israel*[^29]. Because he had said: *I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof; but only say the word, and my servant will be healed*[^30]. Thus, then, the Lord, before his passion and glorification, was already designating two peoples: one to whom he had come on account of the promises of the Fathers; and another whom on account of mercy he did not reject: that what was promised to Abraham might be fulfilled: *In your seed all nations shall be blessed*[^31]. Therefore the Apostle too, after the resurrection and ascension of the Lord, where he was despised by the Jews, went on to the gentiles. Nor yet was he silent to the Churches that had believed from among the Jews: *I was,* he says, *unknown by face to the Churches of Judea that are in Christ. But they only heard that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith that he once laid waste: and in me,* he says, *they glorified God*[^32]. So too Christ is called a cornerstone, *who has made both one*[^33]. For a corner joins two walls coming from different directions. What is so different as circumcision and uncircumcision, having one wall from Judea, the other wall from the gentiles? But they are joined by the cornerstone: for *the stone that the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner*[^34]. There is no corner in a building, except when two walls coming from different directions meet into one, and are joined by a certain unity. These two walls, then, according to the type, were the two blind men crying out to the Lord.

**12. 11.** Attend now, most beloved. The Lord was passing by, the blind men were crying out. What is it, he was passing by? He was doing transitory works, as we already said. According to these transitory works our faith is built up. For we believe in the Son of God, not only because he is the Word of God, through whom all things were made: for if, always remaining in the form of God, equal to God, he had not emptied himself, taking the form of a servant[^35]; the blind men would not have perceived him, so as to be able to cry out. But when he was working transitory things—that is, humbling himself, made obedient unto death, even the death of the cross—the two blind men cried out: *Have mercy on us, Son of David*[^36]. For this very thing, that the Lord and creator of David willed to be also the son of David, he did in time, he did this passing by.

**13. 12.** But what is it to cry out to Christ, brothers, except to accord with the grace of Christ by good works? I say this, brothers, lest perhaps we be making a din with our voices, and be mute in our morals. Who is it who cries out to Christ, that the inner blindness may be driven off as Christ passes by—that is, as he dispenses to us the temporal sacraments, by which we are admonished to lay hold of the eternal? Who is it who cries out to Christ? He who despises the world cries out to Christ. He who spurns the world's pleasures cries out to Christ. He who says, not with the tongue, but with his life: *The world is crucified to me, and I to the world*[^37], cries out to Christ. He who *scatters abroad and gives to the poor, that his righteousness may abide for ever and ever*[^38], cries out to Christ. For he who hears, and hears not as a deaf man: *Sell your possessions, and give to the poor; make for yourselves purses that do not grow old, a treasure that does not fail, in heaven*[^39]: as though hearing the sound of the footsteps of Christ passing by, let the blind man cry out at these things, that is, let him do these things. Let his voice be in his deeds. Let him begin to despise the world, to distribute to the needy what is his, to count as nothing what men love; let him despise injuries, not seek to be avenged, offer his cheek to one who strikes, pray for his enemies; if anyone takes away his goods, let him not demand them back; if he has taken anything from anyone, let him restore fourfold.

**14. 13.** When he begins to do these things, all his kinsmen, relatives, and friends are stirred up. Those who love the world contradict him. Why are you mad? You are excessive: are not others Christians too? This is foolishness, this is madness. And other such things the crowd cries out, that the blind men may not cry out. The crowd rebuked those crying out: but it did not overcome their cries. Let those understand what they do who want to be healed. And now Jesus passes by: let those who are beside the road cry out. For these are they who honor with their lips, but their heart is far from God[^40]. They themselves are beside the road, to whom the Lord gives precepts, with their hearts crushed. For when the things that the Lord did, transitory, are recited, Jesus is always shown to us passing by. For until the end of the age there is no lack of blind men sitting by the road. There is need, then, that those sitting by the road cry out. The crowd that was with the Lord checked the cry of those seeking health. Brothers, do you see what I am saying? For I do not know how to say it; but still less do I know how to keep silent. This I say, and openly I say it. For I fear Jesus passing by and abiding: and therefore I cannot keep silent. Good Christians, truly zealous, wanting to do the precepts of God that are written in the Gospel, are forbidden by bad and lukewarm Christians. That very crowd that is with the Lord forbids those crying out; that is, it forbids those who work well, lest by persevering they be healed. Let those cry out, let them not fail, nor be led as it were by the authority of the crowds; nor let them imitate those who were made Christians earlier, living badly and envious of good works. Let them not say: As these many live, so let us live. Why not rather as the Gospel says? Why do you want to live according to the rebuke of the forbidding crowd, and not according to the footsteps of the Lord passing by? They will insult, they will vituperate, they will call you back: you, cry out until you reach the ears of Jesus. For those who persevere in doing such things as Christ commanded, and have not heeded the forbidding crowds, nor set great store by the fact that they seem to follow Christ, that is, that they are called Christians; but have loved more the light that Christ is going to restore than they have feared the din of those forbidding: in no way will they be separated, and Jesus will stand still, and will heal them.

**15. 14.** For how are our eyes healed? As by faith we perceive Christ passing by in temporal dispensation, so let us understand Christ standing still in unchangeable eternity. For there the eye is healed, when the divinity of Christ is understood. Let your Charity grasp this: attend to what I will say, a great sacrament. All the things done temporally by our Lord Jesus Christ engraft faith in us. We believe in the Son of God, not only in the Word through whom all things were made; but in that very Word made flesh, that he might dwell among us, born of the Virgin Mary, and the rest that faith holds, which were displayed to us, that Christ might pass by, and that the blind, hearing the footsteps of the one passing by, might cry out by their works, calling to mind by their life the profession of faith. Now, that those crying out may be healed, Jesus stands still. For he already sees Jesus standing still who says: *Even if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no longer*[^41]. For he was seeing the divinity of Christ, as far as one can in this life. There is the divinity of Christ, there is the humanity of Christ. The divinity stands still, the humanity passes by. What is it, The divinity stands still? It is not changed, it is not shaken, it does not withdraw. For he did not so come to us as to depart from the Father; nor so ascend as to be moved from place. By assuming flesh, he changed place: but God, assuming flesh, because he is not in a place, does not change place. Let us be touched while Christ stands still, let our eyes be healed. But whose eyes? Those of the ones crying out, of course, while he passes by: that is, of those working well through that faith which was dispensed temporally for instructing us little ones.

**15. 15.** But with our eyes healed, what more precious thing can we have, brothers? Those who see this light that was made, which shines from heaven, or which is displayed from a lamp, rejoice. And how wretched do those seem who cannot see it? But why do I speak, why do I say these things, except to exhort all of you to cry out when Jesus passes by? I commend to your Holiness the light that is to be loved, which perhaps you do not see. Believe, while you do not yet see; and cry out, that you may see. How great an unhappiness of men is it thought to be, who do not see this bodily light? Someone is blinded: at once it is said, He had God angry with him, he committed some evil. This Tobit's wife said to her husband. He was crying out about the kid, lest it be from theft; he did not want to hear the sound of theft in his house: she, defending her deed, struck her husband with a reproach: and when he said, Give it back, if it is from theft; she answered insultingly: *Where are your acts of righteousness*[^42]? How blind she was, who was defending theft: and what a light he was seeing, who ordered the theft to be given back! She was outside in the light of the sun; he was within in the light of righteousness. Which of them was in the better light?

**16. 16.** To love this light, brothers, we exhort your Love; that you may cry out by your works when the Lord passes by: let the voice of faith sound; that Jesus standing still—that is, the unchangeably abiding Wisdom of God, and the majesty of the Word of God, through whom all things were made[^43]—may open your eyes. This same Tobit, admonishing his son, admonished him to this, to cry out: that is, he admonished him to good works. He told him to give to the poor, he charged him to do alms for the needy, and he taught, saying: *Son, alms do not allow one to go into the darkness*[^44]. The blind man was giving counsel for receiving and obtaining light. *Alms,* he says, *do not allow one to go into the darkness.* If the son were to answer him, marveling, What then, father, did you not do alms, that you now speak as a blind man? Are you not now in the darkness, who say to me: *Alms do not allow one to go into the darkness?* He knew of what light he was teaching his son, he knew in the inner man what he was seeing. The son held out his hand to his father, that he might walk on the earth; and the father to the son, that he might dwell in heaven.

**16. 17.** Briefly, to conclude this sermon, brothers, from that which most touches and distresses us, see that there are crowds that rebuke the blind men crying out. All you who in this crowd want to be healed, let them not deter you: because there are many Christians in name and impious in works; let them not deter you from good works. Cry out amid the crowds that check, recall, insult, and live badly. For the bad suppress good Christians not only with their voices, but also with their bad works. A good Christian does not want to go to watch the games. This very thing, that he reins in his desire, lest he go to the theater, is crying out after Christ, is crying out to be healed. Others rush there, but perhaps pagans, perhaps Jews. Indeed, there would be so few in the theaters that they would leave in shame, if Christians did not go to the theaters. So those too run, carrying the holy name to their own punishment. Cry out, then, by not going, pressing down in your heart your temporal desire; and hold yourself in a strong and persevering cry to the ears of the Savior, that Jesus may stand still and cure you. Cry out amid the very crowds; do not despair of the ears of the Lord. For those blind men too did not cry out from that side where the crowd was not, so as to be heard from the side where there was no hindrance of those forbidding. They cried out amid the very crowds: and yet the Lord heard them. So you too, even amid sinners and the self-indulgent, amid lovers of worldly vanities, cry out there, that the Lord may heal you. Do not cry out to the Lord by another route, do not go to the heretics and there cry out to the Lord. Attend, brothers, that in that crowd which was forbidding them to cry out, there those were healed who were crying out.

**17. 18.** For let your Holiness attend to this too, what it is to persevere in crying out. I will say what many have experienced with me in the name of Christ: for the Church does not cease to bear such men. When any Christian has begun to live well, to be fervent in good works, and to despise the world; in the very newness of his works he suffers reproachers and contradictors, lukewarm Christians. But if he has persevered, and has overcome them by holding out, and has not failed in good works; those same men now comply, who before were forbidding. For they rebuke and disturb and forbid only so long as they presume that they can be yielded to. But if they are conquered by the perseverance of those making progress, they turn themselves around, and begin to say: A great man, a holy man: happy is he to whom God has granted it. They honor, congratulate, bless, praise: like that crowd which was with the Lord. It was forbidding the blind men to cry out; but after they had so cried out as to deserve to be heard and to obtain the mercy of the Lord, that same crowd in turn says: *Jesus calls you*[^45]. Now they become exhorters, who a little before were rebuking them to be silent. But that man alone is not called by the Lord who does not labor in this age. But who in this life does not labor in his sins and iniquities? But if all labor, it is said to all: *Come to me, all you who labor*[^46]. But if it is said to all, why do you assign your fault to the one who invites you? Come. His house is not made narrow for you: equally by all, wholly by each, the kingdom of God is possessed; with the number of possessors growing it is not lessened, because it is not divided. To each it is whole, what is held in concord by many.

**18. 19.** This, however, in the mystery of this reading we have learned, brothers, which in other places of the holy Books sounds most plainly, that there are within the Church both good and bad, which we often call the grain and the chaff. Let no one before the time leave the threshing floor; let him tolerate the chaff in the threshing, let him tolerate it on the floor. For what he does not tolerate, he will not have in the granary. The winnower will come, who will divide the bad from the good. There will be even a bodily separation, which now a spiritual one precedes. From the bad in heart always be disjoined; for the time, be cautiously joined in body. Yet do not be negligent in correcting your own people—those, namely, who in any way pertain to your care—by warning, teaching, exhorting, frightening. By whatever means you can, act. Nor, when you find in the Scriptures and in the examples of the saints—whether those who lived before or those who lived after the Lord's coming in this life—that the bad do not stain the good in unity, become lazy in correcting the bad. In two ways the bad man does not stain you: if you do not consent, and if you reprove him: that is, not to commune, not to consent. For one communes when, to his deed, one joins a fellowship of will or of approval. Admonishing us of this, then, the Apostle says: *Do not commune with the unfruitful works of darkness*[^47]. And because it was too little not to consent, if negligence of discipline followed: *but rather,* he says, *even reprove them*[^48]. See how he embraced both: *Do not commune; but rather, even reprove them.* What is it: *Do not commune?* Do not consent, do not praise, do not approve. What is it: *But rather, even reprove them?* Reprehend, rebuke, restrain.

**18. 20.** Then, in the very correction or restraining of others' sins, one must beware lest the one who rebukes another exalt himself; and that apostolic saying must be pondered: *Wherefore, let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall*[^49]. Outside let the rebuke sound terribly; within let the love of gentleness be kept. *If a man is overtaken in any fault,* as the same apostle says, *you who are spiritual, instruct such a one in a spirit of meekness, attending to yourself, lest you too be tempted. Bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^50]. Likewise in another place: *The servant of the Lord,* he says, *must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all, teachable, patient, with modesty correcting those who think differently: lest perhaps God give them repentance to know the truth, and they come to their senses out of the snares of the devil, by whom they are held captive at his will*[^51]. Therefore be neither consenting to the bad, so as to approve them; nor negligent, so as not to reprove them; nor proud, so as to reprove them insultingly.

**18. 21.** But whoever forsakes unity violates love: and whoever violates love, whatever great thing he may have, he himself is nothing. If he speaks with the tongues of men and of angels, if he knows all mysteries, if he has all faith so as to move mountains, if he distributes all his goods to the poor, if he hands over his body to be burned, but has not love; he is nothing, it profits him nothing[^52]. He has all things uselessly who does not have that one thing by which he may use all things.

**19. 21.** Let us therefore embrace love, being eager *to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace*[^53]. Let not those seduce us who understand carnally and, making a bodily separation, are separated by a spiritual sacrilege from the grain of the Church spread throughout the whole world. For throughout the whole world the good seed was sown. That good sower, the Son of man, scattered the good seed not in Africa alone, but everywhere. But the enemy oversowed weeds. Yet what does the head of the household say? *Let both grow until the harvest*[^54]. Grow through what? Through the field, of course. What is the field? Is it Africa? No. What, then, is it? Let us not interpret; let the Lord say it; let us allow no one to surmise anything by his own judgment. For the disciples said to the Master: *Explain to us the parable of the weeds*[^55]. And the Lord explained: *The good seed,* he said, *are the sons of the kingdom; the weeds are the sons of the evil one.* Who sowed them? *The enemy who sowed them,* he says, *is the devil.* What is the field? *The field,* he says, *is this world.* What is the harvest? *The harvest,* he says, *is the end of the age.* Who are the reapers? *The reapers,* he says, *are the angels*[^56]. Is Africa the world? Is this time the harvest? Is Donatus the reaper? Throughout the whole world await the harvest, throughout the whole world grow toward the harvest, throughout the whole world tolerate the weeds until the harvest. Let not the perverse seduce you, the all-too-light chaff that flies off the floor before the coming of the winnower: let them not seduce you. Hold them to this one comparison alone of the weeds, and do not allow them to speak further. That man handed over the books. No. But that man handed over the books. Whoever handed them over, did the faithlessness of the betrayers make void the faith of God? What is the faith of God? What he promised to Abraham, saying: *In your seed all nations shall be blessed*[^57]. What is the faith of God? *Let both grow until the harvest.* Grow through what? Through the field. What is, Through the field? Through the world.

**19. 22.** Here they say: Both indeed had grown throughout the world, but now the grain has been diminished, and recalled into this region and small number of ours. The Lord does not permit you to interpret what you want. He himself who expounds this parable shuts your mouth—your sacrilegious mouth, your impious mouth, your profane mouth, your mouth contrary to itself, you who contradict the testator, even though he calls you too to the inheritance. How does he shut your mouth? By saying: *Let both grow until the harvest*[^58]. If the harvest had already come, let us believe the grain to be diminished. Although not even then will it be diminished, but stored in the granary. For he says thus: *Gather first the weeds, and bind them in bundles to be burned; but store the grain in my granary*[^59]. If, then, they grow until the harvest, after the harvest they are stored away; when, you wicked, impious one, are they diminished? I grant that in comparison with the weeds and with the chaff together the grain is fewer: yet both grow until the harvest. For when iniquity abounds, the love of many grows cold[^60]: the weeds grow, the chaff grows. But because in the whole field there cannot be lacking grain that, by persevering until the end, is made safe, both grow until the harvest[^61]. And if on account of the abundance of the bad it was said: *Do you think the Son of man, when he comes, will find faith on the earth*[^62]? and by this name are signified whoever, by transgression of the law, imitate him to whom it was said: *You are earth, and to earth you shall go*[^63]: yet, on account of the abundance of the good, and on account of him to whom it was said: *So shall your seed be, as the stars of heaven, and as the sand of the sea*[^64], this too was not left unsaid: *that many will come from east and west, and will recline with Abraham and Isaac in the kingdom of God*[^65]. Both, then, grow until the harvest; and the weeds, or the chaff, have their own statements in the Scriptures, and the grain its own. Those who do not understand them confound and are confounded; and so they make a din with blind desire, that they refuse to fall silent even when the truth is laid open.

**20. 23.** Behold, they say, the prophet says: *Depart, go out from there, and touch no unclean thing*[^66]: how then shall we tolerate the bad for the sake of peace, from whom we are commanded to go out and depart, that we may not touch the unclean thing? We understand that departure spiritually, they bodily. For I too cry out with the prophet, and whatever sort of vessels we may be, God uses us for your stewardship: we too cry out, and we say to you: *Depart, go out from there, and touch no unclean thing;* but by the contact of the heart, not of the body. For what is it to touch the unclean thing, except to consent to sins? And what is it to go out from there, except to do what pertains to the correction of the bad, as far as can be done for each one's rank and person with peace preserved? It displeased you that someone sinned; you did not touch the unclean thing. You reproved, rebuked, admonished, applied even, if the matter required, fitting discipline that does not violate unity; you went out from there. Attend to the deeds of the saints, lest perhaps this seem our own interpretation. As the saints understood these words, so surely they must be understood. *Go out from there,* says the prophet. First, by the usual custom of the word itself, I assert this meaning, and afterward I show that it is not my own. Often men are accused; and when they have been accused, they defend themselves: but when the accused man has defended himself reasonably and justly, those who hear say: He has gone out of it. Where did he go out of? Remaining in place, he went out of it. How did he go out of it? By rendering an account, and by a most just defense. This is what the saints did, when they shook the dust from their feet against those who did not accept the peace announced to them[^67]. That watchman went out of it, to whom it was said: *I have set you a watchman for the house of Israel*[^68]. For to him it is said: *If you tell the wicked man, and he does not turn from his iniquity and from his way; that wicked man will die in his iniquity, and you will free your own soul*[^69]. If he does this, he goes out of it, not by separation of body, but by the defense of his own work. For this man did what had to be done: even if that man did not obey, who ought to have obeyed. This is: *Go out from there.*

**21. 24.** Moses cried this out, Isaiah cried it out, Jeremiah cried it out, Ezekiel cried it out. Let us see whether they themselves did this, whether they abandoned the people of God and transferred themselves to other nations. How many things, and how vehemently, did Jeremiah rebuke against the sinners and the wicked of his people! Yet he was among them, he entered the one temple with them, he celebrated the same sacraments: he lived in that congregation of wicked men, but by crying out he went out from there. This is to go out from there, this is not to touch the unclean thing, and not to consent by will, and not to spare with the mouth. What shall I say of Jeremiah, of Isaiah, of Daniel, of Ezekiel, of the rest of the Prophets, who did not depart from the bad people, lest they forsake the good mixed with that people, where they themselves could be such? Moses himself, brothers, when he was receiving the law on the mountain, the people below made an idol. The people of God, the people led across as the waves of the Red Sea gave way, which overwhelmed the pursuing enemies, after such great signs and miracles in the plagues of the Egyptians unto death, and in their own protection unto safety, nevertheless demanded an idol, extorted an idol, made an idol, adored an idol, sacrificed to an idol. God informs his servant of the people's deed, and says he will destroy them from his face. Moses intercedes, about to return to that very people: and he had occasion to withdraw and go out from them, as these people understand it, lest he touch the unclean thing, lest he live with such men; yet he did not do it. And lest perhaps he should seem to have done this from necessity rather than love, God offered him another people: *I will make you,* he said, *into a great nation*[^70]; that he might destroy those. But he did not accept it; he clings to the sinners, he asks for the sinners. And how does he ask? A great proof of love, brothers. How does he ask? See that maternal love, in a way, of which we have often spoken. When God was threatening the sacrilegious people, the devout heart of Moses trembled, he set himself against the wrath of God for them. *Lord,* he said, *if you forgive them their sin, forgive it: but if not, blot me out of your book that you have written*[^71]. With what fatherly and motherly heart, how secure he said this, attending to the justice and mercy of God! that, because he is just, he might not destroy the just one; because he is merciful, he might forgive the sinners.

**22. 25.** Surely it is now manifest to your prudence how all such testimonies of the Scriptures are to be taken: so that when Scripture sounds that we ought to depart from the bad, we are commanded to understand nothing else but that we depart in heart; lest we commit a greater evil in the separation of the good than we flee in the conjunction with the bad, as the Donatists themselves did. If they truly, being good, reproved the bad, and did not rather, being bad themselves, defame the good, they would tolerate any sort of men for the sake of peace—they who received the Maximianists as whole, whom they had previously condemned as lost. Surely the prophet plainly said: *Depart and go out from there, and touch no unclean thing*[^72]. That I may understand what he said, I attend to what he did. By his deed he expounds his saying to me. He said: *Depart.* To whom did he say it? To the just, surely. From whom did he tell them to depart? From sinners and the wicked, surely. I ask whether he himself departed from such men. I find that he did not depart. Therefore he understood it otherwise. For surely he would first do what he commanded. He departed in heart, he chided and reproved. By restraining himself from consent he did not touch the unclean thing: but by chiding he went out free in the sight of God; to whom God neither imputes his own sins, because he did not commit them; nor others', because he did not approve them; nor negligence, because he did not keep silent; nor pride, because he remained in unity. So then, my brothers, however many you have among you who are still weighed down by love of the world—the greedy, the perjurers, the adulterers, the spectators of trifles, the consulters of astrologers, fanatics, augurs, soothsayers, drunkards, the self-indulgent, whatever of the bad you know to be among you—as far as you can, condemn them, that you may depart in heart; reprove them, and go out from there; and do not consent, that you may not touch the unclean thing.
