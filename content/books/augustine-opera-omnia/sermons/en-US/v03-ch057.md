# Sermon 90/A: A Sermon of Saint Augustine the Bishop on the Love of God and Neighbor

**1.** In this Gospel reading that has just been read out, as though we too, seeing the Lord—not with the eyes of the flesh, but, what is more wholesome, with the eyes of faith—let us take up the disposition of the one questioning, what the great commandment is in the law[^1]. For that man who was questioning, because he saw not with the eyes of faith but of the flesh, was tempting rather than seeking. But let us, believing, seek, that seeking we may find; let us too say: Lord, *what is the great commandment in the law?* But let us say it, not with the cunning of one tempting, but with the disposition of one learning. For he answers us now what he answered that man, who, if he did not believe, asked for us, not for himself; but if he believed, let the hearer now be the more instructed, if then the tempter could be corrected.

**2.** Let us first observe how that man asked about one commandment, desiring to know what was the commandment in the law—not which was the only one, but which was the great one; but the Lord answered not one, but two commandments. For perhaps that man, when he had heard which was the great one, would ask for several to follow; but the Lord, lest after the great one several be sought, joined a second one, so that there might be fulfilled in him what had so long before been prophesied: *For the Lord will make an abridging and consummating word upon the earth*[^2]. Now it is done, in this reading it is completed. For many are the precepts of the law, and as though an incomprehensible forest it teems through all the pages with commandments. And who could fulfill what no one is able to retain in mind? But the Lord Christ, full of mercy, as he showed himself great in a small body, so he enclosed the broad law in a brief precept. In that brevity of body we have the whole Son of God, in the brevity of these commandments we hold the whole law of God. Mercy made an end of laziness. Do not think how long you may learn, but rather how you may do what you have quickly learned.

**3.** *You shall love,* he says, *the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second,* he says, *is like it. You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang the whole law and the prophets*[^3]. Why did you extend yourself through the spreading of the branches? Hold these roots, and the whole tree is in your hand. The Lord, as we see, intimated this briefly; we are compelled to say more about these two commandments. Or perhaps we are not compelled, and it suffices what we heard from the Lord? Plainly it suffices, but not for all. For the greater anyone is, the more what is said briefly suffices for him. The great seek brief things; the small, because they understand less, desire more. We fear to offend the fastidiousness of the former, we do not want to burden the weakness of the latter. Yet if we keep silent, those who understand less complain; and let those who already understand bear with us, that those who do not yet understand may take us in.

**4.** What better, what briefer could be said to you, O man, than that you love *the Lord God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind*[^4]? Do it, and be secure both about eternal life and about the blessed life. For if you love the Lord your God with all your heart, all your soul, all your mind, you leave nothing for yourself by which you might love yourself. With all your heart and all your soul and all your mind love, love your God. And what will remain by which you may love yourself? But if nothing remains by which you may love yourself, in the second precept how will you *love your neighbor as yourself?* Behold one question, hear it. The Lord says, as we noticed in the reading itself when it was read out: *On these two commandments hang the whole law and the prophets*[^5]. But further, the apostle Paul, if you noticed when his letter too was read, says that the man fulfills the law who loves his neighbor[^6], not adding the first and great commandment, that one love God with all his heart, soul, mind. For he says thus: *For, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not commit murder, You shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. The love of neighbor works no evil. But the fullness of the law is love*[^7]. Here he says: *He who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law*[^8]. And if he said only certain three precepts: *You shall not commit adultery, You shall not commit murder, You shall not covet,* we would think that in the love of neighbor only these three precepts are retained. But when he added and said: *If there is any other commandment,* he enclosed the whole in the love of neighbor. What here remains for the love of God? When you hear: *God with all your heart, all your soul, all your mind,* nothing seems to remain for the love of neighbor. Again, when you hear: This and this and this, *and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself,* nothing seems to remain for the love of God. How, then, did the Lord say, not "on one," but, *on these two commandments hang the whole law and the prophets?*

**5.** So that we may expound briefly, as far as we can with the Lord's help, what I have proposed, let us begin rather from the love of neighbor. We are also men, mortal, ignorant, not yet made equal to the angels, much unlike the fellowship of incorruption, and thereby, by that very unlikeness, God is far from us, although by mercy he is near. Who, then, are we, and with what powers of our thought should we dare to conjecture the Lord? We have a neighbor; in the neighbor now strive, so that you may love God with all your heart, all your soul, all your mind. O man, *if you do not love the brother whom you see, how will you be able to love God whom you do not see?* You recognize the words drawn from the sermon of the apostle John[^9]. It is prescribed for us, then: let us begin from the neighbor, that we may come to God.

**6.** But someone will say to me: "I love God and my neighbor, not in words, but in deeds." Prove it. "I love," he says, "my neighbor." What great thing do you do? Do you not see in irrational animals how mutual love prevails, how birds want to be with birds of their own kind and weary of solitude? Do you not see, when animals have shared a manger together, how on a journey they desire to follow one another, and the one is most painfully separated from the other? What great thing, then, do you do, if you, a man, want to be with a man? This still belongs to beasts of burden. I do not know whether such love is required of us by God. You say perhaps: "I love my neighbor; for I love my son, and as myself." This too is easy. Tigers love their young. For none of these would be propagated, unless one were loved by another. Transcend the things that have been given into your power: none of these was made to the image of God. God made man to his own image, that he might have *power over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth*[^10]. See what you have under you, and you—with what love of his image, formed far otherwise to your Creator. Finally, whence do you prove that you love your son? That very son, I say, whence do you prove that you love him? Because you were saving for him an inheritance that he cannot possess together with you? For he does not hold it with you, but on your departing he succeeds. Do you not remember that into that inheritance your father already made his passage before you? And if it remains the same from of old, your grandfather too passed through it: all passing, none remaining. You leave, then, mortal things to one who is to die, or, what is truer, you do not know for whom you gather them[^11], and you boast yourself a lover of your son.

**7.** *They will declare to their sons, that they may set their hope in God*[^12]. If you love thus, you love. If you love otherwise, you do not even love, because you do not even love yourself. For surely what did you hear? *You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* This rule I set down—rather, I do not set it down, but I recognize it. For I both behold and recall it set down for all of us. This is the rule: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* Now I do not say: Your son, your spouse, your neighboring friend, your neighboring acquaintance *you shall love as yourself.* Perhaps you answer: "I love them." First I ask whether you love yourself. Here is the whole force of the precept, here the whole question turns: for you will not be able to love your neighbor as yourself, if you do not yet love yourself. "For who is there," you say, "who does not love himself?" I would wish to find someone who loves himself. For I do not attend to how the creature errs, but to what the Creator teaches. He who made us knows us better. Let us, then, hear him. Since you were saying: "I love myself," even if I demanded that you prove it, you would answer me: "When I am hungry, I refresh my body, because I love myself; I do not want to be wearied with toils, because I love myself; I do not want to be distressed by need, because I love myself; I do not want to have a fever, because I love myself; I do not want to suffer pain, because I love myself." Do you want to hear what he who made you says? See only how very well you love yourself: whether you do not love iniquity. *For he who loves iniquity hates his own soul*[^13]. I do not question you; question yourself. If you want to grow by another's harm, if you want it to be ill with another that it may be well with you, surely if you so want, if you want this, you love iniquity, you hate your own soul. If, then, you hate your own soul, I do not entrust your neighbor to you, that you should love him as yourself. Shall I entrust to you another man, so as to seek two? You who have lost yourself, are you to preserve me? First, then, love yourself, that you may know how to love your neighbor as yourself.

**8.** How you should love yourself, do you wait to hear from me? Let us rather hear him who made both you and me. Behold how you should love yourself: attend to the great precept, that you may love yourself. For toward that which you love, you must want to bring the one you love. And you love iniquity: thither you will lead the one you love as yourself. Very many human pursuits, whether bad or good, are set before the eyes of all. You love a charioteer: you incite all whom you love to watch with you, to love with you, to cheer with you, to go mad with you. If they do not love it, you insult them, you call them ignoramuses—as yourself. And though you do not want to give him half, you want the one you love as yourself to have as much. For you do not want him to grow rich at your loss, you do not want his good with the diminishment of your own good. Why? Because you think gold is good, therefore you esteem yourself great because you have gold. You want him to grow, not yourself to decrease. Why do you love this which you would lessen by loss? In all these things you love iniquity, you hate your own soul[^14]. When you want securely to draw your neighbor whom you love as yourself, draw him to that good which no multitude of partakers straitens; which good, however many possess it, is whole for all and each. Unless you love such a good, how will you love your neighbor as yourself?

**9.** What is that? In the first and great commandment you have it: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind*[^15]. For when you have begun to love God, then you love yourself. Do not fear: however much you love God, you will not be excessive. The measure of loving God is to love without measure. Love, then, *with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind,* because you have no more. For what more do you have, by which to love your God, than your whole self? Do not, then, be afraid that, by leaving nothing for yourself by which to love yourself, you may perish. You do not perish, because by loving God with your whole self you will be there where you do not perish. Rather, if from there you relax your love back toward yourself, you will not be in him, but in yourself. Then you perish, because you will be in one who perishes. If you do not want to perish, be in him who cannot perish. This is the force of love, this is the ardor of love. In these foulest and basest pursuits let us see this. Lovers of a charioteer in the spectacle hang wholly on him, they are nowhere but in the one they watch; that lover does not regard himself at all, he does not know where he is. Accordingly, more lukewarm in that pursuit is the one who stands next to him and sees him so fervent, who says: "He is not with himself." And you, if it can be, when you are in God, do not be with yourself. If you are with yourself, you entrust yourself to yourself, you will lose yourself, you are not fit to preserve yourself.

**10.** Remember how he went off and perished, who said to his father, his preserver: *Give me my substance that falls to me*[^16]. Behold, he went away, behold, he consumed everything, behold, he fed swine, behold, he was crushed in want[^17]: made far from his father, when he wanted to be with himself. For wanting to be with himself, he did not even remain with himself. If you slip away from your God, you more quickly slip also from yourself, and you go out from yourself, and you let go even of yourself. Therefore to such men it is said: *Return, transgressors, to the heart*[^18], return to yourselves, that you may be able to return also to him who made you. Hence too that man, when he was in want, his father forsaken, forsaken by himself, what was said of him? *And returning to himself he said*[^19]. *Returning to himself:* you see that he had let go even of himself. Well, son, you have corrected yourself, you have returned to yourself. Do not remain in yourself, lest you lose yourself again. This too that man remembered, partly amended. For as soon as he returned to himself, he did not want to remain in himself, because *returning to himself he said: I will arise, and go to my father*[^20]. Well returning, he understood that he ought to be there whence he had fallen.

**11.** If you love this, if you cherish it, I entrust your neighbor to you. For I see whither you tend and where you would have yourself. Thither lead him, nor can you lead the one you love as yourself elsewhere: for now you love even yourself. Lead your neighbor, draw him, snatch him, press in season[^21]. If the day of the public games dawned, a lover of the hunter, you would not be lulled by due sleep, nor would the hour of going to the amphitheater pass you by. And when the hour of going had come, your neighbor too, perhaps lulled by sleep and preferring to sleep rather than to go, you would most troublesomely rouse; you would press upon the lazy one; you would want, if it could be done, to snatch him from his bed and set him in the amphitheater, nor would you be troublesome to him except until the sleep was shaken off. For with sleep shaken off he would go on and give thanks for your trouble. And perhaps now, with him snatched along with you to the amphitheater, whither you both had gone in haste, with the one you loved defeated, you would return confounded. Love God wholly, *with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind:* so only, so will you love yourself. In this way alone do you love your neighbor as yourself. For you snatch him to him of whom you cannot be ashamed.

**12.** Therefore it was necessary that these two precepts be commended, because he who loves his neighbor does not love unless he first loves God. For when he loves God, he does not love iniquity, lest by loving iniquity he hate his own soul[^22]. If, then, he does not love iniquity, let him love equity, and there he loves God. Let him not seek him as with eyes: let him seek him with the mind, let him love him more with the heart and with affection. Let him not set before himself what is not God, lest he love and it be not God, lest he love an empty thought. For in this thinking, lest perhaps we err and frame God for ourselves according to a carnal disposition and feign him as we would, Scripture, removing us from thoughts of this kind, says: *God is love*[^23]. If, then, you love, love by that whence you love, and you love God. Have you not heard? *For he who loves iniquity hates his own soul.* If you love, love also that very thing whence you love, and you love God. For that whence you love *is love.* You love by love; love love, and you have loved God, since *God is love, and he who abides in love abides in God*[^24]. Therefore the two precepts had to be commended. For one would suffice: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself;* but lest a man err in loving his neighbor, because he was erring in loving himself, the Lord willed to form, in the love of God, your love by which you love yourself; and then he entrusted your neighbor to you, whom you should love as yourself.

**13.** Now, then, if you please, let the one precept of the Apostle also suffice for you. You have now understood two; even one suffices. For before you understand two, one does not suffice. For you begin to love yourself badly, and you love badly the one you love as yourself. And it should not be said: You love badly, but: You do not love. Therefore *You shall not commit adultery, You shall not commit murder, You shall not covet*[^25]—where the whole is, there it recalls you to yourself. For you can not commit adultery out of fear of punishment, not out of love of righteousness. *You shall not commit murder:* you can even want to kill, but fear the punishment. The murder will not be in your hand, but in your heart you will be held guilty. For you want to kill the man, but you are afraid; you want to kill: you do not yet love not to kill. Let it be within, what you do; let it be there, where he sees who crowns; there fight, there conquer: for there you have one who watches you. Therefore *You shall not commit adultery, You shall not commit murder, You shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^26]. You love God too. You cannot have this without that; this second follows the first. Let there be the first, and it draws the second. The second cannot be without the first. Fulfill, then, one, you who were thinking about two. You cannot fulfill one, except in the two. For the second too is called second from following. It follows, then. *Love your neighbor as yourself:* it suffices for me. Will you not also be able to think about God? How, then, do you begin to love yourself? *The love of neighbor works no evil. But the fullness of the law is love*[^27]. But where is love? In the love of God and in the love of neighbor. Choose which love you want. You choose the love of neighbor: it will not be true, unless God is loved. You choose the love of God: it will not be true, unless the neighbor is understood along with it.

**14.** But you do not yet have love: groan, believe, ask, obtain. What is commanded, what the law orders, faith obtains. For if you now have what it obtains—*for what do you have that you have not received*[^28]? But if you do not yet have it, ask that you may receive. It is *love* that we ask for; if we do not yet have it, let us ask, lest we remain empty. For whence shall we have it from ourselves, who have deserved no good, but evil? We shall have it from him to whom our soul says: *Bless the Lord, O my soul, and do not forget all his recompenses, who is gracious to all your iniquities*[^29]. This happens in baptism. But if only this were done, what should we remain like? But there follows: *Who heals all your infirmities*[^30]. For with our infirmities healed, we will not loathe our bread. See, then, what follows, with the infirmities healed: *Who redeems your life from corruption*[^31]. This happens now in the resurrection of the dead. And what follows the redemption from the corruption of our life? *Who crowns you.* Perhaps by your own merits? Attend to what follows: *Who crowns you in compassion and mercy*[^32]. *For judgment without mercy to him who has not done mercy*[^33]. With our sins forgiven, then, our infirmities healed, our life redeemed from corruption, our crown rendered to us in his mercy, what shall we do, what shall we have? *Who satisfies you with good things*[^34], not evil. You were greedy: you were not satisfied with gold, because the greedy man is not satisfied with gold. Be just, and be satisfied with God. Altogether nothing satisfies you but God; nothing suffices you but God. *Show us the Father, and it is enough for us*[^35]. Let us, then, love the works of mercy, while our infirmities are being healed, so that, with our infirmities healed, our desires may flourish; and, healed, may flourish, and flourishing be satisfied, so that there may be for us judgment, but with mercy. For it is grievous that it be without mercy. It is hard that he not find in you something to punish. You have now pleased yourself well: for he knows something that lies hidden from you, he finds in you what you were concealing, or perhaps even what you did not know. Let the works of mercy, then, be fervent, and in this need of temporal things let us love our neighbors, that we may deserve to feel judgment with mercy.
