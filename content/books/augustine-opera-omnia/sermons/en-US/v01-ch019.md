# Sermon 16: A Treatise of Saint Augustine the Bishop

On What Is Written:

"Who Is the Man Who Wants Life

And Desires to See Good Days?"

**1.** The Spirit of God, calling the human race, by commanding what we ought to do and promising what we ought to hope for, first inflames our mind toward the reward, that we may do what is commanded rather by loving the good than by fearing the evil. *Who is the man,* it says, *who wants life and loves to see good days?*[^1] He is asked who this man is, as though one could be found who is not. For who does not want life? Who does not love to see good days? Hear, then, what follows, whoever you are, O man, who want and love this; hear, then, every man, what follows: *Restrain,* it says, *your tongue from evil, and let your lips not speak guile. Turn away from evil, and do good; seek peace, and pursue it*[^2]. Of all these, the others above are in the commandment, the last in the reward. For that we should restrain our tongue from evil and our lips not speak guile, that we should turn away from evil and do good, and seek peace, is commanded us. But that we should pursue it is promised us. What is this peace, except that which the world does not have? What is this peace, except that which this life does not have, which in comparison with that life is not even life? For he would not say of this life, *Who is the man who wants life?* and exhort, by the commandments that follow, either to keep or to prolong it, as though anyone did not want even this. For this too is wished at least to be long, since it cannot be everlasting; and through it a man can come to that life, if, as he wishes it long, so he also wished it good. But how much is long in this life which will someday be ended? And what was long will be nothing, because even when it was, it did not stand still; when it was being prolonged, it was not being increased, nor did it grow by adding, because by coming it was passing away.

**2.** Whoever, then, you are, a lover of long life, be rather a lover of a good life. For if you wish to live badly, a long life will not truly be a good, but it will be a long evil. But see how absurd and perverse you are, when, although you confess that you love life more than your country estate, you wish rather to have a good estate than a good life. For while by gaping and coveting wickedly you obtain a good estate by defrauding, you make your life evil. Yet if it were said to you, if you were asked, whether you would rather lack a good estate by losing it, or die by an evil life, you would answer that, if you could not keep both, you were more ready that the estate be taken from you. Why, then, is life not so loved that it may also be good, which by you is preferred to all your goods even when it is evil? You certainly desire that it be long, though it be evil. Rather, make it good, and do not fear that it be short. For if, while you are anxious, it goes well, then, while you are secure, it will be quickly ended. For eternal life succeeds it, blessed without fear, long without end. Concerning that life he asks, who says, *Who is the man who wants life and loves to see good days?*[^3] But in this life the Apostle commands us to redeem the time, since the days are evil. And what is it to redeem the time, except, when there is need, even by the loss of temporal advantages, to procure spaces of time for seeking and grasping eternal things? Whence the Lord too commands, saying, *If anyone wishes to contend with you in judgment and to take your tunic, let him have your cloak also*[^4]. That is, some temporal thing being lost, you may spend on quiet the time you were going to spend on litigation.

**3.** That, accordingly, the Spirit of God does not speak of the life and days of this time when it says, *Who is the man who wants life and desires to see good days?*[^5]—the things that follow teach. For it adds such commandments that, by obeying them, we may be able to have life and good days, that this life which we now lead and these days must generally be lost for the sake of fulfilling those same commandments. Accordingly, if we understand this life in which we now are in what was said, *Who is the man who wants life?* and for the sake of having this we do the commandments that are joined to it, what shall we do when someone powerful in malice has threatened us with death unless we bear false witness? For surely if we do what is here commanded, *Restrain your tongue from evil*, so that for the sake of this commandment we refuse the falsehood of testimony, we will seem, as it were, deceived. Because for the sake of the desire of having life we undertook to keep the commandment, and we rather lost it by keeping the commandment. But if we understand the life eternally blessed which after this one God will give to those who obey him, of which the Lord said to a certain man, *If you wish to come to life, keep the commandments*[^6], then truly to the one asking, *Who is the man who wants life?* we will answer that we want life, if even under the very blow of the striker we keep truth in testimony, we despise death in the world, we obtain life in heaven.

**4.** Let us understand this of the good days. For if, for the sake of the days of the present age which are called good and are not, in the burial of the heart through heaps of feasts, in the whirlpools of luxury and wine-bibbing, in the most shameful pleasures of gluttony—if, then, for the sake of those days, as though for the sake of good days, we undertook the commandment that our lips should not speak guile, generally such days compel their lovers to speak guile, and such days are denied to those who do not speak guile. For what else is it to "speak guile," except to bring forth one thing with the lips when another is shut up in the breast? For this purpose especially the business of flatterers has blazed up, because almost always, lest they be kept from rich tables and well-furnished banquets, by flattering they do not keep silent what is false, and they are kept from these, if, loving God, they speak the truth. Therefore for the sake of those days which they think good, that these may be granted them, they speak guile, and they are denied them, if they do not speak guile. There are, then, other good days, of which we are admonished that, if we love to see them, we should restrain our tongue from evil and not speak guile. Those days are not of this age; the heaven that will pass away does not have them, but the heaven that will remain; the land of the dying does not know them, but the land of the living. Whoever has understood and loved these, let him restrain his tongue from evil; and if the terror of death compels it to evil, let his lips not speak guile; and if by falsely good days he is invited to evil, let him turn away from evil even among good things, let him do good even among evil things; let him seek peace, which is not upon the earth, and pursue it in him who made heaven and earth.

**5.** Accordingly, brothers, covet life and love to see good days where there will be no night, life in which an evil day is not feared, good days in which life is never ended. But if you love this reward, beware lest you refuse the work of which it is the reward. By seeking that peace, pursue it. But seek it with your hands by night before God, and you will not be deceived. For what is "with your hands," except in a good work? What is "by night," except in tribulation? What is "before God," except in purity of conscience? Living so and loving this, you will have God in contemplation, and in him life without failing, good days without darkening, peace without dissension.
