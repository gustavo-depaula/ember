# Sermon 112a

**1.** We ought not to linger on matters already treated, but, just as we ought not to linger on them, so they ought to be recalled. Your prudence remembers that last Sunday we undertook a sermon to be discoursed on the two sons, who were recited today too from the Gospel [^1], which could not be brought to its end. But the Lord our God, after that tribulation, wished us to speak with you today too. The debt of the sermon must be repaid, the debt of love must always be held; the Lord will be present, that our humility may suffice for your expectation.

**2.** A man having two sons is God having two peoples; the elder son is the people of the Jews; the younger, the people of the Gentiles. The substance received from the Father is mind, understanding, memory, talent, and whatever God gave us for understanding and worshiping him. Having received this patrimony, the younger son *set out into a far country* [^2]; far, namely, even unto the forgetting of his Creator. *He squandered his substance by living prodigally* [^3]; spending, and not acquiring; expending what he had, and not receiving what he did not have; that is, consuming all his talent in luxuries, in idols, in all the depraved desires which the Truth called harlots.

**3.** Nor is it a wonder that a famine followed this luxury. *But there was want in that country* [^4]; not a want of visible bread, but a want of invisible Truth. From want *he rushed in upon a certain ruler of that country* [^5]. This ruler is understood as the devil, the prince of demons, upon whom all the curious rush; for all unlawful curiosity is a pestilent want of truth. But he, torn from God by a famine of talent, was reduced to servitude, and was allotted the office of feeding swine; that is, of him whose servitude the lowest and unclean demons are wont to rejoice in; for not in vain did the Lord allow the demons to go into the herd of swine [^6]. But he was fed here on husks, with which he himself was not filled; we understand the husks as worldly doctrines, sounding, not fattening, fit for the food of swine, not of men; that is, from which the demons rejoice, not from which the faithful are justified.

**4.** At last at some time he saw where he was, what he had lost, whom he had offended, and upon whom he had rushed; *and he returned to himself* [^7]; first to himself, and so to the father. For he had said perhaps: *My heart has forsaken me* [^8]; whence it was necessary that he should first return to himself, and so recognize himself to be far from the father. This Scripture rebukes in certain men, saying: *Return, transgressors, to the heart* [^9]. Returning to himself, he found himself wretched: *Tribulation*, he says, *and grief I found, and I called on the name of the Lord* [^10]. *How many*, he says, *of my father's hired servants abound in bread! But I here perish with hunger* [^11]. Whence would this come into his mind, except that the name of God was already being preached? And there was bread among certain men, who indeed did not hold it well, and sought something else, of whom it is said: *Amen I say to you, they have received their reward* [^12]. For such men are to be held hired servants, not sons, such as the Apostle designates, when he says: *Whether by occasion, or in truth, Christ is announced* [^13]. For he wants certain men to be understood, who, by seeking their own, are hired servants, by announcing Christ abound in bread.

**5.** He rises, and returns; for by lying down and falling he had remained. The father sees him from afar, and runs to meet him; for his is the voice in the psalm: *You have known my thoughts from afar* [^14]. What thoughts? Those by which he said within himself: *I will say to my Father: I have sinned against heaven and before you, I am no longer worthy to be called your son, make me as one of your hired servants* [^15]. For he was not yet saying it, but was thinking to say it; yet the father heard him as though already saying it. For sometimes anyone set in some tribulation and temptation thinks to pray; and in that very thought he meditates what he will say to God in prayer, as a son demanding by his right the mercy of the father. And he says within himself: I will say to my God this and that; for I do not fear, when I have said this, when I have so wept, that my God will not hear me. Saying these things, very often he is already heard; for when he thought these things, he did not hide his thought from the eyes of God. He was there, when he was planning to pray, who was going to be there when he should begin to pray; therefore it is said in another psalm: *I said, I will declare against myself my offense to the Lord* [^16]. See how he still says what he said within himself, what he planned; and at once he added: *And you forgave the impiety of my heart* [^17]. How near is God's mercy to the one confessing! For God is not far from the contrite of heart; for so you have it written: *The Lord is near to those who have crushed their heart* [^18]. So then this man had crushed his heart in the country of want; for he had returned to the heart, to crush the heart; he had left the heart proud, he had returned to the heart angry. He is angry at himself, about to punish, but his own evil; he had returned about to deserve the father's good. He spoke angry, according to what is said: *Be angry, and do not sin* [^19]. For every penitent is angry at himself; for, because he is angry, he punishes himself. Hence all those movements in the penitent who truly repents, who truly grieves; hence the tearing of hair, hence the girding with sackcloth, hence the beating of the breast. Certainly all these are signs of a man raging against himself, and angry at himself. What the hand does outside, this the conscience does within; in thoughts it strikes itself, beats itself, and, to say it more truly, kills itself. For by killing himself he offers *to God the sacrifice of a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God does not despise* [^20]. Accordingly that man, crushing his heart, humbling it, beating it, kills his heart.

**6.** Although he was still planning to say to the father, and was saying within himself: *I will arise, and go, and say* [^21], when he knew his thoughts from afar, the father ran to meet him. What is it to run to meet, except to bestow mercy beforehand? *When he was still*, he says, *far off, the father, moved with mercy, ran to meet him* [^22]. Why was he moved with mercy? Because this one was already worn out with misery. *He fell upon him, running* [^23]; that is, he put his arm upon his neck. The Father's arm is the Son: he gave that he should carry Christ; which burden does not weigh down, but lifts up. *My yoke*, he says, is easy, *and my burden is light* [^24]. He fell upon him raised up; falling upon him, he did not allow him to fall again. So light is the burden of Christ, that it not only does not press down, but even lifts up. For not as those are called light burdens which are less burdensome, yet have some weight of their own; and it is one thing to carry a heavy burden, another to carry a light burden, another to carry no burden. He seems pressed down who carries a heavy burden; he is less pressed who carries a light burden, but still is pressed; but he seems to walk with most unencumbered shoulders who carries no burden. Christ's burden is not such: for it is expedient to carry it, that you may be lifted up; if you put it down, you are more pressed. Nor let this seem to you, brothers, as though impossible. Perhaps some example is found, from which you may see even bodily what I say; and it is even a wonder, and altogether incredible. Note this in birds. Every bird carries its feathers: attend, and see how they fold their wings, when they come down to earth, that they may rest, and lay them, in a way, on their sides. Do you think them burdened? let them take off the burden, and they will fall; the less the bird carried that burden, the less it flew. So you take off that burden from them as though merciful; if you want to be merciful, spare it; or, if the feathers are now removed, nourish it, that the burden may grow, and it will fly from the earth. Such a burden indeed he desired who said: *Who will give me wings like a dove's, and I will fly, and rest* [^25]? In that the father fell upon his son's neck, he lifted him up, did not press him down; he honored, did not burden him. For how is a man fit to carry God, except because, carried by God, he carries him?

**7.** So the father commands the first robe to be brought to him, which by sinning Adam had lost. Now that the son is received in peace, now that he is kissed, he commands the robe to be brought, the hope of immortality in baptism. He commands a ring to be given, the pledge of the Holy Spirit, and sandals on the feet in the preparation of the Gospel of peace, that the feet of the one announcing good might be beautiful [^26]. This, then, God does through his servants, that is, through the ministers of the Church. Do they give the robe of their own, the ring or the sandals of their own? They owe ministry, they expend service; he gives, from whose storeroom and from whose treasure these are brought forth. He commanded the fatted calf also to be slain [^27], that is, that he should be admitted to the table at which Christ is fed upon slain; for to each one coming from afar and running to the Church it is then slain, when it is preached as slain, when one is admitted to his body. The fatted calf is slain, because he who had perished is found.

**8.** And the elder brother, returning from the field, is angry, and refuses to enter. He is the people of the Jews, whose disposition appeared also in those who had already believed in Christ. For the Jews were vexed that the Gentiles came by so great a saving, with no burdens of the law imposed, without the pain of carnal circumcision, that in their sin they received the saving baptism; they were vexed to feast on the fatted calf. Indeed those had already believed: and an account was given them, and they were quieted. But when even now perhaps some Jew, who has had the law of God in mind, and lived there without complaint—such as Saul said he himself had been there, who among us became Paul, the greater the lesser, the more exalted the more he was made least—for Paul is least; whence we say: A little while later I speak to you, a little while before. See what it is, a little while before: a short time before. What, then, is Paulus? *For I am the least of the Apostles* [^28], he himself said—so any Jew there is such that he knows and has in his conscience that from his early age he worshiped the one God, the God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, the God preached through the Law and the Prophets, and observed the preachings of the Law, begins to think about the Church, seeing the human race run in the name of Christ; when he thinks about the Church, he draws near to the house from the field. For so it is written: *When the elder brother came from the field, and drew near to the house* [^29]. For just as the younger son grows daily among the believing pagans, so the elder son, though rarely, yet returns among the Jews. They think on the Church, they marvel what that is; they see the law with themselves, the law with us: the Prophets with themselves, the Prophets with us: with themselves now no sacrifice, with us a daily sacrifice; they see that they were in the Father's field, but yet do not eat of the calf.

**9.** A symphony too is heard sounding from the house, and a chorus. What is a symphony? a concord of voices; those who disagree, sound discordant; those who agree, sound concordant. This symphony the Apostle taught, saying: *I beseech you, brothers, that you all say the same thing, and that there be no schisms among you* [^30]. Whom would this holy symphony not delight, that is, an agreement of voices not discrepant, not in anything absurd and dissonant, which would offend the hearing of a good listener? And the chorus pertains to that very concord; in a chorus nothing delights but the one voice of many, tempered, having from all a unity, not sounding discordant in any disagreeing variety.

**10.** When he had heard these things sounding from the house, *angry, he refused to enter* [^31]. As it truly happens, that a Jew of good merit among his own says, can the Christians do so much? We hold the ancestral laws; God spoke to Abraham, from whom we are born. Moses received the Law, who, freeing us from the land of Egypt, led us through the Red Sea. Behold, these men holding our Scriptures sing our psalms throughout the whole world, and have a daily sacrifice; but we have lost both the sacrifice and the temple. And he asks the servant, what is being done here? Let the Jew ask any servant; let him open the Prophets, let him open the Apostle, let him ask any one; neither the Old Testament nor the New kept silent about the calling of the nations. Let us understand the servant questioned as the Book examined; you will find Scripture there saying to you: *Your brother has returned, and your father has slain for him the fatted calf, because he received him safe* [^32]. Let the servant say this: whom did the father receive safe? The one who was dead and lived again, him he received to be saved. And the slaying of the fatted calf was owed to him who had gone far off; for by going far off from God he was impious. The servant, the apostle Paul, answered: *For Christ died for the impious* [^33]. Vexed within himself he does not enter angry, but when the father addresses him he entered; he refused to enter at the servant's answer. And truly, my brothers, this happens; we very often convince the Jews from the Scriptures of God, but the servant is still speaking, the son is angry; so that, though they are conquered, they refuse to enter. What is this? The voices of the symphony moved you, the chorus moved you, the festivity and feast of the house, the feasting on the slain fatted calf, these moved you. No one shuts you out. To whom do you speak? As long as the servant addresses, that one is angry, he refuses to enter.

**11.** Return to the Lord saying: *No one comes to me, unless the Father has drawn him* [^34]. The Father, then, goes out, and asks the son: this is to draw; a superior applies a greater force by asking than by commanding. But this is what happens, dearly beloved, when such men, occupied with the Scriptures, and having some sort of conscience in good works, so that they can say to their father: *Father, I have not transgressed your commandment* [^35], hear it. Then, therefore, when one is conquered from the Scriptures, and they find nothing to answer, they grow angry, they resist, as though wishing to conquer. Afterward you leave him with his thought, and God begins to speak within; this is the Father going out, and speaking to the son: Enter and feast.

**12.** And he in turn: *Behold, so many years I serve you, and I never transgressed your commandment; and you never gave me a kid, that I might eat it with my friends. Behold, this son of yours has come, who consumed his patrimony with harlots, and you have slain for him the fatted calf* [^36]. These are inner thoughts, where now in hidden ways the Father speaks; for he acts, and answers within himself, no longer the servant giving an answer, but the father in a way asking, gently admonishing: What is this? We hold the Scriptures of God, and we have not departed from the one God; we did not spread out our hands to a strange god [^37]: him alone we have known, him we have always worshiped, who made heaven and earth; and we did not receive a kid. Where do we find a kid? among sinners. Why does this elder son complain to himself that a kid was not given him? He was seeking to sin, of which he might feast; plainly he was vexed at that. This is what the Jews now grieve at, this is what they recover their senses about, and understand that Christ was not given them for this reason, because they thought him a kid. For they recognize their voice in the Gospel, in those Jews above saying: We know that *this man is a sinner* [^38]. He was a calf, but, when you thought him a kid, you remained without this feast. *You never gave me a kid*; because the Father did not have a kid, who knew him as a calf. You are outside: because you did not receive a kid, enter now to the calf.

**13.** For what does the father answer? Son, *you are always with me* [^39]. The father attested that the Jews were near, who always worshiped the one God. We have the testimony of the Apostle saying that they were near, but the nations far. For speaking to the nations he says: *Coming*, namely Christ, *he evangelized peace to you who were far off, and peace to those who were near* [^40]. Far off, as to the younger son; showing the Jews, because they did not go far off to feed swine, did not forsake the one God, did not adore idols, did not serve demons. I do not speak of all: lest the lost and seditious Jews come to your mind; let those come to mind by whom these are rebuked, the grave, keeping the commandments of the Law, not yet entering to the fatted calf, but already able to say: *I did not transgress your commandment* [^41]; who, when he begins to enter, the Father says to him: *You are always with me*. You are indeed with me, because you did not set out far off, but yet you are still wrongly outside the house; I do not want you to be apart from our feast. Do not envy the younger brother: You are always with me. God indeed did not attest to that which had perhaps been said less cautiously and boastfully: *I never transgressed your commandment*; but he says: *You are always with me*; he does not say: You never transgressed my commandment. This is true, what God said, not that of which the man perhaps rashly boasted, though in certain things perhaps a transgressor, yet not departing from the one God; so that the father too speaks truly: *You are always with me*, and *all that is mine is yours* [^42]. Because they are yours, are they not the brother's? How are they yours? To one having them in common, not to one quarreling apart. *All that is mine*, he says, *is yours*. What he says is his own, he gave as into possession. Did he subject heaven and earth, or the heights of the angels? It must not be so understood; for the angels will not truly be subjected to us, to whose equality the Lord promises us we shall come in a great reward: *They will be*, he says, *equal to the angels of God* [^43]. But there are other angels, whom the saints will judge: *Do you not know*, says the Apostle, *that we shall judge angels* [^44]? For there are angels always holy, there are angels that are transgressors; we shall be made equal to the good angels, we shall judge the evil angels. How, then, is all that is mine yours? Truly all that is God's is ours, yet not all subjected; for you say one thing: My servant, another: My brother. Whatever you say is yours, you say truly, when you speak truly, it is yours; but is the brother yours by the same right as the servant? You say one thing, my house, another, my spouse; you say one thing, my sons, another, my father, my mother; apart from me, I hear, all things are yours. My God, you say; but yet, my God, is it as my servant? Rather, my God, as my Lord. So we have one superior, our Lord, whom we may enjoy; we have the other things inferior, which we may rule over. All things, then, are ours, if we are his.

**14.** *All that is mine*, he says, *is yours* [^45]. If you will be peaceable, if you are pleased, if you rejoice at the brother's return, if our feasts do not sadden you, if you do not remain outside the house, although you have now come from the field, all that is mine is yours. *But it is fitting* for us *to feast and rejoice* [^46], because *Christ died for the impious* [^47], and rose again. For this is what was said: *Because your brother was dead, and lived again; he had perished, and is found* [^48].
