# Sermon 110a

**1.** We have often admonished Your Charity, and, to the measure of our strength, with the Lord bestowing it, we have taught that in the holy Gospel the wondrous deeds of our Lord Jesus Christ are not only mighty acts and works—for what else are they, but the more wonderful the more divine?—but are also, as it were, visible words, admonishing us to understand also what they signify. And if we do not understand them, they delight by the miracle alone and lift up the heart in the praise of God. Accordingly, just as one admires and praises a scribe writing beautifully, merely by looking at what he writes, even one who does not know letters and, ignorant of the meaning, is delighted by the beauty of the characters—not knowing the sense, he praises the stroke; but one who also knows how to read takes a rich fruit—so when we hear what wonders the Lord did, even if we do not understand what they signify, like those who cannot read and gaze on those characters alone, we marvel at the work of the doer, even if we do not know the will of the one signifying. What more wonderful than this deed? For eighteen years a woman was held bent over by infirmity: in one moment she was straightened by the word of the Lord, and an illness of many years yielded to him commanding, because it obeyed him ruling. He loosed the bond of the one who entangled her, he did the work of the Creator. She was straightened who was bent, she was loosed who was bound [^1], and this by a word, because he who made the word—and *all things were made through him* [^2]—came to seek what had perished [^3]. Let us marvel, let us praise, let us love. Yet, after admiring the stroke of this writing, let us a little more attentively seek its meaning.

**2.** For in that same gospel place, in that same context where there is mention of eighteen men upon whom the tower of Siloam fell and they died [^4], the woman infirm for eighteen years was also healed and straightened [^5]. Both are there: it is narrated in one continuous passage. In that same continuous passage too is what the Lord says about that tree, to which he came and found it unfruitful, in the third year already. When he commanded it to be cut down, the vinedresser interceded: that he should apply some dung to it after digging around it, and, if it bore fruit, well; if not, that he should come and cut it down [^6]. This about the tree was said in a figure, not done in a miracle. But that about the men upon whom the tower fell was neither said in a figure by the Lord, nor was the matter commended by any miracle: the men were crushed by a collapse [^7]. As though it could be reckoned to chance, except that the impious understand that not even a sparrow falls without the will of the Creator [^8]. But that the Lord might of his own accord recall those eighteen men whom the collapse of the tower crushed, it had been reported to him that certain men had been slain by king Herod in a horrid manner, so that he mingled their blood with their sacrifices [^9]. When men shuddered at this, they reported it. And the Lord said: *Do you suppose that they, because this happened to them, were sinners above other men? Amen, I say to you: unless you repent, you will all likewise perish* [^10]. Then of his own accord he added, and recalling it joined: *Or those eighteen men upon whom the tower in Siloam fell and they died, do you suppose that they were debtors above other men*—that is, sinners? *Amen, I say to you, that unless you repent, you will all likewise perish* [^11]. Then he speaks of that fig tree, and now straightens the woman [^12]. I beseech you, help our weakness with your attention; first what this means I think we ought not negligently to seek.

**3.** *Amen*, he says, *I say to you: unless you repent, you will all likewise die*. *Likewise* how? Like those, namely, *whose blood Herod mingled with their sacrifices*. Again: *unless you repent, you will all likewise die*. How *likewise?* Like those *upon whom the tower fell*. Since they died unalike—differently surely those whose blood was mingled with their sacrifices, differently those whom the collapse crushed—how will all, unless they repent, die likewise? Those who are to die in that way are not to die in this way, and those in this way, surely not in that; and yet by their deserts they die likewise. Divine Scripture cannot lie, the mouth of truth cannot lie. Plainly, if we understand it, it is true. All the wicked, the unjust, all the lost, the criminal, the shameful, lovers of the world, the wanton and the abject, unless they repent, die *likewise*. How *likewise?* Both as those, and as those. Not bodily and visibly, therefore *likewise*. For if bodily, they cannot die in both ways. But if we understand and compare spiritual things with spiritual [^13], and draw certain signs from bodily likenesses to intelligible things, all die *likewise*. For all the unjust ask evil things of God, and what they think good for themselves are evil, because they think badly. From what? From evil desire, from the work of flesh and blood, from the work of corruption, namely, which will not possess the kingdom of God [^14]. So their sacrifices, that is, their prayers, are mingled with their blood, because they ask according to flesh and blood. This a certain Scripture indicates and rebukes, saying: *You ask and do not receive, because you ask badly, that you may consume it in your desires* [^15]. You, then, offer your sacrifice: let it be mingled with your blood; for you relish carnally, and according to what you relish carnally, you ask God. For you are of flesh and blood; you want to bend God to your flesh, when you ought to lift up your heart to God, and you do not attend to what the Apostle says to you: *If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, sitting at the right hand of God; relish the things that are above, not the things that are on earth* [^16], lest your prayers, coming from flesh and blood, punish you with such a punishment as that with which those were punished whose blood the angry king mingled with their sacrifices [^17]. Separate your sacrifice from your blood, say to your God: *I will repay you my vows, which my lips have distinguished* [^18]. *For the spiritual man judges all things. He himself is judged by no one* [^19]. By judging all things, he does not mingle the sacrifice with the blood.

**4.** How, then, do those who do not repent die likewise, as those died upon whom the tower of Siloam fell? "Siloam" is interpreted "Sent." Who is the Sent one, except our Lord? The tower in Siloam, the cross of Christ. If, then, you are not mortified, fixed to his cross by the evil desires of flesh and blood, the crucified one falls upon you. Why do you sign yourself? If you do not act, you do not sign. This is a collapse, not grace. Acknowledge the crucified, acknowledge the one suffering, acknowledge the one praying for his enemies [^20], acknowledge the one loving and wishing to heal those from whom he suffered such things. If you acknowledge, repent, and if you ever willed evil, make yourself will good. Blot out the fault, lest you fear the penalty. But if you do not, will Christ profit you anything? *He who eats unworthily eats and drinks judgment to himself* [^21]. If by eating unworthily he eats and drinks judgment to himself, what else but that the tower falls upon him? *For you will all likewise die*, he says—and he says truly—*unless you repent* of your evil deeds. Further, if you act, you will be straightened and will be like that woman [^22]. Further, if you act, you receive the filth of penitence, like a mound of dung, not unfruitfully, lest you be cut down [^23].

**5.** It remains to ask about the number, why eighteen men, and eighteen years, and how they accord with the three years of that tree. That angry ruler of the synagogue admonished us to resolve a question of this kind, because the Lord Jesus on the Sabbath healed that bent-over woman. For he said to the crowds: *There are six days on which one ought to work; come then and be healed, not on the Sabbath day* [^24]. He is indeed convicted, beast by beast. *Hypocrites*, he says, *does not each of you on the Sabbath day loose his ox and lead it to water* [^25]? Nor ought he to be convicted from elsewhere, who was like the horse and the mule, having no understanding [^26]. And yet he seemed to himself to speak from Scripture, when he said: *There are six days on which one ought to work*. From Scripture indeed, but he mingled the sacrifice with the blood, since he relished carnally what was said spiritually. Yet let us take from him an occasion of understanding, and we too recall that in six days God made all that we see, heaven and earth and all that is in them; but on the seventh day God rested [^27]. And all those six days have an evening, because *heaven and earth will pass away* [^28]; but that seventh day of rest has no evening, because, when we too have rested after good works, we shall have no end in that rest. We find, then, that in six days all things were made: by the number six time was signified. Further, if time is signified to you by the number six, look at the three years of the tree, because there a threefold time is signified: one before the law, another under the law, the third which we now pass under grace. Whoever even in this time is not corrected, let him await the axe, not security. Since, then, that tree, at least in the third time, was to be corrected and changed from barrenness to fruitfulness [^29], so as to hear him who baptizes unto repentance: *Bear fruits worthy of repentance* [^30], and the number six signifies time, without doubt those eighteen whom the tower crushed [^31] were not corrected in that whole threefold time. For if six signifies time, three times six are eighteen.

**6.** But that woman, in the time of grace, is straightened in eighteen years [^32], as if she had said: *They have bent down my soul* [^33]. For the soul is bent which is weighed down by earthly things, bent is the soul which is burdened by the desires of things, bent is the soul which lies in saying it has its heart lifted up to the Lord. For if you have your "heart lifted up," you are not bent. If you always seek earthly things, if you desire to be happy from earthly things, if you do not think you worship God fruitfully unless earthly happiness has abounded for you, you are bent: your heart is not with the Lord. But this punishment came from the dominion of the devil. The whole human race was held bent under the devil, the whole human race was weighed down by earthly desires; there came one who would promise the kingdom of heaven. There is another life, there is the fellowship of angels; there is a homeland in which no violent man, no enemy is to be feared; there is a homeland walled by the will of God, surrounded by the shield of God's good will [^34], into which the enemy is not allowed, from which a friend does not perish; there is a homeland where no one departs, no one succeeds. That homeland is called Jerusalem, "Jerusalem" is interpreted "vision of peace." Be straightened from your crookedness, do not relish the earth. You have risen with Christ [^35], he is in heaven: stretch toward him, and you will not be bent.

**7.** But you did not do this, O malignant human race, you did not do this before the law, you did not do this under the law: do it under grace. After eighteen years he found you bent, who came upright, who came to be crucified for you, that he might loose you from the bond of the devil [^36]. The creditor's bond held you bent. For the devil had bound his debtors and, in a way, put them in fetters; that one came, no debtor, paid what he did not owe, and blotted out our handwriting [^37]. The handwriting of our debt, from where was it blotted out? By the blood of the just one. For why would the one set over death hold men unto death? He held them, plainly, by the condition of sin, as his own tree: he conquered and held, conquered and possessed. By right he held those whom by evil persuasion he had deceived. There came one persuading good things, having nothing evil, born of a virgin, not bearing the offshoot of sin, but clothed with the penalty of death, having the likeness of sinful flesh, that he might free us from sinful flesh [^38]. Hear him going to his passion and saying: *Behold, the ruler of the world comes, and in me he will find nothing* [^39]. Not as the world in you, not as in the whole human race; *in me he will find nothing*. So if he will find nothing in you, why will you die? What follows? After he said: *In me he will find nothing*, as though it were said to him: "Why then will you die?", *but that all may know*, he says, *that I do the will of my Father: rise, let us go from here* [^40]. He rises and goes to his passion, that he may fulfill the will of the Father, like the one sent to Siloam, not because he owed it, but that he might repay for the debtors, as it was said of him in prophecy: *What I did not seize, then I was paying* [^41]. He alone, then, was unworthy of death. But we are all worthy, because we have together drawn the desert of death. He drew no desert of death, and yet he undertook death for us. Therefore he paid what he did not owe, freed the debtors, blotted out the old handwriting [^42], set up new documents. What need have we now to keep an account with the old documents? He burned them with the fire of the Holy Spirit.

**8.** Acknowledge the redeemer, do not make yourself a debtor any longer. But for certain daily, even if lesser, sins, he gave a daily remedy. From faith say, from the heart say, from good will say: *Forgive us our sins*. For add, not only by saying, but also by doing, what follows: *As we also forgive our debtors* [^43]. So God, whose debtor you are, wanted you to have a debtor. Your debtor is he who does you wrong, who oppresses you unjustly, who takes something from you unfairly. He is your debtor, he owes you a penalty. Do you want to know that he is your debtor? Look, read. *An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth* [^44], and so on. The law decreed that each should suffer what he did. On the contrary, when the Gospel says: *I say to you: love your enemies* [^45], it does not speak against the law, as some, ill understanding, suppose, that the gentleness of the Gospel is contrary to the harshness of the law. It is not so. The law shows what is owed to you. The Gospel would not make you a remitter, unless the law showed you a debtor.
