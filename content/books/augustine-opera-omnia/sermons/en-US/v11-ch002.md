# Sermon 359/B: (Dolbeau 2)

**1.** The disturbance of yesterday, ours as much as yours—and, on your account, ours more than yours—commanded silence, I confess; but because love, which we must serve, bids us speak about our lord and brother—and because there is in you too a desire to hear, on which we pray that God may deign to bestow fruit in your conduct and obedience—we serve you in the name of Christ, since we serve Christ, whose members you are. Yet this I confess in the sight of God, before him and before you, to whose ears my thought is a voice: I call him to witness upon my soul that what brings us to this city, to speak to you something the Lord grants, is above all the love of your bishop—I do not mean the love by which we love him, but rather that by which we feel ourselves loved by him without any pretense. For although you know us, you can in no way know us as we ourselves know one another, who serve you in the love of Christ. For we do love you, and you see it, God knows it. And yet, dearly beloved, know this—I say it before Christ—that if you longed for us more than all the things you long for, and we sensed in the heart of your bishop even a slight will set against us, you would not be seeing us here.

**2.** For just now he deigned to send us such a letter as to say that, if we thought our coming should be either postponed or made a matter of deliberation, whether to come or not, we would offend love itself, which has been called God. Consider our weakness, undertaking such long journeys in the middle of winter. Let your love grasp something further. The blessed and venerable old man Xanthippus, primate of Numidia, summoned a council at Constantina for the twenty-eighth of January. The council was summoned by the primate of Numidia for the bishops of Numidia, among whom we too are numbered. And in that city of Constantina, your love knows, I think, that there is a bishop of our own side. For he was reared in the word of God and held the presbyterate in the town of Thagaste, and from there was given as bishop to Constantina. We cannot tell you with what letters he wished to draw me, that I should come to Constantina: with the council also summoned, he removed all my delays and hesitations. And yet, dearly beloved, such a letter came to my lowliness from my lord and brother, your bishop Aurelius, as to overcome by the gravity and weight of its sentiments whatever I had previously arranged, and not so much to bring me as to seize me, setting all else aside. For I considered that, since he deigned to command not with great confidence only but even with such great insistence—not on his own behalf but on God's—I should say to myself that, if I came here and yielded to his will—indeed to God's will through him—I might satisfy with his letter my departure from there before the lord and old man Xanthippus, lest perchance he be angry with me because, leaving the council of the Numidians, where the reckoning of my own order required me to be present, I chose rather to go here.

**3.** But to what does all this that I have said tend? Yesterday my eyes saw that there was a space not filled by the brethren, near to our ears and our voice; we were not the authors of the proposal that the discussion be held there instead, because we saw that it was better for those few to comply by coming to the bishops than for the bishops to come to those places, with great disturbance to the larger crowd that had already, in a manner, settled itself around the apse with the assurance of hearing: a far greater crowd would have had to be moved from these places to those. But were the men, perhaps, to be disregarded—what then of the weaker sex? Without doubt the women, once they began to press in out of eagerness to draw near, would they not have made a greater commotion and uttered cries that it would be most unseemly to hear in church? This alone, then, was asked: that those few who were leaning on the rails would deign to come up to the spaces nearest the place where we were speaking. What great thing was it that we were asking? But this they were unwilling even to hear, and a disturbance arose, and great sorrow for us, which God has just now deigned to wipe away through the sermon of your truly venerable bishop. But for the rest, brethren, we warn you not to think that a place to preach was denied me by him there, where it was so stubbornly demanded and tumultuously clamored for by certain people. See why I have said all this: because, if we felt even a slight contraction in the heart of our most blessed brother, we would not—especially when other necessities press upon us—have come to this city.

**4.** For what of it? Does your love not recall that I argued against the party of Donatus from that place, in the middle of the rails, for four days running? Was it waited for that you should ask? Was it even suggested by us? He himself saw of his own accord that it should be done, and did it; he did not see that it should be done, and did not do it. But perhaps you say: *What great or difficult thing were we asking?* Even if what you were asking is a small thing, the obedience we require of you is not a small matter. For from this rather let me speak. For I heard some of the brethren saying: *See, he himself argued that the weak must be served. He argued this the day before; on another day he did not act on it. Let us, then, be served. Why did he come down?* Why I came down, I tell your holiness. For he himself ought rather to pardon me, since I came down without his command. And my plan in coming down was such that I did not even consult him, lest he forbid it. And surely if I had consulted him and he had forbidden it, I could then do nothing but obey: it would have been necessary not to come down. I preferred, then, to ask pardon, because I came down without consulting or being commanded by him, rather than not to do what I thought ought to be done.

**5.** Now then, hear why I thought this ought to be done. How this people has always obeyed its bishop when present, not only we know, but all Africa, and perhaps the whole world, wherever the church of this city is known. For what dissoluteness and confusion of women and men there was here before, we all know, since we ourselves in our former years were part of this stain. It was brought about by the Lord through his servant that the mingled sexes should no longer keep vigil together. As a boy keeping vigil while I studied in this city, I kept such vigils amid women mingled with the wantonness of men, where occasion sometimes even tempted chastity. How honorably now the vigils are kept, how chastely, how holily! This diligence cannot displease even those against whom it was established. The wanton themselves, the insolent themselves, the very plotters against the chastity of others may grieve at this diligence, but they cannot fault it. But was this done only that we might rejoice in this one thing? What of the various approaches and entrances? How carefully it was managed, with what prudence devised, with what persistence accomplished, that those who, once they had entered, were to be in separate places, should also enter by separate ways, lest, entering in a narrow space, those wicked and shameless servants should begin what they would afterward strive to carry out—the things they are wont to say to the faces of matrons passing by! How vigilantly these things were seen to, with what vigor done away with! In the church of Mappalia, by the memorial of the blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian, how great were the things that used to happen—if we remember, perhaps we shall still grieve; if we forget, we give God less thanks. Let your love recall it with us, brethren: I am commemorating God's benefits to you through your bishop. Where then shameless little songs used to resound, now hymns ring out; where vigils were kept for indulgence, now they are kept for holiness; in short, where God was offended, God is appeased. I ask your love not to forget these things: they are near, they can be compared; they were yesterday, today they are not. And when could your bishop accomplish these things, unless he had an obedient people? That I may say something—and no small something—even about the good of your obedience: if, when your bishop attempted such things, your zeal did not consent, could he have carried it out in any way at all? God's mercy, then, was present both in his diligence and in your obedience. Since, therefore, we knew how you are wont to be submissive, since we set you before others as an example to imitate, so as to say to the small congregations in the countryside that make an uproar and resist their bishops, *Go, look at the people of Carthage*—since, then, we rejoiced abundantly in your good example, how could we yesterday be saddened, brethren, at your disobedience, as though our frequent coming had taught you disobedience?

**6.** Let your love, then, attend. As for my coming down, I grieved at the wretchedness of my ministry. You were asking to hear. What could the speaker build up, when the hearer was threatening ruin? *Why ruin?* you say. *What great thing were we asking? What evil were we seeking?* I will tell you what ruin—I will tell you that you may fear, not that you may fall. Do you not know that from a spark a fire arises? Do you not know that the tiniest drops fill rivers and sweep away foundations? Do not let disobedience seem to you a slight sin. Let us put it plainly: it made no difference whether you heard here or there; that there were spaces around us that could be filled by the crowd, both we know and you. What, then, remained, that you were unwilling to move over here, but mere obstinacy: *Either let only what we want be done, or let not be done what you want?* For we wanted you to hear, and what we wanted was useful to all. But certain people, because they were clinging to the rails, when their unreasonable wish was not granted, even shouted: *Let there be the dismissal!* Surely, however far off you are and however slowly I said it, *Let there be the dismissal*—see, you all heard it, because you patiently kept silence. What if we wanted to prove your very obedience? *But in a small matter,* someone says, *how could you prove it?* If in a small matter you did not obey, will you obey in a greater? Have you not read the Lord saying: *He who is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much, and he who is unfaithful in a very little is unfaithful also in much*[^1]?

**7.** Do you wish to know what an evil disobedience is, since I said, *The hearer was threatening ruin?* God planted all things in his paradise good. If in the whole world he made all things good—as Scripture says, *And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good*[^2]—if all things, how much more those that he set, more joyful, in paradise. Where, then, he had planted all things good, what does this mean: *Do not touch that tree*[^3]? Do you not know that people are wont to make a problem of this, who cannot see either how great a good obedience is or how great an evil disobedience? See, all good things had been planted. *Do not touch,* God says. *What am I not to touch? Did you put something evil here? If you put something evil here, take it away, and do not forbid me to touch.* *Do not touch this tree,* he says—which surely, unless it were good, would not be in paradise. Or do you perhaps think this, that outside paradise God had filled the earth with all good things and in paradise had planted an evil? In the rest of the earth too there were indeed good things, but in paradise certainly better ones. Yet because, among all the good things set in paradise, obedience was better, God forbade something from some quarter, lest by forbidding nothing he should not be Lord. And what? Perhaps someone thinks that God wished to be Lord out of his own arrogance. God's lordship is useful not to God but to him over whom he is Lord. He is neither less when we despise him nor greater when we serve him. To be under such a Lord is to our advantage, not to his. He who wishes to be Lord over us wishes it for our benefit, not for his own. He needs no good of ours; we are in need of all his goods, and of God himself, our highest good. For our highest and best good, than which nothing is better, is God himself. See the servant confessing; hear him from the psalm: *I said to the Lord, You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^4]. Therefore God forbade something, that he might impose a precept, that the one who is Lord might be served, that obedience might be distinguished from disobedience as virtue from vice; and that tree was called the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, not because fruits of good and evil hung there, but it was called the tree of the knowledge of good and evil because, if a man touched it against the precept, in that tree he would learn by experience what difference there is between the good of obedience and the evil of disobedience. For from that tree, the precept being despised, death followed; the precept being kept, immortality would have followed. You see, then, how great an evil disobedience is, my brethren: it was itself the first ruin of man.

**8.** Surely we wish to rise from that fall: why do we go back to where we fell from? Let Adam suffice; Christ has come. *In Adam all die, in Christ all will be made alive*[^5]. From Adam the root of evil, disobedience, passed into the human race; in Christ the root of immortality is obedience. Therefore Adam was for us the author and example of disobedience, but Christ of obedience. And how is Christ an example of obedience? Though he is equal to the Father, he says that he serves the Father. Surely you acknowledge with me, catholics, the catholic faith; you acknowledge: *I and the Father are one*[^6]; you acknowledge: *He who sees me sees the Father*[^7]; you acknowledge: *And the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^8]; you acknowledge: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*[^9]. For to Christ equality is not a robbery but his nature: he for whom it was a robbery fell while standing; he for whom it was his nature remained even while descending. But let the same apostle Paul set forth the commendation of the obedience of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. It is worth the effort to recall and hear the whole passage. See, behold the Son equal to the Father in the form of God, but read what follows: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped.* *But what did he do?* *But he emptied himself*[^10]. *How did he empty himself? I fear he may have lost his equality.* Do not suspect it: hear what follows, hear from what he emptied himself, hear that he emptied himself not by losing what he was but by taking on what he was not. *He emptied himself,* he says. *How? I beg you, tell me now. Taking the form of a servant.* *Who took the form of a servant?* He who, *though he was in the form of God*—there he *was,* here *taking*—*taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men*—made, surely, in a mother whom he had made—*being made in the likeness of men and found in form as a man.* *But we were speaking of obedience. We have now said much, and we have not heard of obedience. I heard* He emptied himself, *I heard* taking the form of a servant, *I heard* being made in the likeness of men, *but prove that he did this by obeying.* Hear at the end: *being made in the likeness of men and found in form as a man, he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death*[^11].

**9.** Let us look upon the Lord of the servant: the Lord obeys, and the servant despises? Let no one say in his heart: *But he did this because he is Lord.* What did he do because he is Lord? Did I say this to you: *Light the sun, let the moon run its monthly waning and waxing; make the stars shine from heaven, the springs gush from the earth, the animals walk, the birds fly, the fish swim?* Finally, did I even say this: *Open the eyes of the blind, unstop the ears of the deaf, drive off the fever of the sick, raise up the flesh of the dead?* I say none of these things: these he did as God; the obedience I demand of you is the obedience he rendered first as a servant. Obedience, I say, I demand. Through immortality you will be like him; through obedience he was made like you. He will give you his life, for he took on your death. But you say: *He obeyed God the Father.* Whom, then, are you bidden to obey? He obeyed God the Father, as an equal. Or do you think it matters that he served God the Father, and to you it is said, *Obey your bishop?* For who set the bishop over you, that you should obey him? Or has the Gospel slipped your mind: *He who hears you hears me*[^12]; *he who receives you receives me, and he who receives me receives him who sent me*[^13]? Finally you say: *He served the Father.* What if he served you too? Are you perhaps his father and mother? Because here he deigned to have a mother, not a father, that he might show both his generations to be wonderful, the divine and the human—the divine without a mother, the human without a father—and yet he was a boy subject to his parents (read the Gospel), in his boyhood subject to his parents, in his divine majesty the Lord of his parents—yet you are not his mother, even though he served you too. For he taught us to serve not by word only but by example. It was too little for the Lord to say to his disciples, *Serve one another.* For the Lord commands it to be done: surely it should have sufficed that the Lord commanded. Or was it to be expected that he should show what he commanded? Would any of us really dare to require that our Lord first carry out what he commanded? And yet, though none of us dared to require it, he gave from himself an example of serving one another to his disciples: *Whoever,* he says, *wishes to be greater among you shall be your servant*[^14]. And at once, lest the disciples be saddened at the servile name and say to him, *Then, Lord, shall we be servants, we whom you redeemed? Shall we be servants, for whom you pour out your blood? Do we not recognize the price of our freedom in our cost, your blood?*—he consoled, perhaps, their saddened pride, not yet healed: *He shall be,* he says, *your servant, just as the Son of man came not to be served but to serve*[^15]. See, he became our minister too, and we are not his mother. Or are we perhaps also his mother? *These are my brethren and my mother, who do the will of my Father*[^16].

**10.** But see, someone says, let my bishop take an example from my Lord, and serve me. I say to your love—let him understand who can—: unless he served, he would not command. For he serves who commands what is useful; he serves by his vigilance, he serves by his care, he serves by his solicitude, he serves, in short, by his love. For he too who was here made a minister surely commanded his disciples. Hear him commanding and them serving: *Where do you wish us to prepare the Passover for you?*[^17] And he sends whom he wishes where he wishes; he bids it be prepared for him where he wishes. What he commanded was done, and yet he himself served the more; for he did not lie when he said: *Just as the Son of man came not to be served but to serve.* How did he *come not to be served but to serve?* See, I see the disciples running, preparing the Passover, arranging the supper. How did he *come not to be served but to serve?* But what is it to *serve?* It follows: *And to lay down his life for his friends*[^18]. Do you wish to know what he ministered to us? From this we live today, and from his table we are fed today, the table he ministered then.

**11.** *Go,* he says, *into the village that is opposite you, and there you will find the colt of a donkey tied, on which no one has sat; bring it, and if they say to you, "What are you doing? Where are you leading the colt?", answer, "The Lord has need of it," and they will let it go to you*[^19]. They heard, they went, they did it. Did anyone resist? Did anyone say: *Why does he want a colt brought to him? Surely he who raised the dead has not given out from walking?* Hear, servant; do what is commanded by him who surely wants you well, by him who takes care for your salvation. To ask why he commanded is to argue, not to comply. First be a diligent complier, that you may be a fit arguer. *Wash yourselves, be clean*—these are the words of one commanding. *Remove the evils from your souls and from before my eyes*—these are the words of one commanding. *Learn to do good, give judgment for the orphan, do justice for the widow*—have you done what he commanded?—*and come, let us argue, says the Lord*[^20]. The colt was loosed from the village that was opposite them. What is the village opposite the disciples but this world? *Go into the village that is opposite you.* This world is opposite the disciples, falsehood opposite truth, lust opposite chastity, disobedience opposite obedience. In this village, which is the world, was a colt tied, on which no one had sat. Who is this colt tied in the opposing village, on which no one had sat? Who is it but the people of the gentiles, bound by the chain of the devil, on which no one had sat, because it had carried no prophet? It is loosed, it is led, it carries God, it is governed by the Lord, it is led along the way by the Lord, it is admonished by the Lord's whip; and in those who complied, that they might bring the colt, there was obedience, and in those who at once let the colt go, hearing that the Lord had need of it, there was obedience. You may understand those people as you wish. For perhaps the ones who had tied the colt are contrary powers; perhaps those men who had tied the colt signify the devil and his angels, by whom the people of the gentiles had been bound with ruinous superstition: yet so great is the power of the one commanding that even they dare not hold back the one whom the Lord says he has need of. What are you, my brethren? What do you wish to be? The looseners of the colt, or the colt? Far be it that you should be those who had tied the colt—and yet not even they resisted. What, then, do you wish to be, my brethren: those who loosed the colt, or the colt itself? You do not dare to claim for yourselves the person of those through whom the colt was loosed: the apostles did this. That is the person of the prelates: this we ourselves, according to the strength the Lord deigns to grant, sustain with all solicitude; from this we speak to you. You are the colt; obey those who lead you to carry the Lord. Surely, dearly beloved, consider how the disciples loosed the colt and led it to the Lord. They led, it followed; they did not drag, and it did not kick back. And yet, because we are speaking of our servitude, when the disciples led the colt to the Lord, they were rather serving the colt; so we too serve you, when we lead you to the Lord, when we teach and admonish obedience: if your weakness were not served, you would not hear us today.

**12.** Surely, dearly beloved, we too are men; weak ourselves, we carry the weak. You disturbed us yesterday, and in that very disturbance we feared for you more, lest you sadden in us the Spirit of God, through whom we serve you. How do you suppose, brethren, that I, now standing and about to speak to you—how do you suppose I could be disturbed, considering—as I have already recalled—how obedient you are wont to be, and that you showed yourselves disobedient while we were present, as though we teach disobedience, as though we do not, according to our small measure, hold your little rein, to lead you to the Lord? And yet, dearly beloved—as you heard from him, and it is true—however great our care for you or on your behalf, can it be greater than his who serves you in a special way, who so presides as to be beneath you? We seem to preside from a higher place: yet so great is the burden of solicitude and care that it makes us be beneath your feet. In short, tread on us and live. What is it, my brethren, what is any virtue at all in a servant of God without obedience? For what is obedience? You love love: obedience is its daughter, obedience is the daughter of love. But love cannot be barren. Let no one at all deceive you, let no one say: *I do not have obedience, but I have love.* You have no love at all. Wherever this mother is, she gives birth. If she is there, she has given birth; if she has not given birth, she is not there. The root is hidden, brethren, the fruit is in the open. I do not believe it clings in the soil unless I see what hangs on the branch. Do you have love? Show me its fruit: let me see obedience, let me rejoice in obedience, let me embrace the offspring, that I may acknowledge the mother.

**13.** See how great are the goods that even pretenders seem to have. Yesterday we heard the praises of a true martyr: what torments he endured, how immense, how many, how relentless! Were love lacking, it would be madness. Whence do we praise, whence do we proclaim, whence do we rejoice together, unless because we see in what Church, for what faith, what commanding king he resisted? For it is not because he resisted one commanding, but because he resisted one commanding what it is sin to comply with—for that is not even to be called obedience, where something ruinous and sacrilegious is commanded. For just as there is no faith when something false is believed, so there is no obedience where something harmful is commanded. For how could I call him obedient who believes a man and despises God? The powers in this world are ordered, and above all powers is the divine power. You would not be obedient if, by complying, you, a servant, were perhaps to despise your master in obeying your father. This I say: if perhaps you were a servant, and your father and a fellow servant commanded you one thing against what your master had commanded you, and you obeyed your father rather than your master, would I not call you disobedient and a subverter of order? For he is rather to be heard who has the greater power, he who has the lawful power. I would not, then, call obedient one who complies with the procurator against the proconsul, with the proconsul against the emperor; so I do not call obedient one who complies with the emperor against God.

**14.** Whence, then, was Vincent obedient, whence holy, whence the winner of the true crown, victor over so many sufferings, and true to his own name? Whence? See who commanded, whom he commanded, what he commanded. The emperor commanded, he commanded a Christian, he commanded that he offer incense to idols. If you attend to the rank of the one commanding, the emperor commanded a provincial. *Some place is given to obedience, as it were, when I hear who commanded, whom he commanded.* But wait, attend to what he commanded: to offer incense to idols. *Let him who does not offer incense to idols be punished.* The provincial now complies, if a greater power commands nothing to the contrary. But raise your ear, hear two voices: hear one from the tribunal, hear the other from heaven. What did you hear from the tribunal? *He who does not sacrifice to the gods shall be punished.* What from heaven? *He who sacrifices to the gods shall be rooted out*[^21]. Here let your obedience be proved, martyr. Distinguish the voices, set apart the powers. You see him who commands; rather fear him who forbids. This is the crown of martyrdom, this the triumph, the devil being utterly conquered and trampled underfoot, whom the martyr feared when he flattered, whom he had despised when he raged. He himself expressed this—just as the things that were read were being heard, he expressed it in his own words: when the one who was raging wished, as it were, to show mercy, then most of all he seemed to need to be guarded against. For a deceitful mercy did more harm than open cruelty. In the roar of one raging, he did not dread the lion; in the flattery of one showing mercy, he did not keep silent that he had feared the dragon. For it is the very lion and dragon of which it was said: *You shall trample on the lion and the dragon*[^22].

**15.** Let no one, then, say, dearly beloved brethren, let no one say—for everyone who says it deceives himself—that the Church now suffers no persecution, because the emperors are catholic, because, to render account to God for their rule, they command all things for the Church and are vigilant for its increase; let no one thereafter say that the Church suffers no persecution: it does not suffer the lion, but the dragon does not sleep. Hear that lion, in the open passion of the saints, brought to mind by Peter as he exhorts the martyrs to the triumph of victory: *Your adversary the devil,* he says, *prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour*[^23]. For then there were dreadful threats and great cruelty of the gentiles against the saints of God, then the onslaught of hostile commands and the great roaring of the powers: the lion was roaring, but neither did the dragon cease. You have heard Peter exhorting against the lion; hear Paul too making us wary against the dragon: *I betrothed you,* he says, *to one husband.* For many wished to become the husbands of one wife. But consider, brethren, consider what many husbands do to one wife. They make her what must be thought of so as to be detested, not spoken of so as to be abhorred. There were, then, many husbands wishing to belong to one wife. But that friend of the bridegroom, zealous for the bridegroom and not for himself: *I betrothed you,* he says, *to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. But I fear that, as the serpent seduced Eve, so also your minds may be corrupted from the chastity that is toward God in Christ*[^24]. He feared that she would be corrupted not by the cruelty of the lion but by the flattery of the dragon. Peter admonished you to take no account of the lion for God's sake; Paul admonishes you to be vigilant against the dragon for God's sake, and in God to trample on the lion and the dragon.

**16.** For do you wish to know what kind of dragon this is, how his snares are to be avoided, how great is the cunning of the enemy? See, now for nearly six thousand years, practiced in the temptations of the saints, against the one and true God he has made many false gods. But one Son of God came, foretold by his heralds sent before him; the Son of God came, undid the works of the devil as it were the binding of that colt, taught by word, confirmed by example; he showed that the one true God is to be worshiped, that he himself is to be adored, and not the angels in his stead, since even the angels, because they love God, because love reigns especially in them, wish God to be loved together with themselves, not themselves in place of God. When, therefore, this Christian teaching had taught these things, it taught this: that the saints must die, if need be, for this teaching. For what teaching? The one that has love *from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*[^25]. For this teaching he taught the saints of God to die, and commended them to the Church to be venerated. How venerated? *Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^26]. Hence precious the death of Peter, hence precious the death of Paul, hence precious the death of Vincent, hence precious the death of Cyprian. Whence precious? From pure love and a good conscience and faith unfeigned. But that serpent saw this, that ancient serpent saw the martyrs being honored, the temples deserted: cunning and venomous in his vigilance against us, because he could not make false gods for Christians, he made false martyrs. But O you, catholic offspring, compare with us a little these false martyrs to the true martyrs, and by pious faith distinguish what the devil tries to confound by his venomous deceit.

**17.** That enemy wishes to obscure for us the distinction between true martyrs and false; he wishes to extinguish the eye of the heart, lest we discern these things; he wished to oppose them with a like appearance, but against the devil let us attend to the apostle, who says of certain people: *Holding the appearance of godliness but denying its power*[^27]. What is the power of godliness? Love. It is that mother of obedience. Therefore attend to the appearance by which the devil tries to join false martyrs to true martyrs. *See,* he says, *these too suffer persecution.* Still, O enemy, still you confound. They suffer persecution, you say. Set before me also robbers, murderers, parricides, adulterers, poisoners: do not all these too suffer persecution? Did my Lord—whom I will to be mine, and yours, like it or not, since he is mine even though I had not willed it, but my Lord for my good because I will it, for my ill if I had not willed it—did he not foresee you, O ancient enemy, and in foreseeing admonish, when, exhorting his disciples to the glory of suffering, he said: *Blessed are they who suffer persecution for righteousness' sake*[^28]? Against all your poisons, against your two-tongued or three-tongued or many-tongued attempts, one word kept watch: *for righteousness' sake.* On account of this word, murderers suffer persecution and are not martyrs, adulterers suffer and are not martyrs. Now show your martyrs: you boast that they suffer; I ask why they suffer. You praise the penalty; I examine the cause. The cause, I say, I examine; the cause I ask. Tell me why he suffers whom you parade as suffering. For righteousness? Teach this: for that is the cause of the martyrs. For it is not the penalty that crowns martyrs, but the cause.

**18.** O enemy and cunning seducer, against your false martyrs the true martyrs cried out in the psalm: *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from a nation that is not holy*[^29]. *Judge me,* he says, *and distinguish my cause.* See, dearly beloved, how much he passed over: he wished his cause to be distinguished. For it is the cause that is distinguished. The true martyr did not say: *Distinguish my fasts*—for they too fast; he did not say: *Distinguish my works that they do for the poor*—for they too do them; he did not say: *Distinguish my baptism*—they have the same; he did not say: *Distinguish my creed*—they too confess it; in all things he found himself like them, and prayed only that his cause be distinguished. *Distinguish my cause.* I fast, and he fasts. But why I, why he? I for Christ. *But I too,* he says, *for Christ.* Is it so for Christ? If for Christ, I believe, then surely also for the words of Christ; if against the words of Christ, then assuredly against Christ. *And what are the things of Christ,* he says, *against which I suffer?* Do you so rage in a false passion that your heart has fallen away from truthful preaching? Look upon the Lord himself, our common Lord, whom you confess with me, even when you do not believe.

**19.** See whether Christ did not wish to show both himself and his Church, so that those invited to the wedding and clothed in the wedding garment might err in neither—neither in the bridegroom nor in the bride. How did the bridegroom show himself? *It behooved Christ to suffer and to rise on the third day*[^30]. I recognize the bridegroom: this the prophets too said, this said those servants who were sent before to invite to the wedding. *It behooved Christ to suffer and to rise on the third day.* Such he showed himself to them; he demonstrated to those who doubted that this had been fulfilled in him according to the prophets. What of the bride? Did he by any means keep silent? At once he showed her too. For he saw that he was now consequently desired. *It behooved Christ,* he says, *to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day.* See, I see the bridegroom, see, I recognize him. What of the bride? *And that repentance and forgiveness be preached in his name among all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^31]. This Church, expressed by the mouth of Christ, foretold by the proclamation of the prophets, acknowledge, if you are a martyr; hold to this one; in this and for this pour out your blood, render what was advanced to you. Hear the apostle John: *Therefore,* he says, *Christ laid down his life for us, because we too ought to lay down our life for the brethren*[^32]. Wake up: *for the brethren,* not against the brethren. What does it profit that you confess the bridegroom, that you honor the head of the household, and his wife you—I will not say neglect, but—assail with false charges? You, a man, having a wife whom you did not redeem with your blood, yet love her so that, if someone were to be obsequious to you, kept watch at your house daily, fell at your feet, attended you with every proclamation, never and nowhere kept silent your praises, but said one charge against your wife, he would pour away all the service he had rendered.

**20.** Therefore let the confessions of true and false martyrs be brought forth and compared; let us set before our eyes what we beheld yesterday. For we saw a most delightful spectacle: a martyr contending against the impious one who compelled him, a true faith yielding under no penalty, Vincent conquering everywhere. We saw, we were present. That reading spoke with our heart: we were delighted. That serpent, envious of the martyrs, that snake whom Vincent guarded against when he flattered, because he could not blame that passion, stirred up sedition among us. Let those acknowledge it and grieve who lent him their tongues for his ministry. For what did those cries mean—*Give the dismissal, the dismissal, give the dismissal*—but that that martyr should not be praised a little longer? Let them be compared, then; let the confessions of true and false martyrs be compared. You call yourself a martyr because you resist the power. But what does the power command? See, I hear what was commanded to the true martyrs; let me hear also what is commanded to you. I see how gloriously they refused, because I see what they refused; you, show what you refuse: let me compare your voices, let me see what I ought to imitate, what to follow. *Offer incense to the gods.* *I will not.* We have heard the glorious voice of a true martyr; let us hear also from that side: *Make peace with your brother.* O abominable voice, worthy of damnation not only by the most high God but even by human powers! *Make peace with your brother.* *I will not.* Surely you suffer against Christ.

**21.** Open the Gospel, read: *If you offer your gift at the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there and go first to be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift*[^33]. *If,* he says, *you remember that your brother has something against you.* What is *has something against you?* You offended him, you did something unjustly against him. *Go to be reconciled to your brother:* I accept the gift from those at peace. Why do you offer a gift at the altar of God, when in your heart is the seat of the devil? Who sowed discord there? Who planted it? Who, in short, dwells there? Is it not he who is always the sower of discords, the author of dissensions, the source of quarrels? If Christ said this of two men, instilled such great care, such great fear, demands concord so urgently, as if to say: *You offer one thing, I require another. I take even what you have offered, if you bring what I have required*—if this in two men, how much more in two peoples? If to have such a cause against one man is dangerous and ruinous, how much more against the whole world, how much more against that whole bride of Christ, spread *among all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^34]?

**22.** But perhaps you have a cause against me? For the Lord says this: *If you remember that your brother has something against you.* So you will say to me: *Your brother, he says, has something against you; I have something against you, for you have nothing against me.* Let us inquire, then: if it be found in me, I correct it; if it be found in you, you correct it. Set out for me the cause you have against me, and I will quickly tell what I have against you. This I have against you, which my Lord has against you. You speak charges against his matron, and his matron is the whole world in his saints and faithful; against her you direct your charge. Did you investigate anything, did you judge? On what tribunal were you sitting, when the whole world stood before you to be heard? You do not know the cause of your neighbor: how, then, did you judge concerning the whole world? I read out the records, I show who were the betrayers, I read that they themselves who confessed their own crimes sat in judgment against the innocent Caecilian; the public archives proclaim that your forefathers were the first to bring the cause of Caecilian, bishop of this city, to the emperor Constantine: yet I do not charge you with the crime of your forefathers. All the acts are on my side, and I do not throw others' crimes at you. But you bring forward nothing by which to convict even my forefathers, and you accuse me, who was born so long afterward. *If, then,* he says, *you do not charge me with the deeds of my forefathers, why do you charge me with your own deeds?* What deeds of mine? *Because you are not with me.* And what evil befalls you because I am not with you? Did I not say that I have a cause against you, which my Lord has against you? Hear him speaking: *He who is not with me is against me*[^35].

**23.** And so, dearly beloved, as much as we could, and perhaps beyond what we could, compelled by your zeal that we might remedy the sorrow of yesterday, we have perhaps been more talkative than the weakness of standing, whether ours or yours, required. But this above all I urge, and to this I rather bring the sermon to a close: that, since I have recalled the saying of the Lord, not to be heard carelessly—*If you offer your gift at the altar, go first to be reconciled to your brother*[^36]—our hearts be reconciled to one another. First and chiefly, do not think that either yesterday or ever at all your bishop is angry out of hatred, but out of love: what is owed to you for Christ's sake is never shaken out of his breast. Let yesterday's clouds clear; and let not only the love that never departed, but also the former joy be recalled, so that it be alike our care—as we have exhorted you in Christ—to serve the weak for their benefit, not for their harmful will. For, dearly beloved, just because one must serve the sick man, when perhaps he asks for food or refuses food, so that he is forced by you, with trouble and solicitude or even some importunity, to take it lest he die—because the sick man is served thus—it does not follow that it must be given to him even if he asks for poison. Do not, then, grow accustomed to the great evil of disobedience. Nor let anyone again say: *What of it? Did we ask for poison, because we wished the pulpit moved from place to place?* Disobedience is poison; it first killed man. Do we, brethren, fault this, that you asked? Truly we say to your love that, even if you had asked somewhat longer, yet by asking you could not displease us, as you displeased us by being angry and saying: *Let there be the dismissal.* This is what we want you to grieve over. When you ask, if it seems good, it is granted to you; if it does not seem good, turn yourselves from asking to compliance; but as for anger, abuse, provocation of those who serve you in Christ with such great solicitude—if you choose to break out into these, this is already poison, if indeed it is not death itself. Do not, brethren, we beg you, we beseech you; distinguish the church of God from the theaters. Here all those things that are done wickedly in the theaters are wont to be punished, to be healed, to be brought to confession and to penance, not to be brought in here. To rise up here, to bawl here, to lord it here—may God avert it both from your hearts and from our grief, and may we always rejoice in your obedience.
