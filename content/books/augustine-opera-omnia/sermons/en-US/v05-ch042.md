# Sermon 178

**1. 1.** When the Epistle of the blessed Apostle was read about the appointing of bishops, it surely reminded us to look upon ourselves; and it reminded you not to judge us; most of all because we all heard the last sentence of the passage of the recent gospel reading: *Do not judge according to persons, but judge a just judgment*[^1]. So in judging, no one takes on another's person, if he does not take on his own. The blessed Apostle says in a certain place, *I do not so fight as one beating the air; but I chastise my body and bring it into servitude; lest perhaps, preaching to others, I myself be found rejected*[^2]. By his fear he terrified us. For what will the lamb do where the ram trembles? Among the many things, then, by which the Apostle wrote what kind a bishop ought to be, we heard this too, on which now perhaps it may suffice to speak and discourse. For if we try to examine each one, and discourse on each as is fitting, neither do our strengths suffice for speaking, nor yours for hearing. What, then, is it I wish to say, if he who terrified me help me? Among other things he says that a bishop ought to be powerful in sound doctrine, that he may be able to refute those who contradict. It is a great work, a grave burden, a steep slope. But: *I will hope*, he says, *in God, for he will free me from the snare of the hunters, and from the harsh word*[^3]. For there is no cause that makes the dispenser of God more slothful to refute those who contradict than the fear of the harsh word.

**2. 2.** First, then, what it is *to refute those who contradict*, as the Lord grants, I will expound to you. Those who contradict are not to be understood in one way. For very few contradict us by speaking; but many by living badly. When does a Christian dare to say to me that it is good to seize others' goods; since he does not dare to say that it is good tenaciously to keep his own? For did that rich man, whose region had yielded a crop, and who did not find where to put his fruits, and rejoiced that he had found the plan of pulling down the old storehouses and building new and larger ones, that he might fill them, and say to his soul, *Soul, you have many goods for much time, be glad, rejoice, be filled*—did that rich man, then, seek others' goods? He was arranging to gather his own fruits, taking counsel where to put them, thinking not about anyone's neighboring fields, nor of a disturbed boundary, nor of a despoiled poor man, nor of a circumvented simple man, but only about gathering his own. Hear what he heard who tenaciously kept his own; and from this understand what awaits those who seize others'. So when he reckoned that he had found a most prudent plan—of casting down the old narrow storehouses, and building new and larger ones, and gathering and storing all his fruits, not coveting and seizing others'—God said to him, *Fool*; where you seem wise to yourself, there a fool. *Fool*, he says, *this night they demand your soul from you; these things which you have prepared, whose will they be*[^4]? If you keep them, they will not be yours; if you distribute them, they will be yours. Why, he says, do you store up what you are going to leave behind? Behold, the fool was rebuked for storing up badly. If he is a fool who stores up his own, find a name for him who takes another's. If the storer of his own is sordid, the seizer of another's is ulcerous. But not such an ulcerous one as the one who lay before the rich man's gate, and whose ulcers the dogs licked. For that one was ulcerous in body; the seizer, in heart.

**3. 3.** Perhaps someone may answer and say: It was not a very great punishment for that man, to whom God said, *Fool*. God does not say *Fool* the way a man says it. Such a word of God against anyone is a judgment. For is God going to give the kingdom of heaven to fools? But to those to whom he is not going to give the kingdom of heaven, what remains for them but the punishment of hell? We seem to conjecture this: let us see it openly and manifestly. For that rich man too, before whose gate lay the most poor ulcerous man, was not called a seizer of others' goods. *There was a certain rich man*, it says, *who clothed himself in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day*. *A rich man*, it says, *there was*; it did not say: A slanderer; it did not say: An oppressor of the poor; it did not say: A seizer of others' goods, or an informer, or a receiver; it did not say: A despoiler of orphans; it did not say: A persecutor of widows; none of these, but: *There was a certain rich man*. What is great about that? He was rich, he was rich from his own. Whom had he robbed? Or would he perhaps rob, and the Lord be silent about him, and take on his person, if he hid his crimes—he who says to us, *Do not judge according to persons*? If, then, you wish to hear the crime of that rich man, do not seek more than you hear from the Truth. *He was rich; he clothed himself in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day*. What, then, was his crime? An ulcerous man lying before the gate, and not helped. For this was said openly of him, that he was unmerciful. For surely, dearly beloved, if that poor man lying before the gate had received sufficient bread from the rich man, would it be said of him that *he desired to be filled with the crumbs that fell from the rich man's table*? On account of this inhumanity alone, by which he despised the poor man lying before his gate, and did not fittingly and worthily feed him, he died and was buried; and when he was in torments in the underworld, he lifted up his eyes, and saw the poor man in the bosom of Abraham[^5]. And why should I linger on more? He desired a drop, who did not give a crumb; he did not receive by a just sentence, who did not give out of cruel greed. If, then, this is the punishment of the greedy, what is the punishment of the seizers?

**4. 4.** But the seizer of others' goods says to me: I am not like that rich man. I make love-feasts, I send food to those bound in prison, I clothe the naked, I receive strangers. Do you think you give? Do not take, and you have given. The one to whom you give rejoices; the one from whom you take weeps; which of these two will the Lord hear? You say to the one to whom you give: Give thanks because you have received. But the other on the other side says to you: I groan, from whom you took. And you have kept nearly the whole, and given him a little. If, then, you had given to the needy what you took from another—but God does not love such works. God says to you: Fool, I commanded you to give, but not of another's. If you have, give of your own; if you do not have what to give of your own, it is better to give to no one than to despoil others. The Lord Christ is going to say, when he sits in his judgment, and has separated some to the right and others to the left, to those who worked well, *Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the kingdom*; but to the barren, who worked nothing good toward the poor: *Go into the eternal fire*. And what is he going to say to the good? *For I was hungry, and you gave me to eat*, and the rest. And they will answer him: *Lord, when did we see you hungry*? And he to them: *When you did it to one of my least, you did it to me*. Understand, then, fool, you who wish to give alms from plunder, that whenever you feed a Christian, you feed Christ; when you despoil a Christian, you despoil Christ. Attend to what he is going to say to those on the left: *Go into the eternal fire*. Why? *For I was hungry, and you did not give me to eat; I was naked, and you did not clothe me*[^6]. *Go*. Where? *Into the eternal fire*. Plainly, *go*. Why? *I was naked, and you did not clothe me*. If, then, into the eternal fire goes the one to whom Christ is going to say: I was naked, and you did not clothe me; what place in the eternal fire will he have to whom he is going to say: I was clothed, and you despoiled me?

**5. 5.** Here perhaps, to escape this voice, lest Christ say to you: I was clothed, and you despoiled me; with your habit changed, you think of despoiling a pagan, and clothing a Christian. And here too Christ will answer you—rather, now he will answer you through his servant, some minister of his; Christ will answer you and say: Even here spare my losses. For when, as a Christian, you despoil a pagan, you hinder him from becoming a Christian. Here too perhaps you will still answer: I do not inflict punishment out of hatred, but rather out of love of discipline; therefore I despoil the pagan, that by this harsh and wholesome discipline I may make him a Christian. I would hear and believe, if what you took from the pagan, you gave to a Christian.

**5. 6.** We have spoken against the one vice of plundering, by which human affairs are everywhere laid waste; we have spoken, and no one contradicts us. For who dares contradict the most open truth by speaking? Therefore we do not do what the Apostle admonished, we do not refute those who contradict; we address the obedient, we instruct those who praise; we do not refute those who contradict. But they do not contradict by tongue, but by life. I admonish, he plunders; I teach, he plunders; I command, he plunders; I rebuke, he plunders; how does he not contradict? Let me say, then, what I think suffices on this matter. Abstain, brothers, abstain, sons, abstain from the habit of plundering; and you who groan under the hands of plunderers, abstain from the desire of plundering. Another is powerful, and plunders; you groan in the hand of the plunderer; because you cannot plunder, therefore you do not do it. Have the power, and there I will praise the desire subdued.

**6. 7.** Holy Scripture calls blessed *the one who did not go after gold; who could transgress, and did not transgress; who could do evils, and did not do them*[^7]. But you say: I never denied another's deposit. Because perhaps no one entrusted you anything; or perhaps he entrusted it, but entrusted it before witnesses. Tell me, did you return it, when from one man alone, you alone, where God had been between you, received it? If you returned it then, if to the dead man who entrusted it, to the ignorant son you returned it; then I will praise you, because you did not go after gold; because you could transgress, and did not transgress; because you could do evils, and did not do them. If perhaps you found in the road another's purse of gold pieces, where no one saw you, and without any delay you returned it to him whose it was. Come, brothers, return to yourselves, inspect yourselves, question yourselves, answer yourselves truly, and judge yourselves not according to persons, but judge a just judgment. Behold, you are a Christian, you frequent the church, you hear the word of God, you are most gladly moved by the reading of the word of God. You praise the one treating it, I seek the one doing it; you, I say, praise the one speaking, I seek the one doing. You are a Christian, you frequent the church, you love the word of God, and gladly hear it. Behold, in what I propose, examine yourself in it, weigh yourself in it, ascend in it the tribunal of your mind, and set yourself before yourself, and judge yourself; and if you find yourself depraved, correct yourself. I propose, then. God says in his law that a thing found must be returned[^8]; God says in his law, which he gave to the first people, for whom Christ had not yet died, that a thing found, as another's, must be returned; if anyone, for example, finds in the road another's purse of gold pieces, he ought to return it. But he does not know to whom? Ignorance does not excuse him, if greed does not rule.

**7. 8.** I will tell your Charity—since the gifts of God exist, and there are in the people of God those who do not hear the word of God in vain—I will tell what a most poor man did, when we were at Milan; so poor that he was the usher of a grammarian; but plainly a Christian, although that grammarian was a pagan; better at the curtain than in the chair. He found a purse, unless perhaps the number deceives me, with nearly two hundred gold pieces; mindful of the law, he posted a notice publicly. For he knew it must be returned; but to whom to return it, he did not know. He posted a notice publicly: Let the one who lost gold pieces come to that place, and seek that man. He who, lamenting, was wandering everywhere, having found and read the notice, came to the man. And lest perhaps he seek another's, he asked for the signs, inquired the quality of the purse, the seal, even the number of the gold pieces. And when the other had faithfully answered all, he returned what he had found. But that man, filled with joy, and seeking to repay the kindness, offered him as it were a tithe, twenty gold pieces; who refused to take them. He offered at least ten; he refused to take them. He at least asked him to take five; he was unwilling. Indignant, the man threw down the purse: I have lost nothing, he said; if you do not wish to take anything from me, neither have I lost anything. What a contest, my brothers, what a contest? what a fight, what a conflict? The world the theater, God the spectator. At last, overcome, he took what was offered; at once he distributed it all to the poor, he did not leave one gold piece in his house.

**8. 9.** What is this? If I have done anything in your hearts, if the word of God has settled in you, if it has found rest with you, do this, my brothers; do not think you suffer loss if you do it; it is a great gain, if you do what I say. I lost twenty gold pieces, I lost two hundred, five hundred. What did you lose? They had perished from your house; another had lost them, not you. The earth is common, you are in one house, in this world both travelers, you have entered one lodging of this life. The one put them down, the other forgot them; they fell from one, you found them elsewhere. Who found them? A Christian. Who found them? You who heard the law, a Christian who heard the law. Who found them? You who, when you heard, praised many things, you found them. If, then, you praised truly, return what you found. If, then, you did not return what you found; when you praised, you gave testimony against yourself. Be faithful finders, and then blame the unjust plunderers. For what you found and did not return, you plundered. As much as you could, you did; because you could not do more, therefore you did not do more. He who denies another's deposit, if he can, even takes it. What you do not take, fear forbids; you do not do a good, but you fear an evil.

**9. 10.** What great thing is it, to fear evil? It is a great thing, not to do evil; it is a great thing, to love good. For even the robber fears evil; and where he cannot, he does not do it; and yet he is a robber. For God questions the heart, not the hand. The wolf comes to the sheepfold of the sheep, seeks to invade, seeks to slaughter, seeks to devour; the shepherds keep watch, the dogs bark; it can do nothing, it does not take, it does not kill; but yet the wolf came, the wolf returns. Surely it is not because it did not take a sheep that the wolf came, and the sheep returns? The wolf came raging, the wolf returns trembling; yet it is a wolf both raging and trembling. Question yourself, then, whoever you are who wish to judge; and see whether you do not then do evil, when you can do it and not be punished by man. Then you fear God. There is no one there, but you and the one to whom you do evil, and God who sees both; see, there fear. It is too little that I say: See, there fear evil. There love good. For even if from fear of hell you do not do evil, you are not yet perfect. I dare to say, if from fear of hell you do not do evil, there is indeed faith in you, because you believe there will be a judgment of God; I rejoice in your faith, but I still fear your malice. What is it that I have said? That if from fear of hell you do not do evil, you do not do good from love of righteousness.

**10. 11.** It is one thing to fear punishment; another to love righteousness. There ought to be in you a chaste love, by which love you desire to see, not heaven and earth, not the liquid plains of the sea, not trifling spectacles, not the flashings and brilliances of gems; but desire to see your God, to love your God; because it is said, *Beloved, we are sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be; but we know that when it has appeared, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^9]. Behold, for the sake of which vision do good, behold, for the sake of which do not do evil. For if you love to see your God, if in this pilgrimage you sigh with that love; behold, the Lord your God tests you, as if he said to you: Behold, do what you wish, fulfill your desires, extend your wickedness, broaden your luxury, whatever pleases, reckon it lawful; I do not punish you for this, I do not send you into hell, I will only deny you my face. If you were terrified, you loved; if at this which was said—Your God will deny you his face—your heart trembled, if in not seeing your God you reckoned it a great punishment; you loved freely. If, then, my sermon has found in your hearts some spark of the free love of God, nourish it; call yourselves to increase it by prayer, by humility, by the grief of penitence, by love of righteousness, by good works, by sincere groans, by praiseworthy conduct, by faithful friendship. Blow upon this spark of good love in yourselves, nourish it in yourselves; when it has grown, and made a most worthy and most ample flame, it consumes the straw of all carnal desires.
