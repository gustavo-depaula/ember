# (Erfurt 4 = Sermon 350/F) (Erfurt 4)

**1.** There are those who think that alms are to be provided only to the just, and that nothing of the kind ought to be given to sinners. In this error the Manichees hold the first place of sacrilege, who believe that the members of God are mingled and bound up and held fast in every food, and who judge that these are to be spared, lest they be polluted by sinners and entangled in more wretched knots. This insanity is perhaps repelled less worthily than it offends the sense of all sound people, if it is merely set forth. But some, while feeling nothing of the kind, think that sinners are not to be fed, lest we strive against God, whose indignation is declared against them—as though for this reason too he might be angry with us, because we wish to come to the aid of those whom he himself wishes to punish. They even bring forward testimonies of the holy Scriptures where we read, *Give to the merciful, and do not receive the sinner, and render vengeance to the impious and to sinners*; *do good to the lowly, and do not give to the impious, for the Most High also hates sinners and will render vengeance to the impious*[^1]. Not understanding how these words are to be taken, they clothe themselves in a detestable cruelty. Hence, brethren, we must address your love briefly on this matter, lest by a perverse thought, when you do not understand the divine will in the divine books, you consent to human depravity.

**2.** For the apostle Paul, teaching most plainly that mercy is to be shown to all, says, *So then, while we have time, let us tirelessly do good to all, but especially to those of the household of faith*[^2]. From this it indeed appears clearly enough that in works of this kind the just are to be preferred. For whom else are we to understand as *the household of faith*, when elsewhere it is plainly stated, *The just lives by faith*[^3]? Yet the bowels of mercy are not to be shut against other people, even sinners, not even if they bear a hostile mind against us, since our Savior himself says and admonishes, *Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you*[^4]. Nor is this passed over in silence in the old books; for there it is read, *If your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he thirsts, give him drink*[^5], a testimony that the Apostle also used. Nor are those things which we set down above thereby false, since they too are divine precepts: *Give to the merciful, and do not receive the sinner*[^6]. For those things were said for this reason, that you should not do good to any sinner because he is a sinner, but should do good to one who hates you, not because he is a sinner, but because he is a man. Thus you will keep both precepts, neither lax in punishing nor inhuman in coming to aid. For everyone who rightly rebukes a sinner—what else does he do but not want him to be a sinner? He hates, then, in him what God too hates, so that what man made may be destroyed and what God made may be set free. For man made the sin, but God made the man himself. And when we say these two names, "man" and "sinner," they are surely not said in vain. Because, then, he is a sinner, correct him, and because he is a man, have mercy on him. Nor will you set the man free at all unless you have pursued the sinner.

**3.** To this duty every discipline keeps watch, as it is fitting and suited to each one who governs: not only the bishop who governs his people, but also the poor man who governs his household, the rich man who governs his family, the husband who governs his wife, the father who governs his offspring, the judge who governs his province, the king who governs his nation. All these, when they are good, surely will well for those whom they govern, and, according to the power imparted by the Lord of all, who governs even those who govern, they give their effort that these same ones whom they govern may both be preserved as men and perish as sinners. Thus they fulfill what is written, *Give to the merciful, and do not receive the sinner*[^7], so that they may not wish to be safe in him that which is a sinner; *and render vengeance to the impious and to sinners*[^8], so that this very thing—that they are sinners and impious—may be wiped out in them; *do good to the lowly*[^9], because of the fact that he is lowly, *and do not give to the impious*, because of the fact that he is impious, *for the Most High also hates sinners and will render vengeance to the impious*[^10]; who nevertheless, because they are not only sinners and impious but also men, *makes his sun rise on the good and the evil and sends rain on the just and the unjust*[^11]. Thus mercy is to be shut against no man, and impunity is to be relaxed for no sin.

**4.** For what do we do in rebuking but punish sins? And when anyone turns to God by repenting, what else does he do but rebuke and punish himself? In no way, then, do you stray from the work of mercy when you pursue in another the very thing which you yourself mercifully desire to be pursued in you. See what the prophet declares concerning the future preachers of the kingdom of heaven: *The saints will exult in glory, they will rejoice on their beds; the high praises of God in their throats and two-edged swords in their hands, to execute vengeance on the nations*[^12]. And lest anyone should think that with such swords blood is shed and the slaughter of bodies is done, the prophet, as it were meeting such thoughts by explaining, when he had said *to execute vengeance on the nations*, added what kind of vengeance: *rebukes,* he says, *among the peoples*[^13]. This the *swords in their hands* do—that is, given into the power of using them. For they are two-edged with the pain of present things and the fear of things to come. *Who is there,* says the Apostle, *to make me glad but the one who is grieved by me?*[^14] Behold the pain of present things. And, *When I come,* he says, *I will not spare. Do you want to receive proof of Christ who speaks in me?*[^15] Behold the fear of things to come. Again, in another place he commends in this way the rule of the Lord himself over men: *For if we judged ourselves,* he says, *we would not be judged by the Lord. When we are judged, we are corrected by the Lord, lest we be condemned with the world*[^16]. In the blow of correction is the pain of present things; in the threat of damnation, the fear of things to come. These are the two-edged swords; this is the persecution owed to the sinner, lest mercy be denied to the man.

**5.** But it may seem remarkable to those who attend little, and perhaps incredible, how a sinner is received and fed for this very reason, that he is a sinner. For it is one thing when someone feeds a man because he knows or believes him to be just, looking to that reward of which it is said, *The one who receives a just man in the name of a just man will receive a just man's reward*[^17]; but another when someone feeds any man whatever simply as a man serving a man, obeying that general command where the Lord says, *Whatever good you wish men to do to you, do you also to them*[^18]. But there are those who love the very sins in sinners and, on account of these, spend whatever they bestow on them, so that you may see a kind of work altogether contrary to that work which is shown to the just because they are just. For just as the just, endowed with praiseworthy devotion, hasten to offer the service of kindness to the servants of God, by whom they too are received into the eternal dwellings, so on the contrary the sacrilegious and impious seek out kinds of wickedness in men, by purchasing which, as it were, they squander their temporal goods, and with whom they will afterward come into everlasting torments. Between these two kinds there is a certain middle place, where kindness is shown by man to man not on account of justice nor on account of sin, but on account of the fellowship of nature itself. This middle kind both the devout knowingly use, and even the wicked are sometimes touched by it.

**6.** Since, then, it is one thing to minister the necessities to a dispenser of the kingdom of heaven, as Onesiphorus did to Paul, another to hold out alms to a beggar, as that man received who sat at the Beautiful Gate, another to distribute rewards to disgraceful acts, as actors, charioteers, and hunters are enriched by madmen, the Church frequents that first and that middle kind of giving, but condemns, rebukes, and corrects this kind, which is contrary to that first and best one. Indeed, from this very contrary our slothful ones are to be roused, who scarcely break bread for the hungry Christ, when the patrons of the theater scarcely leave bread for their own children. But on account of that middle kind, by which kindness is owed by man to man, it can happen both that a son of Gehenna, moved by some compassion, feeds a dispenser of God, and that a son of the Church, if he finds even a hunter failing from hunger, feeds him. The former indeed did not love justice, but he could not everywhere despise the common condition of mortality; the latter did not receive a sinner, but did not deny mercy to a man.

**7.** Of that first kind of good work the Lord speaks thus: *The one who receives a just man in the name of a just man will receive a just man's reward, and the one who receives a prophet in the name of a prophet will receive a prophet's reward, and whoever gives a cup of cold water to one of these least ones only in the name of a disciple, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward*[^19]; and that which I mentioned a little before, *Make friends for yourselves from the mammon of iniquity, so that they too may receive you into the eternal dwellings*[^20]; and from there comes also that, *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which has been prepared for you from the foundation of the world, for I was hungry and you gave me to eat* and the rest[^21]. But they say, *When did we see you hungry?* To whom he answers, *When you did it to one of these least ones of mine, you did it to me*[^22]. Of that kind of mercy, then, in which no man's misery is to be despised—although no mercy is owed to a sinner for the fact that he is a sinner—he admonished us thus, when he said to a certain man by whom he had been invited along with others, *When you give a feast, do not call your friends, by whom you too can be invited, but call the lame, the blind, the weak, the beggars, who have nothing to repay you. But it will be repaid to you in the resurrection of the just*[^23]. Hence we can also notice, from the Lord's own custom, that the disciples supposed he had commanded Judas, that betrayer of his, to prepare something to be given to the poor on the feast day, since he himself kept the money box, when he said to him, *What you do, do quickly*[^24]. For from what would they have suspected this, if the Lord had never by his own action taught any such almsgivings? For of that first kind of good works, in which one serves the just because of the merit of their justice, such services were rather ministered to him himself by others. For from what did those money boxes gather their coin except from the offerings of those who served him? And quite openly certain devout women too are named in the Gospel, who, accompanying his journeys with serving devotion, ministered to him out of their substance.

**8.** From this, therefore, it is above all to be understood how the alms is not to be despised which is bestowed on any poor people whatever by the right of human fellowship, since the Lord relieved the need of the poor out of those money boxes which he filled from the wealth of others. But if perhaps someone should say that neither those weak and beggars whom the Lord commanded to be invited, nor those to whom he was accustomed to dispense from the money boxes, were sinners, and that therefore it does not follow that, on account of the gospel testimonies, even sinners should be commanded to be received or fed by the merciful, let him attend to what I already mentioned above: that surely those are sinners, and most of all wicked, who hate and persecute the Church, of whom nevertheless it is said, *Do good to those who hate you*[^25], and this is supported by the example of God the Father, *who makes his sun rise on the good and the evil and sends rain on the just and the unjust*[^26]. Let us not, therefore, receive sinners for the fact that they are sinners, but let us nevertheless treat those same ones, because they too are men, with human regard. Let us pursue in them their own iniquity, let us have mercy on the common condition, and so *tirelessly, while we have time, let us do good to all, but especially to those of the household of faith*[^27].
