# Sermon 385: This Admonition of Saint Augustine Shows That There Are Many Degrees in Perfect Love and Charity, Useful and Very Necessary

**1.** Not only in the New, but also in the Old Testament, we are admonished, most beloved brothers, how we ought to hold perfect love. For thus the Lord himself said in the Gospel: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^1]. Let us treat, then, a little of the love of man for man: for there are perverse loves of men. He himself loves another perversely who also loves himself perversely; but he who loves himself rightly also loves another rightly. For example, there are shameful, detestable loves: the loves of adulterers, the loves of seducers, unclean loves. All human laws and divine laws detest evil loves. Remove, then, these unlawful ones; let us seek the lawful.

**2.** Lawful love begins with marriage; but as yet it is carnal. You see that it is common with the beasts: even those sparrows that chirp have marriages, and make nests, together they hatch the eggs, together they feed the chicks. This love is indeed lawful in men, but you see that it is carnal. The second love is that of children, but as yet it too is carnal: for he who loves his children is not to be praised, but he who does not love them is to be detested. For am I going to praise as something great in a man what I see in a tigress? Serpents love their offspring, lions and wolves love their offspring. Do not, then, think it something great that you love your children: in this love you are still compared to serpents; if you do not love them, you are surpassed by serpents. I am now speaking of honorable loves: for I have excluded those shameful ones. Another love, that of kinsmen, now seems proper to men, if it is not merely of habit. For the love that is extended beyond kinsmen is greater than that held among kinsmen. He who loves his kinsmen still loves his own blood. Let him love others, who are not kinsmen, let him receive the stranger: now this love has been much widened. And it grows so much that it reaches from spouse to children, from children to kinsmen, from kinsmen to strangers, from strangers to enemies. But to reach that point, it has many degrees.

**3.** About friendship, then, see what I say. There are friends—setting aside the friendship which is not even to be called friendship, which an evil conscience makes: for there are men who together commit evils, and therefore seem joined to themselves, because they are bound by an evil conscience—setting aside, then, that wicked friendship, there is a certain friendship still carnal, through the habit of living together, conversing together, associating together; so that a man is saddened when he is deserted by a friend with whom he is accustomed to converse and have dealings. Two men meet, they walk together for three days, and now they do not want to part from each other. And this is a certain sweetness of friendship, honorable indeed: but let us still examine it, because we are seeking the degrees of this love; and let us see how far we have come—as far as such friendship as I have described. This, then, is the friendship of habit, not of reason: the beasts have it too. Two horses feed together, they long for each other: if on another day one goes ahead, the other hurries, longing as it were for its friend; it is scarcely governed by its rider, and so long, by its own impulse, it presses on, until it reaches the other. When it has reached the one that went ahead, it is calmed: a weight was bearing it, it was driven by the weight of love; it comes as it were to its own place, and is at rest. This friendship of habit too is still in the beasts: let us rise still further even from this. There is another, higher friendship, not of habit, but of reason, by which we love a man for the sake of faith and mutual goodwill in this mortal life. Whatever we shall find higher than this is divine. Let man begin to love God, and he will love in man nothing but God.

**4.** For let your love first see how the love of friendship ought to be free. For you ought not to have a friend, or to love him, in order that he may render you something: if you love him for this, that he may render you either money or some temporal advantage, you do not love him, but that which he renders. A friend is to be loved freely, for his own sake, not for the sake of something else. If the rule of friendship urges you to love a man freely, how freely is God to be loved, who commands you to love a man? There is nothing more delightful than God. For in a man there are things that give offense: yet through friendship you compel yourself to bear, for friendship's sake, even those things in the man that give offense. If, then, you ought not, for the sake of certain things to be borne, to dissolve a man's friendship, by what things ought the friendship of God to be compelled to be dissolved by you? You find nothing more delightful than God: God has nothing in himself whereby he may offend you, if you do not offend him; nothing more beautiful than he, nothing sweeter than he. But you will say to me: I do not see him: how shall I love one whom I do not see? Behold how you learn to love one whom you do not see: now I show you whence you may try to see what you cannot see with these eyes. Behold, you love a friend: what do you love in him? You love him freely. But perhaps this friend of yours—to leave the rest aside—is an old man: for it can happen that you have an old friend. What do you love in the old man? A bent body, a white head, wrinkles on the brow, a sunken jaw? If it is the body you see, nothing is more deformed than it through old age: and yet you love something, and you do not love the body you see, because it is deformed. By what do you see what you love? For if I ask you: Why do you love? you will answer me: He is a faithful man. Therefore you love faith. If you love faith, by the eyes by which faith is seen, by those same eyes God is seen. Begin, then, to love God, and you will love man for the sake of God.

**5.** Hear a great testimony. The devil is surely the accuser of the saints; and because he urges his charges before a judge whom he cannot deceive, he cannot speak false accusations against us. He knows before whom he speaks. So, because he cannot speak false things against us, he seeks true things to say. Therefore he tempts, that he may have something to say. This adversary of ours, then, who envies us the kingdom of heaven, who does not want us to be there whence he himself was cast down: *Does Job*, he says, *worship God for nothing?*[^2] By the adversary we are challenged to this, that we worship God for nothing, when he, seeking what to object, thought he had found something great, because he said: *Does Job worship God for nothing?* Not because he had seen Job's heart, but because he saw his riches. We ought to take care not to love God for a reward. For what—are you going to love God for the sake of a reward? What sort of reward is it that God is going to give you? Whatever else he gives you is less than himself. You worship not for nothing, in order to receive something from him. Worship for nothing, and you will receive him himself: for God keeps himself for you, that you may enjoy him. And if you love the things he made, what sort is he who made them? If the world is beautiful, what sort is the artificer of the world? Tear your heart away, then, from the love of the creature, that you may cling to the Creator, and say what is written in the psalm: *But for me it is good to cling to God*[^3].

**6.** But if you desert him who made you, and love the things he made, you who made you being deserted, you are an adulterer. So the *Epistle of James* cries out, calling them adulterers: *Adulterers*. And whence adulterers? Do you ask whence? *Do you not know*, he says, *that the friendship of this world is enmity with God? Whoever, then, wishes to be a friend of this age is made an enemy of God*[^4]. He explained what he meant by saying, *Adulterers*. The soul that deserts the Creator and loves the creature is an adulteress. For nothing is more chaste than his love, nothing more delightful: deserting him, embracing this, you are made unclean. O soul, that you may be worthy of his embrace, let go of these things, and cling to him for nothing. For from this the psalm said: *But for me it is good to cling to God*. In the prior verse it said thus: *You have destroyed all who fornicate away from you*[^5]. And as if to show what fornication is, it added: *But for me it is good to cling to God*. I want nothing else, but him: to cling to him, this is my good, this my freely given good; for it is also called grace, because it stands free. When, then, you have begun to love God for nothing, there is security: because you both love your friend for nothing, and love him for this, that he may love God with you. For attend to that ordinary friendship from which we began, through which we have made our degrees—attend to it. The husband loves the wife, and the wife the husband; without doubt both he wants her, and she wants him, to be safe. He wants to have her unharmed, he wants to have her happy. He loves to this end, because she wants to be unharmed and happy: what he wants for himself, this he wants for her too. He loves his children: who but wants to have his children safe? He loves a friend: who but wants to have him unharmed? So much so that, if perhaps something befalls him, he trembles, he is saddened, he is disturbed, he runs lest it happen: when it has happened, he laments. What, then, does he want? To have him safe. If, then, everyone who loves wants to have safe what he loves, if he understands what true safety is, he begins to love it in himself, and is compelled to love that very true safety in his friend too.

**7.** If you seek God with the eyes of the flesh, see the three children freed from the fire; if you seek God by faith, see the Maccabees crowned in the fire. That safety, then, is to be loved; this is to be used: for this is necessary for use, since it is to pass away. For it is not the true safety, brothers, of which the physicians speak. We are in a way soothed: for there is a perpetual sickness in this fragility of the flesh. For do you think that a man is sick only when he has a fever, and well when he is hungry? He is well, it is said. Do you want to see how great an evil it is to be hungry? Leave him without remedy for seven days, he is killed; but because you apply the remedy daily, he lives. The remedy of hunger is food; the remedy of thirst is drink; the remedy of weariness is sleep; the remedy of sitting is walking; the remedy of walking is sitting; the remedy of fatigue is sleeping; the remedy of sleeping is waking. And see how feeble the human body is: this very aid which I have spoken of—he who takes it, if he persists in it, fails. By hungering you sought the aid of food: behold the aid of food: you eat, you are refreshed; if you refresh yourself more, you fail more. As the aid of thirst you sought drink: by drinking much you are choked, you who were pressed by thirsting. You have wearied yourself by walking, you want to sit: sit perpetually, see whether you will not weary yourself. So whatever a man takes to drive off something else, in that very thing, if he persists in it, he fails.

**8.** What sort, then, is this safety, brothers—passing, fragile, perishable, empty? Truly, as it has been said: *For what is your life? It is a vapor appearing for a little while*[^6]. *He*, then, who in this life *loves his soul, will lose it. But he who* in this age *hates his soul, keeps it unto eternal life*[^7]. What is eternal life? True safety. And if you see your friend, whom you loved in this age that he might be safe—because you now desire that safety which is eternal—you love your friend for that very safety; and the whole of whatever you want to bestow on your friend, you want to bestow for this, that he may hold that safety with you. For you love righteousness, you want him to be righteous: you love to be under God, you want him too to be under God: you love eternal life, you want him there to reign with you forever. You see that enemy of yours persecuting you: it is iniquity that persecutes you. In him you ought to be angry out of mercy: he has a fever in his soul. As, then, the friend of this age, loving according to the age, as it were his own soul, wants to drive the fever out of his friend, whom he likewise loves as himself for the sake of present safety, so you, whomever you love, love for the sake of eternal life: when you find anger, indignation, hatred, iniquity, try to drive out the disease of the soul just as the friend of the age the disease of the body. For love to this end, that you may make him what you too are, and there will be in you perfect love. If you find this, love your spouse to this end, love your child to this end, love your kinsman to this end, love your neighbor to this end, love the stranger to this end, love your enemy to this end, and there will be in you perfect love. And if it exists, you conquer the world, and the prince of the world is cast out. For you heard what the Lord says: *The prince of this world has been cast out*[^8]; because he himself was about to suffer, and through his passion to bring about love in men. *No one has greater love than this, that he lay down his soul for his friends*[^9]. So that he might be loved, he loved first; that no one might fear to die in his name, he died first for all. To this end, then, that he might build up love in the hearts of men, he cast the devil out. Out where? Out of the hearts of men. Cupidity sends him in, love casts him out.

**9.** But we, brothers, thinking with great diligence on the degrees of love written above, let us not repay the Lord evil for good. And because he, coming, bound the strong man, that is, the devil, and took away from his power all of us who had been his vessels, being emptied by his grace of all evils, let us strive to be filled with abundant goods, fearing that which the Lord himself said: *When the unclean spirit has gone out of a man, he walks through dry places, seeking rest, and finds it not; after this, returning and finding empty the house from which he went out, he brought with him seven spirits worse than himself; and the last state of that man became worse than the first*[^10]. Lest we too, then, suffer something of the kind, let us labor as much as we can to bring virtues into the places of vices, that we may be able to come to the mercy of God.
