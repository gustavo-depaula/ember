# Sermon 110/A: (Dolbeau 17)

**1.** We have often admonished your love and, according to the small measure of our strength, with the Lord granting it, we have taught that in the holy Gospel the wonderful deeds of our Lord Jesus Christ are not only mighty works and works—for what else are they, but the more wonderful the more divine?—but are also as it were visible words, admonishing us to understand also what they signify. If we do not understand them, they delight by the miracle alone and lift up the heart in the praise of God. Accordingly, just as even one who does not know letters admires and praises a scribe writing beautifully, merely by looking at what he writes, and, ignorant of the meaning, is delighted by the beauty of the strokes—not knowing the sense, he praises the form; but he who also knows how to read gathers an abundant fruit—so when we hear what wonders the Lord did, even if we do not understand what they signify, like those who do not know how to read and look only at those, as it were, letters, we admire the work of the one doing it, even if we do not know the will of the one signifying. What is more wonderful than this deed? For eighteen years a bent woman was held by infirmity: in one moment by the word of the Lord she was made upright, and a most aged sickness yielded to the one commanding, because it obeyed the one ruling. He loosed the bond of the one who entangles, he did the work of the creator. She who was bent was made upright, she who was bound was loosed, and this by a word, because he who made the word—and *all things were made through him*[^1]—came to seek what had perished. Let us admire, praise, love. Yet after admiring the strokes of this writing, let us seek a little more attentively its meaning.

**2.** For in the same Gospel passage, in the same context where it is recalled concerning eighteen men that the tower of Siloam fell and they died, the woman, infirm for eighteen years, was healed and made upright. Both are there: it is narrated in one connected account. In that very connection too is what the Lord says about that tree, to which he came and found it unfruitful, now in the third year. When he ordered it to be cut down, the vinedresser interceded: that he should apply to it, dug around, some dung, and, if it bore fruit, well; but if not, he would come and cut it down. This about the tree was said figuratively, not done by a miracle. But that about the men on whom the tower fell was neither said figuratively by the Lord, nor was the matter commended by any miracle: men were crushed by the ruin. It can be reckoned as it were by chance, except that the impious understand that not even a sparrow falls without the will of the Creator. But that the Lord might of his own accord recall those eighteen men whom the ruin of the tower crushed, it had been reported to him that certain men had been killed by King Herod in a dreadful manner, so that he mingled their blood with their sacrifices. When men shuddered at this, they reported it. And the Lord said: *Do you think that they, because this befell them, were sinners above the rest of men? Amen, I say to you: unless you do penance, you will all likewise perish*[^2]. Then of his own accord he added, and in recalling subjoined, saying: *Or those eighteen men on whom the tower in Siloam fell and died, do you think that they were debtors above other men*—that is, sinners? *Amen, I say to you, that unless you do penance, you will all likewise perish*[^3]. Then he speaks of that fig tree, he now raises up the woman. I beseech you, aid our weakness by your attention; first what this means, I think, we ought not negligently to inquire.

**3.** *Amen,* he says, *I say to you: unless you do penance, you will all likewise die.* *Likewise* how? Like those, namely, *whose blood Herod mingled with their sacrifices.* Again, *unless you do penance, you will all likewise die.* How *likewise?* Like those *on whom the tower fell.* Since they died unlike one another—surely in one way those with whose sacrifices their blood was mingled, in another those whom the ruin crushed—how will all, unless they do penance, likewise die? Those who are to die in that way are not to die in this way, and those in this way, surely not in that; and yet by their merits they likewise die. The divine Scripture cannot lie, the mouth of truth cannot lie. Clearly, if we understand it, it is true. All the evil, the wicked, all the lost, the criminal, the disgraceful, the lovers of the world, the wanton and abject, unless they do penance, *likewise* die. How *likewise?* Both like those and like those. Not bodily and visibly, therefore *likewise.* For if bodily, they cannot die in both ways. But if we understand and compare spiritual things with spiritual and draw certain signs from bodily likenesses to intelligible things, all *likewise* die. For all the wicked ask evil things of God, and what they think good for themselves are evil, because they think badly. Whence? From evil concupiscence, from the work of flesh and blood, from the work of corruption, namely, which will not possess the kingdom of God. Therefore their sacrifices, that is, their prayers, are mingled with their blood, because they ask according to flesh and blood. This a certain Scripture designates and reproves, saying: *You ask and do not receive, because you ask badly, that you may consume it in your concupiscences*[^4]. You, then, offer your sacrifice: let it be mingled with your blood; for you are wise according to the flesh and, according as you are wise carnally, you ask God. For you are of flesh and blood; you wish to incline God to your flesh, when you ought to lift your heart up to God, nor do you attend to what the apostle says to you: *If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, sitting at the right hand of God; be wise about the things that are above, not the things on the earth*[^5], lest your prayers, coming from flesh and blood, punish you with such a punishment as those were punished with whose blood the angry king mingled with their sacrifices. Separate your sacrifice from your blood, say to your God: *I will render to you my vows, which my lips have distinguished*[^6]. *For the spiritual man judges all things. But he himself is judged by no one*[^7]. By judging all things, he does not mingle the sacrifice with the blood.

**4.** How, then, do those who do not do penance die likewise, just as those died on whom the tower of Siloam fell? &lt;Siloam is interpreted "Sent."&gt; Who is Sent, but our Lord? The tower in Siloam, the cross of Christ. If, then, you have not been mortified, fixed to his cross by the evil concupiscences of flesh and blood, the crucified one falls upon you. Why do you sign yourself? If you do not act, you do not sign yourself. This is a ruin, not grace. Acknowledge the crucified one, acknowledge the one suffering, acknowledge the one praying for his enemies, acknowledge the one loving and wishing to heal those by whom he suffered such things. If you acknowledge, do penance and, if you ever willed badly, make it that you will well. Blot out the fault, lest you fear the penalty. But if you do not do this, will Christ profit you anything? *He who eats unworthily, eats and drinks judgment to himself*[^8]. If by eating unworthily he eats and drinks judgment to himself, what else but a tower falls upon him? *For you will all likewise die,* he says—and he says truly—*unless you do penance* for your evil deeds. Furthermore, if you do it, you are raised up and you will be like that woman. Furthermore, if you do it, you receive the filth of penance, as it were a heap of dung, not unfruitfully, lest you be cut off.

**5.** It remains to ask about the number, why eighteen men, and eighteen years, and how they fit the three years of that tree. That angry ruler of the synagogue admonished us to solve a question of this kind, since the Lord Jesus healed that bent woman on the Sabbath. For he said to the crowds: *For there are six days on which one ought to work; come then and be healed, not on the Sabbath day*[^9]. The beast indeed is convicted by the beast. *Hypocrites,* he says, *does not each of you on the Sabbath day loose his beast of burden and lead it to water?*[^10] Nor was it fitting that he be convicted otherwise, who was like the horse and the mule, having no understanding. And yet he seemed to himself to speak from Scripture, when he said: *There are six days on which one ought to work.* From Scripture indeed, but he mingled the sacrifice with the blood, since what was said spiritually he understood carnally. Let us, however, take from him an occasion of understanding, and let us too recall that in six days God worked all things that we see, heaven and earth and all things that are in them; but on the seventh day God rested. And all those six days have an evening, because *heaven and earth will pass away;* but that seventh day of rest has no evening, because, when we too after good works have rested, we will have no end in that rest. We find, then, that in six days all things were made: by the number six it signified time. Furthermore, if time is signified to you by the number six, look to the three years of the tree, because there a threefold time is signified: one before the law, another under the law, the third which we now spend, surely under grace. Whoever is not corrected even in this time, let him expect the axe, not security. Therefore, because that tree, at least in the third time, was to be corrected and changed from sterility to fruitfulness, that it might hear the one baptizing unto penance: *Bring forth fruits worthy of penance*[^11], and the number six signifies time, beyond doubt those eighteen whom the tower crushed were not corrected in all that threefold time. For if six signifies time, three times six is eighteen.

**6.** But that woman in the time of grace is raised up in eighteen years, as if she were the one who had said: *They bent down my soul*[^12]. For the soul is bent down which is pressed by earthly things, the soul is bent down which is weighed down by the desires of things, the soul is bent down which lies in saying that it has its heart up to the Lord. For if you have your "heart up," you are not bent. If you always seek earthly things, if you desire to be happy from earthly things, if you do not think you worship God fruitfully unless earthly happiness has abounded for you, you are bent: your heart is not with the Lord. But this penalty came from the dominion of the devil. The whole human race was held bent under the devil, the whole human race was weighed down by earthly desires; there came one who would promise the kingdom of heaven. There is another life, there is the fellowship of the angels; there is a fatherland where no violent man, no enemy is to be feared; there is a fatherland walled by the will of God, surrounded by the shield of the good will of God, by which the enemy is not permitted in, whence a friend does not perish; there is a fatherland where no one departs, no one succeeds. That fatherland is called Jerusalem, Jerusalem is interpreted "vision of peace." Be raised up from your bentness, do not be wise about the earth. You have risen with Christ, he is in heaven: stretch toward him and you will not be bent.

**7.** But you did not do this, O malignant human race, you did not do this before the law, you did not do this under the law: do it under grace. In eighteen years he found you bent, who came upright, who came to be crucified for you, that he might loose you from the bond of the devil. The creditor's bond held you bent. For the devil had bound his debtors and had as it were put them in fetters; he came, no debtor, and paid what he did not owe and blotted out our bond. The bond of our debt, whence was it blotted out? By the blood of the just one. For why would the one set over death hold men unto death? He clearly held them, by the condition of sin, as his own tree: he conquered and held, he conquered and possessed. He held by right those whom by evil persuasion he had deceived. He came, persuading good things, having nothing evil, born of a virgin, not bearing the propagation of sin, but clothed with the penalty of death, having the likeness of sinful flesh, that he might free us from the flesh of sin. Hear him about to go to the passion and saying: *Behold, the prince of the world comes, and in me he will find nothing*[^13]. Not as the world in you, not as in all the human race, *in me he will find nothing.* Therefore if he will find nothing in you, why will you die? What follows? After he said: *In me he will find nothing,* as if it were said to him: "Why then will you die?", *but that all may know,* he says, *that I do the will of my Father: arise, let us go hence*[^14]. He arises and goes to the passion, that he may fulfill the will of the Father, as it were sent as Siloam, not because he owed it, but that he might pay for the debtors, as if it were said of him in prophecy: *What I did not seize, then I paid back*[^15]. He alone, then, undeserving of death. But we are all deserving, because we have all drawn the merit of death together. He drew to himself no merit of death, and yet he took on death for us. Therefore he paid what he did not owe, freed the debtors, blotted out the ancient records, established new records. What need have we now to keep account with the old records? He burned them with the fire of the Holy Spirit.

**8.** Acknowledge the redeemer, do not make yourself further a debtor. But on account of certain daily sins, even if lesser, he gave a daily remedy. From faith say, from the heart say, from good will say: *Forgive us our sins.* For add, not only by saying, but also by doing, what follows: *As we too forgive our debtors*[^16]. Therefore God, whose debtor you are, wished you to have a debtor. Your debtor is he who does iniquity to you, who oppresses you unjustly, who takes something from you unjustly. He is your debtor, he owes you a penalty. Do you wish to know that he is your debtor? Look, read. *An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth*[^17], etc. The law established that each one suffer what he has done. On the contrary, the Gospel, when it says: *But I say to you: love your enemies*[^18], does not speak against the law, as some who understand badly suppose, that the gentleness of the Gospel is contrary to the harshness of the law. It is not so. The law shows what is owed you. The Gospel would not make you a forgiver, unless the law showed you a debtor.
