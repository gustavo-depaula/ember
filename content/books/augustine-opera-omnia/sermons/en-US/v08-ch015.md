# Sermon 285: On the Heavenly Birthday of the Martyrs Castus and Aemilius

**1.** The courage of the holy martyrs, not merely great but devout—for useful courage, indeed the only courage that should truly be called courage, serves God rather than pride—prompts us to speak to Your Charity and urge you to celebrate the martyrs’ feasts in such a way that you delight in imitating them by following in their footsteps. For they themselves did not derive their bravery from their own resources. That fountain did not flow only as far as them. He who gave to them is able to give to us too, since one price was paid for us all.

**2.** Above all, then, you must be reminded of what you ought constantly to be reminded of and always to consider: it is the cause, not the punishment, that makes God’s martyr. For God delights in our righteousness, not our torments; and in the judgment of the almighty and truthful one, the question is not what someone suffers but why he suffers. It is the Lord’s cause, not his punishment, that has made us sign ourselves with the Lord’s cross. For if his punishment had done this, the robbers’ similar punishment would have had the same power. The three who were crucified were in one place, with the Lord in the middle, he who *was numbered among the unjust*. They placed two robbers, one on either side, but their cause was not the same. They hung beside him, yet were far separated from him. Their own crimes crucified them; ours crucified him. Nevertheless, even in one of them it became quite clear how much power lay not in the torment of the man hanging there, but in the devotion of the man confessing. The robber gained in pain what Peter had lost in fear. He committed a crime and mounted a cross; he changed his cause and gained paradise. He certainly deserved to change his cause, since he did not despise Christ because their punishment was alike. The Jews despised him as he performed miracles; this man believed in him as he hung there. He recognized the Lord in his companion on the cross, and by believing he seized the kingdom of heaven. The robber believed in Christ at the very time when the apostles’ faith faltered. Rightly he deserved to hear, *Today you will be with me in paradise.* He had certainly not promised himself this. He was commending himself to great mercy, but also considering what he deserved. *Lord,* he said, *remember me when you come into your kingdom.* He expected to remain in punishment until the Lord came into his kingdom, and begged that mercy might at least be shown him at the Lord’s coming. Thus the robber, considering his own deserts, postponed his hope; but the Lord offered the robber what he had not dared to hope for, as though saying, “You ask me to remember you when I come into my kingdom. *Amen, amen, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise*. Recognize the one to whom you commend yourself. You believe I will come; before I come, I am everywhere. Therefore, although I am about to descend to the underworld, I have you today in paradise, entrusted not to someone else but with me. For my humility descends to mortal human beings and to the dead themselves, but my divinity never leaves paradise.” Thus there were three crosses and three causes. One robber insulted Christ; the other confessed his own evils and commended himself to Christ’s mercy. Christ’s cross in the middle was a judgment seat rather than a punishment: from the cross he condemned the one who insulted and freed the one who believed. Fear, you who insult; rejoice, you who believe. He will do in glory what he did in humility.

**3.** Divine gifts come from the depth of God’s judgment: we can marvel at them, but cannot investigate them. For *who* *has known the mind of the Lord*? And, *How inscrutable are his judgments, and how unsearchable his ways*! Peter, who followed Christ at every step, is thrown into confusion and denies him; he is looked upon and weeps. His tears wash away what fear had stained. This was Peter’s instruction, not his abandonment. For when asked whether he loved the Lord, he had presumed in his heart that he would even die for him. He had attributed this to his own strength. Unless he were left for a little while by his guide, he would not be shown to himself. He dared to say, *I will lay down my life for you.* The presumptuous man boasted that he would lay down his life for Christ, when the Deliverer had not yet laid down his life for him. Then, when he is thrown into confusion by fear, he denies three times, as the Lord had foretold, the one for whom he had promised to die. As it is written, *The Lord looked upon him.* And he *wept bitterly*. The memory of his denial was bitter, so that the grace of redemption might be sweet. Unless he had been left to himself, he would not have denied; unless he had been looked upon, he would not have wept. God hates those who presume on their own strength, and in those he loves he cuts away that swelling like a physician. By cutting he indeed causes pain, but afterward he establishes health. So the risen Lord entrusts his sheep to Peter, the man who denied him. But he denied because he presumed; afterward he became a shepherd because he loved. For why does he question his love three times, except to pierce the heart of the man who denied him three times? Thus Peter afterward accomplished by God’s grace what he could not at first accomplish through confidence in himself. For after entrusting the sheep to him—not Peter’s sheep, but his own, so that Peter would tend them for the Lord rather than himself—he foretold the future suffering which Peter had earlier missed because he hurried ahead too rashly. *When you have grown old,* he said, *another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish to go. He said this to signify by what death he was to glorify the Lord*. It happened: Peter, who had washed away his denial with tears, reached his martyrdom. What the Savior had promised him, the tempter could not take away.

**4.** I think something like this happened also in these holy martyrs, Castus and Aemilius, whose feast we celebrate today. Perhaps they too had previously relied on their own strength, and that is why they failed. He showed them who they were and who he was. He checked them when they presumed and called them when they believed; he helped them when they fought and crowned them when they conquered. Indeed, the enemy was already rejoicing over them in the first encounter, when they gave way before pain. He counted them on his side; he was already exulting, already claiming them as his own. But how much was granted them through the Lord’s mercy! Other martyrs defeated the devil as he tempted; these defeated him even as he triumphed. So, my brothers and sisters, let us remember whose feast we celebrate today. Let us not wish to imitate their defeat, but rather their victory. The falls of great people were not concealed precisely so that those who have presumed on themselves might fear. Everywhere the humility of the good Teacher is most carefully commended to us. Indeed, our salvation in Christ is Christ’s humility. For we would have no salvation unless Christ had deigned to become humble for our sake. Let us remember not to put our confidence in ourselves. Let us entrust to God what we have; let us ask him for what we lack.

**5.** The martyrs’ righteousness is perfect because they were perfected in their very suffering. That is why prayer is not offered for them in the Church. Prayer is offered for other departed believers, but not for the martyrs: they left this life so perfected that they are not our clients but our advocates. Nor is this in themselves, but in him to whom they clung as perfected members to their head. For he is truly the one advocate, who intercedes for us, seated at the Father’s right hand; yet he is the one advocate just as he is the one shepherd. For he says, *I must* *also bring those sheep that are not of this fold*. Does Christ’s being shepherd mean Peter is not a shepherd? On the contrary, Peter too is a shepherd, and others like him are unquestionably shepherds. For if he is not a shepherd, how can he be told, *Feed my sheep*? Yet the true shepherd is the one who feeds his own sheep. For Peter was told not, “Feed your sheep,” but *mine.* Peter, therefore, is a shepherd not in himself but in the shepherd’s body. For if he fed his own sheep, those he fed would immediately become goats.

**6.** In contrast to what is said to Peter, *Feed my sheep,* it is said in the Song of Songs, *Unless you know yourself, O beautiful one among women.* We certainly recognize who is being addressed, and in her we too hear these words. For the Church hears this from Christ; the bride hears it from the bridegroom: *Unless you know yourself, O beautiful one among women, go out yourself*. What a dreadful word: *Go out! From us,* it says, *they went out, but they were not of us*. Opposite to that sorrowful word, *Go out,* stands this good and joyful word: *Enter into the joy of your Lord*. Therefore, *Unless you know yourself, O beautiful one among women*—O Catholic Church, beautiful among the heresies—*unless you know yourself, O beautiful one among women, go out yourself.* For I am not casting you out; rather, *go out yourself.* For those went out from us *who separate themselves, earthly-minded people who do not have the Spirit*. It was not said, “They were cast out,” but, *They went out.* Divine justice observed this even with the first sinners: as people already falling under their own weight, he let them go from paradise rather than forcing them out. Therefore, *unless* *you know yourself, O beautiful one among women, go out yourself.* I am not casting you out; *go out yourself.* I want you to be healed within my body; you want your rottenness to be cut off. This was said to those who were foreseen as going to leave, so that those who would remain might recognize themselves and be on their guard. For why did they too go out, except because they did not know themselves? If they knew themselves, they would see there that what they gave was God’s, not their own. “I give it; what I give is mine, and it is holy because I give it.” You have not known yourself; rightly you have gone out. For you refused to hear him say, *Unless you know yourself, O beautiful one among women.* You were beautiful once, when you clung to your bridegroom’s members. Thus you refused to hear and weigh the meaning of *Unless you know yourself:* he found you ugly, made you beautiful from being ugly, and whitened you from being black. *For what do you have that you did not receive*? So you do not consider what is meant by *Unless you know yourself, go out yourself.* And you thought you ought to feed your own sheep, contrary to what was said to Peter, *Feed my sheep.* But see what he added for you, what this foretold to you: *Go out yourself in the tracks of the flocks.* Not “the flock,” but *the flocks.* For Christ’s sheep are fed where there is one flock and one shepherd. Therefore, *go out* *yourself in the tracks of the flocks,* divisible, divided, torn apart. *Go out yourself in the tracks of the flocks, and feed your goats.* Not, as Peter does, *my sheep,* but *your goats, in the tents of the shepherds,* not in the shepherd’s tent. Peter enters through love; you go out through hostility. Because Peter knew himself, he wept over his reliance on himself and deserved to find a helper. Therefore, *go out yourself.* He feeds *my sheep;* you feed *your goats.* He is in the shepherd’s tent; you are *in the tents of the shepherds.* Why, then, do you boast of the evil punishment you suffer, when you do not have a good cause?

**7.** Let us therefore honor the martyrs within, in the shepherd’s tent, in the shepherd’s members, possessing grace, not audacity; devotion, not recklessness; steadfastness, not obstinacy; unity, not division. Therefore, if you want to imitate true martyrs, choose your cause, so that you may say to the Lord, *Judge me, Lord, and distinguish my cause from an unholy nation*. Distinguish not my punishment, for an unholy nation also suffers that, but my cause, which only a holy nation possesses. So choose your cause, hold fast to a good and righteous cause, and with the Lord’s help fear no punishment. Turning to the Lord, etc.
