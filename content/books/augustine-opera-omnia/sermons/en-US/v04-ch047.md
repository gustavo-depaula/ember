# Sermon 130

**1.** A great miracle was done, dearly beloved, that from five loaves and two fishes five thousand men were filled, and the remaining fragments filled twelve baskets [^1]. A great miracle: but we shall not much marvel that it was done, if we attend to the doer. He multiplied in the hands of those breaking them five loaves, who multiplies the seeds sprouting in the earth, so that few grains are cast in and the granaries are filled. But because he does that every year, no one marvels. It is not the cheapness of the deed that takes away wonder, but its constancy. But the Lord, when he did these things, spoke not only through words, but also through the very miracles, to those who understand. The five loaves signified the five books of the Law of Moses. The old law is barley to the gospel wheat. Great mysteries about Christ are contained in those books. Whence he himself says: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too; for he wrote about me* [^2]. But just as in barley the kernel lies hidden under the chaff; so in the veil of the mysteries of the law Christ lies hidden. As those mysteries are expounded and unfolded as bread; so those loaves too grew, when they were broken. And this which I have expounded to you, I have broken bread for you. The five thousand men signify the people set under the five books of the law. The twelve baskets are the twelve Apostles, who too were filled from the fragments of the law. The two fishes are either the two precepts of love of God and neighbor, or the two peoples from circumcision and uncircumcision, or those two sacred persons of king and priest. These things, when they are expounded, are broken; when they are understood, are eaten.

**2.** Let us turn to him who did these things. He himself is the bread, which came down from heaven [^3]; but the bread that refreshes, and does not fail; the bread that can be taken, cannot be consumed. That bread the manna too signified. Whence it was said: *He gave them the bread of heaven, man ate the bread of angels* [^4]. What is the bread of heaven, except Christ? But that man might eat the bread of angels, the Lord of angels was made man. For if he had not become this, we would not have his flesh; if we did not have his flesh, we would not eat the bread of the altar. Let us hasten to the inheritance, because we have received a great pledge of it. My brothers, let us desire the life of Christ, because we hold as a pledge the death of Christ. How will he not give us his goods, who suffered our evils? In these lands, in this malignant world, what abounds, except to be born, to labor and to die? Examine human affairs, convict me, if I lie; attend to all men, whether they are in this world for anything else, than to be born, to labor and to die. These are the wares of our region, these abound here. To such wares that Merchant came down. And since every merchant gives and receives; he gives what he has, and receives what he does not have; when he buys something, he gives money, and receives what he buys; Christ too in this trade gave and received. But what did he receive? What abounds here, to be born, to labor and to die. And what did he give? To be reborn, to rise again, and to reign forever. O good Merchant, buy us. What shall I say, buy us, when we ought to give thanks, because you have bought us? You dole out to us our price, we drink your blood; so you dole out to us our price. And we read the Gospel, our deed of purchase. We are your servants, we are your creation; you made us, you redeemed us. Anyone can buy his servant, he cannot create him; but the Lord both created and redeemed his servants; created them, that they might be; redeemed them, lest they be captive forever. For we fell into the hands of the prince of this world, who seduced Adam and made him a servant, and began to possess us as homeborn slaves. But the Redeemer came, and the deceiver was conquered. And what did our Redeemer do to our captor? For our price he stretched out a trap, his cross; he set there, as it were as bait, his blood. But he could pour out that blood, he did not deserve to drink it. And in that he poured out the blood of one who was no debtor, he was bidden to give back the debtors; he poured out the blood of an innocent one, he was bidden to depart from the guilty. For he poured out his blood for this, that he might blot out our sins. Whence, then, the deceiver held us, was blotted out by the blood of the Redeemer. For he held us only by the chains of our sins. These were the chains of the captives. He came, bound the strong one with the chains of his passion; he entered his house, that is, into the hearts where he dwelt, and snatched his vessels [^5]. We are the vessels. These he had filled with his bitterness. This bitterness he gave even to our Redeemer to drink in gall. He had filled us, then, as his vessels; but our Lord, snatching his vessels and making them his own, poured out the bitterness, filled them with sweetness.

**3.** Let us love him, then, because he is sweet. *Taste, and see that the Lord is gentle* [^6]. He is to be feared, but more to be loved. He is man and God: one Christ man and God; as one man, soul and body; but God and man are not two persons. In Christ there are indeed two substances: God and man; but one person, that the Trinity may remain, lest, with the man being added, a quaternity come to be. How, then, can it come about that God does not have mercy on us, for whose sake God became man? Much is what he did: more wonderful is what he did, than what he promised; and from what he did, we ought to believe what he promised. For this which he did, we would scarcely believe, unless we also saw it. Where do we see it? In the believing peoples, in the multitude brought to him. Because what was promised to Abraham was fulfilled; and from the things we see, we believe the things we do not see. Abraham was one man, and it was said to him: *In your seed all the nations will be blessed* [^7]. If he attended to himself, when would he believe? He was one man, and was already old, and had a barren wife, and one already so advanced in age that she could not conceive, even if she were not barren. There was nothing at all from which anything could be hoped. But he attended to the one promising, and believed what he did not see. Behold, what he believed, we see. Therefore from the things we see, we ought to believe the things we do not see. He begot Isaac, we did not see it; and Isaac begot Jacob, and this we did not see; and Jacob begot twelve sons, and these we did not see; and his twelve sons begot the people of Israel; a great people we see. Now I have begun to say the things we see. From the people of Israel the Virgin Mary was born, and bore Christ; and behold, in Christ all the nations are blessed. What is truer? What more certain? What more open? Desire with me the age to come, you who have been gathered from the nations. In this world God fulfilled his promise about the seed of Abraham. How, then, will he not give us his eternal promises, us whom he made to be the seed of Abraham? For this the Apostle says: *But if you are Christ's*, they are the Apostle's words, *then you are the seed of Abraham* [^8].

**4.** We have begun to be something great; let no one despise himself: we were nothing, but we are something. We said to the Lord: *Remember that we are dust* [^9]; but he made man from dust, and gave life to the dust, and in Christ our Lord has already led that very dust to the kingdoms of heaven. Because from here he took flesh, from here he took earth, and lifted earth to heaven, who made earth and heaven. If, then, two new things not yet done were set before us, and it were asked of us: What is more wonderful, that he who is God should become man, or that he who is man should become a man of God? What is more wonderful, what more difficult? What did Christ promise us? What we do not yet see: that is, that we be men of his, and reign with him, and not die forever. As though this is believed difficult, that a man born should come to that life, where he never dies. This is what we believe with a heart shaken out, shaken out, I say, from the dust of the world, lest the dust itself close the eyes of our faith. This is what we are bidden to believe, that when we are dead, even with dead bodies we shall be in life, where we shall never die. This is wonderful: but more wonderful is what Christ did. For what is more incredible, that a man should live always, or that God should at some time die? Is it more credible that men receive life from God? That God receive death from men, I think is more incredible. And it has already been done: let us believe too what is to be. If what is more incredible has been done, will he not give us what is more credible? For God is able to make men angels, who made earthly and horrible seeds into men. What shall we be? Angels. What were we! It is shameful to recall; I am forced to consider, and I blush to say. What were we? From what did God make men? What were we before we were at all? We were nothing. When we were in our mothers' wombs, what were we? It suffices that you recall it. Lift up your minds from that from which you were made, and think what you are. You live: but the grasses and trees too live. You feel: the beasts too feel. You are men: you have surpassed the beasts, you are higher than the beasts; because you understand how much he has bestowed on us. You live, you feel, you understand, you are men. But to this benefit what can be compared? You are Christians. For if we had not received this, what would it profit us that we were men? So we are Christians, we belong to Christ. Let the world rage, it does not break us; because we belong to Christ. Let the world flatter, it does not seduce us; we belong to Christ.

**5.** We have found a great patron, brothers. You know that men make themselves great from their patrons. To one threatening someone, the client of a greater man answers: My lord's head being safe, you do nothing to me. How much more strongly and certainly do we say: Our head being safe, you do nothing to us? Since our patron is our head. Whoever make themselves great from some man as patron, are his clients; we are the members of our patron. Let him bestow us on himself, and let no one tear us away from him. Since whatever labors we have endured in this world, the whole that passes is nothing. Goods will come that will not pass; through labors one comes to them. But when one has come, no one tears us away from there. The gates of Jerusalem are shut, they receive even bars, so that it is said to that city: *Praise the Lord, Jerusalem; praise your God, Zion. For he strengthened the bars of your gates, he blessed your sons in you. Who set your borders peace* [^10]. The gates shut, the bars set, no friend goes out, no enemy comes in. There we have true and certain security, if here we do not let go the truth.
