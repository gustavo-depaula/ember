# Sermon 99

**1.** Since we are admonished by the Lord's words from the divine readings, believing from this that God wishes to speak to us, we set forth to Your Charity, with his help, a sermon on the remission of sins. For when the Gospel was being read, you listened most attentively, and the event was narrated and turned over before the eyes of your heart. For you saw, not with the flesh but with the mind, the Lord Jesus Christ reclining in the house of the Pharisee [^1], and, having been invited by him, not disdaining it. You saw also the woman, notorious in the city—notorious indeed by an evil reputation—who was a sinner, bursting in uninvited upon the banquet where her physician was reclining, and seeking health with devout shamelessness. Bursting in as though importunate upon the banquet, but opportune for the benefit (for she knew how great a disease she labored under, and she knew that the one to whom she had come was fit to heal her): she approached, then, not to the head of the Lord, but to his feet, and she who had long walked badly sought the right footsteps. First she poured out tears, the blood of the heart, and washed the Lord's feet with the service of confession. With her hair she wiped them, she kissed them, she anointed them: silent, she was speaking; she did not utter speech, but she displayed devotion.

**2.** Because, then, she touched the Lord, by bathing, kissing, wiping, anointing his feet: the Pharisee who had invited the Lord Jesus Christ, because he was of that kind of proud men of whom the prophet Isaiah says: *Who say, Depart far from me, do not touch me, for I am clean* [^2]; supposed that the Lord did not know the woman. This he was thinking within himself, and saying in his heart: *If this man were a prophet, he would know what woman has come to his feet* [^3]. He believed him not to know, because he did not drive her away, because he did not forbid her to approach, because he allowed himself to be touched by a sinful woman. For how did he know that the Lord did not know? What if he did know, O Pharisee, inviter and mocker of the Lord? You feed the Lord, and you do not understand by whom you are to be fed. How do you know that the Lord did not know what that woman had been, except because she was allowed to approach, except because, while he allowed it, she kissed his feet, except because she wiped them, except because she anointed them? For ought not this to have been permitted—an unclean woman to do these things at clean feet? If such a woman, then, had approached the feet of that Pharisee, he would have said what Isaiah says of such: *Depart from me, do not touch me, for I am clean* [^4]. But she approached the Lord unclean, that she might return clean: she approached sick, that she might return whole: she approached confessing, that she might return professing.

**3.** For the Lord heard the Pharisee thinking. Now from this let the Pharisee understand—if he could not see her sinning—that the one who was able to hear him thinking. He set before the man, then, a likeness of two who owed money to one creditor. For he wanted to heal him too, that he might not eat bread with him for nothing: he was hungry for the one feeding him; he wanted to amend him, to slay him, to eat him, to pass him into his own body: just as he said also to that Samaritan woman: *I thirst* [^5]. What is: *I thirst?* I desire your faith. The Lord's words, then, are spoken in this likeness; and both things are accomplished, so that both that inviter may be healed, together with his fellow guests, who alike saw the Lord Jesus Christ, alike did not know him; and so that that woman may have confidence in her confession, and be pricked no further by the stings of her conscience. *One owed*, he said, *fifty denarii, the other five hundred; he forgave them both: which loved him more* [^6]? He to whom the likeness was set forth answered what reason of course compelled him to answer: *I suppose*, Lord, *the one to whom he forgave more* [^7]. And looking toward the woman, he said to Simon: *Do you see this woman? I entered your house, you gave no water for my feet: she has washed my feet with her tears, and wiped them with her hair. You gave me no kiss: but she, from the time she entered, has not ceased to kiss my feet. You did not anoint my head with oil: but she has anointed my feet with ointment. Therefore I say: Her sins, which are many, are forgiven, because she has loved much. But to whom little is forgiven, the same loves little* [^8].

**4.** A question arises that must surely be resolved, one that desires the attention of Your Charity, lest perhaps we be unable, given the brevity of the time, to remove and clear away all its obscurity with words; especially since this flesh, wearied by the heat, now longs to be refreshed, and, demanding its due, hindering the soul's eagerness, shows what was said: *The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak* [^9]. We must fear, and greatly fear, lest from these words of the Lord, not well understood, there should creep upon those who favor their carnal desires and are sluggish to be led out from them into liberty, that opinion which arose in the tongues of slanderers even when the Apostles were preaching, whence the apostle Paul says: *And as some say that we say, Let us do evil that good may come* [^10]. For someone says: If to one to whom little is forgiven, he loves little; but to one to whom more is forgiven, he loves more: it is expedient to love more than to love less; therefore we ought to sin much, and to owe much that we may desire to have forgiven us, so that we may love more the forgiver of great debts. For that sinful woman, the more she owed, the more she loved the forgiver of her debts, the Lord himself saying: *Her sins, which are many, are forgiven, because she has loved much*. And why did she love much, except because she owed much? Then he added and joined: *But to whom little is forgiven, the same loves little*. Is it not expedient, he says, that much rather than little be forgiven me, so that I may love the more my Lord? You certainly see the depth of the question: I know, you see it. You see the narrowness of the time, and this too you see and feel.

**5.** Receive, then, a few words. If I do not do justice to the magnitude of the question, store up the present sermon meanwhile, and hold me debtor for the future. Suppose now two men, that under clearer examples you may consider what I have proposed. One of them is full of sins, he has long lived most wickedly; the other of them has sinned little: both come to grace, both are baptized; they enter as debtors, they go out free: more was forgiven to the one, less to the other. I ask how much each loves. If I find that the one to whom more sins were forgiven loves more, then it was more useful that he sinned much, the great iniquity was more useful, so that love might not be lukewarm. I ask the other how much he loves, I find less: for if I find that he too loves as much as the one to whom much was forgiven, how shall I answer the words of the Lord? how will it be true, what the Truth said: *To whom little is forgiven, loves little* [^11]? Behold, someone says, little was forgiven me, I did not sin much; and I love as much as this man, to whom much was forgiven. Do you speak the truth, or Christ? Was your lie forgiven you for this, that you should lay a charge against the forgiver of the lie? If little was forgiven you, you love little. For if little was forgiven you, and you love much, you contradict him who said: *To whom little is forgiven, loves little*. So I believe him the more, who knows you better. You think little was forgiven you—you love just a little. What then, he says, ought I to have done? To commit many evils, that there might be many for him to forgive me, so that I might be able to love more? It cramps us; but the Lord, who set forth these true things, free me from the straits.

**6.** This was said on account of that Pharisee, who thought he had either no sins or few. For he would not have invited the Lord unless he loved him somewhat. But how little it was! He gave no kiss, not even water for the feet, much less tears: he did not attend him with that service with which that woman did, who knew of what she was being healed, and by whom she was being healed. O Pharisee, you love little for this reason, that you suspect little is forgiven you: not because little is forgiven, but because you think what is forgiven to be little. What then, says he? I, who have not committed murder, am I to be reckoned a murderer? I, who have not committed adultery, am I to be punished as for adultery? Or are those things to be forgiven me which I have not committed? Behold, again let us set up two men, and let us speak to them. One comes, a suppliant sinner, covered with thorns like a hedgehog, and exceedingly timid like a hare. But the rock is a refuge for hedgehogs and hares [^12]. He comes, then, to the rock, finds refuge, receives help. The other has not committed many sins: what shall we do with him, that he may love much? what shall we persuade him? Shall we go against the Lord's words: *To whom little is forgiven, loves little* [^13]? Yes, clearly, to whom little is forgiven. But O you who say you have not committed many sins, why? under whose governance? Thanks be to God, that by your movement and your voice you have shown that you have understood. Now, as I see, the question is resolved. This man committed many sins, and was made a debtor of many; that one, by God's governance, committed few. To the one God credits what he forgave; to this one he likewise credits what he did not commit. You were not an adulterer in that past life of yours, full of ignorance, not yet enlightened, not yet discerning good and evil, not yet believing in him who was governing you while you knew it not. This your God says to you: I was governing you for myself, I was keeping you for myself. That you might not commit adultery, the persuader was lacking: that the persuader might be lacking, I brought it about. Place and time were lacking; and that these might be lacking, I brought it about. The persuader was present, the place was not lacking, the time was not lacking: that you might not consent, I frightened you. Acknowledge, then, the grace of him to whom you owe even what you did not commit. To me that man owes what was done, and which you saw forgiven: to me you too owe what you did not do. For there is no sin which one man has done, that another man cannot do, if the ruler by whom man was made is absent.

**7.** Now then, since we have resolved this deep question, as best we could, in so very small a brevity of time—or if we have not yet resolved it, let us be held as debtors, as I said—let us rather look briefly at the matter of the remission of sins. Christ was thought to be a man, both by the one who invited him and by those who reclined with him. I know not what more that sinful woman had seen in the Lord. For why did she do all those things, except that her sins might be forgiven her? She knew, then, that he could forgive sins: but they knew that a man cannot forgive. And we must believe that all of them—that is, both those reclining and that woman approaching the Lord's feet—all of them knew that a man cannot forgive sins. Since, then, they all knew this, she who believed that he could forgive sins understood him to be more than a man. Indeed, when he had said to the woman, *Your sins are forgiven you*, at once they said: *Who is this who even forgives sins* [^14]? Who is this, whom now the sinful woman has recognized? You, reclining as though well, do not know the physician: because perhaps you have lost even your mind to a greater fever. For a man in a frenzy laughs, and is wept over by the healthy. Yet you know this well, you hold it well: hold it, that a man cannot forgive sins. She who believed they were forgiven her by Christ, believed Christ to be not only a man but also God. *Who is this*, they say, *who even forgives sins?* And the Lord, to those who said, *Who is this*, did not say: The Son of God, the Word of God: he did not say this, but, allowing them to remain a while in what they thought, he resolved the question of their being moved. For he who saw those reclining heard those thinking; turning to the woman: *Your faith*, he says, *has saved you* [^15]. Those who say, *Who is this who even forgives sins?* who think me a man—let them think me a man. *Your faith has saved you*.

**8.** The good Physician healed not only the sick who were present, but also foresaw those to come. There would be men who would say: I forgive sins, I justify, I sanctify, I heal whomever I baptize. Of that same number are these too, who say: *Do not touch me* [^16]. So much are they of that number, that recently, at our conference—which you can also read in the very Proceedings—when a seat was offered them by the official, that they might sit with us, they thought they should answer: It is written for us not to sit with such men, namely lest through the contact of the benches our contagion, as it were, should reach them. See whether it is not: *Do not touch me, for I am clean* [^17]. But on another day, where it was more opportune, we reminded them of this most wretched vanity, when the matter of the Church was being discussed, that the wicked in her do not contaminate the good; we answered them, since they were unwilling to sit with us and said they had been admonished by the Scripture of God, namely because it is written: *I have not sat in the council of vanity* [^18]: we said, If you were unwilling to sit with us because it is written, *I have not sat in the council of vanity*, why did you enter together with us, when it is written next: *And with those who do iniquity I will not go in* [^19]? Therefore in this, that they say, *Do not touch me, for I am clean*, they are like that Pharisee who had invited the Lord, and for that reason thought that he did not know the woman, because he had not forbidden her the touch of his feet. But in another respect the Pharisee was better, because, while he thought Christ a man, he did not believe that sins could be forgiven by a man. So the understanding of the Jews appeared better than that of the heretics. What did the Jews say? *Who is this who even forgives sins* [^20]? Does a man dare to claim this for himself? What says the heretic on the contrary? I forgive, I cleanse, I sanctify. Let Christ answer him—not I, but Christ: O man, when I was thought a man by the Jews, I gave the forgiveness of sins to faith. Not I, Christ answers you. O heretic, you, being a man, say: Come, woman; I make you whole! When I was thought a man, I said: *Go, woman; your faith has made you whole* [^21].

**9.** They answer, *understanding neither what they say nor the things about which they make assertions* [^22]: they answer and say: If men do not forgive sins, then what Christ says is false: *What you loose on earth will be loosed also in heaven* [^23]. You do not know why this was said, how it was said. The Lord was about to give the Holy Spirit to men, and he wanted it to be understood that sins are forgiven by the Holy Spirit himself to his faithful, not by the merits of men. For what are you, O man, but a sick man to be healed? Do you want to be my physician? Seek a physician along with me. For that the Lord might show this more plainly—that sins are forgiven by the Holy Spirit whom he gave to his faithful, not by the merits of men—in a certain place, rising from the dead, he said: *Receive the Holy Spirit*; and when he had said, *Receive the Holy Spirit*, he at once added: *If you forgive anyone's sins, they are forgiven him* [^24]: that is, the Spirit forgives, not you. But the Spirit is God. So God forgives, not you. But what are you in relation to the Spirit? *Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you* [^25]? And again: *Do you not know that your bodies are a temple in you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God* [^26]? So God dwells in his holy temple, that is, in his holy faithful, in his Church: through them he forgives sins, because they are living temples.

**10.** But he who forgives through a man can also forgive apart from a man. For he is no less fit to give by himself, who is able to give through another. He gave to some through John; to John himself, through whom did he give? Rightly, then, God wishing to show this, and to attest to this truth, when certain men in Samaria had been evangelized, and had been baptized—baptized by Philip the evangelist, one of the seven deacons first chosen—they did not receive the Holy Spirit, though they had been baptized. Word was sent to the disciples who were in Jerusalem, and they came to Samaria, so that those who had been baptized might receive the Holy Spirit through the laying on of their hands [^27]. And so it was done: they came and laid hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit. For then the Holy Spirit was given in such a way that he even appeared to have been given. For those who received him spoke in the tongues of all nations: to signify that the Church among the nations would speak in the tongues of all. They received the Holy Spirit, then, and he plainly appeared in them. When Simon had seen this, thinking it to be something of men, he wanted it to be his too. What he thought to be of men, he wanted to buy from men. *How much money*, he said, *do you want to take from me, that the Holy Spirit may be given through the laying on of my hands?* Then Peter, detesting him, said: *You have no part nor lot in this faith. Did you think the gift of God could be bought with money? Let your money be with you unto perdition* [^28]: and the rest that he fittingly said there.

**11.** But why I wished to recall this, let Your Charity attend. It was necessary that God should first show that he works through men: but then by himself, lest men should think, as Simon thought, that it was something of men, not of God. Although the disciples themselves already knew this. For a hundred and twenty men had been gathered, when the Holy Spirit came upon them through the laying on of no one's hands [^29]. For who had then laid hands on them? And yet he came, and filled the first ones. After that scandal of Simon, what did God do? See the teacher, not by words, but by deeds. That same Philip, who had baptized the men, and the Holy Spirit had not come upon them, unless the Apostles had assembled and laid hands on them—he baptized the eunuch, that is, a certain officer of queen Candace, who had worshiped in Jerusalem, and returning from there was reading the prophet Isaiah in his chariot, and did not understand. Admonished, Philip approached the chariot, expounded the reading, instructed his faith, evangelized Christ. The eunuch believed in Christ, and said, when they had come to a certain water: *Behold, water, what prevents me from being baptized* [^30]? Philip said to him: *If you believe in Jesus Christ?* He answered: *I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God* [^31]. At once he went down with him into the water. The mystery and sacrament of baptism being completed, lest the gift of the Holy Spirit should be thought of men, it was not awaited, as then, that the Apostles should come, but at once the Holy Spirit came [^32]. Simon's notion was undone, lest he should have imitators in such a notion.

**12.** Then another, more wonderful example. Peter came to Cornelius the centurion, to an uncircumcised gentile man: he began to preach Christ Jesus, both to him and to those who were with him. While Peter was still speaking—I do not say, before he had laid on hands, but before he had even baptized, and while those who were with Peter were doubting whether the uncircumcised should be baptized (for a scandal had arisen between the Jews who had believed and those who had been made faithful from among the Gentiles, that is, between the Jews and the Christians who were being baptized uncircumcised)—that God might remove this question, while Peter was speaking, the Holy Spirit came, filled Cornelius, filled those who were with him. And by that very attestation of a great deed, it was as though it were cried out to Peter: Why do you doubt about the water? I am already here [^33].

**13.** Therefore let any soul, secure in being freed from much wickedness by the Lord's grace—being cleansed in the Church as from unclean prostitution—believe, approach the feet of the Lord, seek the footsteps of the Lord, confess while pouring out tears, wipe them with her hair. The feet of the Lord are the preachers of the Gospel. The hair of the woman is her superfluous possessions. Let her wipe with her hair, let her wipe indeed, let her work mercy: and when she has wiped, let her kiss; let her receive peace, that she may have love. She approached such a one, she was baptized by such a one as the apostle Paul was: let her hear from him: *Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ* [^34]. But if she was baptized by some other who seeks his own, not the things of Jesus Christ [^35]: let her hear from the Lord: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do* [^36]. Let her be secure even in him, whether she fall in with a good evangelist, or with one who does not do what he says. For from the Lord she securely hears: *Go, woman; your faith has saved you* [^37].
