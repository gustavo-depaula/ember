# Sermon 164

**1. 1.** Truth, through the Apostle, admonishes us all to bear one another's burdens; and in the very thing in which he admonishes us to bear one another's burdens, he shows with what fruit we do it, adding and saying, *And so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^1]; which will not be fulfilled, unless we bear one another's burdens. What these burdens are, and how they are to be borne—since indeed we all according to our strength ought to strive to fulfill the law of Christ—with the Lord's help I will try to show. What I have proposed to demonstrate, remember to demand; and when I have rendered it, do not ask for it. This I have proposed to demonstrate, the Lord helping my intention and your prayers for me: what the burdens are which the Apostle commands us to bear for one another, and how they are to be borne. If we do this, that wherein he placed the fruit will of its own accord follow—that we may fulfill the law of Christ.

**1. 2.** Someone says: Did the Apostle, then, speak obscurely, that you should try to expound what these burdens are, or how they are to be borne for one another? There is there a question which forces us to distinguish the burdens. For in the very passage of the reading you have it set down there: *But each one will bear his own burden*[^2]. Now then it occurs to your minds: If *each one will bear his own burden*, how does he say, *Bear one another's burdens*[^3]? Unless the burdens are to be distinguished, lest the Apostle be thought to speak things contrary to himself. For not far off, not in another Epistle, not in this very one far above or below; but in that very place, so that the same words are contiguous to one another, he set down both: both that each one will bear his own burden, and what he admonished and exhorted, that we bear one another's burdens.

**2. 3.** There are, then, some burdens in which each one bears his own, nor does one bear it with another, nor cast it on another; and there are other burdens in which you rightly say to a brother: I bear it with you, or: I bear it for you. If, then, distinction is needed, the understanding is not easy. Against those, therefore, who thought that a man could be contaminated by another's sins, the Apostle answered, *Each one will bear his own burden*. Likewise, against those into whom negligence could creep through this, so that, made as it were secure that they would not be contaminated by another's sins, they would care to correct no one: *Bear one another's burdens*[^4]. It was briefly said, and briefly distinguished; and, as far as I reckon, it did not impede the manifestation of the truth. For you both heard briefly, and quickly understood. I did not see your hearts; but I heard the voices, witnesses of the heart. Now then, as it were secure about what has been understood, let us at last discourse more broadly; not that what is to be understood may be insinuated, but that what has been understood may be commended.

**2. 4.** The burdens which each one bears as his own are sins. To men carrying these packs of detestable burdens, and sweating in vain under them, the Lord says, *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you*[^5]. How does he refresh those burdened with sins, but by the forgiveness of sins? The preacher of the world, from a certain watchtower of lofty authority, cries out: Hear, human race; hear, sons of Adam; hear, laborious and unfruitful race: I see your labor, see my gift. I know, you labor and are burdened; and, what is more wretched, you bind pernicious packs to your shoulders; in addition, what is worse, you ask that burdens be added to you, not laid down.

**3. 5.** Which of us can in a short time discuss the multiplicity and variety of these packs? Yet let us mention a few of them, and from these conjecture the rest. See a man burdened with the pack of greed, see him sweating under this pack, panting, thirsting, and by laboring adding to the pack. What do you await, O greedy one, embracing your burden, and binding an evil pack under your shoulders with the chains of desire? What do you await? Why do you labor? Why do you gape? Why do you covet? Surely to satisfy greed. O empty vows and most worthless deeds! Do you await, then, to satisfy greed? It can press you, you cannot satisfy it. Or is it perhaps not heavy? Have you under this pack lost even your senses? Is greed not heavy? Why, then, does it rouse you from sleep, which sometimes does not even allow you to sleep? And perhaps you have with it another burden, of laziness, and these two most worthless burdens, fighting with one another, press you and tear you apart. For they do not command equal things, do not order similar things. Laziness says: Sleep; greed says: Get up. Laziness says: Do not endure cold days; greed says: Bear even storms at sea. The one says: Rest; the other does not allow you to rest. It commands not only: Go forth; but also: Sail across the sea, seek lands you do not know. Goods must be carried to India; you do not know the language of the Indians, but the speech of greed seems intelligible. You will come unknown to the unknown; you give, receive, buy, carry; having been in peril you arrived, with perils you return, you cry out, tossed at sea by a storm: God, free me. Do you not hear him answering: Why? Did I send you? Greed commanded you to acquire what you did not have; I commanded you, without labor, before your door, to give to the poor man what you had. It sent you to the Indians to bring back gold; I set Christ at your door, from whom you might buy the kingdom of heaven. You labor at the command of greed, at my command you do not labor. We both commanded; you did not hear me: let the one you obeyed free you.

**4. 6.** How many carry these packs? How many, set under those very packs, cry out to me as I now speak against them? With their packs they entered, with their packs they go out; greedy they came in, greedy they depart. I labored by speaking against those packs. If you cry out, lay down what you carry. Finally, do not listen to me; listen to your Emperor crying out, *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened*. For you do not come, unless you cease to labor. You wish to run to me, but with heavy packs you cannot. *Come*, he says, *to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you*[^6]. I give pardon of past sins, I will take away what pressed your eyes, I will heal what harmed your shoulders. I will indeed take away the packs, but I will not let you go empty of packs: I will take away the evil packs, and impose good ones. For when he had said, *And I will refresh you*; he added, *Take my yoke upon you*[^7]. Desire had subjugated you badly, let love subjugate you wholesomely.

**4. 7.** *Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me*[^8]. If any human teaching whatever has become cheap to you, *learn from me*. Christ the master cries out, the only Son of God, the only truthful one, the true one, the truth cries out: *Learn from me*. What? That *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God, and all things were made through him*[^9]? Can we learn this from him—to fashion the world, to fill the heaven with lights, to order the alternations of day and night, to bid the times and ages to run their course, to grant power to seeds, to fill the earth with animals? The heavenly master commands us to learn none of these things: those he does as God.

**5. 7.** But because this one deigned to be both God and man, in that which is God, hear that you may be recreated; in that which is man, hear that you may imitate. *Learn*, he says, *from me*; not to fashion the world, and create natures; nor those other things which this hidden God, manifest as man, did; nor does he say: Learn from me to drive fevers from the sick, to put demons to flight, to raise the dead, to command the winds and the waves, to walk upon the waters; nor does he say this: *Learn from me*. For these things he gave to certain of his disciples, to certain he did not give them: but this, *Learn from me*, he says to all; from this precept let no one excuse himself: *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart*[^10]. Why do you hesitate to bear this pack? Is this pack heavy—humility and devotion? Is this pack heavy—faith, hope, love? For these make humble, these make gentle. And see that you will not be burdened, if you hear him. *For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light*[^11]. What is, *it is light*? What if it has weight, but less? Has greed more, righteousness less? I do not wish you to understand it thus. This pack is not the weight of one burdened, but the wings of one about to fly. For the birds too have the packs of their feathers. And what do we say? They carry them, and are carried. They carry them on earth, they are carried by them in heaven. If you wish to show mercy to a bird, especially in summer, and say: This poor little bird is burdened by its feathers, and you take away this burden; it will remain on the earth, the one you wished to help. Carry, then, the feathers of peace, take the wings of love. This is the pack, thus the law of Christ will be fulfilled.

**6. 8.** The burdens are distinguished. See now, some greedy man enters; you know him as greedy, he stands with you, and you are not greedy; but even merciful, you give to the poor man what you have, you do not gape after the things you do not have; you hear the Apostle saying, *Command the rich of this world not to be proudly wise, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy; let them be rich in good works, give easily, share, treasure up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^12]. You heard, you acknowledged, you learned, you held, you did it. Do what you do, do not grow slack, do not cease. *He who perseveres to the end, this one will be saved*[^13]. You did good to a man, the man is ungrateful; do not repent of having done good, lest by repenting you pour out what you fulfilled by showing mercy; say in your heart: This one does not see for whom I did it, but he sees on whose account I did it; because this one, if he saw, if he were not ungrateful, would profit himself rather than me. Let me hold to God, from whom what I do is not hidden; not only what I do, but even what I do in my heart; let me hope in him as the repayer, who does not seek a witness of my deed. You are such; and perhaps among the people of God there stands beside you a greedy plunderer, gaping after others' goods. Whom you know to be such, and he is faithful, or rather called faithful, you cannot drive him from the Church, you have no avenue to correct him by chastising and rebuking, he is going to approach the altar with you; do not fear: *Each one will bear his own burden*. Remember the Apostle, that you may approach secure: *Each one will bear his own burden*. Only let him not say to you: Bear it with me. For if you wish to share greed with him, the burden will not be lessened, but two will be weighed down. Let him carry his own pack, then, and you yours: since, when your Lord shook off such a pack from your shoulders, he imposed another; he shook off that of desire, imposed that of love. Therefore, according to his own desires each one bears his own pack, the evil man an evil one, the good man a good one.

**7. 9.** Turn now to that precept too: *Bear one another's burdens*[^14]. For you have the pack of Christ; whence you may bear with another his own burden. He is poor, you are rich; his burden is poverty; you do not have such a burden. See lest perhaps, when the poor man appeals to you, you say: *Each one will bear his own burden*. Here hear the other precept: *Bear one another's burdens*. Poverty is not my burden, but is the burden of my brother. See lest riches be your greater burden. For you do not have the burden, poverty, but you have the burden, riches. If you attend well, it is a burden. He has one burden, you another. Carry with him, and let him carry with you, that you may bear one another's burdens. What is the burden of poverty? Not to have. What is the burden of riches? To have more than is needed. Both he is burdened, and you are burdened. Carry with him not-having, let him carry with you having-more; that your packs may be made equal. For if you give to the needy, you lessen for him, the not-having one, his burden, which was not-having; if you give to him, he begins to have; the burden which is called not-having is lessened for him; he too lessens your burden, which is called having-more. Two of you walk the way of God in the pilgrimage of this age: you carried great superfluous provisions, but he had no provisions; he cleaved to you, desiring to be your companion; do not neglect, do not despise, do not abandon. Do you not see how much you carry? Give some of it to the one carrying nothing and having nothing, and you will both help your companion and relieve yourself. The apostolic sentence has been sufficiently, as I reckon, expounded.

**7. 10.** Let them not sell you smoke who say: We are holy, we do not carry your packs, therefore we do not commune with you. These greater men carry the packs of division, they carry the greater packs of cutting-off, the packs of schism, the packs of heresy, the packs of dissension, the packs of animosity, the packs of false testimonies, the packs of slanderous accusations. These packs we have tried, and we try, to lay down from the shoulders of our brothers. They love holding them to themselves, they are unwilling to be smaller, because they have swelled with those very packs. For even one who lays down a pack that he carried on his neck becomes as it were smaller; but he has laid down weight, not stature.

**7. 11.** But I, you say, do not commune in others' sins. As if I said this to you: Come, commune in others' sins. I do not say this, I know what the Apostle says; but I say this. On account of others' sins, if they were true, and were not rather yours, you would not desert the flock of God, mixed with sheep and goats; you would not abandon the Lord's threshing-floor as long as the chaff is being threshed; you would not break the Lord's nets as long as they draw good and bad fish to the shore. And how, you say, would I bear one whom I know to be evil? Would you not rather bear him, than bear yourself out? Behold how you would bear him: if you attended to the Apostle saying, *Each one will bear his own burden*; this sentence would free you. For you would not share greed with him, but you would share with him the table of Christ. And what would harm you, if you shared with him the table of Christ? The Apostle says, *For he who eats and drinks unworthily, eats and drinks judgment to himself*[^15]. *To himself*, not to you. Of course, if you are a judge, if you have received the power of judging by ecclesiastical rule, if he is accused before you, if he is convicted by true evidence and witnesses, restrain, rebuke, excommunicate, degrade. So let toleration keep watch, that discipline does not sleep.

**8. 12.** But Caecilian was condemned, they say. Condemned? By whom? First in his absence, then by traitors—innocent. These things were alleged, inserted into the records, proved. They tried indeed to weaken the strength of the truth, and strove, as much as they could, to becloud its serenity with the mists of empty prosecutions. The Lord was present, his serenity conquered their mists. And see how, not knowing it, they absolved the Church of the whole world, in whose communion we rejoice, whatever sort we are in it. We protect, defend, hold not ourselves, but that Church, defending the Lord's threshing-floor; for the Lord's threshing-floor I cry out. What sort I am in it, I do not wish you to care; I await the winnowing-fan[^16]. I do not wish, I say, that you care for this; or if you wish to care, do not care with strife, that you may be able to heal the brothers. Care for the chaff, if you can; but do not abandon the wheat, if you cannot care for the chaff. Sometimes even grains are shaken from the Lord's threshing-floor along with the chaff; meanwhile even grains, but not far off. But there are good workers, they go around the threshing-floor, and the things shaken outside they draw with certain cleansing tools and recall onto the threshing-floor, even if by drawing, even if by forcing. The cleansing tools are these worldly laws. Recall, draw the wheat even with the earth, lest on account of the earth the wheat perish. Caecilian was condemned, they say. He was condemned once in absence, three times absolved present. We answered them; and we admonished men, untaught, as briefly as we could, about their own deeds, and said: Why do you recite against Caecilian the council of seventy bishops, pronouncing sentences against an absent man? More were pronounced by the council of the Maximianists against the absent Primian. We said: Caecilian was condemned absent by those, Primian was condemned absent by these. As these do not prejudge the absent Primian, so neither could those prejudge the absent Caecilian.

**9. 13.** What do you think they answered in this strait? For what would they say? By what would they escape, enclosed in the nets of truth? That they might violently break these nets, what did they say briefly and absolutely for us? And indeed many things, and nearly all for us, as the records will indicate, which your Charity is now to read as they are set forth. But in this place I ask you, and beseech you through Christ, that you hold it, say it, always have it in your mouth. For no briefer, surer, clearer sentence could be brought forth for us. What, then, did he say, when we objected this: Thus these do not prejudge Caecilian, as neither those Primian? And that defender of theirs: *Neither does a cause prejudge a cause, nor a person a person*. O answer brief, clear, true! For he did not know what he said; but, like Caiaphas, since he was high priest he prophesied[^17]: *Neither does a cause prejudge a cause, nor a person a person*. If neither a cause prejudges a cause, nor a person a person, then each one bears his own pack. Let him now go and object Caecilian to you; not to you, any man whatever, but let him object Caecilian to the whole world itself. When he does this, he objects an innocent man to innocent men. Plainly the records will indicate most clearly. Caecilian was cleared. But suppose him not cleared, suppose him found criminal; hear your own voice from the whole world: *Neither does a cause prejudge a cause, nor a person a person*. Heretical soul, incurable, animated, when you pronounce sentence against yourself, why do you accuse the judge? If I corrupted him, to judge for me; who corrupted you, to condemn yourself?

**10. 14.** Would that they think on these things at some time, even late, even with their animosity subsiding; that they return to themselves, question themselves, examine themselves, answer themselves, for the truth not fear those to whom they have sold falsehood for so very long. For they fear to offend those very people; they blush before human infirmity, and do not blush before the most invincible truth. Surely they fear this, lest it be said to them: Why, then, did you deceive us? Why did you seduce us? Why did you say so many evil and false things? They ought to answer, if they feared God. It was human to err, it is devilish to remain in error through animosity. It would indeed be better if we never erred; but let us at least do the second-best thing, to amend the error at some time. We deceived, because we were deceived; we preached false things, who believed those who preached false things. Let them say to their own: Together we erred, together let us withdraw from the error. We were leaders to you to the pit, and you followed when we led to the pit; and now follow when we lead to the Church. They could say these things; they would say them to the indignant, say them to the angry, and at some time even those would lay aside their indignation, would love unity at least late.

**10. 15.** Yet let us, brothers, be patient toward them. They are in fever and swelling, the eyes that we are treating. I do not say that we should cease to treat them; but that we should not provoke them by insults to greater bitternesses; let us render the reason gently, let us not exult proudly over victory. *For the servant of the Lord ought not to quarrel*, the Apostle says, *but to be gentle to all, teachable, patient, with modesty rebuking those who think differently; lest perhaps God give them repentance, and they recover from the snares of the devil, by whom they are held captive according to his will*[^18]. Bear patiently, then, if you are sound, bear patiently, in proportion as you are sound. For who is perfectly sound? When the just King sits on his throne, who will boast that he has a clean heart, or who will boast that he is clean from sin[^19]? Therefore, as long as we are such, we owe this to ourselves, that we bear one another's burdens. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
