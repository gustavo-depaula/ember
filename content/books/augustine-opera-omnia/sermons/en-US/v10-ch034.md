# Sermon 358/A: A Treatise of Saint Augustine on the Good of Mercy

**1.** We desire to admonish your holiness about the good of mercy: although in every good work we have often experienced you to be ready, yet it is necessary that we have a sermon carefully conferred about this matter. We treat, then, what is mercy? Nothing else but a certain compassion of the heart at misery. From grief at misery (miseria) mercy (misericordia) is named: both sound there, both misery and heart (cor). When, then, another's misery touches and strikes your heart, it is called mercy. Attend, therefore, my brothers, that all good works which we do in life pertain to mercy. For instance, you hold out bread to the hungry: hold out mercy from the heart, not out of contempt; lest you have as a dog a man like yourself. When, then, you do mercy, whether you hold out bread, grieve for the hungry one: if you give drink, grieve for the thirsty one: if you hold out a garment, grieve for the naked one: if you receive into hospitality, grieve for the pilgrim: if you visit one sick, grieve for the ill one: if you bury one dead, grieve for the dead one: if you reconcile a quarreler, grieve for the one quarreling. All these things, if we love God and neighbor, we do not do without grief of heart. These are the good works which confirm us to be Christians. For the holy Apostle says: *While we have time, let us work good toward all*[^1]. And likewise what does he himself say about that good work in the same place? *But this I say: that he who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly*[^2]. He introduced sowing, he promised a harvest.

**2.** But when you sow, because you do a work of mercy, while you grieve for the one for whom you do it, you sow in tears[^3]. Yet sometime after our end these seeds of mercy will not be needed; because in that kingdom, those who here on account of God were straitened, will not be wretched. But in truth, in that recompense, to whom would you hold out bread, where no one hungers? whom, naked, would you clothe, where all are clothed with immortality? to whom would you offer hospitality, when all will live in their own homeland? whom, sick, would you visit, where there is eternal health? whom, dead, would you bury, where life is forever? whom, quarreling, would you reconcile, where that peace is full which here was promised? There will be no works there, then, no mercy. Why? because you now carry sheaves, you do not throw seed. Let us not, then, fail: with tears we sow, that is, from labor and grief. In the work of mercy, then, do not fail: for you will receive the reward of your seed[^4]. Winter sows in labor: but did the harshness of winter ever deter the farmer from casting into the earth the fruit cleansed with so great labor? He goes forth, and casts into the earth what he had gathered from the earth, what had been cleansed from the earth: he goes forth, and casts into the earth, trembling with cold, untiring. Whence untiring in the cold? Faith and hope shake off the sloth. Does he see the harvest? But he believes it will rise. Does he already gather the fruit? but he hopes he will gather it; and he encourages himself by this faith, by this hope, so that with the great labor of cold he sends the seed into the earth, and, secure, with God granting, may be able, according to the work of his labor, to take up rich fruits. *The end.*

[^1]: Gal 6:10
[^2]: 2 Cor 9:6
[^3]: See Ps 126:5 (125:5)
[^4]: See Ps 126:6 (125:6)
