# Sermon 348: On the Fear of God

**1. 1.** I do not doubt, most beloved brothers, that the fear of God is implanted in your hearts, by which you may be led to true and solid fortitude. For although he is called strong who fears no one, he is perversely strong who first does not wish to fear God, so that by fearing he may hear, by hearing love, and by loving not fear. Then he will be truly most strong, not by proud hardness, but by secure justice. So too it is written: *The fear of the Lord is the hope of fortitude*[^1]. For when the punishment he threatens is feared, one learns to love the reward he promises: and so through the fear of punishment a good life is kept; through a good life a good conscience is obtained, so that through a good conscience no punishment is feared. Wherefore let him learn to fear who does not wish to fear. Let him learn to be anxious for a time who wishes always to be secure. For as John says: *Fear is not in love, but perfect love casts out fear*[^2]. He said it indeed, and said it truly. If, then, you do not wish to have fear, first see whether you already have that perfect love which casts out fear. But if before this perfection fear is excluded, pride puffs up, love does not build up. For just as in good health hunger is driven out not by disgust but by food; so in a good mind fear is to be driven out not by vanity but by love.

**2. 2.** Examine, then, your conscience, whoever you are who do not now wish to fear. Do not stroke the surface, descend into yourself, penetrate the inner parts of your heart. Search diligently whether no poisoned vein there sucks and drinks in a corrupting love of the world, whether you are moved and captured by no enticement of carnal pleasure, whether you are raised up swollen by no empty boasting, whether you seethe with no care of vanity: dare to announce that you see pure and clear, whatever hiding-places you scrutinize in your conscience, from deeds, from words, from depraved thoughts; if now the diligence of iniquity does not weary you, whether any negligence of equity creeps in. If these things are so, you rightly rejoice; rejoice that you are without fear. But it is the love of God that has excluded it, whom you love with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind. And the love of neighbor too has excluded it, whom you love as yourself: and therefore you are busy for him, that he too may love God with you with his whole heart, with his whole soul, with his whole mind; because you do not rightly love even yourself otherwise than because you so love God that you do not for that reason love him less, because you turn even to yourself. But if, even within yourself, you are provoked by no desire (which indeed who would dare to boast of himself?), yet if you love yourself in yourself, and please yourself with yourself, you ought to fear this very thing more vehemently, because you fear nothing. For fear is not to be cast out by any love whatever, but by that right love by which we love God wholly, and therefore the neighbor, so that he too may thus love God. But to love oneself in oneself, and to please oneself, is not the love of justice, but the vanity of pride. And on this account the Apostle struck with just reproof those who love themselves and please themselves[^3]. *Perfect love*, then, *casts out fear*. But that is to be called love which is not worthlessness. But what is more worthless than a man without God? Behold what he loves, who loves himself not in God, but in himself. Rightly is it said to him: *Do not be high-minded, but fear*[^4]. For because he is high-minded, and therefore does not fear, surely he perniciously does not fear, who is not set upon something solid, but is tossed by the wind of pride. Nor is he meek and pious who loves and praises himself in himself: but exalted and fierce, he does not know how to say: *In the Lord shall my soul be praised; let the meek hear, and be glad*[^5]. For what good does he love, who perhaps loves this very thing for its own sake, to fear nothing? For he can persuade himself of this, not by health, but by monstrousness. For example, there is some most daring robber, the more perversely strong, the more dangerously cruel, who, on account of that very love by which he loves to fear nothing, contrives huge crimes, so that he may exercise what he loves, and by exercising it strengthen it: the greater his deeds have been, the greater will be the daring of the one who does not fear. This, then, is not to be loved as a great good, which can be found even in the worst man.

**2. 3.** Wherefore the philosophers of this world are to be laughed at, not only the Epicureans, who hold even justice itself for sale, at the price of carnal pleasure. For they say that the wise man ought to be just for this reason, that he may either acquire or keep pleasure of the body. For these too boast themselves most strong, and say that they fear nothing at all: because they think that God does not care for human affairs, and that, this life being spent, they believe there will be none afterward; and if any adversity befalls them in this very life, they consider themselves fortified by this, that the pleasure of the body, when they cannot hold it in the body itself, they can yet think of in the mind, and, delighting themselves by that thought, can guard the blessedness of bodily pleasure, even against the onslaught of bodily pain. Does not love among these too cast out fear? But the love of the most sordid pleasure, indeed the love of the most shameful vanity. For when invading pain has excluded pleasure itself from the members of the body, through a false image of it the vanity will remain in the mind. Which vanity is so loved that, when a vain man embraces it with all the strength of his heart, even the cruelty of pain is mitigated. Not only these, then, are to be derided, but also the Stoics themselves. For these two sects of the Epicureans and Stoics, as we read in the Acts of the Apostles, dared to puff out their smoke against the light of our Paul[^6]. For the Stoics too set themselves forward as most strong, and not on account of pleasure of the body, but on account of virtue of the mind, they guard this very fearing-nothing for the sake of fearing nothing, swollen with conceit, and not healed by wisdom, but hardened by error. For they are the less and less healthy, the more they have believed that the sick mind can be healed by themselves. But they think this to be health of the mind, that they say the wise man ought not even to pity. For if he pities, they say, he grieves; but what grieves is not healthy. O foolish blindness! What, if he grieves the less, the less healthy he is? For it makes a difference whether he does not grieve by perfect health: such as the body and the mind of the saints will be in the resurrection of the dead, which these do not believe; because they have unlearned teachers, since they have themselves. It makes a difference, then, whether one does not grieve by health, or by numbness. For according to the health of this mortality, healthy flesh, when it is pricked, grieves. Such too is the mind, well-disposed according to this life, which, pierced by the misery of one laboring, suffers along with it by mercy. But the flesh, numbed by a graver disease, or even dead with the spirit lost, does not grieve even when pricked: such is the mind of those who philosophize without God, or rather are choked. For just as the body, breathed into by the mind, so the mind itself, breathed into by God, lives. Let these, then, who do not grieve nor fear, see whether perhaps they are not healthy, but dead.

**3. 4.** But let the Christian fear, before perfect love casts out fear: let him believe and understand that he is on pilgrimage away from the Lord, as long as he lives in the body which is corrupted and weighs down the soul. Let his fear be the less, the nearer the homeland to which we tend. For the fear of those on pilgrimage ought to be greater, that of those drawing near less, that of those arriving none. So fear leads to love, and perfect love casts out fear. But let the Christian fear, not those who kill the body, and afterward have nothing they can do; but him who has the power to kill both body and soul in the fire of hell[^7]. But there is another fear of the Lord, *chaste, remaining unto the age of the age*[^8]. This, then, perfect love does not cast out, otherwise it would not remain unto the age of the age: nor without cause, when it had been said, *the fear of the Lord*, was added, *chaste*; and so it is joined: *remaining unto the age of the age*. Why, except because that fear which love casts out pricks the soul for this reason, lest something be lost which is loved in a creature, whether bodily health and rest itself, or something of the kind, after death? For on this account both the punishments in hell and the griefs and torments of the hells are feared. But when the soul takes heed lest, having abandoned God, it be abandoned by him, it is the chaste fear remaining unto the age of the age. Of which I would speak more at length, did not the sermon, now rather long, compel me to spare both my aged strength and perhaps your satiety.
