# Sermon 162

**1.** The question from the Epistle of the blessed apostle Paul to the Corinthians, where he says, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*[^1], I do not know whether it can be resolved with full clarity, although, by the Lord's gift, something can probably be said about it: so deep is it. For when, earlier in the same Epistle, the Apostle said, *Do not err; neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor sodomites, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor the rapacious will possess the kingdom of God*[^2]; and a little after, *Do you not know*, he says, *that your bodies are members of Christ? Shall I, then, take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot? Far from it. Or do you not know that he who cleaves to a harlot is one body? For they will be*, he says, *two in one flesh. But he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit. Flee fornication*[^3]; and there he added, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body. Or do you not know that your body is a temple in you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought with a great price; glorify and carry God in your body*[^4]; when, then, he had first enumerated many and horrible sins of men in this passage, to whom the kingdom of God will not be given, which nevertheless can be perpetrated by men only through the body—which body, indeed, of the faithful now, he says is a temple of the Holy Spirit whom we have from God; and the very members of our body he affirms to be members of Christ; arguing about which and as it were questioning, he says, *Shall I, then, take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot*? and he answers himself, *Far from it*! Let him still add and say, *Do you not know that he who cleaves to a harlot is one body? For they will be*, he says, *two in one flesh; but he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit*; and let him conclude, *Flee fornication*; yet he himself goes on and says, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*; as though indeed those sins which he enumerated, saying, *Do not err; neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor sodomites, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor the rapacious will possess the kingdom of God*—all these crimes and shameful deeds, can they be done or carried out only through the body? What man of sound head would deny this? For this whole passage the Apostle was pleading and defending on account of the body itself, now bought with a great price—that is, the precious blood of Christ—made by the Lord a temple of the Holy Spirit, that it not be polluted by such shameful deeds, but rather be kept inviolate as a dwelling of God. Why, then, did he wish to add this, from which a difficult question would arise—that is, to say, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*; when both fornication itself, or other sins of this kind which are done only through the body, most like to baseness and fornication, are carried out and exercised only through the body itself? For what? Can anyone (to be silent about the others mentioned above) be a thief, or a drunkard, or a reviler, or rapacious, outside the operation of this body? Although neither idolatry itself nor greed itself could come to its own use and enjoyment apart from the service of this body. What is it, then: *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*? First, because a man established in this body, whatever he can iniquitously covet by the mind alone, cannot be said to do outside this body, since it is plain that he does this by carnal sense and carnal prudence, still encompassed by this body. For even what is written in the Psalm, *The fool has said in his heart, There is no God*[^5], the same blessed apostle Paul could surely not separate from a corporeal work, in that place where he says, *We will all stand before the tribunal of Christ, that each may receive according to the things he did through the body, whether good or evil*[^6]. Because, namely, only when established in the body could the ungodly man say, *There is no God*. Not to mention that in another Epistle the same Teacher of the Gentiles says, *But the works of the flesh are manifest*; and he goes on: *Which are fornications, uncleannesses, debaucheries, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkennesses, and the like; of which I foretell you, as I have foretold, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^7]. For do not those other things which he interposed there—jealousies, animosities, dissensions, envies, heresies—seem to us to be done outside the body? And yet the Teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth attributes these to the works of the flesh. What is it, then: *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body*; and naming only one sin, of fornication, he says, *But he who fornicates sins against his own body*?

**2.** It appears, therefore, to anyone, however slow and dull, how difficult this question is; on which, if the Lord deigns to shed a little light and reveal it to our devout intention, we shall be able to say something reasonably. For the blessed Apostle, in whom Christ was speaking, seems either to have wished to heighten the evil of fornication above all other sins, which, even if they are committed through the body, yet do not make the human soul so bound and liable to carnal concupiscence as, in the work of bodily fornication alone, the immense force of lust makes the soul mingle with the body itself, and as it were be glued and bound to it as one; so much so that at that very moment and experience of so great a shameful deed a man is permitted to think or attend to nothing else but what addicts the mind to itself, the mind which the very submersion, and as it were absorption, of lust and carnal concupiscence holds captive and subjects; so that this seems to be what was said, *But he who fornicates sins against his own body*; because then the heart of the fornicating man becomes properly and intimately a slave of the body, especially at the time of that most wicked operation itself; so much so that the Apostle himself, wishing more emphatically to commend that this evil must be guarded against by men, said, *Shall I, then, take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot?* and, abhorring and detesting it, answered, *Far from it! Or do you not know*, he says, *that he who cleaves to a harlot is one body? For they will be*, he says, *two in one flesh*. Could this be said of any other crimes whatever of men? For in any other crimes the human soul is free both to do some one of them, and at that very time to be stretched out elsewhere in thought; which in the very work and time of fornication is not permitted to the soul, to be free to think of anything else. For the whole man is so absorbed by the body and in the body itself that he can no longer be said to be his own soul; but the whole man at once can be said to be flesh, and a spirit going and not returning[^8]. So, then, we can understand that *every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*; so that the Apostle seems, as I said, to have wished so to heighten the evil of fornication, that in comparison with this fornication he reckoned all other sins, whatever they be, to be held outside the body; and said that by this one evil of fornication alone one sins against one's own body, because by a greater ardor of lust, than which there is none higher, the pleasure of the body itself holds him a slave and makes him captive.

**3.** Let these things be said of the special fornication of this body. But because in the sacred Letters fornication is censured and named not only specially, but also generally; let us try, with God's help, to say something probable about this too. The general fornication, then, is openly manifested in the Psalm, where it is said, *For behold, those who make themselves far from you will perish; you have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you*[^9]. Where, subsequently, he added how this general fornication can be escaped and fled, saying, *But for me to cleave to God is good*[^10]. So that from there we may easily notice that this is the general fornication of the human soul, by which anyone, not cleaving to God, cleaves to the world. Hence the blessed apostle John says, *If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him*[^11]. And the apostle James says, *Adulterers, do you not know that the friendship of this world is enmity with God*[^12]? Briefly, then, it has been defined that he cannot have the love of God who has the love of the world; and that he is an enemy of God who wishes to be a friend of the world. To this also pertains what the Lord says in the Gospel, *No one can serve two masters; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will bear with the one and despise the other*. And he concludes, *You cannot serve God and mammon*[^13]. This, then, is the general fornication of the soul, as has been said, containing in itself absolutely all things, by which one does not cleave to God while cleaving to the world; so that we may also be able to understand, according to this general fornication, what the Apostle says, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*. Because, if the human soul does not fornicate, by cleaving to God, and by not cleaving to the world, whatever other sins, utterly foreign to carnal concupiscence, a man may incur, on account of the very frailty of mortality, whether by ignorance, or negligence, or forgetfulness, or not understanding, this is what was said, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body*; because no sin of corporeal or temporal concupiscence can be found here; whence rightly any such sin seems to be said to be outside the body. But if the worldly man, cleaving to the world, makes himself far from God, by fornicating away from God himself, he sins against his own body; because by corporeal concupiscence the human soul is dragged into temporal and carnal things by carnal sense and prudence, and is distracted, serving the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed unto the ages.

**4.** Thus, then, as far as it seems to me, with faith preserved, the evil of both fornications—both the special and the universal—can be understood in this one passage of so great and so mighty a Teacher, where he says, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*; so that either a heightening was made by the Apostle of this special fornication, by which one is rightly understood to sin against one's own body, because nowhere is the whole man so addicted to the pleasure of the body itself, and ineffably or inevitably fixed to it, that in comparison with this so great an evil, the other sins seem to be outside the body, even if they are exercised through the body. Just as a certain force of imperious lust, belonging to fornication alone, subjects the man to its own condition, and makes him the worst chattel of his own body, especially at the time of that most unclean operation itself; so that, beyond what he is doing in the body itself, the human mind is not free either to think or to attend to anything else. But if the Apostle also wished to signify the general fornication, on account of which he seems to have said, *Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*; it is to be taken and understood thus: that anyone who, while not cleaving to God, cleaves to the world, loving and coveting all temporal things, is rightly said to sin against his own body—that is, given over and subjected to the whole carnal concupiscence, as it were a complete slave of the creature, estranged from the Creator himself, through that pride which is the beginning of all sin, of which *the beginning of pride is, as it is written, to apostatize from God*[^14]. From which evil of general fornication anyone who is free, whatever other sin he may incur as a man still corruptible and mortal, let this be understood to be outside the body—that is, foreign to the evil of all corporeal and temporal concupiscence, to be, as has often been said, outside the body. For only by the evil of carnal and general concupiscence does the soul in all things fornicate away from God, as though bound and fettered by corporeal and temporal desires and delights, and sin against its own body, serving whose universal concupiscence it is bent toward the world and estranged from God; which is, as has been said, *The beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*. On account of which evil of general fornication that must be guarded against, the blessed John admonishes, saying, *Do not love the world, nor the things that are in the world; for the things that are in the world are the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the ambition of the age, which is not from the Father, but from the world. And the world passes away, and its concupiscence. But he who does the will of God remains forever, as he remains forever*[^15]. This love of the world, then, which contains in itself the universal concupiscence of the world, is the general fornication, by which one sins against one's own body; in that the human soul unceasingly serves all corporeal and visible and temporal desires and pleasures, abandoned and forsaken by the very Creator of all things.
