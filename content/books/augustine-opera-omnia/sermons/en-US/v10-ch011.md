# Sermon 346/C: On the Tribulations and Afflictions of the World

**1.** As often as we suffer anything of affliction or tribulation, they are admonitions and at the same time corrections for us. For even our holy Scriptures do not promise us peace, security, and rest, but tribulations, afflictions, scandals—the Gospel does not keep silent; but *he who perseveres to the end, he will be saved*[^1]. For what good has this life ever had, from the first man himself, from the time he merited death, from the time he received the curse, from which curse Christ the Lord freed us? Therefore we must not murmur, brothers, *as some of them murmured*, as the Apostle says, *and perished by serpents*[^2]. What unaccustomed thing of the sort does the human race now suffer, brothers, which the fathers did not suffer? Or when do we suffer such things as we have learned they suffered? And you find men murmuring about their own times, and saying that those times of our parents were good. What if they could be recalled to the times of their parents, and they murmured there too? For the times you think were good, because they are now past, are therefore for that reason good. If you are now freed from the curse, if you have now believed in the Son of God, if you are now either steeped or instructed in the sacred Scriptures, I marvel that you reckon Adam to have had good times. And your parents bore Adam too. Surely that is Adam, to whom it was said: *In the sweat of your face you shall eat your bread, and you shall work the earth from which you were taken; thorns and thistles it will bring forth for you*[^3]. This he merited, this he received, this he obtained from the just judgment of God. Why, then, do you think past times were better than yours? From that Adam to the Adam of today, labor and sweat, thorns and thistles. Has the flood escaped our memory? Have the laborious times of famine and wars escaped us, which were written for this reason, lest we murmur against God about the present time? Has that time escaped us, among our fathers far most remote from your times, when the dead head of an ass was sold for so much gold, when dove's dung was bought for no small silver, when women made a pact about eating their own infants; and when one had been killed and consumed, and the other was unwilling to kill her own, such a case came to the judge, came to the king, where he found himself rather a defendant than a judge[^4]? And who is able to recall the wars or famines of that time? What sort of times, then, were those! Do we not all shudder at what we hear, at what we read? So that we may rather have something to be thankful for, than something to murmur about concerning our own times.

**2.** When, then, was it ever well with the human race? When was there not fear, when not grief, when sure happiness, when not true unhappiness? If you do not have, you burn to acquire; do you have? You tremble lest you lose; and what is more wretched, both in that burning and in this fear you think yourself healthy. Is a wife to be taken? If bad, she will be your punishment; if good, woe lest perhaps she die. Children, unborn, torment with griefs, born, with fears; how rejoicing one born makes us, who is at once feared lest he be lamented when carried out for burial! Where will there be a secure life? Is not all this earth as it were a great ship, carrying those tossed by waves, in peril, subject to so many storms and tempests? They fear shipwreck, they sigh for the harbor, who now understand themselves to be on pilgrimage. Therefore what are good days? Uncertain days, days flying past, days finished before they come, days that come for this purpose, that they should not be. *Who, then, is the man who wishes for life, and loves to see good days?*[^5] But here is neither life, nor good days: for the good days are eternity itself. The days that are without end are called days. *I will dwell*, he says, *unto the age, in length of days*[^6]. Likewise it is said, *that one day in your house is better than thousands*[^7]: better one without end. Let us, then, desire such a thing. Such a thing is promised to us, with familiar names, but unfamiliar realities. *Who is the man who wishes for life?* Daily life and life is spoken of: but what is it to that life? *And loves to see good days?* Daily good days too are spoken of: but if they are examined, they are not found. I have had a good day today. If you found your friend, you would call him good, if he were willing to stay with you: does not a man always complain of his friend, if he sees him, and he passes by? Such, then, is that good day, which sees you and passes by. I have had a good one. Where is it? Bring it to me. I have had a good day. The one you rejoice over as had, you lament as gone by. *Who, then, is the man who wishes for life, and loves to see good days?* We all answer: I do: but after this life, after these days. If, then, we are deferred, what is said to us, that we may do it and arrive at that for which we are deferred? What, then, shall I do in this life, such as it is, that I may come to life and good days? What follows in that psalm: *Restrain your tongue from evil, and your lips that they speak no guile; turn aside from evil, and do good*[^8]. Do, then, what is commanded, and you will receive what is promised. If you think it laborious, and are cast down by the labor of the work, be lifted up by the splendor of the reward.

[^2]: See 1 Cor 10:9-10

[^4]: See 2 Kgs 6:25-29
