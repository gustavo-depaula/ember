# Sermon 32

**1** God and our Lord, caring for and healing every sickness of the soul, has brought forth many medicines from the holy Scriptures, as from certain cabinets of his own, when the divine readings were read. These are to be applied through our ministry to our wounds. For we do not profess to be the boys of the physician through whom he deigns to heal others in such a way that we ourselves no longer need treatment. If we attend to him, if we offer ourselves to him with our whole heart to be cured, we shall all be healed. Many things have been read, both great and necessary. Although all are so, yet some things are hidden more secretly in the Scriptures to exercise those who seek, while others are set ready and in the open to cure those who long. This psalm contains great secrets indeed, which, if we wanted to treat all one by one, I fear our common weakness would not bear—whether on account of the heat of the season, or the body's strength, or slowness of understanding, or even on account of our own insufficient adequacy. We will therefore taste a few things from it, as much as we judge sufficient for our office and the attention of your Charity.

**2** First, its title is: *To Goliath*[^1]. Those who are not unskilled in the divine Scriptures, who love to frequent this school, who do not hate the teacher like hopeless boys, and offer an attentive ear in church to the readers, and open the receptacle of their heart to the stream of the divine Scripture, who do not, within these walls of their own house, busy themselves and delight in household tales, so that they gather for this purpose, to find people with whom to speak trifles, not people with whom to hear useful things, who do not love to talk of others' affairs when they have failed in their own—those, then, who do not gather thus, and gather frequently, are not unskilled in this title of the psalm. What is written, *To Goliath*, they know who Goliath was. Yet on account of others who are attentive now but at another time less attentive, or who perhaps, by the secular thorns—that is, the cares of worldly business—are accustomed to choke the word in their heart like a useful seed, let us narrate even these very old and familiar things to those attentive and devoted to the divine Scriptures.

**3** Goliath was one of the Allophyles—that is, of the foreigners—who were waging war at that time against the children of Israel. At that time the holy David, whose this psalter is—or rather, through whom the Holy Spirit ministered this psalter—was a boy pasturing his father's sheep, of tender age, scarcely yet a youth. His brothers, already young men, were soldiers and were in the king's army. Sent by his parents, he brought them something from home for their use. So at that time, when the war was being waged, he was found in the army, not yet a soldier, but the minister and brother of soldiers. There appeared then this Goliath of whom mention was made, huge in stature of body, furnished with arms, trained too in strength, lifted up with boasting, who proudly challenged the adversary people to single combat—that is, that one chosen from them should come forth against him, so that, two fighting, the decision of the whole war should turn in the midst, with the pact and agreement added that, if either of those two conquered, the victory should be granted to the whole side on which he stood. The king of the people of the Jews, the children of Israel, was Saul. He was anguished, he burned, he sought in the whole army an equal to him. But he found none, neither in form of body nor in boldness of challenge. While he was burning, this boy David dared—not presuming on his own strength, but in the name of his God—to go forth against him. It was announced to the king, not with the boldness of boyhood, but with the confidence of devotion. Nor did the king refuse, he did not decline. He understood, when he saw the boy daring, that there was something of divinity in him, and that that tender age could not presume such things without divine prompting. He gladly accepted. He went forth against Goliath.

**4** In all, then, who were on that side from which David went forth, there was no presumption except in God. But in those others all hope was in the strength of one man. But what is man, except what he sang in this psalm? *Man has been likened to vanity; his days pass like a shadow*[^2]. Therefore their hope was empty, placed in a passing shadow. But David was armed, so that, since he was unequal in age and strength, he might at least be equal in arms. But the old arms did not help so much as burden the new age. And to this pertains what the apostolic reading before the song of the psalm also prefigured, saying: *Put off the old man and put on the new*[^3]. David did not want the oldness of the arms. He cast it off. He said it was burdensome, because it entangled him. Most unencumbered, he wished to go forth to battle, strong not in himself but in the Lord, armed not so much with iron as with faith.

**5** Yet, having cast off the arms, he chose something with which to fight. And this not without a sacred sign. For you see, as it were, two lives—one in the foreigners, the old, the other in the Israelites, the new—fighting against each other. On that side the body of the devil, on this a prefiguration of the Lord Jesus Christ. He took five stones from the torrent, from the river, and put them in the shepherd's vessel in which milk is usually milked. So he went forth armed. The five stones were the law; for the law is contained in the five books of Moses. And in that very law there are ten salutary precepts, which ten precepts the rest serve. The law, then, is prefigured by both the number five and the number ten. And therefore David fought with the number five, sang with the number ten, saying: *On a psaltery of ten strings I will sing to you*[^4]. Nor did he hurl all five stones, but took one. For in the number of stones he showed the number of the books, in the one stone the unity of those who fulfill the law. For unity itself fulfills the law—that is, love. Therefore those five stones were taken up from the river. What did the river signify at that time?

**6** For the same things are not always signified in the Scriptures by fixed objects. And this your Holiness ought to know on account of the other rules, that you may listen as docile to the reader. The things set down allegorically in the Scriptures do not always signify this one thing. A mountain does not always signify the Lord, a stone does not always signify the Lord, a lion does not always signify the Lord, not always something good, not always something evil, but according to the places of the Scriptures, to which the rest of the surrounding context of that very reading pertains. Just as the letters in so many thousands of words and discourses are themselves repeated, not increased. Words are infinite, yet the letters are finite. No one can number words; anyone can number the letters from which comes the multitude of words. When one letter is set in various places, and is valid according to the place, it is not valid for one thing. What is so different a thing as God and the devil? Yet at the head there is the letter D when we say "Deus" [God], and when we say "diabolus" [devil]. Just as, then, a letter is valid according to the place, but he errs and is too absurd and within a childish heart who, when he has read, for example, the letter D in the name of God, fears to put it in the name of the devil, lest he do God an injury as it were—so too one who hears the divine Scriptures unskillfully (not to depart from this very example), when he hears, for example, a river set in allegory in the place where it is said, *The rush of the river makes glad the city of God*[^5]—now this was said of the flood of the Holy Spirit, of which in another place the prophet says: *They will be inebriated by the abundance of your house, and you will give them to drink from the torrent of your delight*[^6]—when, then, he has taken the river in a good sense and praised it and been delighted by it, when, according to the place, it is told him that the river signifies men who flow and are given over to temporal things, passing away with the love of passing things, he is dismayed. Because in another place he had taken the river to signify something in a good sense, he is disturbed. So he becomes mute in the Scriptures, just as one is made mute in letters if he refuses to transfer those letters to other words, but holds them only in those words in which he first learned them.

**7** If your Holiness has grasped this, a thing most useful, as we judge, has been told you, one that may help you much, not only to hear our treatments, but even to understand the Scriptures themselves, of which we treat these things for you. The river, then, from which David took the five stones at that time, did not signify anything good at that time. I know indeed that it can occur to some that the river signified something good too, as if someone wanted to understand baptism, so that the stones taken up from baptism—that is, baptized men—are most strong against the devil, who was signified by Goliath. Yet on account of the number five the reasoning holds for us, because we had said that the law is signified by the number five, on account of the five books of Moses. What does it signify that they were taken from the river and put in the shepherd's vessel? We have already said: at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, that the devil might truly be conquered, the law passes over to grace. What signifies grace so well as an abundance of milk? But those stones were taken up from the river. The river signified a flowing people, given over to temporal things, loving passing things, and running down with the rush of desire into the sea of this world, such as was the old people of the Jews. They had received the law, but they trod the law underfoot and passed over the law and were borne into the sea, as a river over those stones. For those stones had not made a boundary for the river, so as to halt the river. For if it were so, they would signify the restraint of the law, and those who, when they had begun to flow with their pleasures and desires, on coming to the precepts of the law, halt and curb the rush of their lusts. But those stones were not so, but were in the river, over which the water passed. As the transgressing people passed over the law. Thence, then, the Lord took the law over to grace—that is, took it from the river and put it in the shepherd's vessel.

**8** Whoever, then, wants to fulfill the law, let him think on grace. Therefore those ten precepts of the psaltery of ten strings are the very ones that were in that old people, but the ten precepts oppressed that people with fear. For in them there was not love, which is through grace, but there was fear. The precepts of the Lord were penal to that people, because they could not be fulfilled by love. They tried, but were overcome by desire. When, then, anyone has made the passage to grace, he fulfills not other precepts, but those same ones that could not be fulfilled by this fear are fulfilled by this love. Yet it is not the force of the precepts, but the force of the grace of God. For if it were of the precepts of the law, the law too would fulfill them. He who passes over to Christ passes from fear to love, and begins now to be able, by love, to do what by fear he could not. And he who trembled in fear does not tremble in love. This he sings on the ten precepts, because David signifies the man who passes over to grace when he says, *On a psaltery of ten strings I will sing to you*[^7]—now to sing on the precepts, that is, to fulfill the precepts cheerfully.

**9** And that you may know, brothers, that grace fulfills this, no one ought to presume on his own strength. For this is to presume on the grace of God. For God calls you and commands that you act, but he himself gives the strength, that what he commands may be able to be fulfilled. But by you a receptive faith must be applied, that by the flood of grace you may humble yourself, supplicate God, presume nothing of yourself, despoil yourself of Goliath, put on David. To this pertains what is said in the same psalm, which we had already begun to recall: *What is man?*[^8] For this warns man not to presume of himself. Now see how he chants against Goliath, who presumed of himself; and he commends to you David, who, weak among men, was most strong in God. *What is man?* And he says what man is: *That you have made yourself known to him*[^9]. This is the whole man, if God becomes known to him. But if God does not become known to him, man is nothing. What is the man to whom God has not become known? *Man has been likened to vanity; his days pass like a shadow*[^10]. Therefore, *What is man, that you have made yourself known to him; and the son of man, that you esteem him?*[^11] What is *you esteem him*? It pleased you to choose him and to set him in some higher and more eminent place. This is of your mercy, not of his merits.

**10** Seek what is man's own, you will find sin. Seek what is man's own, you will find a lie. Take away sin, and whatever you consider in man is God's. Let man, then, not love what is his own. To this too can pertain what the Apostle says: *Let no one seek what is his own*[^12]. For sometimes men hear that from the readers, and are built up to carry off what belongs to others. It matters who says to you, "Do not seek what is your own." For sometimes it is said by an evil persuader, sometimes it is said by a good teacher. God is a good teacher. When, then, you hear from God, "Do not seek what is your own," do not take it as it is usually said. It is something good of which God warns you. As we were saying, seek what is yours, you will find sin. Do not, then, seek sin, and you do not seek what is yours. Do not seek a lie, and you do not seek what is yours. For truth is from God, the lie from you.

**11** And if the devil sometimes suggests something, he holds the one who consents, he does not force the unwilling. For he does not seduce or drag anyone, except one whom he has found already in some part like himself. For he finds him desiring something, and the desire opens the door to the entering suggestion of the devil. He finds him fearing something, and warns him to flee what he found him fearing. He warns him to obtain what he found him desiring. And through these two doors of desire and fear he enters. Shut them, and you fulfill that saying of the Apostle in today's reading: *Do not give place to the devil*[^13]. For there the Apostle wished to show that, although the devil enters and takes possession, yet the man gave him place that he might be able to enter.

**12** Therefore, because man is nothing to whom God has not become known and whom God does not esteem, he gives him his grace, finding in him what to condemn and granting all things to the one who confesses, that he may crown the one who believes. For what did the Lord find in men when he came, except what to condemn? Altogether, brothers, consider and see: whether in that people of the Israelites or in the gentiles, he found nothing except what to condemn. And therefore he wished to come to sinners as humble, not as judge, when he spares them, so that first he might dispense mercy by forgiving sins, and so afterward render severity by punishing sins. Let us not abuse—that is, not use badly—his mercy, and we shall not feel his severity. This, then, is the whole of man: that God becomes known to him, that he gives him his grace, in which David presumed. But Goliath, proud of himself, of his own strength, lifted up, puffed up, first placed the whole victory of his entire side in himself alone. And because all pride has shamelessness of forehead, by the stone coming into his very forehead he was thrown down. The forehead that had the shamelessness of his pride was emptied, and the forehead that had the humility of the cross of Christ conquered.

**13** Therefore we too carry that very sign of the cross on the forehead. Who understands it? This I say, brothers, because many do it and refuse to understand it. God seeks a doer of his signs, not a painter. If you carry on your forehead the sign of the humility of Christ, carry in your heart the imitation of the humility of Christ. But we said, brothers, that the one who opens to the devil the doors of desire or fear gives place to him. But desire of what, or fear of what? For we both desire the kingdom of heaven and fear hell. But just as those doors—desire of temporal things and fear of temporal punishments—often drag toward wickedness and give place to the devil, so desire of eternal things and fear of eternal punishments makes place in the heart for the word of God.

**14** Briefly, then, brothers: if we want to live well, let us love what God promises more than what this world promises. And let us fear what God threatens more than what this world threatens. Is what we have said anything great or long? A temptation to some fraud comes to you. You want to commit a fraud to acquire money. God promises the everlasting kingdom of heaven to those who commit no fraud. Desire for money conquers you. For who is there who does not want the kingdom of heaven? But to want earthly things more, this is to sin; to want present things more, not to believe what is to come; to want more what man sees and not to long for what God promises—when what man sees can even be taken from his eyes, can be lost even when possessed, but what God promises cannot for now be seen by the eye of the flesh, and when anyone has arrived at God's promises he does not fear lest he lose them, because no one is more powerful than the one who gave them. And so, brothers, cling by love to the promises of God, and the desires of the world will not overcome you.

**15** Again a temptation of fear comes. Someone says to you: "Speak false testimony for me." First he promises. But when he has not deceived—if perhaps you set the promises of God before the promises of men—desire does not conquer. Through threatening he tempts and begins to threaten horrible things. He is perhaps powerful in the city, powerful in the world. He seems able to do what he threatens. The fear of a present evil conquers you—an evil that God could surely turn away from you, if this seemed to him to profit you; and if he were unwilling to turn it away, you ought to understand that he would not permit it to happen to you unless he knew that this too profited you. The same God turned away the fire from the three boys. Was God changed because he did not turn away the sword from the martyrs? The same God was the God of the three boys who was the God of the Maccabees. Those escaped the fire, those were tormented by fire. Yet both conquered in the eternal God. For neither were the former delighted by this temporal life, nor were the latter broken by temporal threats.

**16** And so do not fear the man who threatens you. For *what is man? He has been likened to vanity; his days pass like a shadow*[^14]. Either he will not harm you, and that shadow will pass before his sting could pass to you; *for God is powerful*[^15]. Or, if he is permitted to harm, he will harm for a time your shadow—that is, your transitory thing, your temporal life, your old life. For until the very end of death we carry something of the old man. He can harm your temporal life; your eternal life no one can take from you. He will take from you the impediments by which you are held here. And you will cling to God, to whom you had already bound yourself in love, with hope sent ahead.

**17** Therefore in the psalms it is most elegantly said of the evil man: *Like a sharp razor you have wrought deceit*[^16]. Thus the Spirit of God insults him. What does it observe in the razor? Not that men can be killed by a razor, but for what purpose the razor was made. It was made to shave hairs. What is so superfluous in the body as hairs? With what eagerness, with what zeal, with what care, with what attention it is sharpened, to shave a hair. So too the evil man draws himself aside, thinks, rethinks, devises, lays fraud upon fraud, seeks a machination, prepares ministers, procures false witnesses, sharpens the razor. What will he do to the just man, except shave off the superfluous?

**18** And so, brothers, if you want to be ready to follow the will of God—what we say to you, we say first to ourselves, or rather he who securely speaks says it to all—if we want to be ready to follow the will of God, let us not love these things that pass away, let us not think that to be happiness itself which is called such in this world. For this is what those foreigners thought. They placed all happiness in temporal things, all sweetness in a shadow, not in light itself, not in truth itself. Indeed, in this psalm, which is to Goliath, attend to the latter parts of the psalm. With words altogether most lucid and with a most unknotted discourse that seeks no interpreter or expositor, but by the mercy of God they are so set down that no one may say, "See, this man said it as he wished, and interpreted it according to his own wit, took it as he wished"—they are so set down that no one may excuse himself. They are set down by David speaking—that is, by the new life, the life of Christ, the life given to us through Christ—insulting the old life, the old happiness of men, both those who place their hope in it and those who obtain it and rejoice in it.

**19** For the just seem to labor in this world, and the unjust to live happily in this world. And as though God slept, neglecting human affairs, the latter are often lifted up by impunity, the former are often broken by weakness, and they think it profits them nothing that they live well, because they do not have those things in which sinners, criminals, and impious men seem to abound. And as long as they ask such things from God to be granted them as something great, so long they err. And one must beware lest they be given into the power of their own desire. For it is said: *God gave them up to the desires of their heart*[^17]. And God is more favorable when he does not hear, so as to grant, the one asking for superfluous and trifling things, but hears, so as to heal by not granting. For why men seek these things, who does not see? To consume them in their luxuries, in trifles, in the most insane spectacles—men seek from God to have these things.

**20** Give me a man of the world; let him ask riches from God, let them be given, and see him follow innumerable snares of his death. From them he oppresses the poor man, the mortal man takes pride over the man his equal, he seeks empty honors from men. And to obtain them he puts on for them the games of wickedness, the games of evil desire. He buys games and bears, he gives his goods to beast-fighters, while Christ hungers in the poor. What need is there to say more, brothers? Consider yourselves what we pass over in silence—how many evils men do with superfluous things, when the increase of them has fallen to them. Is it not better, when a man is such that he could so use the abundance of present things, that God should take it from him, not give it to him? Is it not then mercy?

**21** And he will say: "I have done well and have carried off nothing of another's, and you did not hear me. From what I have I give to the needy, I take from no one. From you I ask; do you give." As though he gives you an estate unless another loses an estate. If it were said to you, "Sell your estate," you shudder as at a curse, you think an injury has been done you. You keep hatred in your breast, because you heard this from a man, to sell your estate—as if you could buy unless another sells. And so, what you greatly desire to buy and wish to buy, if it is evil to sell, you seek evil for another. It is good to find a little bag of coins on the road, and when you have found it, you say, "God gave it to me"—as though you could find it unless another lost it. Why, then, do you not wish for the goods of those treasures, which all can possess with you without distress? You desire gold, desire justice. You cannot have gold unless another loses it. You both embrace justice, and you are both enlarged.

**22** Let us return, then, to the psalm, that your Charity may understand that those are foreigners who think there is no happiness except the present. But do you judge yourself worthy that God should give these things to you too? Seek how you may use them. If he did not give them, know that it profits you that the good Father does not give them. Because your son too, when he weeps to have you give him a beautiful little knife with a gilded handle, let him weep as much as he wants, you do not give him that by which he may be hurt. *Lord, deliver me from the hand of the sons of foreigners, whose mouth has spoken vanity, and their right hand is the right hand of iniquity*[^18]. And he expounds what vanity he means, and what right hand. For by the right hand of iniquity he means the happiness of this world. Not that it is not found among the good, but the good, when they have it, have it in the left hand, not in the right. They have perpetual happiness in the right hand; they have temporal happiness in the left. But desire of eternal things and of eternal happiness ought not to be mixed with desire of temporal things—that is, of present and temporal happiness. And this is: *Let not your left hand know what your right hand does*[^19]. Therefore: *Their right hand is the right hand of iniquity*.

**23** Hear now how they have spoken vanity, and how they have the right hand of iniquity. Let us all hear; it profits you. Let us hear, lest you say you did not hear, because it was said to the servant: "You should give, and I would demand." And we said yesterday that we servants are the ones who give; there is another who demands. Our sisters, unwilling to hear, are as it were unwilling to endure the exactor. Without cause, my brothers, let no one flatter himself in this. It is one thing not to have received, another to have refused to receive. He who refuses the gift of God is held guilty of that very refusal. For just as it was said to the steward-servant, "Why did you not give?" so it will be said to the servant for whom the other was appointed to dispense, "Why did you not receive?" If there was no one to give, you will excuse yourself. But if the readers sound out, even when the treaters are silent—and everywhere the word of God is preached, and it is truly said: *Their sound went out into all the earth*[^20], and the heat of the word of God is spread everywhere, *nor is there one who can hide himself from its heat*[^21]—there will be nothing to say at the judgment of God. Brothers, let us hear and do. If we want to have hope, let us not excuse ourselves. Often a beggar asking for a single coin chants God's precepts to you at your door.

**24** Let us hear, then: *Whose mouth has spoken vanity, and their right hand is the right hand of iniquity*[^22]. See the happiness of the world, in which those placed their hope who spoke vanity, and whose right hand is of iniquity. For thus he begins to say: *Whose sons are like new plants firmly set*[^23]. The happiness is permitted. He did not here say frauds, perjuries, robberies, crimes. He spoke of the happiness, as it were, of the innocent. And if this is to be despised, how are those to be lamented who even commit robberies, who commit frenzies, crimes, murders, adulteries, and the rest which even worldly happiness itself condemns?

**25** See what sort of man he wants the man of the new life to be, what sort of man he wants the man who belongs to the shepherd's vessel, and to the grace of God, and to the milk by which we are nourished. Attend now: *Whose sons are like new plants firmly set; their daughters adorned like the likeness of a temple*[^24]. Perhaps for this reason the sisters were unwilling to hear. Let them hear, then, willing or unwilling, and learn to come to the Lord's house, not in the pride of Goliath but in the humility of David. For do these things need expounding? Are they obscure? Men speak vanity, and they are called foreigners. They do not belong to the inheritance of Christ, to the kingdom of the one to whom we say: *Our Father*. They are reckoned foreigners. And what happiness do they speak of? *Their sons are like new plants firmly set*, as it were shoot of shoot. "He has many sons, many grandsons; he is secure against the chances of death"—as though one chance does not often carry off thousands of men. *Their sons are like new plants firmly set*. See, suppose *the sons are like new plants firmly set*. Does not the fire of the woods sometimes consume even the new plants near the woods? *Their daughters adorned like the likeness of a temple*. Let us pass quickly from here. The modesty of women must be consulted. Let them rather, by having it, know what they have, which we blush to mention. *Their daughters* are adorned like the likeness of a temple. Their storehouses full, *overflowing from this into that*[^25], as we say of those who abound: "He has nowhere to put it, he does not know what he has." One storehouse is filled and the fruits overflow; possessions overflow, the storehouses overflow from this into that.

**26** Their *sheep fruitful, multiplying in their goings forth*[^26]. Few enter; they give birth, and go out many: multiplying in their goings forth. Last year there were so many, this year there are so many. There is joy and exultation. Goliath swells, and proudly challenges to combat in this happiness: "Who can do anything against me? Who dares against me?" Do not men to whom these things abound say this, if not daily each one feels it in himself? He has something more than his neighbor. Does he not say: "Who can do anything against me? Or if this neighbor does me an injury, do I not show him?" See whether he is not Goliath challenging to combat. But David goes forth, naked of warlike arms, armed with a few stones. He will lay low all pride—that is, the just man; as the martyrs did, they laid low the unjust. And at the very time they seemed to be victors, they themselves were being conquered, when in them their leader the devil was overcome.

**27** But attend to that happiness: *Their sheep multiplying in their goings forth, their oxen fat. There is no fallen wall*[^27]. For a fence is usually a wall. *There is no fallen wall, nor breach*. All whole, all perfect, all full. *Nor outcry in their streets*: no quarrels, no tumults. See what happiness, as it were, of the innocent he describes, lest anyone say to himself: "But he said this of those who carry off others' goods." Nothing was said here of this; elsewhere there is mention of such men. For it is plain that the criminal are to be punished. And from this they ought to understand what punishment awaits them, when anyone innocent, when he uses these things proudly and immoderately, is reprobated by God and reckoned among the sons of foreigners. For did not that rich man too seek others' fruits, *to whom his region succeeded in fruits*, and when he burned, having nowhere to gather his worldly fruits, and did not see the poor in whom he might treasure for himself in heaven: *I will destroy*, he said, *my barns, and make new and larger ones, and fill them*[^28]. From what, except his own fruits? *And I will say to my soul: You have many goods, be satisfied. But God said to him: Fool, this night your soul will be taken from you. The things you have prepared, whose will they be?*[^29] Just as, then, in the Gospel, brothers, a man rejoicing over temporal happiness was insulted—although his happiness was from his own field, not from others' robberies—so in this psalm temporal happiness is insulted, that the soul renewed and regenerated by the grace of the milk may learn to long for that perpetual and everlasting blessedness. Therefore see how he connects it: *Whose sons are like new plants firmly set. Their daughters adorned like the likeness of a temple. Their storehouses full, overflowing from this into that. Their sheep fruitful, multiplying in their goings forth; their oxen fat. There is no fallen wall, nor breach, nor outcry in their streets. They called the people blessed for whom these things are*[^30]. But who called them so? *Those whose mouth has spoken vanity*. For above they were described.

**28** But what do you say? For those *called the people blessed for whom these things are*. What do I say? *Blessed the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^31]. Therefore that is the blessed people which, in place of its sons and adorned daughters, in place of the fatness of oxen, the fruitfulness of sheep, the fullness of storehouses, the soundness of buildings, peace and freedom from quarrels and civil disputes—in place of all this happiness, wills to possess its God, that it may have him in place of all things who founded all things, and say: *But for me to cling to God is good*[^32]. Let it worship him freely. Let it worship him when he gives these things, and when he takes them away, and when he does not give them, and let it fear nothing so much as that he should take himself away. And so the Christian people, brothers, who say in their heart, "Let him take away whatever he wills, let him not take himself from me," *blessed is the people whose God is the Lord himself*.
