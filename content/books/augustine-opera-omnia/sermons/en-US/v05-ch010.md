# Sermon 156

**1. 1.** The loftiness of the word of God exercises study, it does not deny understanding. For if everything were closed, there would be nothing whereby obscure things might be revealed. Again, if everything were uncovered, there would be nothing from which the soul might receive nourishment and have the strength by which it could knock at the closed things. In the earlier apostolic readings, which we expounded to your Charity as far as the Lord deigned to help, we suffered much labor and anxiety. We had compassion on you, and were anxious both for ourselves and for you. But as far as I reckon, the Lord helped both us and you; and the things that seemed utterly most difficult he deigned to untangle through us in such a way that no question remained which might disturb a devout mind. For an ungodly mind hates even understanding itself; and a person, sometimes with a too perverse mind, fears to understand, lest he be compelled to do what he has understood. Of such the Psalm says, *They would not understand, that they might do good*[^1]. But you, dearly beloved, because it is good to think well of you, demand understanding; God demands fruit. For *good understanding*, as it is written, *belongs to all who do it*[^2]. Yet this which remains and was recited today, although it does not have such difficulty as the earlier passages had, which we have now, as we could, with the Lord's help, carried across, nevertheless requires your attention; for it is, as it were, a conclusion, on account of the things that were said in the earlier readings, where there was labor lest perchance the Apostle be made guilty, as it were, of all sins by saying, *For I do not do what I will*[^3]. Then, lest the law should seem either able to suffice for a man having free choice, even if no further divine help were extended, or be believed to have been given in vain, the reason too was stated why the law was given: because it too was given as a help, but not as grace is.

**2. 2.** For it was given, as we have already expounded—and you ought to hold it, and we ought to commend it to you more vehemently and diligently—it was given that man might find himself out, not that the disease might be healed, but that, the disease growing through transgression, a physician might be sought. And who is this physician, but the one who said, *Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^4]? Whoever, then, does not confess the Creator, denies in his pride the Author. But whoever denies his own sickness judges the Savior superfluous. Therefore, both in our nature let us praise the Creator, and on account of the vice which we inflicted on ourselves, let us seek the Savior. And how do we seek the Savior? That he may give a law? It is too little: *For if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would surely be from the law*[^5]. If, then, a law was not given that could give life, why was it given? It follows, and he shows why it was given: because even so it was given as a help, lest you should think yourself healthy. *If*, then, *a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would surely be from the law*. And as though we asked, Why, then, was it given? *But Scripture*, he says, *has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^6]. When you hear of the one who promises, wait for the one who fulfills. Human nature was fit, through free choice, to wound itself; but, now wounded and injured, it is not fit, through free choice, to heal itself. For if you wish to live intemperately so as to fall sick, you do not need a physician for this; you suffice for your own ruin. But when, acting intemperately, you have begun to be sick, you cannot free yourself from the sickness in the way you were able, through intemperance, to plunge yourself into the sickness. And yet the physician prescribes temperance even to the healthy. The good physician does this; he does not wish to be necessary to the sick. So too the Lord God deigned to prescribe temperance to man, created without vice; which if he had kept, he would not afterward have desired a physician for his disease. But because he did not keep it, he became feeble, he fell; being feeble, he created feeble ones—that is, being feeble, he begot feeble ones. And yet in all who are born feeble God works what is good, forming the body, giving life to the body, providing nourishment, giving his rain and his sun upon the good and the evil; there is no reason why either the good or the evil should accuse the good. Moreover, he was unwilling to leave the human race, condemned by his just judgment, in everlasting destruction: but he sent also a physician, sent a Savior, sent one who would heal freely; it is too little to say one who would heal freely—who would even give a reward to those healed. Nothing can be added to this benevolence. Who is there who says: Let me heal you, and I give you a reward? He did most excellently. For he knew that he, rich, had come to the poor: and he heals the sick, and gives to the healed, and gives nothing other than himself. The Savior is the help of the feeble, the same Savior is the prize of the healed.

**3. 3.** *Therefore, brothers*, of which you were admonished today, *we are debtors not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh*[^7]. For to this we have been helped, for this we have received the Spirit of God, for this also in our labors we ask daily help. The law, by threatening the one who does not fulfill what it commands, makes him be under itself; these are under the law, not under grace. *The law is good, if one uses it lawfully*[^8]. What, then, is it to use the law lawfully? Through the law to recognize one's own disease, and to seek divine help for health. Because, as I have said, and it must often be said: *If the law could give life, righteousness would surely be from the law*[^9], and no Savior would be sought, nor would Christ come, nor would he seek with his blood the lost sheep. For so the same Apostle says in another place, *For if righteousness is through the law, then Christ died in vain*[^10]. What, then, is the usefulness of the law, and what its help? That *Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe. And so the law*, he says, *was our pedagogue in Christ Jesus*[^11]. From this comparison, attend to the matter of which I speak. The pedagogue does not lead the boy to himself, but to the master; but when the boy, well instructed, has grown up, he will not be under the pedagogue.

**4. 4.** Treating of these matters, the Apostle in another place too (for he commends this very constantly; but would that not to the deaf), he commends this constantly, commending faith to the gentiles, because by faith they obtain help to fulfill the law—not through the law, but obtaining through faith the strength to fulfill it; for this the Apostle constantly says and commends these things, on account of the Jews, who gloried in the law, and thought the law sufficient for their own free choice; and through this, because they thought the law sufficient for their own free choice, *being ignorant of the righteousness of God*—that is, the righteousness given by God from faith—*and wishing to establish their own*, as if fulfilled by their own strength, not obtained by crying faith, *they have not submitted to the righteousness of God*, as he says. *For the end of the law is Christ*, he says, *unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^12]. So when he treats of this, he raised an objection to himself: *What, then, of the law*? As if: What is the usefulness of the law? He answered, *It was placed for the sake of transgression*. This is what he says elsewhere, *The law crept in, that the offense might abound*. And there what did he add? *But where the offense abounded, grace abounded the more*[^13]. Because in the lighter sickness the help of medicine was despised: the disease grew, and a physician was sought. *What, then, of the law? It was placed for the sake of transgression*; whereby the neck of the proud might be humbled—of those who ascribe much to themselves, and arrogate so much to their own will that they think their free choice able to suffice for righteousness: which free choice, when it was in entire liberty, that is, in paradise, showed its strength, showed how much it could do, but for falling, not for rising. *The law*, then, *was placed for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom it was promised, ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator*[^14].

**5. 5.** *But a mediator is not of one; God, however, is one*[^15]. What is, *A mediator is not of one*? Because a mediator is surely between two. If God is one, and a mediator is not of one; between what and God do we seek a mediator? Because *a mediator is not of one, but God is one*. Between what and what the mediator is, we find by the Apostle himself saying, *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^16]. If you did not lie fallen, you would not have a mediator as necessary; but because you lie fallen and cannot rise, God as it were extended to you his arm as a mediator. *And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed*[^17]? Let no one, then, say: Since we are not under the law, but under grace, therefore let us sin, therefore let us do what we wish. Whoever says this loves sickness, not health. Grace is medicine. Whoever wishes always to be sick is ungrateful to the medicine. *Therefore, brothers*, the help having been received, the divine aid extended to us from above, the arm of the Lord, and the help, the Holy Spirit, extended to us by that arm of the Lord, *we are debtors not to the flesh, to walk according to the flesh*[^18]. Because faith cannot work well except through love. For this is the faith of the faithful, lest it be the faith of demons; for the demons too believe, and tremble[^19]. That, then, is the praiseworthy faith, that is the true faith of grace, which works through love[^20]. But that we may have love, and from it may be able to have good action, can we give it to ourselves, when it is written, *The love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^21]? Love is so much the gift of God that God is called love, the apostle John saying, *God is love, and he who abides in love abides in God, and God in him*[^22].

**6. 6.** *Therefore, brothers, we are debtors not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh. For if you live according to the flesh, you will die*[^23]. Not because the flesh is evil: for it too is a creation of God, and created by the same one by whom the soul too was; neither this nor that is a part of God, but both this and that are a creation of God. The flesh, then, is not evil; but to live according to the flesh is evil. God is supremely good, because he supremely is, who says, *I am who am*[^24]. God, then, is supremely good; the soul is a great good, but not the supreme good. But when you hear, God is supremely good, do not suppose it said of God the Father only, but of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. For this Trinity is one, and is one God, and is supremely good. So plainly is God one that when you are asked about that Trinity, you answer this: lest perhaps, when you have heard, God is one, you suppose that the Father himself is the Son, that he himself is the Holy Spirit. It is not so: but he who is the Father in that Trinity is not the Son; he who is the Son in that Trinity is not the Father; he who is the Holy Spirit in that Trinity is neither the Son nor the Father, but the Spirit of the Father and likewise the Spirit of the Son. For he is the one Holy Spirit of both the Father and the Son, coeternal with the Father and the Son, consubstantial, equal. This whole Trinity is one God, supremely good. But the soul, as I said, is created by the supreme good, yet is not the supreme good, but a great good. Likewise the flesh is neither the supreme good nor a great good, but nevertheless a small good. The soul, then, is a great good, but not the supreme good; living between the supreme good and the small good—that is, between God and the flesh, lower than God, higher than the flesh—why does it not live according to the supreme good, but live according to the small good? This is said more plainly: Why does it not live according to God, but live according to the flesh? For it is a debtor not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh. The flesh ought to live according to it, not it according to the flesh. Let it live according to that by which it lives. Surely let each thing live according to that whence it lives. Whence does your flesh live? From your soul. Whence does your soul live? From your God. Let each of these live according to its own life. For the flesh is not its own life; but the soul is the life of the flesh. The soul is not its own life; but God is the life of the soul. The soul, then, which ought to live according to God—for it is not a debtor to the flesh, to live according to the flesh: that, then, which ought to live according to God, if it lives according to itself, fails; does it live according to the flesh and prosper? But then the flesh lives rightly according to the soul, if the soul lives according to God. For if the soul, I do not say according to the flesh, but according to itself, as I said, should wish to live; I am going to tell you what it is to live according to itself: for it is good that you know this, and very wholesome.

**7. 7.** There were philosophers of this age, some of whom thought there was no happiness except to live according to the flesh, and placed the good of man in the pleasure of the body. These philosophers were called Epicureans, from a certain Epicurus, their author and master, and those others like them. But there were others, proud, as it were removing themselves from the flesh and placing all the hope of their happiness in their own soul, who placed the highest good in their own virtue. The affection of devotion in you has recognized the voice of the Psalm: you know, you are aware, you have recognized how they were mocked in the holy Psalm: *Those who trust in their own virtue*[^25]. Such were the philosophers who were called Stoics. Those living according to the flesh, these living according to the soul; neither those nor these living according to God. Therefore, when the apostle Paul had come to the city of the Athenians, where these disciplines of the philosophers were fervent with zeal and contention, as is read in *the Acts of the Apostles*—where I rejoice to anticipate our sermon by your recognizing and recalling, as it is written there, *Some of the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers conferred with him*[^26]: those living according to the flesh conferred with him, those living according to the soul conferred with him; he, living according to God, conferred with them. The Epicurean said: To enjoy the flesh is good for me. The Stoic said: To enjoy my own mind is good for me. The Apostle said: *But to cleave to God is good for me*. The Epicurean said: Happy the man whose enjoyment is the pleasure of his flesh. The Stoic said: Rather, happy the man whose enjoyment is the virtue of his soul. The Apostle said: *Happy the man whose hope is the name of the Lord*[^27]. The Epicurean errs: for it is false that the man is happy whose enjoyment is the pleasure of his flesh. The Stoic too is deceived: for it is false, and utterly most lying, that the man is happy whose enjoyment is the virtue of his soul. *Happy*, then, *the man whose hope is the name of the Lord*. And because those are vain and lie: *And he has not looked*, it says, *upon vanities and lying follies*[^28].

**8. 8.** *Therefore, brothers, we are debtors not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh*, like the Epicureans. But even if the soul should wish to live according to itself, it will be carnal; it savors the flesh, it does not rise from the flesh. For there is no way for one to rise from there who does not take hold of the arm extended to him as he lies fallen. *For if you live according to the flesh*. For where it was said, *What can man do to me*? there it was said, *What can the flesh do to me*[^29]? *For if you live according to the flesh, you will die*. Not by that death, when one goes out of the body; for by that death you will die even if you live according to the spirit: but by that death of which the Lord in the Gospel terribly says, *Fear him who has power to destroy both soul and body in the hell of fire*[^30]. *If*, then, *you live according to the flesh, you will die*.

**9. 9.** *But if by the spirit you put to death the actions of the flesh, you will live*[^31]. This is our work in this life, to put to death the actions of the flesh by the spirit; daily to afflict, lessen, bridle, slay them. For how many things, as we make progress, no longer delight us which delighted us before? When, then, it delighted and was not consented to, it was being put to death; because it now no longer delights, it has been put to death. Trample the dead, pass on to the living; trample the one lying fallen, struggle with the one resisting. For one delight is dead, but another lives: and that one, while you do not consent, you put to death; when it begins not to delight at all, you have put it to death. This is our action, this is our warfare. In this contest, when we struggle, we have God as spectator; in this contest, when we labor, we ask God as helper. For if he himself does not help us, we shall be able—I do not say to conquer—but not even to fight.

**9. 10.** So when the Apostle had said, *But if by the spirit you put to death the actions of the flesh, you will live*—that is, those covetings of the flesh, not to consent to which is great praise, not to have which is perfection: if you put to death by the spirit these diseased actions of the flesh, which have the struggle of death—you will live. Here now it must be feared lest anyone again presume upon his own spirit to put to death the actions of the flesh. For not only is God spirit: but your soul too is spirit, and your mind is spirit. And when you say, *I serve the law of God with the mind, but the law of sin with the flesh*[^32]; because *the spirit covets against the flesh, and the flesh against the spirit*[^33]. Therefore, lest you presume upon your own spirit to put to death the actions of the flesh, and perish by pride, and resistance be made to you, proud, and grace not be granted to you, humble; for God resists the proud, but to the humble he gives grace[^34].

**10. 10.** Lest, then, perchance this pride should arise in you, see what follows. For when he had said, *If by the spirit you put to death the actions of the flesh, you will live*, lest here the human spirit should exalt itself and boast itself fit and strong for this work, he added and said, *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*[^35]. Why, then, did you now wish to exalt yourself, where you heard, *If by the spirit you put to death the actions of the flesh, you will live*? For you were going to say: This my will can do, this my free choice can do. What will? What free choice? Unless he rules, you fall; unless he raises you up, you lie fallen. How, then, by your own spirit, when you hear the Apostle saying, *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*? Do you wish to lead yourself, to be led by yourself to putting to death the actions of the flesh? What does it profit you that you will not be an Epicurean, and will be a Stoic? Whether you are an Epicurean, or a Stoic, you will not be among the sons of God. *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*. Not those who live according to their own flesh, not those who live according to their own spirit; not those who are led by the pleasure of the flesh, not those who are led by their own spirit; but, *As many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*.

**11. 11.** Someone says to me: Then we are led, we do not lead. I answer: Rather, you both lead and are led; and then you lead well, if you are led by the good one. For the Spirit of God who leads you is a helper to you as you lead. The very name "helper" prescribes to you that you yourself too do something. Recognize what you ask; recognize what you confess, when you say, *Be my helper, do not forsake me*[^36]. Surely you call upon God as helper. No one is helped if nothing is done by him. *For as many*, he says, *as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*: not by the letter, but by the Spirit; not by the law commanding, threatening, promising, but by the Spirit exhorting, enlightening, helping. *We know*, says the same Apostle, *that for those who love God all things work together for good*[^37]. If you were not a worker, he would not be a co-worker.

**11. 12.** But here keep watch strongly, lest perchance your spirit say: If the cooperation of God and the help of God withdraws, my spirit does this; even if with labor, even if with some difficulty it can, yet it can fulfill it. As if someone should say: We arrived indeed by oars, but with some labor; oh, if we have wind, we arrive more easily. Not so is the help of God, not so is the help of Christ, not so is the help of the Holy Spirit. Plainly, if it is lacking, you will be able to do nothing good. You do indeed act with him not helping, by free will, but badly. For this your will, which is called free, is fit, and by acting badly becomes a condemnable slave. When I say to you, Without the help of God you do nothing, I say nothing good. For toward acting badly you have free will without the help of God; although that is not free. *For by whom anyone has been overcome, to him also has he been assigned as a slave*[^38]; and, *Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin*; and, *If the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^39].

**12. 13.** Believe this plainly, that you so act with a good will. Because you live, surely you act. For he is not a helper if you do nothing: for he is not a co-worker if you work nothing. Yet know that you so do good that the ruling Spirit is the helper; who, if he is lacking, you can do nothing good at all. Not as certain people have begun to say, who were at one time constrained to confess grace; and we bless God, because at least at one time they said this; for by coming nearer they will be able to make progress, and to arrive at that which is truly right. Already, then, they say that the grace of God is a help, for doing things more easily. For these are their words: "For this," they say, "God gave his grace to people, that what they are commanded to do through free choice they might be able to fulfill more easily through grace." By sail more easily, by oar more laboriously: yet one goes by oar too. By beast more easily, on foot more laboriously: but yet one arrives on foot too. It is not so. For the true Master, who flatters no one, deceives no one, the truthful teacher and likewise Savior, to whom the most troublesome pedagogue led us; when he spoke of good works—that is, of the fruits of the branches and shoots—did not say: Without me you can indeed do something, but more easily through me; did not say: You can do your fruit without me, but more abundantly through me. He did not say this. Read what he said: the Gospel is holy, the proud necks of all are subdued. It is not Augustine who says these things, it is the Lord who says them. What does the Lord say? *Without me you can do nothing*[^40]. Now then, when you hear, *As many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*, do not let yourselves down. For God does not so build his temple out of you as out of stones that have no motion of their own: they are lifted, they are placed by the builder. Living stones are not like this: *And you, like living stones, are built up into a temple of God*[^41]. You are led, but run also yourselves; you are led, but follow; because when you have followed, that will be true, that without him you can do nothing. *For it is not*, he says, *of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^42].

**13. 14.** Perhaps you were going to say: The law too suffices for us. The law gave fear: and see what the Apostle added on this, when he said, *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*; because when they are led by the Spirit of God, they are led by love—*for the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^43]—he went on to add, *For you have not received a spirit of slavery again unto fear*[^44]. What is, *again*? As under the terrifying, most troublesome pedagogue. What is, *again*? As on Mount Sinai you received a spirit of slavery. Someone says: The spirit of slavery is one, the spirit of liberty another. If it were another, the Apostle would not say, *again*. The same Spirit, then, but on the tablets of stone in fear, on the tablets of the heart in love. Now, the day before yesterday, you who were present heard how the voices, the fire, the smoke on the mountain terrified the people set far off[^45]; but how, when the Holy Spirit came—the same finger of God—on the fiftieth day after the shadow of the Passover, how it came and sat in fiery tongues upon each one of them[^46]. Now, then, not in fear, but in love; that we may be not slaves, but sons. For whoever still acts well only because he fears punishment does not love God, is not yet among the sons; but would that he at least fear punishment. Fear is a slave, love is free; and, so to speak, fear is the slave of love. Lest the devil possess your heart, let the slave go before in your heart, and keep the place for the mistress who is to come. Do, do it even from fear of punishment, if you cannot yet from love of righteousness. The mistress will come, and the slave will depart; because *perfect love casts out fear*[^47]. *For you have not received a spirit of slavery again unto fear*. It is the New Testament, not the Old. *The old things have passed away, and behold, all things have been made new; but all things are from God*[^48].

**14. 15.** In short, what follows? What, then, have we received—as though you were saying that: *But you have received the Spirit of the adoption of sons, in whom we cry: Abba, Father*[^49]. A master is feared, a father is loved. *You have received the Spirit of the adoption of sons, in whom we cry: Abba, Father*. This cry is of the heart, not of the throat, not of the lips; it sounds within, it sounds to the ears of God. With closed mouth and unmoving lips, Susanna cried out with this voice[^50]. *But you have received the Spirit of the adoption of sons, in whom we cry: Abba, Father*. Let the heart cry, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^51]. Why, then, not only *Father*? What does *Abba, Father* mean? For if you ask what *Abba* is, it is answered to you: *Father*. For "Abba" in Hebrew is "father." Why did the Apostle wish to put both? Because he saw the cornerstone, which the builders rejected, and it has become the head of the corner[^52]—not called the cornerstone without cause, except because it receives in a kiss each wall coming from a different direction. On one side circumcision, on the other uncircumcision, as far from each other and between themselves as they are far from the corner; but as near to the corner, so surely near to each other; but in the corner joined together between themselves. *For he himself is our peace, who made both one*[^53]. Therefore, on one side circumcision, on the other uncircumcision, the concord of the walls, the glory of the corner. *You have received the Spirit of the adoption of sons, in whom we cry: Abba, Father*.

**15. 16.** What a thing it is, if the pledge is such! Indeed, it is to be called not a pledge, but an earnest. For a pledge, when it is put down, is taken back when the thing itself has been returned. But an earnest is given out of the very thing that is promised to be given; so that when the thing is returned, what was given is completed, not changed. Let each one, then, attend to his own heart, whether from the inmost marrow of the heart and with sincere love he says, *Father*. It is not now asked how great that love itself is, whether great, or small, or middling; whether it even exists at all, I ask. If it is born, by lying hidden it grows, by growing it will be perfected, perfected it will remain. For when perfected it does not sink into old age, and from old age come to death; it will be perfected for this: that it may remain eternal. For see what follows. *We cry: Abba, Father. The Spirit himself gives testimony to our spirit that we are sons of God*[^54]. Not our spirit gives testimony to our spirit that we are sons of God; but the Spirit of God, the earnest, gives testimony for that thing which has been promised to us. *The Spirit himself gives testimony to our spirit that we are sons of God*.

**15. 17.** *But if sons, then heirs also*. For not as sons in vain. This is the reward: *And heirs*. This is what I was saying a little before, that our physician both gives us health, and deigns moreover to bestow a reward. What is that reward? An inheritance. But not as is the inheritance of a man, a father. For he leaves it to his sons, he does not possess it with his sons; and yet he makes much of himself, and desires that thanks be given to him, because he wished to give what he cannot take away. For would he take it with him as he died? I think that if he could, he would have left nothing here to his sons. The heirs of God are heirs in such a way that God himself is our inheritance, to whom the Psalm says, *The Lord is the portion of my inheritance*[^55]. *Heirs indeed of God*; if this is too little for you, hear, that you may rejoice the more: *Heirs indeed of God, but co-heirs of Christ*[^56]. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
