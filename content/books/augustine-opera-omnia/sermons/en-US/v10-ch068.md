# Sermon 381: On the Birthday of the Apostles Peter and Paul

**1.** Today is the day of the apostles Peter and Paul, on which, the devil defeated, they earned the triumphal crown, as far as the faith of Rome testifies. To those for whom a solemn festival is held, let a solemn sermon too be rendered. Let them hear praises from us, let them pour out prayers for us. As is held in memory, known by the tradition of the fathers, they did not suffer on one day, the cycle of the heavens running its course. On Peter's birthday, then, Paul suffered—not the day on which he was poured forth from his mother's womb into the number of men, but the day on which, from the bond of the flesh, he was born into the light of the angels; and through this, single days were given to the two in such a way that now one is celebrated for both. This seems to me a great sign of concord: the last to his fellow apostle, the first—he who came, called to the same day, ran, crowned, to the same day. The one was chosen before the Lord's passion, the other after the ascension. Unequal in the order of time, equal in the eternity of happiness: the one out of a fisherman, the other out of a persecutor. In the one were chosen the weak things of the world, that the strong might be confounded; in the other sin abounded, that grace might superabound. In both the great grace of God and his glory shone forth, who made their merits, did not find them. For what else did he deign to show, who first willed to call fishermen to the kingdom, afterward to call emperors, except that *he who glories should glory in the Lord*[^1]? For surely he did not despise the salvation of the noble, the learned, the powerful, before whom he set the lowborn, the unlearned, and the weak. But unless the cheapness of the weak were first chosen, the inflation of the proud would not be healed. If the rich were first called by Christ, they would think and say that nothing was chosen in them but their opulence, their fluency, the learning of their eloquence, the splendor of their knowledge, their nobility, their high birth, their tranquillity, their royal power; and thus, swollen with temporal and worldly felicities, as though they themselves first conferred on Christ what they were, so that he might seem to them to repay, not to bestow, what they were going to be by the grace of God, they would neither understand nor hold it. How much better, then, now, how much more orderly, that he first *raised up the needy from the earth, and lifted up the poor from the dunghill, that he might set him with the princes of his people*[^2]; so that the gift of understanding and teaching might not only be from God, but might also appear to be from God? With how great joy, therefore, and how great glory of God do we behold the riches of an emperor despised by the mind of a fisherman? the prayers of an emperor poured out at the memorial of a fisherman? so that neither would the one lie low because of what he did not have, nor would the other be proud because of what he had. And now, as for that which Christ wrought, that out of his persecutor Paul became his preacher—what it avails for the salvation of men, so that no one conscious of great iniquities should despair of the mercy of God—let the apostle himself rather speak. *It is a human saying*, he says, *and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first. But for this I obtained mercy, that in me first Christ Jesus might show all his longsuffering, as an example to those who would believe in him unto eternal life*[^3]. For who, under the hand of the almighty physician, would despair of salvation, instructed by so great an example, considering the one who evangelizes the faith which he once laid waste, not only to have escaped the punishment of a persecutor, but even to have earned the crown of a teacher, and the one whose blood he longed to shed by raging among his members, to have shed his own blood, by believing, for his name? Rome, therefore, the head of the nations, has two lights of the nations kindled by him who enlightens every man coming into this world: one, in whom God exalted cast-down lowliness; the other, in whom he healed iniquity that deserved condemnation. In the one let us learn not to be proud; in the other, not to despair. How briefly great examples have been set before us, how salutary; which let us always recall, and in their praise let us magnify the true light. Let no one, then, exalt himself because of the height of this world: Peter was a fisherman. Let no one, by thinking of his own iniquity, flee the mercy of God: Paul was a persecutor. The one says: *The Lord has been made a refuge for the poor*[^4]; the other says: *I will teach the unjust your ways, and the impious will turn to you*[^5].

[^1]: 1 Cor 1:31
[^2]: Ps 113:7-8 (112:7-8)
[^3]: 1 Tim 1:15-16
[^4]: Ps 9:9 (9:10)
[^5]: Ps 51:13 (50:15)
