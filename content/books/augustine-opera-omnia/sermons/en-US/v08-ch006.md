# Sermon 277/A: On the Birthday of Saint Vincent

**1.** Christ solemnly bids us to celebrate the most valiant and most glorious passion of the holy martyr Vincent, and not to proclaim it sluggishly. We have seen with the mind, and beheld in thought, how much he endured, what he heard, what he answered, and in a certain way a marvelous spectacle has been set before our eyes: an unjust judge, a bloody torturer, an unconquered martyr, a contest of cruelty and piety; and on this side madness, on that side victory. As the reading sounded in our ears, love blazed up in our hearts: we would wish, if it could be done, to embrace and kiss those torn limbs, which we marveled could endure such great punishments, and with an inexpressible affection we did not wish them to be tortured. For who would wish to see a raging executioner, and a man raging against a human body with his humanity lost? Whom would it please to behold limbs wrenched apart by the contrivance of the torment? Who would not be set against, who would not shudder at, the figure of nature seized by the art of cruelty, bones stretched apart, laid bare by furrowing? And yet all these things, even the horrible ones, the justice of the martyr made beautiful; and that wondrous fortitude itself, for the faith, for piety, for the hope of the age to come, for the love of Christ, suffused the foul and deadly face of the torments and wounds with the beauty of glory. In short, in that very one spectacle we divided our zeal from the persecutor. Him the martyr's punishment delighted, us his cause; him what he was suffering, us why he was suffering; him the torture, us the virtue; him the wounds, us the crown; him that he was held so very long in pains, us that he was in no way broken by the pains; him that he was tormented in the flesh, us that he remained in the faith. And so, where his savagery fed him, there the martyr's truth tortured him; but we scarcely bore the horrible things at his command, yet we conquered as Vincent grew faint.

**2.** Yet our wrestler himself was not the victor in himself or by his very self, but in him and through him who, exalted above all, supplies help, who, having suffered before all, left an example. He exhorts to the battle, who calls to the reward; and he so watches the one contending that he aids the one laboring. To his athlete he so prescribes what he is to do, and sets before him what he is to receive, that he even comes to his aid lest he fail. Let him pray simply, then, who wishes simply to contend, to overcome swiftly, to reign happily. We heard the fellow servant speaking, and convicting the persecutor's tongue with steadfast and truthful answers; but first we heard the Lord saying: *For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^1]. And through this he overcame his adversaries, because he praised his words in the Lord. He knew how to say: *In God I will praise the word, in the Lord I will praise the speech; in God I will hope, I will not fear what man may do to me*[^2]. We saw the martyr most patiently bearing monstrous torments: but his soul was subject to God, for from him was his endurance. And lest human frailty, failing through impatience, should deny Christ, and come to the joy of the enemy, he knew to whom he was saying: *My God, rescue me from the hand of the sinner, from the hand of the transgressor of the law and the unjust man; for you are my endurance*[^3]. For he who sang these things signified thus how a Christian ought to ask to be rescued from the power of his enemies—not, indeed, by suffering nothing, but by enduring most patiently the things that he suffers. *Rescue me from the hand of the sinner, from the hand of the transgressor of the law and the unjust man.* But if you ask how he wishes to be rescued, attend to what follows: *for you are my endurance.* There is the glorious passion, where this devout confession is: that *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^4]. Let no one, therefore, presume upon his own heart when he has completed his speech: let no one presume upon his own strength when he undergoes temptation; because, that we may speak good things, our wisdom is from him: that we may bear evil things, our endurance is from him. It is ours, then, to will, but, being called, we are required to will; it is ours to ask, but we do not know what to ask; it is ours to receive, but what shall we receive, if we do not have it? it is ours to have, but what do we have, if we do not receive? Therefore *let him who glories glory in the Lord.* Worthy, then, was the martyr Vincent to be crowned by the Lord, in whom he chose to glory both through wisdom and through endurance; worthy of solemn celebration, worthy of eternal happiness, for the obtaining of which whatever the most threatening judge could terrify with, whatever the bloody executioner inflicted, was light. For what he endured is past and gone; what he received will not pass away. For though his limbs were so torn, his inward parts so tortured, the torments so frequently and so cruelly repeated—so, so, just as these things were done, and even if much heavier things were done, *the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that will be revealed in us*[^5].

[^1]: Matt 10:20
[^2]: Ps 56:10-11 (55:11-12)
[^3]: Ps 71:4-5 (70:4-5)
[^4]: 1 Cor 1:31
[^5]: Rom 8:18
