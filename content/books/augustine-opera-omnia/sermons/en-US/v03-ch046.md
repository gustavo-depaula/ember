# Sermon 80: On the Words of the Gospel of Matthew 17:18-20: "Why Could We Not Cast It Out," and What Follows; on Prayer

**1.** Our Lord Jesus Christ rebuked unbelief even in his disciples, as we just now heard when the Gospel was read[^1]. For when they said: *Why could we not cast it out?* he answered: *Because of your unbelief*[^2]. If the Apostles are unbelieving, who is faithful? What do the lambs do, if the rams falter? Nor yet did the mercy of the Lord despise them as unbelieving; but he rebuked, nourished, perfected, crowned them. For they themselves too, mindful of their weakness, in a certain place in the Gospel, as we read, said to him: *Lord, increase our faith*[^3]. *Lord*, they say, *increase our faith.* The first usefulness was knowledge, to know what they lacked: the greater happiness, to know whence they asked. *Lord*, they say, *increase our faith.* See whether they did not carry their hearts as it were to a fountain, and knock that it be opened to them, whence they might fill them. He wished himself to be knocked at, not to repel those knocking, but to exercise those desiring.

**2.** For do you think, brothers, that God does not know what is necessary for you? He knows it, anticipating our desires, who knows our want. Finally, when he taught the prayer, and admonished his disciples not to be wordy in prayer: *Do not*, he says, *be wordy; for your Father knows what is necessary for you, before you ask him*[^4]. Now the Lord says another thing. What is it? Wishing that we not speak much in prayer, he said to us: *Do not speak much* when you pray; for *your Father knows what is necessary for you, before you ask him.* If our Father knows what is necessary for us before we ask him, why do we speak even a little? What is the reason for prayer itself, if our Father already knows what is necessary for us? He says to someone: Do not ask me at length; for I know what is necessary for you. If you know, Lord, why should I even ask? You do not wish me to have a long petition, rather you command that I have almost none. And where is that other place? He who says: *Do not speak much in prayer:* in another place says: *Ask, and it will be given to you?* And lest you think it was said to you in passing that you should ask, he added: *Seek, and you will find.* And lest you reckon this transitory, see what he added, see where he concluded: *Knock, and it will be opened to you*[^5]: see what he added. He wished you, that you may receive, to ask; that you may find, to seek; that you may enter, to knock. How then, since our Father already knows what is necessary for us, why do we ask? why do we seek? why do we knock? why do we weary ourselves in asking and seeking and knocking, to instruct one who knows? In another place are the Lord's words: *One must always pray, and not faint*[^6]. If one must always pray, how does he say: *Do not be wordy?* How do I pray *always*, who quickly finish? Here you command me to finish quickly, here you command me to *always pray, and not faint:* what is this? And that you may understand this, ask, seek, knock. For it is closed for this reason, not that it may spurn you, but that it may exercise you. So, brothers, we ought to exhort to prayer, both we and you. For there is no other hope in us amid the many evils of the present age, except to knock in prayer itself, to believe and hold fixed in the heart, that your Father does not give you this, which he knows is not expedient for you. For what you desire, you know; what profits you, he knows. Suppose you are under me—I say it—and weak, as it is true: for all this our life is a weakness; and a long life is nothing else than a long weakness: suppose, then, that you are sick under a physician. It delighted you to take something fresh, it delighted you to take a drink of wine, to ask it of the physician. You are not forbidden to ask, lest perhaps it not harm you, and it be expedient for you to receive. Do not hesitate to ask: ask, do not delay; but if you do not receive, do not be saddened. If this under a man, the physician of your flesh; how much more under God, the physician, creator, restorer both of your flesh and of your soul?

**3.** Accordingly, since in this chapter the Lord exhorted to prayer, where he said: *Because of your unbelief you could not cast out this demon:* for, exhorting to prayer, he thus concluded: *This kind is not cast out, except by fastings and prayers*[^7]. If a man prays, to cast out another's demon; how much more, to cast out his own avarice? how much more, to cast out his own drunkenness? how much more, to cast out his own luxury? how much more, to cast out his own uncleanness? How many things there are in a man which, if they persist, do not admit to the kingdom of heaven? See, brothers, how a physician is asked for temporal health, how, if anyone is desperately ill, is he ashamed, or loath, to hold the feet of a man, to wash with tears the footprints of a most skilled chief physician? And what, if the physician says to him: You cannot be healed otherwise, unless I bind you, cauterize you, cut you? He answers: Do what you wish, only heal me. With what ardor he desires the steamy health of a few days, so that for it he is willing both to be bound, and cut, and burned, and kept from eating what delights him, from drinking what delights him, nor when it delights him? He endures all, that he may die later: and he is unwilling to suffer a few things, that he may never die! If God, who is the heavenly physician over us, says to you: Do you wish to be healed? what will you say, except, To be healed? Perhaps you do not say it, because you think yourself well, which is to be more grievously ill.

**4.** For if you set up two sick men; one who weeping asks the physician, the other who in his sickness, with mind lost, mocks the physician; the physician promises hope to the weeping one, mourns over the laughing one. Why; except because he is so much the more dangerously ill, the more he thinks himself well? So too were the Jews. To the sick Christ came, he found all sick. Let no one flatter himself about health, lest the physician renounce him. He found all sick; it is the apostolic sentence: *For all have sinned, and need the glory of God*[^8]. When, then, he had found all sick, there were two kinds of sick men. Some came to the physician, clung to Christ, heard him, honored him, followed him, were converted. He received all without any disdain, going to heal them, who healed freely, because he cured by omnipotence. When, then, he received them, and joined them to himself to be healed, they exulted. But another kind of sick men, who by the sickness of iniquity had now lost their mind, and did not know they were sick; insulted him, because he received the sick, and said to his disciples: *Behold what kind of master yours is, who eats with sinners and publicans*[^9]. And he who knew what they were, and who they were, answered them: *The healthy do not need a physician, but those who are ill*[^10]. And he showed them who were healthy, and who sick: *I did not come*, he says, *to call the just, but sinners*[^11]. If, he says, sinners do not approach me, why did I come? for whom did I come? If all are healthy, why did so great a physician come down from heaven? why did he make for us a medicine not from his cupboard, but from his blood? So that kind of sick men who were more mildly ill, who felt that they were ill, clung to the physician, that they might be healed. But those who were more dangerously ill insulted the physician, slandered the sick. To what at last did their frenzy proceed? That they held the physician, bound him, scourged him, crowned him with thorns, hung him on the wood, killed him on the cross. Why do you wonder? The sick man killed the physician: but the physician killed healed the frenzied man.

**5.** For first, not forgetting his person on the cross, and showing us his patience, and giving an example of loving our enemies; seeing them rage around, he who knew their disease, because he was a physician, who knew the frenzy in which they had lost their mind, at once to the Father: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^12]. But do you think that those Jews were not malignant, savage, bloody, turbulent, enemies to the Son of God? Do you think that voice was empty, and was in vain: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do?* He saw all, but he knew that his own would be there. Finally he died, because so it was expedient, that by his death he might kill death. God died, that a kind of heavenly exchange might be made, that man might not see death. For Christ is God, but he did not die there where he is God. For the same is God, the same man: for there is one Christ, God and man. The man was assumed, that we might be changed for the better, it did not twist God to worse things. For he assumed what he was not, he did not lose what he was. When, then, he was God and man, wishing us to live from his own, he died from ours. For he had nothing from which he himself might die: but neither we anything from which we might live. For what was he, who had nothing from which he might die? *In the beginning was the Word; and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^13]. Seek of God whence he might die, you will not find it. But we die, who are flesh, a man of sin bearing the flesh of sin. Seek of sin whence it may live, it does not have it. So neither could he have death from his own, nor we life from our own: but we life from his, he death from ours. What kinds of exchanges! what did he give, and what did he receive? Trading men come to exchanges, to barter things. For the ancient exchange was the bartering of things. A man gave what he had, and received what he did not have. For example, he had wheat, but did not have barley; another had barley, and did not have wheat: that one gave the wheat which he had, received the barley which he did not have. How valuable was it that a greater quantity should make up for a cheap kind? Behold, then, another gives barley, that he may receive wheat: finally another gives lead, that he may receive silver; but he gives much lead against a little silver: another gives wool, that he may receive a garment. And who enumerates all? Yet no one gives life, that he may receive death. So the voice of the physician hanging on the wood was not empty. For that he might die for us, because the Word could not die: *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^14]. He hung on the cross, but in the flesh. There was the cheapness, which the Jews despised: there was the love, by which the Jews were freed. For it was said for them: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^15]. And that voice was not empty. He died, was buried, rose again; having spent forty days with his disciples, he ascended into heaven, sent the Holy Spirit upon those who awaited the promise. They, the Holy Spirit received, were filled, and began to speak in the tongues of all nations. Then the Jews who were present, terrified that men, simple, unskilled, whom they had known nurtured among themselves in one tongue, were speaking in the name of Christ in all tongues, were terrified: whence this gift was, they learned by Peter speaking. He gave this gift, who hung on the wood. He gave this gift, who was mocked hanging on the wood, that he might give the Holy Spirit sitting in heaven. They heard, they believed, those of whom he had said: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do.* They believed, were baptized, and the conversion was made. What conversion? The blood of Christ, which, raging, they shed, believing they drank.

**6.** So, that we may conclude our sermon where we began, let us pray, and presume of God: let us live as he commands, and where we falter in this very life, let us call upon him, as the disciples called upon him, saying: *Lord, increase our faith*[^16]. And Peter presumed, and faltered: nor yet was he despised, sunk, but lifted up and raised. For what he presumed, whence was it? It was not from his own: it was from the Lord's. How? *Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you upon the water*[^17]. For the Lord was walking upon the waters. *If it is you, command me to come to you upon the water.* For I know that if it is you, you command and it is done. And he said: *Come.* He went down at his command, and trembled in his weakness. Yet when he trembled, he cried out to him: *Lord*, he says, *free me.* Then the Lord held out his hand to him, and said: *O you of little faith, why did you doubt?*[^18] He himself invited, he himself freed the one wavering and faltering; that there might be fulfilled what was said in the Psalm: *If I said, My foot is moved; your mercy, O Lord, helped me*[^19].

**7.** There are, then, two kinds of benefits, temporal and eternal. Temporal are health, substance, honor, friends, house, children, wife, and the other things of this life where we are pilgrims. Let us set ourselves, then, in the inn of this life, as pilgrims about to pass on, not as possessors about to remain. But eternal benefits are, first, eternal life itself, the incorruption and immortality of flesh and soul, the fellowship of the angels, the heavenly city, dignity that does not fail, the Father and the fatherland, he without death, she without an enemy. These benefits let us desire with all ardor, ask with all perseverance, not with a long discourse, but with groaning as witness. Desire always prays, even if the tongue is silent. If you always desire, you always pray. When does prayer slumber? When desire grows cold. So let us ask those everlasting benefits with all eagerness, let us seek those goods with all intent, let us ask those goods secure. For to him who has them those goods profit, they cannot harm. But these temporal things sometimes profit, sometimes harm. To many poverty has profited, and riches harmed: to many a private life has profited, and high honor harmed. And again to some money has profited, dignity has profited; to those using them well it has profited: but to those using them badly, not taken away it harmed more. And through this, brothers, let us ask these temporal things too in moderation, secure that if we receive them, he gives who knows what is expedient for us. Did you ask, and what you asked was not given you? Believe the Father, who, if it were expedient for you, would give it to you. Behold, from yourself make a conjecture for yourself. For such as your son, ignorant of human affairs, is with you, such are you with the Lord, ignorant of divine affairs. Behold, before you your son all day weeping, that you give him a knife, that is, a sword: you refuse to give it, you do not give it, you despise the one weeping, lest you mourn the one dying. Let him weep, afflict himself, dash himself, that you lift him onto a horse: you do not do it, because he cannot manage it; he will dash himself and kill himself. To whom you deny a part, you keep the whole for him. But that he may grow up, and securely possess the whole, you do not give him a small dangerous thing.

**8.** Therefore we say, brothers, pray as much as you can. Evils abound, and God willed that evils abound. Would that the bad did not abound, and evils did not abound. Evil times, laborious times, this men say. Let us live well, and the times are good. We are the times: such as we are, such are the times. But what shall we do? Can we not convert to a good life the multitude of men? Let the few who hear live well: let the few living well bear the many living badly. They are grains, they are on the threshing floor: they can have the chaff with them on the threshing floor, in the barn they will not have it. Let them bear what they do not wish, that they may come to what they wish. Why are we saddened and complain of God? Evils abound in the world, that the world may not be loved. Great men, the faithful saints, despised the beautiful world: we cannot despise even the foul one. The world is bad, behold it is bad, and yet it is loved as if it were good. But what is the bad world? For the heaven is not bad, and the earth, and the waters, and the things that are in them, fish, birds, trees. All these are good: but the bad world bad men make. But since we cannot be without bad men, as long as we live, as I said, let us groan to the Lord our God; and let us bear evils, that we may come to good things. Let us not reproach the householder; for he is dear. He carries us, not we him. He knows how to govern what he made: do what he commanded, and hope for what he promised.
