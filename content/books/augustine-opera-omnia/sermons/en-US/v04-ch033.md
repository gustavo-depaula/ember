# Sermon 117

**1.** The passage of the Gospel that was read, dearly beloved brothers, requires a pure eye of the heart. For we have received our Lord Jesus Christ, according to his divinity for the founding of all creation, according to his humanity for the restoring of fallen creation, with John evangelizing. But in that very Gospel we find what kind and how great a man John was, that from the dignity of the dispenser may be understood of how great a price is the Word, which could be spoken through such a one; rather, how of no price is that which surpasses all things. For by a price either a salable thing is bought, or is set below it, or exceeds it. When anyone buys a thing for as much as it is worth, the price is made equal to the thing that is bought; when for less, it is set below it; when for more, it exceeds it. But to the Word of God nothing can be made equal, nor be set below it for exchange, nor anything set above it. For all things can be set below the Word of God, because *all things were made through him* [^1]; yet they are not set below it as though they were the price of the Word, that anyone might give something, to receive it. Yet if it can be said, and some reasoning or custom of speaking admits this name, the price for buying the Word is the buyer himself, who gives himself for himself to this Word. And so when we buy something, we seek something to give, that we may have the thing we want to buy, the price given. And what we give is outside us; and if it was with us, that which we give becomes outside us, that what we buy may be with us. Whatever price he who buys something finds, he must find such a thing, that he gives what he has, and receives what he does not have; yet let the one from whom the price departs remain, and let that come to him for which he gives the price. But he who wants to buy this Word, who wants to have it, let him not seek outside himself what to give, let him give himself. And when he has done this, he does not lose himself, as he loses the price, when he buys something.

**2.** The Word of God, then, is set before all: let them buy who can; and those can who devoutly will. For in that Word is peace: *And peace on earth to men of good will* [^2]. Therefore let him who wants to buy give himself. This is, as it were, the price of the Word, if it can in any way be said, when the one who gives does not lose himself, and acquires the Word for which he gives himself, and acquires himself in the Word to which he gives himself. And what does he give to the Word? Not something alien to it, for which he gives himself; but what was made through the Word itself, this is rendered to it to be remade. *All things were made through him* [^3]. If all things, surely man too. If heaven, if earth, if sea, if all that is in them, if the whole creation; surely he more manifestly, who, made in the image of God, was made man through the Word.  
We do not now treat, brothers, how what was said can be understood: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God* [^4]. It can be understood ineffably; it is not by the words of man that it comes to be understood. We treat the Word of God, and say why it is not understood. We do not now say it that it may be understood, but we say what hinders it from being understood. For it is a certain form, a form not formed, but the form of all formed things; an unchangeable form, without fall, without defect, without time, without place, surpassing all things, being to all both a certain foundation in which they are, and a summit under which they are. If you say that all things are in it, you do not lie. For that very Word was called the Wisdom of God; and we have it written: *You made all things in Wisdom* [^5]. Therefore in it are all things; and yet, because it is God, under it are all things. We say how incomprehensible is what was read; yet it was read, not that it might be comprehended by man, but that man might grieve because he does not comprehend, and find from what he is hindered from comprehending, and remove those things, and long for the perception of the unchangeable Word, himself changed from worse to better. For the Word does not make progress or grow, with a knower coming to it; but it is whole, if you remain; whole, if you depart; whole, when you return; remaining in itself, and renewing all things. So it is the form of all things, an unfashioned form, without time, as we said, and without spaces of places. For whatever is contained in place is circumscribed. A form is circumscribed by bounds, it has limits from where and to where it is. Then what is contained in place and stretched out by a certain mass and space is less in part, than in the whole. May God make you understand.

**3.** But daily, from the bodies that are before our eyes, which we see, which we touch, among which we are, we can judge that any body in place has a form. But everything that occupies a space of place is less in part than in the whole. A part of the human body, for example, the arm; surely the arm is less than the whole body. And if the arm is less, it occupies a smaller place. Likewise the head, because it is a part of the body, is in a smaller place, and the head is less than the whole body. So all things that are in place are smaller in part than in the whole. Let us feel nothing such about that Word, let us think nothing such. Let us not imagine spiritual things from the suggestion of the flesh. That speech, that God, is not less in part than in the whole.  
But you cannot think anything such. Such ignorance is more devout than presumed knowledge. For we speak of God. It was said: *And the Word was God* [^6]. We speak of God, what wonder if you do not comprehend? For if you comprehend, it is not God. Let there be a devout confession of ignorance rather than a rash profession of knowledge. To touch God somewhat with the mind is a great blessedness; but to comprehend him is altogether impossible. To the mind God pertains, he is to be understood; to the eyes body, it is to be seen. But do you think you comprehend a body with the eye? You cannot at all. For whatever you look at, you do not look at the whole. Whose man's face you see, you do not see his back, at the time you see his face; and when you see his back, at that time you do not see his face. So you do not so see, as to comprehend; but when you look at another part, which you had not seen, unless memory works with you to remember that you had seen what you depart from, you would never say you had comprehended anything even on the surface. You handle what you see, you turn it this way and that, or you yourself go round it to see the whole. So by one look you cannot see the whole. And as long as you turn it to see, you see parts; and by joining together that you saw the other parts, you seem to inspect the whole. But here it is not the sight of the eyes, but the liveliness of memory, that is understood. What, then, can be said of that Word, brothers? Behold, we speak of bodies subject to our eyes, they cannot be comprehended by a look; what eye of the heart, then, comprehends God? It suffices that it touch him, if the eye is pure. But if it touches, it touches with a certain incorporeal and spiritual touch, yet does not comprehend; and this, if it is pure. And a man becomes blessed by touching with the heart that which always remains blessed; and that is perpetual blessedness itself, and from it a man becomes living, perpetual life; from it a man becomes wise, perfect wisdom; from it a man becomes enlightened, it is everlasting light. And see how you, by touching, are made what you were not, while you do not make that which you touch to be what it was not. This I say: God does not grow from the knower, but the knower from the knowledge of God.

**4.** Let us not think, dearly beloved brothers, that we bestow a benefit on God, because we said that we give a price in a way. For we do not give him anything by which he may be increased, who is both whole when you fall, and remains whole when you return, ready to be seen so as to make blessed those converted, and to punish with blindness those turned away. For he avenges first in the soul turned away from him, by the beginning of penalties, by blindness itself. For he who turns himself from the true light, that is, from God, is at once made blind. He does not yet feel the punishment, but he already has it.  
And so, dearly beloved brothers, let us understand the Word of God to be born incorporeally, inviolably, unchangeably, without temporal birth, yet born from God. Do we think we can in any way persuade certain unbelievers that what is said by us as the Catholic faith—which is contrary to the Arians, by whom the Church of God has often been tempted—does not shrink from the truth, when carnal men more easily accept what they are accustomed to see? For some have dared to say: The Father is greater than the Son, and precedes him in time; that is, the Father is greater than the Son, and the Son is less than the Father, and is preceded by the Father in time. And so they argue: If he was born, surely the Father was before the Son was born to him. Attend; let him be present to us, with your prayers helping, and with us desiring with devout intent to take in what he grants, what he suggests; let him be present to us, that we may be able in some way to explain what we have begun. Yet, brothers, I say beforehand, if I cannot explain it, do not think that reason has failed, but the man. And so I exhort and beseech you to pray; let the mercy of God be present, and make the matter to be so said by us, as you ought to hear and we to say. This, then, they say: If he is the Son of God, he was born. This we confess. For he would not be the Son, if he were not born. It is manifest, faith admits it, the Catholic Church approves it, it is true. They add then: If the Son was born to the Father, the Father was before the Son was born to him. This faith spits out, Catholic ears spit it out; he who relishes this is anathematized, is outside, does not belong to the participation and fellowship of the saints. Therefore, he says, give me a reason, how the Son could both be born to the Father, and be coeval with him from whom he was born?

**5.** And what do we do, brothers, when we suggest spiritual things to carnal men—if indeed we ourselves too are not carnal—when we convey these spiritual things to carnal men, to a man accustomed to earthly birth, and seeing the order of this creation, where there is succession and decease, where he distinguishes begetters and begotten by age? For after the father the son is born, surely to succeed his father who is to die. This we find in men, this in other living things, parents earlier in time, sons later in time. By this custom of seeing, they desire to transfer carnal things to spiritual things, and by attention to carnal things are the more easily seduced. For the reason of the hearers does not follow those who preach such things, but the custom, which also loosed those very men, so that they preached such things. And what do we do? Shall we be silent? Would that it were allowed! For perhaps by being silent something worthy might be thought of the ineffable thing. For whatever can be spoken is not ineffable. But God is ineffable. For if the apostle Paul says he was caught up to the third heaven, and says he heard ineffable words [^7]; how much more is he himself ineffable, who showed such things, which he to whom they were shown cannot speak? And so, brothers, it would be better if we could be silent, and say: This faith has, so we believe; you cannot grasp it, you are a little one; it must be patiently borne, until you nourish your wings; lest, when you want to fly unfledged, it be not the breeze of liberty, but the fall of rashness. What says he in reply? O if he had what to say, he would say it to me! For the one who fails this is an excuse. He is conquered by truths, who does not want to answer. The one to whom this is said, if he does not answer, even if in himself he is not conquered, yet he is conquered among the wavering brothers. For weak brothers hear, and think there really is nothing to be said; and perhaps they think it true, that there is nothing to be said, yet not that there is nothing to be felt. For a man can say nothing that he cannot also feel; he can also feel something that he cannot say.  
Yet, the ineffability of that majesty being preserved, lest, when we have given some likenesses against them, anyone think that by these likenesses we have already arrived at that which can neither be said nor thought by little ones (surely even if it can by some greater ones, it can in part, it can in a riddle, it can through a mirror; but not yet face to face); let us too give some likenesses against them, by which they may be refuted, not by which it may be grasped. For when we say that it can very well be, can be understood, that he was both born and coeternal with him from whom he was born, in order to refute this and show it as though false, they give us likenesses. From where? From the creature, and they say to us: A man surely existed before he begot a son, he is greater than his son; and a horse existed before it begot its offspring, and a sheep, and the other animals. They apply likenesses from creatures.

**6.** What, must we labor, that we too may find likenesses of the things we assert? What? If I did not find any, could I not rightly say: The birth of the Creator perhaps has no likeness in the creature? For as much as he surpasses, in what is there, the things that are here, so much does he surpass, in what is born there, the things that are born here. All things are here through God; and what yet is to be compared to God? So all things that are born here, are born with him bringing it about. And so perhaps no likeness of his birth is found, just as no likeness is found of his substance, and his immutability, divinity, his majesty. For what like can be found here? If, then, perhaps not even a likeness of his birth is found, am I therefore oppressed, because I did not find likenesses for the Creator of all, desiring to find in the creature what was like the Creator?  
And truly, brothers, I shall not find temporal likenesses, which I can compare to eternity. But even those you have found, what are they? For what have you found? That the father is greater in time than the son; and therefore you want the Son of God to be less in time than the eternal Father, because you found the son less than the temporal father. Give me an eternal father here, and you have found a likeness. You find the son less than the father in time, the temporal son less than the temporal father. Have you found me a temporal son less than the eternal Father?

**7.** Since, then, in eternity there is stability, but in time variety; in eternity all things stand, in time some come, some succeed; you can find a son less in the variety of time succeeding his father, because he himself too succeeded his father, the temporal son the not-eternal father. What, then, can we, my brothers, find in the creature coeternal, when in the creature we find nothing eternal? Find an eternal father in the creature, and I find a coeternal son. But if you do not find an eternal one, and they conquer themselves in time; it suffices that for a likeness we find a coeval one. For one thing is coeternal, another coeval. We daily call coeval those who have the same measure of times; not one preceded by the other in time, yet both began to be, whom we call coeval. If we can find something coeval that is born to that from which it is born; if there can be found a coeval begetter and begotten; here we find coeval things, there let us understand coeternal things. If here I find the begotten to have begun to be from that from which the begetter began, surely we understand that the Son of God did not begin to be from that from which the begetter did not begin. Behold, perhaps, brothers, we find something in the creature, which is born from another thing, and yet begins to be from that time from which began that from which it is born. This is from that from which that began, that from that from which that did not begin. This, then, is coeval, that coeternal.

**8.** I judge Your Holiness has already understood what I say, that temporal things cannot be compared to eternal; but that coeval things can, by some thin and small likeness, be compared to coeternal things. And so let us find coeval things, and be admonished by the Scriptures to these likenesses. We read in the Scriptures of Wisdom itself: *For it is the brightness of eternal light* [^8]. Likewise we read: *A mirror without stain of God's majesty* [^9]. Wisdom itself was called the brightness of eternal light, was called the image of the Father; hence let us take a likeness, that we may find coeval things, from which we may understand coeternal things. O Arian, if I find that the begetter does not precede in time that which it begot, that the begotten is not less in time than that from which it was begotten; it is just that you grant me that these coeternal things can be found in the Creator, when coeval things could be found in the creature. Now indeed I think this occurs to some brothers. For some anticipated me from the time I said: *For it is the brightness of eternal light*. For fire pours out light, light is poured out from fire. If we ask what exists from what, daily when we light a lamp, we are admonished of a certain invisible and inexpressible thing, that some lamp of our understanding may be able to be lit in this night of the age. Attend to him who lights the lamp. The lamp not lit, there is not yet fire, there is not yet the brightness that goes out from the fire. But I ask, and say: Does the brightness exist from the fire, or the fire from the brightness? Every soul answers me: for God willed to sow in every soul the beginnings of understanding, the beginnings of wisdom; every soul answers me, and no one doubts, that that splendor exists from the fire, not the fire from the splendor. Let us set, then, the fire as the father of that splendor; because we have already said in advance that we seek coeval things, not coeternal. If I want to light a lamp, there is not yet fire there, not yet that splendor; but as soon as I have lit it, together with the fire the splendor too exists. Give me here fire without splendor, and I believe you that the Father was without the Son.

**9.** Attend: so great a thing has been said by us as we could, with the Lord helping the intent of your prayer and the preparation of your heart, you have received as much as you could grasp. Yet those things are ineffable. Think nothing worthy was said, even in that very point by which coeval things are compared to coeternal, temporal to ever-abiding, extinguishable to immortal. But because the Son was called the image of the Father, let us take from here too some likeness in things far differing, as we said in advance. An image exists from a mirror of a man gazing at the mirror. It cannot serve us for the evidence of this matter, which we somehow try to explain. For it is said to me: He who attends to the mirror, surely already existed, and was already born. The image exists as soon as the gazer has come to exist. For he who looks existed even before he came to the mirror. What, then, shall we find, from which we can dig out such a likeness, as we dug out from fire and splendor? Let us begin from the smallest thing. You easily know how water often returns the images of bodies. This we say: When anyone either passes over water or stands, he sees there his image. Let us set, then, something born over water, like a shoot or grass, is it not born with its image? As soon as it begins to exist, its image begins to exist with it, it does not precede its image in being born; it is not shown me that something was born over water, and afterward its image appeared, while that first appeared without an image; but it is born with its image; and yet the image is from it, not it from the image. It is born, then, with its image, and the shoot and its image begin to be together. Do you not confess that the image is from that shoot, not the shoot begotten from the image? Therefore from that shoot you confess the image. And so both the begetter, and what is begotten, began to be together. Therefore they are coeval. If the shoot is always, the image too is always from the shoot. But what is from another, surely is born. Therefore the begetter can always be, and always with him what is born from him. For there we boiled over, there we labored, how the everlasting birth might be understood. Therefore the Son of God is so called by that by which the Father too is, that he has one from whom he is; not by this, that the Father was first, and afterward the Son. Always the Father, always the Son from the Father. And because whatever is from another is born; always therefore the Son born. Always the Father, always the image from him; just as that image of the shoot was born from the shoot, and if the shoot is always, the image too would always be born from the shoot. You could not find coeternal things begotten to eternal begetters, and you found coeval things born to temporal begetters. I understand the coeternal Son born to the eternal begetter. For what is coeval to the temporal, this is coeternal to the eternal.

**10.** Here now there is a little thing for you to note, brothers, on account of blasphemies. For it is always said: Behold, you have given likenesses; but the splendor that is poured out from fire shines less than the fire itself; and the image of the shoot surely has less of the proper quality, than the shoot from which the image is. These things have a likeness, but do not have a likeness in every way; therefore they do not seem to be of the same substance. What, then, shall we say, if someone says: Such, then, is the Son to the Father, as the splendor to the fire, and the image to the shoot? Behold, I have understood the eternal Father, I have understood the coeternal Son—yet do we say, like the poured-out splendor shining less than the fire, or like the poured-out image existing less than the shoot? No: but the equality is in every way. I do not believe it, he says, because you have not found a likeness. But believe the Apostle, because he could see what I said. For he says: *He did not count it robbery to be equal to God* [^10]. Equality is joined in every way. And what did he say? *Not robbery*. Why? Because that is robbery, which is another's.  
Yet from these two comparisons and two kinds, we have perhaps found a likeness in the creature, by which we may understand the Son both coeternal with the Father and in no way less. But we cannot find that in one kind of likenesses; let us join both kinds. How both kinds? One from which they themselves give likenesses, and the other from which we have given them. For they gave likenesses from those things which are born in time, and are preceded in time by those from which they are born, as man from man. The one greater in time first born; but yet man and man, that is, of the same substance. For man begets man, and horse a horse, and beast a beast. They beget unto the same substance, but not unto the same time. They are diverse in time, but are not diverse in nature. What, then, do we praise here in that birth? Surely equality of nature. But what is lacking? Equality of time. Let us hold here the one thing that is praised, that is, equality of nature. But in that kind of likenesses, which we gave from the splendor of fire and from the image of the shoot, you do not find equality of nature, you find coevality. What do we praise here? Coevality. What is lacking? Equality of nature. Join the things you praise. For in creatures something you praise is lacking, in the Creator nothing can be lacking; because what you find in the creature proceeded from the Creator the maker. What, then, in coeval things? Should not this which you praise there be given to God? But what is lacking is not to be attributed to the majesty, in which there is no defect. Behold, I offer you coeval begetters with begotten; you praise coevality there, but you blame the inequality. What you blame, do not attribute to God; what you praise, attribute it; and you attribute to him from that kind of likenesses, for coevality, coeternity, that he is coeternal who is born with him from whom he is born. But from the other kind of likenesses, which is itself too a creature of God, and ought to praise the Creator, what do you praise there? Equality of nature. Now on account of that distinction you had given coeternity; give on account of this, equality; and the birth of the same substance is perfect. For what is more demented, my brothers, than that I should praise in the creature something that is not in the Creator? I praise in man equality of nature, and do not believe it in him who made man? What is born of man is man; and what is born of God, will it not be that which he is from whom it is born? I do not dwell on works that God did not make. Let all his works, then, praise the Creator. I find here coeval, I recognize there coeternal. Here I find equality of nature, there I understand equality of substance. The whole, then, is there which here is found in single parts and single things. The whole, then, is there together, and not only this which is in creatures; the whole I find there, but as in the Creator, by so much the greater, in that these are visible, those invisible; these temporal, those eternal; these changeable, those unchangeable; these corruptible, those incorruptible. In short, in man himself those which we find, man and man, are two men; there the Father and the Son are one God.  
To the Lord our God I give inexpressible thanks, that from this most scrupulous and most laborious place he has deigned to free my weakness, at your asking. Before all things yet keep this, that whatever we could gather from the creature, either by sense of body, or by thought of mind, the Creator inexpressibly transcends. But do you want to touch him with the mind? Purge the mind, purge your heart. Make clean the eye, by which that, whatever it is, may be touched. Make clean the eye of the heart: *For blessed are the pure of heart, for they will see God* [^11]. But for the unclean heart, what more mercifully could be procured or granted by him, than that that Word, of which we have said so many and so great things, and said nothing worthy; than that that Word, through which all things were made, should become what we are, that we might be able to touch that which we are not? For we are not God; but we can with the mind, or the inner sharpness of the heart, see God. Our sharpnesses, worn down by sins, blunted, cast down by weakness, desire to see; but we are in hope, in the reality we are not yet. *We are sons of God*. This John says, who says: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God* [^12]; who reclined upon the breast of the Lord, who drew these secrets from the bosom of that heart; he himself says: *Most beloved, we are sons of God; and it has not yet appeared what we shall be; we know that when it appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is* [^13]. This is promised us.  
But that we may arrive, if we cannot yet see the Word God, let us hear the Word flesh; because we have been made carnal, let us hear the Word made flesh. For therefore he came, therefore he took on our weakness, that you might be able to grasp the firm speech of God bearing your weakness. And it is rightly called milk. For he gives milk to little ones, that he may give the food of wisdom to the greater. Suckle patiently, that you may be eagerly fed. For how does milk too come to be, by which infants are suckled? Was it not food on the table? But the infant is unfit to eat the food that is on the table; what does the mother do? She incarnates the food, and makes milk from it. She makes for us what we can grasp. So the Word was made flesh, that we little ones might be nourished by milk, who were indeed infants for food. But there is this difference, that when the mother makes the incarnated food into milk, the food is turned into milk; but the Word, remaining unchangeably, took on flesh, that it might be in a way interwoven. What it is, it did not corrupt, did not change, that through your condition it might speak to you, not transmuted and converted into man. For inconvertible and unchangeable, and remaining altogether inviolable, it was made what you are to you, what it itself is to the Father.  
For he himself, to the weak, what does he say, that they may, that sight recovered, touch from some part the Word, through which all things were made? *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart* [^14]. What does the master, the Son of God, the Wisdom of God, through whom all things were made, proclaim? He calls the human race, and says: *Come to me, all you who labor, and learn from me*. You thought perhaps the Wisdom of God would say: Learn how I made the heavens and the stars; all things too in me, before they were made, were numbered; how in the power of unchangeable reasons even your hairs are numbered [^15]. Did you think these and such things she would say? No. But first that: *For I am gentle and humble of heart*. Behold what you may grasp, see, brothers, surely it is small. We stretch toward great things, let us grasp small things, and we shall be great. Do you want to grasp the loftiness of God? Grasp first the humility of God. Deign to be humble for your own sake, because God deigned to be humble for that same you; for not for his own sake. Grasp, then, the humility of Christ, learn to be humble, do not be proud. Confess your weakness, lie patiently before the physician. When you have grasped his humility, you rise with him; not as though he too rises insofar as he is the Word; but you rather, that more and more he may be grasped by you. You understood at first waveringly and hesitatingly; you understand afterward more certainly and clearly. He does not grow, but you make progress, and as it were he seems to rise with you. So it is, brothers. Believe the precepts of God, and do them, and he will grant you the strength of understanding. Do not presume, and as it were set knowledge before the precept of God; lest you remain lower, not more solid. Attend to the tree; it seeks the depths first, that it may grow up high; it fixes its root in the lowly, that it may stretch its top to heaven. Does it strive except from lowliness? But you, without love, want to comprehend lofty things; without a root you seek the breezes? That is a fall, not an increase. With Christ dwelling by faith in your hearts, be rooted and founded in love, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God [^16].
