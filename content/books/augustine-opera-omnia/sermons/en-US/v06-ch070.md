# Sermon 229/A: A Tractate on the Lord's Day of Holy Easter

**1.** You who have been reborn into a new life, for which reason you are called *infants*—you especially, who in this way see what these things mean, as we had promised you, listen. Listen also, you faithful who are accustomed to seeing this; a reminder is good, lest forgetfulness steal it away. What you see on the table of the Lord, as far as concerns the appearance of the things themselves, you are accustomed to seeing on your own tables too; it is the same in appearance, but not the same in power. For you yourselves are the same men you were; you have not brought us new faces. And yet you are new; old in the appearance of the body, new by the grace of holiness, just as this too is new. As yet, indeed, in the way you see it, it is bread and wine; sanctification is added, and that bread will be the body of Christ, and that wine will be the blood of Christ. This the name of Christ accomplishes, this the grace of Christ accomplishes, that the very thing that was seen should be seen, and yet should not have the value it had. For before, if it were eaten, it would fill the belly; now, when it is eaten, it builds up the mind. But just as when you were baptized—indeed, before you were baptized—on the Sabbath day we spoke to you about the sacrament of the font, in which you were to be dipped, and we told you, which I believe you have not forgotten, that this is what baptism was and is worth, namely, that it is a burial with Christ, since the Apostle says: *For we have been buried together with Christ through baptism into death, so that as he rose from the dead, so we too might walk in newness of life*[^1]; so now too, not from our own heart, nor from our own presumption, nor from human arguments, but from the authority of the Apostle, we must commend to you and impress upon you what it is that you have received or are about to receive. Listen, then, briefly to the Apostle, or rather to Christ through the Apostle, on the sacrament of the Lord's table, what he says: *We who are many are one bread, one body*[^2]. There it is, the whole of it; I have said it quickly; but weigh the words, do not count them. If you count the words, it is brief; if you weigh them, it is great. *One bread,* he said. However many loaves are placed there, *one bread*; however many loaves are on the altars of Christ today throughout the whole world, it is *one bread.* But what is the *one bread*? He explained it most briefly: *We who are many are one body.* This bread is the body of Christ, of which the Apostle speaks, addressing the Church: *And you are the body of Christ and his members*[^3]. What you receive, you are, by the grace by which you have been redeemed; you subscribe to it when you answer *Amen.* What you see is the sacrament of unity.

**2.** Now since the Apostle has briefly intimated to us what this is, consider it more carefully and see how it is made. How is bread made? It is threshed, ground, and goes from the kneading to the baking; in the kneading it is cleansed, in the baking it is made firm. Where was your threshing? This is what you became: it was in the fasts, in the observances, in the vigils, in the exorcisms. You were being ground when you were exorcized. There is no kneading without water: you were baptized. The baking is troublesome, but useful. For what is the baking? The fire of temptations, without which this life is not lived. But how is it useful? *The furnace tests the potter's vessel, and the trial of tribulation tests righteous men*[^4]. And just as out of single grains gathered into one and in a way mingled together by the kneading there is made one bread, so the one body of Christ is made by the concord of love. And what the body of Christ has in the grains, the blood has in the clusters of grapes; for wine too comes forth from the press, and what was singly in many flows together into one, and becomes wine. Therefore both in the bread and in the cup there is the mystery of unity.

**3.** As for what you heard at the table of the Lord, *The Lord be with you,* this we are accustomed to say also when we greet you from the apse, and we say it as often as we pray; for it is to our advantage that the Lord be always with us, since without him we are nothing. As for what sounded in your ears, see what you say at the altar of God. For in a way we question and admonish you, and we say: *Lift up your heart.* Do not let it be downward: the heart rots in the earth, lift it up to heaven. But to what end is *Lift up your heart*? What do you answer? To what end is *Lift up your heart*? *We have lifted it up to the Lord.* For this very lifting up of the heart is sometimes good, sometimes evil. How is it evil? It is evil in those of whom it is said: *You cast them down while they were being lifted up*[^5]. To *lift up the heart,* if it be not to the Lord, is not righteousness but pride; and so, when we have said *Lift up your heart,* because the heart lifted up can still be a heart of pride, you answer: *We have lifted it up to the Lord.* It is therefore a gracious gift, not an exaltation: and because this is a gracious gift, that we should have our heart lifted up to the Lord, did we accomplish it ourselves? Could we do it by our own strength? Did we raise the earth, which we were, to heaven? Far from it: he did it, he granted it, he put forth his hand, he extended his grace, he made to be above what was below. Therefore, when we have said *Lift up your heart,* and you have answered *We have lifted it up to the Lord,* lest you attribute it to yourselves that you have your heart lifted up, I added: *Let us give thanks to the Lord our God.* These are mysteries that are brief, but great: we call them brief in words, but great in affection. For you say these things to yourselves quickly, without a book, without a reading, without a long disputation. Remind yourselves of what you are, and of that in which you ought to persevere, that you may be able to come to the things God has promised.

[^1]: Rom 6:4
[^2]: 1 Cor 10:17
[^3]: 1 Cor 12:27
[^4]: Sir 27:5
[^5]: Ps 73:18 (72:18)
