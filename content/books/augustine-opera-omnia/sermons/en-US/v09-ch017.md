# Sermon 313/C: A Treatise on the Feast of Saint Cyprian

**1.** The distinguished martyr of Christ, through whom he especially ruled, increased, adorned, and illumined this Church, today by the anniversary celebration of his passion commends to our memory—not recalling him as one slipped away, but as fixed and abiding—more joyfully and gratefully. So it behooves us with a solemn sermon to praise in the Lord the soul of his servant, *that the meek may hear and be glad*[^1]. For that soul, while it dwelt in these dying members, despised the death that would someday come, in order to acquire the life that would abide without end; by faithful and prudent counsel losing what, even retained, would perish, in order to find what could not perish. For temporal life, if it is retained by denying Christ, both is itself ended, and the everlasting is not found. How much more advisedly, then, to acquire the one by neglecting the other, than by wrongly loving the one to lose both? This is the common counsel of the holy martyrs, the common exchange: to despise fleeing things, in order to acquire abiding things; by dying to live, lest they die by living: and to live forever by dying once, rather than to die twice and afterward not deserve to live, by deferring the death that is to come, and, when it has come deferred, by not at all coming to the life that is to abide. This, I say, is the common counsel of the holy martyrs, the common exchange; this they learned from their teacher and redeemer and Lord alike, because he said to all: *He who loves his soul will lose it, and he who loses it for my sake will find it in eternal life*[^2]. Accordingly the soul, when it is loved, perishes, and is found when it is lost; let it perish if it loves, lest it perish while it is loved. For in two ways can what was said be understood: *He who loves his soul will lose it.* He who loves his soul in this world, will lose it in the world to come; and: he who loves his soul in the world to come, will lose it in this world. According to the former way, he who loves his soul by fearing to die for Christ, will lose it so as not to live in Christ; and he who loves his soul so as to live in Christ, will lose it by dying for Christ. For it follows: *And he who loses it for my sake, will find it in eternal life.* And he who said: For my sake, *this is the true God, and eternal life*[^3].

**2.** Since, then, this is the office of all the saints, this martyr of ours has today a reward, not only from himself, but also from many. For he diffused the good fragrance of Christ far and wide by teaching, by living, by dying; by teaching about Christ, by living in Christ, by dying for Christ. Wherefore, dearly beloved, since no other than Christ can be understood here, where the bride the Church says: *My beloved is a cluster of cyprus to me*[^4], signifying by the most fragrant tree the most fragrant grace; surely, just as in his right faith he was made a Christian by Christ, so in his good fragrance he was made Cyprian from cyprus. For the apostle Paul too says: *We are the good fragrance of Christ in every place*[^5]. So the good fragrance of Christ is in our Cyprian from this seat; not that it sufficed for this city only, nor for Africa alone, of which this is the head; but it so diffused itself far and wide, that from the rising of the sun to its setting the name of the Lord is praised in him, in whom his soul will be praised, that *the meek may hear and be glad*[^6]. For what region on earth can be found, where his eloquence is not read, his teaching praised, his love loved, his life proclaimed, his death venerated, the festivity of his passion celebrated? How many warriors against the devil did the trumpet of his sermon kindle to the example of his passion! How many afterward, not only reading his words, but also marveling at his fame, having loved him by learning, by loving followed him! So some by teaching he sent ahead to be imitated, others by suffering he preceded as those who would imitate. The mouths of those barking against the teaching of Christ he confounded; he rendered virgins beautiful to Christ, not in bodies and colors, but in conduct; he broke the stings of zeal and envy, and checked their poisons; disputing healthfully on the Lord's Prayer, so that we may understand what we ask; and he made a book for the lapsed, pastorally counseling and clemently sympathizing, and brought down the contemptuous to the humility of sorrow, and raised the sorrowing from the depth of despair; he praised patience, persuaded it, displayed it; he ground down the brows of heretics by the demonstration and preaching of unity; with him treating of mortality, and commending the joys of immortal life, in the minds of the faithful all fear of the dying and grief of the mourning blushed for shame; he convicted the most vain and most pernicious worship of idols even by the attestation of secular letters; to the greatest usefulness of almsgiving and heavenly gains, earthly avarice being refuted, he inflamed Christian souls. And why say more? Many everywhere have the great body of his books. But let us give the more abundant thanks to the Lord, that we have merited to have the holy body of his members; where with more ardent affection we may pour out our prayers to the Lord, to whom it pleased, and by whose grace he became such, and may praise him in the Lord, through whom we rejoice that the Lord was so praised.

[^2]: John 12:25
[^3]: 1 John 5:20
[^4]: Song 1:14
[^5]: 2 Cor 2:15
