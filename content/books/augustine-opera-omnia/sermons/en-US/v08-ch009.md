# Sermon 280: On the Heavenly Birthday of the Martyrs Perpetua and Felicity

**1.** Today, returning with its yearly anniversary, recalls to our memory and in a way makes present again the day on which God’s holy servants Perpetua and Felicity, adorned with the crowns of martyrdom, blossomed in perpetual felicity. They held fast to Christ’s name in the battle and together found their own names in the reward. We have heard, as the account was read, how divine revelations encouraged them and how their sufferings ended in triumph. All these things, arranged and illuminated by the light of words, we received with our ears, watched with our minds, honored with devotion, and praised with love. Yet we too owe a customary sermon to this devout celebration. If what I offer falls short of their merits, I nevertheless show an eager affection in the joy of so great a festival. For what could be more glorious than these women, whom men find it easier to admire than to imitate? Yet the praise belongs above all to him in whom they believed, and in whose name they came together with faithful zeal. In their inner selves they are found to be neither male nor female, so that even in those who are female in body, strength of mind conceals the sex of the flesh, and one is reluctant to think about their bodily condition when it could not be seen in their deeds. Thus the dragon was trodden under a chaste foot and a victorious step when the ladder was shown standing upright, by which blessed Perpetua was to go to God. In this way the head of the ancient serpent, which had been the precipice for the woman as she fell, became a step for a woman as she ascended.

**2.** What could be sweeter than this spectacle, braver than this contest, more glorious than this victory? Then, when their holy bodies were thrown to the beasts, the nations raged throughout the amphitheater, and the peoples devised vain things. But he who dwells in heaven laughed at them, and the Lord mocked them. Now, however, the descendants of those whose voices raged impiously against the martyrs’ flesh praise the martyrs’ merits with devout voices. Nor was the arena of cruelty then filled with so great a crowd to kill them as now fills the church of devotion to honor them. Every year love watches with reverence what impiety committed with sacrilege on a single day. They too watched, but with a very different intention. By their shouting they did what the beasts with their biting did not accomplish. We, however, both pity what the impious did and venerate what the devout suffered. They saw with bodily eyes something to feed the savagery of their hearts; we behold with the eyes of the heart what was withheld from them so that they could not see it. They rejoiced in the martyrs’ dead bodies; we grieve over their dead minds. Without the light of faith, they thought the martyrs extinguished; with the surest vision of faith, we see them crowned. Their taunting has ultimately become our rejoicing. Our rejoicing is devout and everlasting; their taunting was impious then and now has entirely vanished.

**3.** We believe, dearly beloved, that the martyrs’ rewards are exceedingly great, and we are entirely right to believe it. But if we carefully consider their struggles, we will not at all be surprised that their rewards are so great. For although this life is toilsome and temporary, its sweetness is so great that, while people cannot prevent themselves from dying, they nevertheless make many great efforts to prevent themselves from dying soon. Nothing can be done to abolish death, and everything possible is done to postpone it. Certainly, labor is burdensome to every soul; yet even those who hope for nothing, either good or bad, after this life undertake every labor to keep death from putting an end to all labor. What of those who either mistakenly imagine false and fleshly pleasures after death, or hope with right faith for a repose of unspeakable peace and blessedness? Do not they too strive and take great pains to avoid dying soon? For what else is the aim of so many labors for necessary sustenance, of so much service in medicine or other forms of care, whether demanded by the sick or offered to them, except to keep the boundary of death from being reached soon? At what price, then, should we value the absence of death in the life to come, when its mere postponement is so precious in this life? Even this miserable life has such an inexplicable sweetness, and the nature of living beings, however they live, has such a horror of death, that even those who pass through death to a life in which they cannot die do not wish to die.

**4.** With sincere love, sure hope, and unfeigned faith, Christ’s martyrs therefore despise this great delight in living and this fear of dying through their extraordinary courage. In these virtues they leave behind them the world with its promises and threats, and stretch forward to what lies ahead. By these virtues they trample the head of the serpent with its varied hissing and ascend. For whoever subdues the love of this life like a tyrant is the conqueror of all desires, since all desires are its attendants. There is no bond at all by which someone who is not held by love of life itself can be held in this life. Bodily pains, however, can be compared in some measure with the fear of death, for sometimes one and sometimes the other prevails in a person. Someone lies under torture to avoid death; someone facing death also lies to avoid torture. Someone tells the truth, unable to bear the torments, so as not to be tortured for lying in his own defense. But let whichever of these prevails do so in whatever minds it may. Christ’s martyrs overcame both for Christ’s name and righteousness: they feared neither death nor pain. He who lived in them conquered in them, so that those who lived not for themselves but for him would not die even when they died. He himself supplied them with spiritual delights, so that they would not feel bodily distress beyond what was enough to exercise them, without breaking them. For where was that woman when she did not feel herself struggling with the savage cow, and asked when something would happen that had already happened? Where was she? What was she seeing, that she had not seen these things? What was she enjoying, that she had not felt these things? What love had carried her out of herself, what spectacle had drawn her away, what cup had intoxicated her? And she was still bound by the bonds of the flesh, still carried limbs subject to death, still weighed down by a corruptible body. What, then, when the martyrs’ souls, released from these bonds after the labors of their perilous struggle, were welcomed and refreshed amid angelic triumphs, where they are told not, “Fulfill what I commanded,” but, “Receive what I promised”? What delight do they now feast upon in spirit? How secure they are in the Lord, and in what sublime honor they glory—who could teach this by an earthly example?

**5.** And indeed, this life that the blessed martyrs now possess, although none of this world’s happiness or pleasures can be compared with it, fulfills only a small part of the promise—or rather, it is a consolation during the delay. But the day of recompense will come, when, with bodies restored, the whole person will receive what is deserved. Then the limbs of that rich man, once adorned with a purple garment that passed away, will burn in eternal fire, and the transformed flesh of the ulcer-covered poor man will shine among the angels—even though now too the rich man thirsts in the underworld for a drop from the poor man’s finger, while the poor man rests in delight in the righteous man’s bosom. For just as there is a very great difference between the joys or miseries of those who dream and those who are awake, so there is a great difference between the torments or joys of the dead and those of the risen. This is not because the spirits of the departed must be deceived like those who sleep, but because the repose of souls without any bodies is one thing, and the glory and happiness of angels with heavenly bodies is another. The multitude of the faithful at the resurrection will be made equal to them. Among that multitude the most glorious martyrs will shine with the special light of their honor, and the very bodies in which they suffered undeserved torments will be transformed into adornments worthy of them.

**6.** Let us therefore celebrate their feasts, as we do, with the deepest devotion: with sober gladness, a chaste assembly, faithful reflection, and confident proclamation. Rejoicing together in the virtues of those better than ourselves is no small part of imitating them. They are great, we are little; but the Lord has blessed the little together with the great. They have gone ahead of us and risen above us. If we cannot follow them in deed, let us follow them in affection; if not in glory, at least in gladness; if not in merits, in our desires; if not in suffering, in sympathy; if not in eminence, in union. Let us not think it a small thing that we are members of the same body as those whom we cannot equal. *For if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it; so when one member is glorified, all the members rejoice with it*. Glory to the head, from whom care comes both to the hands above and to the feet below. Just as he alone laid down his life for us, so the martyrs imitated him and laid down their lives for their brothers and sisters; and they watered the earth with their blood so that this abundant harvest of peoples might spring up like growing plants. We too, then, are the fruit of their labor. We admire them; they have pity on us. We congratulate them; they pray for us. They spread their bodies like garments when the colt carrying the Lord was led into Jerusalem; we, at least, like people cutting branches from trees, gather hymns and praises from the holy Scriptures to offer for our common joy. Yet all of us obey the same Lord, follow the same Teacher, accompany the same leader, submit to the same head, journey toward the same Jerusalem, pursue the same love, and embrace the same unity.
