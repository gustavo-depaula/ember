# Sermon 82

On the Words of the Gospel of Matthew 18:15-18: "If Your Brother Sins Against You, Rebuke Him Between You and Him Alone." and on the Words of Solomon (Proverbs 10:10, According to the Septuagint): "He Who Winks with the Eyes in Guile Heaps Up Sorrow for Men," and What Follows

**1. 1.** Our Lord admonishes us not to neglect one another's sins, not by seeking what to reproach, but by seeing what to correct. For he said that his eye is keen for casting the speck out of his brother's eye, who does not have a beam in his own eye[^1]. But what this is, I briefly convey to your love. The speck in the eye is anger: the beam in the eye is hatred. When, therefore, one who hates reproaches one who is angry, he wishes to take the speck out of his brother's eye: but he is hindered by the beam, which he himself carries in his own eye. The speck is the beginning of the beam. For a beam, when it comes into being, is first a speck. By watering the speck, you bring it to a beam: by feeding anger with evil suspicions, you bring it to hatred.

**2. 2.** But there is much difference between the sin of one who is angry, and the cruelty of one who hates. For we are angry even at our children: who is found to hate his children? In the very beasts too the mother cow sometimes, by a kind of irritation, turns away the suckling calf, angry: but she embraces it with a mother's bowels. She seems to make it troublesome, when she pushes it away: yet it is sought, if it is missing. Nor do we give discipline to our children otherwise than by being a little angry and indignant: nor yet would we give discipline, except by loving. So far is it that everyone who is angry hates, that sometimes he is rather convicted of hating, if he is not angry. For suppose a boy wishes to play in the water of a river, by whose current he may perish: if you see it, and patiently permit it, you hate him; your patience is his death. How much better it is if you are angry and correct him, than if, by not being angry, you permit him to perish? Hatred, then, above all is to be avoided, the beam is to be cast out of the eye. For it is much another thing, when one being angry exceeds in some way the measure of a word, which he afterward blots out by repenting; and another to keep ambushes shut up in the heart. Finally, there is much difference between these words of Scripture: *My eye is troubled for anger*[^2]. But of the other what was said? *He who hates his brother is a murderer*[^3]. There is much difference between an eye troubled, and one extinguished. The speck troubles, the beam extinguishes.

**2. 3.** Let us, then, first persuade ourselves of this, that what we have been admonished today we may be able to do well and fulfill, above all not to hate. For then, when there is no beam in your eye, you see rightly whatever is in your brother's eye; and you suffer constraint, until you cast out of your brother's eye what you see harms your brother's eye. The light that is in you does not permit you to neglect the light of your brother. For if you hate, and wish to rebuke, how do you mend the light, you who have lost the light? For the same Scripture said this too openly, where it is written: *He who hates his brother is a murderer*[^4]. *He who hates*, it says, *his brother is in darkness even now*[^5]. Hatred is darkness. But it cannot be that one who hates another does not first harm himself. For he tries to wound the other outwardly, he lays waste himself inwardly. But by as much as our soul is greater than the body, by so much ought we to provide for it, lest it itself be harmed. But he harms his own soul, who hates another. And what will he do to the one he hates? what will he do? He takes away money, but surely not faith? He harms the reputation, but surely not the conscience? Whatever harm he does, he does outwardly: attend to what harms yourself. For within he is an enemy to himself, who hates another. But because he does not feel what evil he does to himself, he rages against the other, living the more dangerously, the more he does not feel what evil he does with himself; because even by raging he has lost feeling. You have raged against your enemy: by your raging he has been stripped, you are unjust. There is much difference between one stripped and one unjust. He lost money, you innocence. Seek which has suffered the graver loss. He lost a thing about to perish, you have been made one about to perish.

**3. 4.** Therefore we ought to rebuke by loving; not with an eagerness to harm, but with a zeal for correcting. If we are such, we do most excellently what we have been admonished today: *If your brother sins against you, rebuke him between you and him alone*[^6]. Why do you rebuke him? Because you grieve that he has sinned against you? Far be it. If you do it from love of yourself, you do nothing. If you do it from love of him, you do most excellently. Finally, in the very words attend, from whose love you ought to do it, whether your own, or his. *If he hears you*, he says, *you have gained your brother*[^7]. Therefore do it for his sake, that you may gain him. If by doing it you gain him, unless you had done it he had perished. What is it, then, that very many men despise these sins, and say: What great thing have I done? I have sinned against a man. Do not despise it. You have sinned against a man: do you wish to know that by sinning against a man you have perished? If he against whom you sinned rebukes you between you and him alone, and you hear him, he has gained you. What is, he has gained you; except that you had perished, if he had not gained you? For if you had not perished, how has he gained you? Let no one, then, despise it, when he sins against a brother. For the Apostle says in a certain place: *But sinning so against the brothers, and striking their weak conscience, you sin against Christ*[^8]: because we are all made members of Christ. How do you not sin against Christ, who sin against a member of Christ?

**3. 5.** Let no one, then, say, because I did not sin against God, but I sinned against a brother, I sinned against a man: it is a light sin, or no sin. Perhaps you say it is light, because it is quickly healed. You have sinned against a brother: make satisfaction, and you are healed. You quickly did a deadly thing, but you quickly found a remedy. Which of us, my brothers, may hope for the kingdom of heaven, when the Gospel says: *He who says to his brother, You fool, shall be liable to the fire of hell*[^9]? A great terror; but see there the remedy: *If you offer your gift at the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you; leave there your gift before the altar*[^10]. God is not angry that you delay laying your gift: God seeks you more than your gift. For if, bearing an evil mind against your brother, you come with a gift to your God, he answers you: You have perished, what have you brought me? You offer your gift, and you yourself are not the gift of God. Christ seeks more the one whom he redeemed with his blood, than what you found in your barn. So *leave there your gift before the altar, and go first to be reconciled to your brother; and so coming you will offer your gift*[^11]. Behold how quickly that guilt of hell was loosed. Not yet reconciled, you were guilty of hell: reconciled, you securely offer your gift at the altar.

**4. 6.** But men are ready to inflict injuries, and slow to seek concord. Ask, he says, pardon of the man you offended, of the man you harmed. He answers: I will not humble myself. At least hear your God, if you despise your brother: *He who humbles himself will be exalted*[^12]. Are you unwilling to humble yourself, you who have fallen? There is much difference between one humbling himself and one lying prostrate. You already lie prostrate, and are unwilling to humble yourself? You would say well, I do not wish to come down; if you had been unwilling to fall.

**4. 7.** This, then, is what the one who has done the wrong must do. But what must the one who has suffered it do? What we heard today: *If your brother sins against you, rebuke him between you and him alone*[^13]. If you neglect it, you are worse than he. He did the wrong, and in doing the wrong he struck himself with a grievous wound: will you despise your brother's wound? Do you see him perishing, or already perished, and pay no heed? You are worse for keeping silent than he was for insulting you. So when anyone sins against us, let us take great care—not for ourselves, for it is glorious to forget injuries; rather, forget your own injury, but not your brother's wound. So *rebuke him between you and him alone*, aiming at his correction, sparing his shame. For perhaps out of embarrassment he begins to defend his sin, and the one you want to make more corrected you make worse. *Rebuke him,* then, *between you and him alone. If he listens to you, you have gained your brother*[^14]—for he had perished, had you not done it. *But if he does not listen to you*—that is, if he defends his sin as though it were righteousness—*take with you two or three; for at the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall stand. If he does not listen to them, refer it to the Church; if he does not listen even to the Church, let him be to you as a heathen and a tax collector*[^15]. Do not now reckon him in the number of your brothers. Yet not even so is his salvation to be neglected. For even those heathens, that is, the gentiles and pagans, we do not indeed reckon in the number of brothers; but still we always seek their salvation. This, then, is what we heard the Lord admonishing, and prescribing with such care that he even added immediately: *Amen I say to you, whatever you bind upon earth shall be bound also in heaven; and whatever you loose upon earth shall be loosed also in heaven*[^16]. You have begun to hold your brother as a tax collector; you bind him on earth—but see that you bind him justly. For justice breaks unjust bonds. But when you have corrected him and made peace with your brother, you have loosed him on earth. When you have loosed him on earth, he will be loosed also in heaven. You provide much—not to yourself, but to him—because he did much harm, not to you, but to himself.

**5. 8.** Since this is so, what does Solomon mean, which we also heard first today from another reading: *He who winks with the eye deceitfully heaps up sorrow for men; but he who reproves openly makes peace*[^17]? If, then, *he who reproves openly makes peace,* how is it that one should *rebuke him between you and him alone*? We must fear that the divine precepts are at odds with one another. But let us understand that there is the highest concord there, and not be foolish like certain people who err in supposing that the two Testaments, in the old books and the new, contradict each other—so that we might think this a contradiction, since the one is in the book of Solomon, the other in the Gospel. For if some unlearned slanderer of the divine Scriptures were to say: Behold where the two Testaments contradict each other! The Lord says: *Rebuke him between you and him alone.* Solomon says: *He who reproves openly makes peace.* So does the Lord not know what he has commanded? Solomon wants the forehead of the sinner to be struck; Christ spares the shame of the one who blushes. For there it is written: *He who reproves openly makes peace;* but here: *Rebuke him between you and him alone*—not openly, but in secret and in private. Do you wish to know, whoever you are who think such things, that the two Testaments are not in conflict because the one is found in the book of Solomon and the other in the Gospel? Listen to the Apostle. Surely the Apostle is a minister of the New Testament. Listen, then, to the apostle Paul prescribing to Timothy and saying: *Those who sin, reprove before all, that the rest also may have fear*[^18]. Now it is not the book of Solomon that seems to clash with the Gospel, but the Letter of the apostle Paul. Let us set Solomon a little aside, without doing him injury; let us listen to Christ the Lord and to Paul his servant. What do you say, Lord? *If your brother sins against you, rebuke him between you and him alone.* What do you say, Apostle? *Those who sin, reprove before all, that the rest also may have fear.* What are we to do? Do we hear this controversy as judges? Far from it. Rather, set under the judge, let us knock, that it may be granted us to have it opened to us; let us flee under the wings of the Lord our God. For he did not speak something contrary to his Apostle, because he himself spoke in him too, as he says: *Do you wish to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ*[^19]? Christ in the Gospel, Christ in the Apostle: Christ, then, said both—the one with his own mouth, the other with the mouth of his herald. For when a herald says something from the tribunal, it is not written in the records, The herald said; but he is written as having said it who commanded the herald what to say.

**6. 9.** These two precepts, then, brothers, let us so hear that we understand them, and let us be set at peace between the two precepts. Let us be in concord with our own heart, and holy Scripture is in no part discordant. It is altogether true; both are true. But we must discern that sometimes the one and sometimes the other is to be done: sometimes a brother is to be rebuked between you and him alone, sometimes a brother is to be rebuked before all, that the rest also may have fear. If we do sometimes the one and sometimes the other, we will keep the concord of the Scriptures, and in doing and obeying we will not go astray. But someone says to me: When do I do the one, when the other—lest I rebuke between myself and him alone when I ought to rebuke before all, or rebuke before all when I ought to rebuke in secret?

**7. 10.** Your Charity will quickly see what we ought to do and when; but would that we not be slow to do it. Attend, and see: *If your brother sins against you,* he says, *rebuke him between you and him alone*[^20]. Why? Because he sinned against you. What does *he sinned against you* mean? You know that he sinned. For since it was in secret when he sinned against you, seek secrecy when you correct what he sinned. For if you alone know that he sinned against you, and you wish to reprove him before all, you are not a corrector but a betrayer. Notice how a just man, Joseph, spared so great a disgrace as he had suspected of his wife, with such kindness, before he knew whence she had conceived—for he had perceived that she was pregnant, and he knew that he had not gone in to her. There remained, therefore, a sure suspicion of adultery; and yet, because he alone had perceived it, he alone knew it, what does the Gospel say of him? *But Joseph, being a just man, and not willing to expose her*[^21]. The husband's grief did not seek vengeance; he wished to benefit the sinner, not to punish the one sinning. *Since,* it says, *he was not willing to expose her, he wished to dismiss her quietly.* As he was thinking these things, *behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream*[^22], and showed him what it was: that she had not violated her husband's bed, but had conceived by the Holy Spirit the Lord of them both. So your brother sinned against you; if you alone know it, then he truly sinned against you alone. For if he wronged you with many hearing, he sinned also against those whom he made witnesses of his iniquity. For I say, dearest brothers, what you yourselves can recognize in yourselves. When, in my hearing, anyone wrongs my brother, far be it from me to think that wrong is another's and not mine. Indeed he did it to me as well; nay, he did even more to me, to whom he thought that what he did would be pleasing. Therefore those things must be rebuked before all that are sinned before all; those things must be rebuked more privately that are sinned more privately. Distribute the times, and Scripture is in concord.

**8. 11.** So let us act, and so it must be done, not only when someone sins against us, but when someone sins in a way unknown to anyone else. We ought to rebuke in secret, to reprove in secret, lest by wanting to reprove publicly we betray the man. We want to rebuke and correct; but what if an enemy is looking to hear something he may punish? For a bishop knows of some murderer or other, and no one else knows him. I want to rebuke publicly, but you are looking to indict. By no means do I either betray or neglect: I rebuke in secret; I set before his eyes the judgment of God; I terrify his bloodstained conscience; I urge repentance. We ought to be furnished with this love. Hence sometimes people reproach us, as though we do not rebuke; either they think we know what we do not know, or they think we keep silent about what we know. But perhaps what you know, I too know; yet I do not rebuke you in your presence, because I want to cure, not to accuse. There are men who are adulterers in their own homes, who sin in secret; sometimes they are betrayed to us by their wives, often by jealous ones, sometimes by ones seeking their husbands' salvation. We do not betray them openly, but reprove them in secret. Where the evil happened, there let the evil die. Yet we do not neglect that wound; before all else we show the man set in such a sin and bearing a wounded conscience that that wound is deadly—a thing which those who commit it sometimes despise out of some perversity or other; and from somewhere or other they hunt up for themselves empty and worthless testimonies, saying, God does not care about sins of the flesh. Where is what we heard today: *Fornicators and adulterers God will judge*[^23]? Behold, attend, whoever you are who labor under such a disease. Hear what God says, not what your own mind, indulgent to your sins, says to you, or a friend bound with you in the same chain of iniquity—or rather an enemy to you and to himself. Listen, then, to what the Apostle says: *Marriage,* he says, *is honorable in all, and the bed undefiled. But fornicators and adulterers God will judge*[^24].

**9. 12.** Come then, brother, be corrected. You fear lest an enemy indict you; and do you not fear lest God judge you? Where is your faith? Fear when there is reason to fear. The day of judgment is indeed far off; but the last day of each individual man cannot be far off, for life is short. And since that very shortness is always uncertain, you do not know when your last day will be. Correct yourself today, for the sake of tomorrow. Let the rebuke profit you even now, in secret. For I speak openly, but I reprove in secret. I strike the ears of all, but I address the consciences of certain ones. If I were to say, You, adulterer, correct yourself, perhaps first of all I would be saying what I do not know; perhaps I would be suspecting something I had rashly heard. I do not say, You, adulterer, correct yourself; but, whoever among this people is an adulterer, correct yourself. The rebuke is public, but the correction is secret. I know that the one who has feared corrects himself.

**10. 13.** Let him not say in his heart, God does not care about sins of the flesh. *Do you not know,* says the Apostle, *that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you? Whoever violates the temple of God, God will destroy him*[^25]. Let no one deceive himself. But perhaps someone says, My mind is the temple of God, not my body; and he even adds a testimony: *All flesh is grass, and all the glory of the flesh is as the flower of grass*[^26]. Unhappy interpretation! Thought to be punished! Flesh is called grass because it dies; but what dies for a time, let it not rise again with the charge of crime. Do you wish to know an open statement from that very source too? *Do you not know,* says the same apostle, *that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God*[^27]? (Do not now despise bodily sins: behold, because *your bodies too are the temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God.*) Did you despise a bodily sin; do you despise that you sin against the temple? Your very body is the temple of the Spirit of God within you. Now see what you do with the temple of God. If you chose to commit adultery in the church within these walls, what would be more wicked of you? But now you yourself are the temple of God. You enter the temple, you leave the temple, you remain a temple in your house, you rise a temple. See what you do; see that you do not offend the temple's indweller, lest he forsake you and you fall into ruin. *Do you not know,* he says, *that your bodies* (and the Apostle was speaking of this in regard to fornication, lest they despise bodily sins) *are the temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought with a great price*[^28]. If you despise your body, consider your price.

**11. 14.** I know, and with me every man who considers a little more attentively, that no one who fears God fails to correct himself under his words, except the one who thinks he has more life to live. This is the very thing that kills many, when they say, Tomorrow, tomorrow—and suddenly the door is shut. He has remained outside with the raven's croak, because he had not the dove's groan. Tomorrow, tomorrow: the raven's voice. Groan like the dove, and beat your breast; but in giving yourself blows on the breast, be beaten and corrected; so that you do not seem to be beating your conscience, but rather pounding your bad conscience with your fists, making it more solid, not more corrected. Groan not with an empty groan. For perhaps you say to yourself: God promised me pardon when I have corrected myself; I am secure; I read the divine Scripture: *On whatever day the wicked man is converted from his iniquities and does righteousness, I will forget all his iniquities*[^29]. I am secure; when I have corrected myself, God gives me pardon for my evils. And what am I to say? Am I to cry out against God? Am I to say to God: Do not give him pardon? Am I to say that this is not written, that God did not promise this? If I were to say these things, I would say all falsehoods. You speak well, you speak truly; God promised pardon to your correction, I cannot deny it. But tell me, I beg you—behold, I consent and concede and acknowledge that God promised you pardon: who promised you tomorrow? Where you read to me that you will receive pardon if you correct yourself, read me there how long you will live. I do not read it, you say. Then you do not know how long you will live. Be corrected and always ready. Do not fear the last day as a thief who breaks through the wall while you sleep; but watch, and correct yourself today. Why do you put it off until tomorrow? Life will be long. Let that long life be a good one. No one puts off a good meal because it is long, and do you want to have a long evil life? Surely if it will be long, better that it be good; if it will be short, it is well done that a good life be prolonged. But people so neglect their own life that they are unwilling to have a bad one—except that very one. You buy an estate, you look for a good one; you want to take a wife, you choose a good one; you want sons to be born to you, you wish for good ones; you order boots, and you do not want bad ones—and you love a bad life! What has your life done to offend you, that you want it alone to be bad, so that among all your good things you alone are bad?

**12. 15.** Therefore, my brothers, if I wished to rebuke one of you in private, perhaps he would listen to me; I rebuke many of you publicly; all praise me; let someone listen to me. I do not love a praiser in the voice and a despiser in the heart. For when you praise and do not correct yourself, you are a witness against yourself. If you are bad, and what I say pleases you, be displeased with yourself; for if as a bad man you have been displeasing to yourself, once corrected you will be pleasing to yourself—which, unless I am mistaken, I said the day before yesterday. In all my words I set up a mirror. Nor are these my words; but I speak at the Lord's bidding, and at his terrifying command I do not keep silent. For who would not choose to keep silent, and not to render an account for you? But we have already taken up a burden that we cannot, and ought not, shake from our shoulders. You heard, my brothers, when the Letter to the Hebrews was read: *Obey your superiors and be subject to them; for they keep watch over your souls, as those who must render an account for you; that they may do this with joy and not with sorrow, for that is not profitable for you*[^30]. When do we do these things with joy? When we see people making progress in the words of God. When does the laborer work with joy in the field? When he looks at the tree and sees fruit; when he looks at the crop and sees the abundance of grain on the threshing floor: he did not labor without reason, he did not bend his back without reason, he did not wear away his hands without reason, he did not endure cold and heat without reason. This is what he says: *That they may do this with joy and not with sorrow, for that is not profitable for you.* Did he say: It is not profitable for them? No; but he said: *It is not profitable for you.* For when those superiors are saddened over your evils, it is profitable for them; that very sorrow benefits them—but it is not profitable for you. But we want nothing to profit us that does not profit you. Together, then, in the Lord's field, brothers, let us do good work, that together we may rejoice in the reward.
