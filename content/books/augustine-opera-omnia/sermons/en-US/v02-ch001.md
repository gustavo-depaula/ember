# Sermon 20

**1** With one voice and with one heart, praying to the Lord for our very heart, we have said: *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and put a new and right spirit within me*[^1]. From this text, in his grace, we will set before you the little that the Lord may grant. It is the psalm of one who is doing penance, of one who longs to recover lost hope, who lies prostrate from his fall and prays to rise by the Lord's hand, like one who has been able to wound himself but cannot heal himself. For just as we can strike and wound our own flesh whenever we wish, but to have it healed we look for a physician, and we are not healed by our own power as we are injured by our own power, so too the soul is sufficient unto itself for sinning, but for the healing of what it has injured by sinning it implores the healing right hand of God. Hence he says in another psalm: *I said, O Lord, be gracious to me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^2]. He said this, *I said, O Lord*, so as to set before his eyes that the will and the choice of sinning arise from the soul and suffice it to destroy itself, but that it belongs to God to seek what was lost and to save what had wounded itself. For *the Son of man came to seek and to save the lost*[^3]. Pouring out our prayer to him, we say: *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and put a new and right spirit within me*[^4]. Let the soul that has sinned speak, lest, having destroyed itself by transgressing, it perish further by despairing.

**2** Before all else, of course, we must take care not to sin, not to strike up a kind of familiarity and friendship with sin as though with a serpent. For it kills the sinner with its venomous bite, and it is not the sort of thing with which a friendship should be made. But if perhaps it has overpowered the weak, or crept up on the unwary, or caught one who was straying, or deceived one in a second error, let the soul not be reluctant to confess, nor to seek an excuse for itself, but rather an accusation of itself. For this is why he prayed in a certain psalm and said: *Set a guard, O Lord, over my mouth, and a door of restraint about my lips; incline not my heart to evil words, to make excuses for sins*[^5]. Does someone urge a sin upon you? Before all else, let it be refused. But has it been carried through? Let it not be excused but rather accused. For this very man who said *Create in me a clean heart, O God*[^6] began thus: *Have mercy on me, O Lord, according to your great mercy*[^7]. The great sinner implores great mercy. The great wound longs for great medicine. There it is said: *Turn your face away from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities. Create in me a clean heart, O God*[^8]. God therefore turns his face away from the sins of one who confesses and accuses himself and prays for God's help and mercy. For he turns his face away from his sins without turning away from the man himself. For to him to whom it is said, *Turn your face away from my sins*[^9], it is said elsewhere, *Do not turn your face away from me*[^10]. He turns away because he does not take notice; for if he takes notice, he overturns. This is why judges who pronounce sentence against convicted defendants are said to "take notice." That God may not do this, that is, that he may not take notice, we say to him: *Turn your face away from my sins*[^11]. So that he may not recognize, he forgives. And just as we say "noble" and call one who is "not noble" ignoble, so we say "knowing" and call one who does "not know" forgiving. Yet if you wish him to forgive, do you acknowledge. A sin cannot go unpunished; it is not fitting, not right, not just that it go unpunished. Therefore, since a sin ought not to go unpunished, let it be punished by you, lest you be punished on its account. Let your sin have you as its judge, not its advocate. Mount the tribunal of your own mind against yourself, and set yourself before yourself as the accused. Do not place yourself behind yourself, lest God place you before himself. This is why he says in the same psalm, that he may obtain pardon most easily: *For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my sin is ever before me*[^12]. As if to say: Since it is before me, let it not be before you; and since I acknowledge it, do you forgive it. Therefore a sin is punished either by you or by God; but by you it is punished without you, by God it is punished together with you. So let it have you as its punisher, that you may find God your defender. Say: "I did it." *I said, O Lord, be gracious to me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^13]. *I*, he says, *said it.* To excuse my sin, I do not ask who sinned in me, or who forced me to sin. I do not say, "Fortune did it." I do not say, "Fate willed this." Last of all I do not say, "The devil did it." For the devil too has the power to persuade, and finally to terrify, and even, if he is permitted, to inflict grievous troubles. From the Lord must we beg the strength not to be captured by enticements or broken by hardships. Against the enticements and threats of the enemy, let him grant us two virtues: self-restraint and endurance—self-restraint over our desires, lest prosperity capture us; endurance under terrors, lest adversity break us. *And when I knew*, he says, *that no one can be self-controlled unless God grants it*[^14], to him then he said: *Create in me a clean heart, O God*[^15]; and: *Woe to those who have lost their endurance*[^16]. Seek therefore to accuse no one, lest you find an accuser against whom you cannot defend yourself. For our enemy the devil himself, when he is accused, rejoices. He wants you above all to accuse him; he wants to bear from you whatever charge you wish to lay, while you lose your confession. Against his cunning that man cries out: *I said, O Lord*[^17]. The enemy lies in wait for me without cause; I know his wiles, he seeks to catch my tongue, he wants me to say, "The devil did it." *I said, O Lord.* By these tricks, then, he leads souls astray and turns them away from the medicine of confession, either by persuading them to excuse themselves and to look for someone to accuse; or by persuading them, now that they have sinned, to despair and to think they cannot possibly attain pardon at all; or by persuading them that God quickly forgives everything, even if a person does not correct himself.

**3** See how many are the things against which the heart of one doing penance must keep watch. Lest he accuse another by excusing himself, let this come to his mind: *I said, O Lord, be gracious to me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^18]. Lest he perish by despairing, so that, because he has sinned and sinned gravely, he should think he can no longer be healed and give himself over to his lusts, to be dragged off by every desire, doing whatever he pleases though it is not permitted. And if he does not do it, he does not do it where he fears men. With an utterly gladiatorial spirit, since he despairs of life, he does whatever he can to glut his desire and lust, like one devoted to death as a victim. Such men perish through despair. Against these, on their behalf—that is, against such thoughts of theirs—Scripture watchfully said: *On whatever day the wicked man turns and does righteousness, I will forget all his iniquities*[^19]. Yet again—when the soul, restored, has believed these words and been freed from the evil of despair—it finds another pit, and what could not perish through despair perishes through hope. And who is it that perishes through hope? See the kind of man I set before you, one who says in his heart: "God has now promised pardon to all who turn away from their sins; on whatever day they turn, he will forget all their iniquities. Therefore I will do whatever I wish. When I wish, I will turn, and what I have done will be blotted out." What do we say? That God does not care for the penitent whenever he turns? God forgives all that is past. If we deny this, we contradict the divine indulgence, we oppose the words of the prophet, we resist the divine utterances. This is not the way of a faithful steward.

**4** And someone presses me and says: "Will you then give license to sins, so that people may do whatever they wish, since pardon is promised, since impunity is promised once they turn?" They loosen the reins for sinning; they are carried along with great force, with nothing to call them back, made desperate by hope. Is it really so—that Scripture would keep watch against those who despair, and not keep watch against those who hope wrongly? Hear its vigilance against evil and perverse hope: *Do not delay to turn to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day. For his wrath will come suddenly, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you*[^20]. What then, you ill-minded hoper? If you despair, you perish; if you hope, you perish. Where will there be a safe place for you, to snatch yourself from both pits and set yourself on the straight road, serving God, having mercy on your soul, pleasing God? You were despairing wrongly; you heard: *On whatever day he turns, I will forget all his iniquities*[^21]. You had begun to hope wrongly; you heard: *Do not delay to turn to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day*[^22]. On every side the providence of God has mercifully surrounded you. What do you say? "God has promised me pardon; when I turn, he will grant it." Plainly he will grant it when you turn, but why do you not turn? "Because when I turn, he will grant it." Quite so—when you turn, he will grant it; but this "when," when is it? Why not today? Why not when you hear? Why not when you cry out? Why not when you give praise? Let my cry be a helper on your behalf; let your cry be a witness against you. Why not today? Why not now? "Tomorrow," he says. God has promised you indulgence; you promise yourself "tomorrow." Or perhaps, just as you read me out of the holy book that indulgence is promised to you once you have turned, is it likewise promised to you that you may put off tomorrow until tomorrow? Did he not first set this down in his medicinal warning—did he not, when he rebuked you, say this: *Do not put it off from day to day, for his wrath comes suddenly*[^23]? But of course you, wise man, fear lest you should have more than two days of a good life. If there will be a tomorrow, let there be a today as well, and let there be two days. For if there will be no tomorrow, today will find you secure. But if there will be a tomorrow, it is added to today. You, however, long to have a long life and do not fear to have a bad life. You want to live long, and to live badly. You seek a long evil; why not rather a long good? And what is it that you do not want to have good? Will your life alone be the thing that turns out badly in you? If I asked you what sort of garment you want, you answer "a good one"; what sort of estate, "a good one"; what sort of wife, "a good one"; what sort of children, "good ones"; what sort of house, "a good one"; only your life "a bad one." And you set life before all your goods, and among all your goods you want it alone to be bad. For all those things you sought to be good—garment, house, estate, and the rest—you are ready to give for your life. If someone says to you, "Either give me all your goods, or I take your life," you are ready to give all your goods and to hold on even to that bad life. Why do you not want it to be good for you, the life for which you give even all your good things, bad though it is? See, the excuse has been taken away; let accusation be present, that condemnation may not come.

**5** We exhort your Charity not to be reluctant to listen to the priests who minister the word of God diligently and watchfully. For the Lord our God is the very Truth, which you hear through whomever he speaks. And no one among us is greater except the one who has been lesser. We had, then, to make this preface, as is customary. And do you act out of love.
