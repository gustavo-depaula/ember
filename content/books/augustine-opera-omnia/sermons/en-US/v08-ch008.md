# Sermon 279: On the Apostle Paul

**FOR THE FEAST OF HIS CONVERSION**

**1.** We have heard the Apostle’s words—or rather, through the Apostle, the words of Christ speaking in him. Christ made him a preacher out of a persecutor, striking and healing, killing and bringing to life: the Lamb killed by wolves, who makes lambs out of wolves. What happened in Paul had been foretold in a splendid prophecy, when the holy patriarch Jacob blessed his sons, touching those before him and looking ahead to things to come. For Paul, as he himself testifies, was of the tribe of Benjamin. When Jacob, blessing his sons, came to bless Benjamin, he said of him, *Benjamin is a ravenous wolf.* What then? If he is a ravenous wolf, will he always be ravenous? Certainly not. What then? *In the morning he will seize his prey; in the evening he will divide the food*. This was fulfilled in the Apostle Paul, because it had also been foretold of him. Now, if you please, let us consider him seizing his prey in the morning and dividing the food in the evening. *Morning* and *evening* are used as though it said, “Before and afterward.” So let us understand it this way: “Before, he will seize his prey; afterward, he will divide the food.” Look at the plunderer: *Saul,* it says, as the Acts of the Apostles testify, *having received letters from the chief priests to seize and bring in any followers of God’s way whom he found*—for punishment, of course—*went on breathing and panting for slaughter.* There he is, seizing his prey in the morning. For when Stephen, the first martyr for Christ’s name, was stoned, Saul too was conspicuously present. He stood by those who were stoning Stephen in such a way that merely stoning him with his own hands would not have satisfied him. To be involved in the hands of everyone who was throwing stones, he himself guarded all their clothes, raging more fiercely by helping them all than by throwing stones with his own hands. We have heard, *In the morning he will seize his prey;* let us see, *in the evening he will divide the food.* He was struck down by Christ’s voice from heaven, and, receiving an order to cease his violence, he fell on his face: first to be cast down, then to be raised up; first to be struck, then to be healed. For Christ would not afterward have lived in him unless he were put to death in respect of the evil life he had lived before. What, then, did he hear as he lay prostrate? *Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goad.* And he said, *Who are you, Lord?* And the voice from above answered, *I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you are persecuting.* While the members were still on earth, the head cried out in heaven. He did not say, “Why are you persecuting my servants?” but, *Why are you persecuting me?* And Saul said, *What do you want me to do*? He who had previously raged in persecution now prepares to obey. The persecutor is already being formed into a preacher, the wolf into a sheep, the enemy into a soldier. He heard what he should do. He was indeed made blind: so that inward light might illumine his heart, outward light was taken away for a time. It was withdrawn from the persecutor to be restored to the preacher. Yet at the very time when he saw nothing else, he saw Jesus. Thus even in his blindness a mystery concerning believers was taking shape: whoever believes in Christ must look upon him and reckon everything else as though it had never come into being, so that creation may lose its value and the Creator grow sweet in the heart.

**2.** Let us look, then. He was brought to Ananias, and Ananias means “Sheep.” See: the ravenous wolf is brought to the sheep to follow it, not to seize it. But lest the sheep be terrified by the wolf’s sudden arrival, the shepherd himself, who was doing all this from heaven, told the sheep that the wolf was coming, but would not rage. Yet such a fearsome reputation had gone before that wolf that the sheep could not hear his name without alarm. For when the Lord Jesus informed this Ananias that Paul had now come to believe, and that Ananias ought to go to him, Ananias said, *Lord, I have heard of this man, that he has done much evil to your saints. And now he has received letters from the chief priests to drag away any followers of your name whom he finds*. But the Lord said to him, *Let it be; and I will show him what he must suffer for my name*. A wonderful and great thing is happening. The wolf is forbidden to rage; the wolf is brought captive to the sheep. Yet such a reputation had gone before the ravenous wolf that, on hearing his name, the sheep was afraid even under the shepherd’s hand. It is reassured, so that it will no longer think him savage or fear him as arrogant. The Lamb who died for the sheep makes the sheep feel safe from the wolf.

**3.** Next, consider him to whom we sang last Sunday, *Lord, who is like you? Do not be silent, and do not be gentle, O God*, and who nevertheless says, *Come to me, and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart*. Let us see how he displays both, and shows in himself that his words agree. He is gentle and humble of heart, because *like a sheep he was led to slaughter, and like a lamb silent before its shearer, so he did not open his mouth*. Hanging on the wood, he endured the unjust flames of hatred; he bore with tongues serving utterly wicked hearts. With those tongues they struck the spotless one and crucified the righteous one. Of their tongues it had been said, *The sons of men—their teeth are weapons and arrows, and their tongue a sharp sword.* And what did the tongue do? What did the sharp sword do? It killed. What did it kill? Death killed life, so that life might kill death. What, then, did their tongue, that sharp sword, do? Hear what it did; see what follows: *Be exalted above the heavens, O God, and let your glory be over all the earth*. That is what the sharp sword did. We know the Lord has been exalted above the heavens, not by seeing but by believing; we know his glory is over all the earth by reading, believing, and seeing. See him, then, gentle and humble of heart, so that he might bring the trophy of his slain flesh to this glory. See how gentle he is. As he hung there he said, *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing*; and, *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart.* Let us learn from you, for you are gentle and humble of heart. Where could this have appeared more clearly, or where should it more fittingly have appeared, than on the cross itself? While his limbs hung on the wood, while his hands and feet were fastened with nails, while they still raged with their tongues, while the shedding of his blood did not satisfy them, while the sick failed to recognize their physician, he said, *Father,* *forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing.* It was as though he said, “I have come to heal the sick; it is the violence of their fever that keeps them from recognizing me.” Gentle and humble of heart, then, he says, *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing.*

**4.** What, then, of *Do not be silent, and do not be gentle, O God*? Let him fulfill this too. See, he was not silent: he cried out from heaven, *Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me*? He fulfilled *Do not be silent;* let him also display *And do not be gentle.* First, he did not spare Paul’s error or his savagery: with his voice he struck down the man panting for slaughter, took away the raging man’s sight, and brought him captive to Ananias, whom he had been seeking to persecute. See him ungentle, see him fierce, against the error, not against the man. This is not enough: let him still neither be silent nor be gentle. To Ananias, frightened and trembling at hearing the name of that notorious wolf, he says, *I will* *show him.* I will show him. See him threatening, see him still fierce: *I will show him. Do not be silent, and do not be gentle, O God.* Show the persecutor not only your goodness but also your severity. Show him: let him suffer what he has done; let him learn to suffer what he used to inflict, and himself feel what he inflicted on others. He says, *I will* *show him what he must suffer.* He speaks as though in anger, fulfilling what was said: *Do not be silent, and do not be gentle, O God.* Let him not depart from this: *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart.* I will show him what he must suffer for my name*.* You have shown your terror; come to his aid, so that the one you made, the one you found, may not suffer and perish. He threatens; he is not silent or gentle; he threatens: *I will show him what he must suffer for my name.* Where there is terror, there is salvation. Let the man who acted against the name suffer for the name. What merciful severity! You see him preparing the knife: he will cut, not destroy; he will heal, not kill. Christ was saying, *I will show him what he must suffer for my name.* But to what end? Listen to the very man who was suffering: *The sufferings of this present time are not worthy.* The sufferer himself says this; he knew for whose name he was suffering and what fruit his suffering would bear. *The sufferings of this present time are not worthy of the future glory that will be revealed in us*. Let the world rage, let the world roar, let it threaten with its tongues and flash its weapons; let it do whatever it can. What will it do in comparison with what we are to receive? I weigh what I suffer against what I hope for. The one I feel; the other I believe. And yet what I believe carries more weight than what I feel. Whatever rages against us for Christ’s name is bearable if we can survive it; if we cannot survive it, it makes us depart from here. It does not extinguish life; it hastens something. What does it hasten? The reward itself, the sweetness itself, which, when it comes, will have no end. The labor has an end; the reward has none.

**5.** This man, then, brothers and sisters, this chosen vessel, was at first Saul, named after Saul. Those of you who know God’s Scriptures will remember who Saul was: a very wicked king, a persecutor of God’s holy servant David. He too, if you remember, was of the tribe of Benjamin. Hence this man was Saul, following the same path of violence, but not destined to remain in it. Later, if Saul was named after Saul, where did Paul come from? Saul came from a savage king, when he was proud, when he was savage, when he was panting for slaughter. But where did Paul come from? Paul, because he was little. Paul is a name of humility. He became Paul after he was brought to the Teacher who says, *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart.* That is why he is Paul. Notice the usage of the Latin language: “paulum” means “a little.” “I will see you a little later”; “wait here a little”—that is, “I will see you after a short time”; “wait here a short time.” Listen, then, to Paul: *I am,* he says, *the least of the apostles*. Yes indeed, *I am the least of the apostles;* and elsewhere, *I am the last of the apostles*.

**6.** Both the least and the last, like the fringe of the Lord’s garment. What is so small, what so far down, as a fringe? Yet by touching it, the woman was healed of her flow of blood. In this little man the great one was present; in the least, the mighty one dwelt. The smaller he was, the less he excluded the great one from himself. Why are we surprised that the great one dwells in a narrow space? He dwells especially in the smallest. Hear him saying, *Upon whom will my Spirit rest? Upon the humble and quiet person who trembles at my words*. The exalted one dwells in the lowly in order to exalt the lowly. For *the Lord is exalted* *and looks upon the lowly, but the lofty he knows from afar*. Humble yourself, and he will draw near to you; exalt yourself, and he will withdraw from you.

**7.** What, then, does this least one say? What we heard today: *With the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation*. Many believe in their hearts and are ashamed to confess with their mouths. Know, brothers and sisters, that there is now almost no pagan who does not privately marvel and recognize that the prophecies about Christ exalted above the heavens are being fulfilled, because they see his glory over all the earth. But by fearing one another and feeling ashamed before one another, they put salvation far from themselves, because *with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.* What good is it to have believed with the heart unto righteousness if the mouth hesitates to express what has been conceived in the heart? God sees faith within, but that is not enough. You fear the proud, and so will not confess the humble one; you prefer the proud to him who, for your sake, incurred the displeasure of the proud. You are afraid to confess the Son of God in his humility. You are not ashamed to confess the great Word of God, the power of God, the wisdom of God; you are ashamed to confess him born, crucified, and dead. Lofty, exalted, and equal to the Father, he through whom all things were made, through whom you too were made, became what you are. For your sake he became a man; for your sake he was born; for your sake he died. Sick person, how will you be healed if you are ashamed of your medicine? Choose your moment. Now is the time. Later, he who was despised will come to be marveled at; he who was judged will come to judge; he who was killed will come to raise the dead; he who was dishonored will come to bestow honor. Now and afterward: now the matter rests on faith; afterward it will be manifest. Choose now which side you will be on in the future. Are you ashamed of Christ’s name? Because you are ashamed before people now, you will have cause for shame when he comes in his glory to give the good what he promised and the wicked what he threatened. Where will you be? What will you do if that exalted one looks at you and says, “You were ashamed of my humility; you will have no place in my glory”? Let harmful shame depart, then; let saving boldness come—if it should be called shamelessness. Still, brothers and sisters, I have forced myself to say it without any fear.

**8.** For I do not want us to be ashamed of Christ’s name. Let people insult us because we believe in one who was crucified, one who was killed. Yes, indeed, in one who was killed; but if his blood had not flowed, the written bond of our sins would still remain. Certainly, I have believed in one who was killed; but what was killed in him was what he took from me, not that by which he made me. Certainly, I believe in one who was killed—but who was this one who was killed? Someone came and took something upon himself. Who came? *He who, being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal to God.* That is who came. What did he take upon himself? *But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men*. The maker was made; the Creator was created. But in what respect was he made and created? In the form of a servant: by taking the form of a servant, not by losing the form of God. In this form of a servant, then, in what he took from us for our sake, he was born, suffered, rose again, and ascended into heaven. I have named four things: he was born, died, rose again, and ascended into heaven. Two came first, two last. The first two: he was born and died. The last two: he rose again and ascended into heaven. In the first two he showed you your own condition; in the last two he gave you a pattern of your reward. You knew birth and death: the realm of mortals is full of these two things. What abounds here in all flesh but birth and death? Human beings share this with the beasts; thus we lead this life in common with the beasts. We have been born; we shall die. This you did not yet know: rising again and ascending into heaven. You knew two things, and did not know two others. He took upon himself what you knew; he showed you what you did not know. Endure what he took upon himself; hope for what he showed you.

**9.** What, after all? If you do not want to die, does that mean you will not die? Why fear what you cannot avoid? You fear what will happen even if you do not want it, and you do not fear what will not happen if you do not want it. What do I mean? For all human beings who are born, God has appointed death, through which they depart from this world. You will be exempt from death if you are exempt from the human race. What are you doing? Are you now being told, “Choose whether you want to be a human being”? You already are one; you have arrived. Think about how you are to leave here. You have been born; you will die. Flee, take precautions, fight it off, buy yourself free: you can postpone death, but you cannot remove it. It will come even if you do not want it; it will come when you do not know. Why, then, fear what will happen even if you do not want it? Fear instead what will not happen if you do not want it. What is that? God has threatened the impious, unbelievers, blasphemers, perjurers, the unjust, and all the wicked with the fires of burning hell and its eternal flames. First compare these two things: a moment’s death and eternal punishments. You fear a moment’s death; it will come even if you do not want it. Fear eternal punishments, which will not come if you do not want them. What you ought to fear is much greater, and you have it in your power to keep it from coming upon you. What you ought to fear is greater, far greater, incomparably greater, and you have it in your power to keep it from coming upon you. For whether you live well or badly, you will die. You will not escape death either by living well or by living badly. But if you choose to live well here, you will not be sent to eternal punishments. Since you cannot choose here not to die, choose while you live not to die forever. This is the faith; this is what Christ showed by dying and rising again. By dying he showed what you will suffer whether you wish it or not; by rising again he showed what you will receive if you live well. Here *with the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.* But you are afraid to confess, lest people insult you—not people who have not believed, for they too believe inwardly, but people who are ashamed to confess. Hear what follows: *For Scripture says, Everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame.* Meditate on these things; remain in them. This is food for the mind, not the belly. The man who seized his prey in the morning was dividing this very food in the evening.

**10.** Since our lord and father bids me speak to you about this too, listen a little more attentively. Through the mercy and work of our chief shepherd, prey has been torn from the wolf’s jaws: we announce it to your ears, and you see it with your eyes. The shepherd has brought in the man about whom the flock cried out. The Lord has not abandoned his servants in their hearts’ distress, but wishes to commend the sweetness of his mercy, *making*, as it is written, *his mercies* *wonderful,* so that distress may precede the joys to follow. The man denounced as an enemy of the Christian faith has embraced the Christian faith. We too could have said what Ananias said, and perhaps some did say it, or perhaps some still say it: “Who? That man a Christian? Has he believed?” We can neither see a human heart nor show it to others. God says, *The things that are manifest belong to you; the hidden things belong to me.* The Apostle Paul says, *Brothers and sisters, judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes and brings to light the hidden things of darkness and makes manifest the thoughts of the heart; and then each will have praise from God.* You cannot look into a new Christian’s heart. Well, can you look into an old Christian’s heart? You will say, “But he believed out of necessity.” The same could be said of the man we were discussing a little earlier, who was at first *a blasphemer, a persecutor, and a violent man.* For a certain necessity was imposed on him too. He was struck down by a heavenly voice; to gain light, he lost light. Threaten any person with whatever you please, and give him as much as you please: what is sweeter than this light? Yet unless Paul had lost it, he would not have received eternal light. “He believed out of necessity.” Tell me, what did he fear? What did he fear? The sheep crying out? Sheep can cry out; they cannot bite. Even in the cry of God’s sheep he could have perceived God’s glory and feared God’s judgment. He was, so to speak, awakened from sleep to consider that what had been foretold about Christ was being fulfilled in Christ. He could have said in his heart that his gods had been defeated in his own person, that his gods had deserted him, that Christ’s name had such power and Christ’s glory such strength. So I will say briefly to Your Charity—I am addressing God’s Church, I am addressing God’s people—if he believed, you have found him; if he feared, you have won.

**11.** Meanwhile, brothers and sisters, let us stay within what human beings can do, and not claim what has not been granted to us. The Apostle says, *Welcome the one who is weak in faith, without passing judgment on his thoughts*. Let us not presume to judge others’ thoughts, but offer God our prayers even for those about whom we may have some doubt. Perhaps his very newness leaves him with some doubts: love the doubter all the more; by your love remove doubt from the weak person’s heart. For now, look upon his face and rejoice; entrust his heart to God and pray for it. Know that the wicked are abandoning him; let him be welcomed by you. Love the man more than you previously hated his error, for even when you cried out against him, you were seeking him. Be confident that your cries were not in vain, and rejoice that the one you were seeking has been found. “Who did this and that?” “Faustinus.” “Who did this and that?” “Faustinus.” “Who was against Christ?” “Faustinus.” “Who has feared Christ?” “Faustinus.” This is how Christ came to heal the sick, as we heard of him in the Gospel: It is not the healthy who need a physician, but those who are ill. And, *What* *man, having lost one sheep, does not leave the ninety-nine in the mountains and go to seek the one that was lost? And when he has found it, he rejoices over it. So my Father rejoices over one who repents more than over ninety-nine righteous people who have no need of repentance*. This is precisely how Christ came to heal the sick; this is how he knows to take vengeance on his enemies. Those who shared his error may be pained at heart; for the moment they are angry, but later perhaps they will imitate him. So, brothers and sisters, we commend him to your prayers, to your love, to your most faithful friendship, and to your support for his weakness. As you lead, so he follows. Teach him the good way; let him find it in you. Now that he has become a Christian, let him discern the difference between what he has left and what he has found. The time to come will prove his life and his devotion to faith in Christ.

**12.** But now, my brothers and sisters, there was no need, nor was it the pastors’ considered judgment, to turn away someone knocking or put off someone seeking. To insist on judging the hidden things of the heart while refusing to accept an openly spoken word was neither our judgment nor our intention. For we know how that merciful eagerness of the Lord, seeking returns on his money from every quarter, threatens the lazy servant who wished to judge what he could not see and was sluggish about gathering his master’s profits: *Wicked servant, out of your own mouth I condemn you. You said I was a hard man, reaping where I had not sown and gathering where I had not scattered.* So you knew my eagerness for gain. *You* *should have given my money to the bankers, and on my return I would have demanded it with interest*. We, therefore, could do nothing but distribute the Lord’s money. He will be the one who collects the debt, not only from this man but from all of us. So let us fulfill the office of the distributor and not presume to take the collector’s place. Brothers and sisters, this work set before your eyes is God’s, not ours. We did not plan what has happened, for we did not even hope for it. Your intention and ours was something else. You know what was shouted here; you know: “Let those in authority not be pagans; let pagans not rule over Christians.” These things were said; and because this name was an object of resentment, Christians cried out many things against it in their zeal for God’s house. Their whole intention was simply that a pagan should not rule over Christians. That the man against whom they cried out should become a Christian was not even contemplated by the Christians, but it was being arranged by Christ. What is written was truly fulfilled: *Many are the thoughts in a man’s heart, but the Lord’s counsel remains forever*. This plan lay hidden—hidden, but about to take effect. Human beings were doing what they could, but Faustinus the banker emerged a new man from Christ’s workshop. So, brothers and sisters, keep God’s work in mind. You were seeking one thing and arranging one thing, and found another. As servants speaking to fellow servants, we commend our Lord’s work. Let us love in this man what our Lord has done more than what we intended to do, for his works are better. We have heard his strong and devoted declaration: “I do not want authority; I want to be a Christian.” Rejoice, exult, love him more than you hated him. With your prayers commend Christ’s work to Christ. Show a faithful, devout, and friendly spirit toward this old man’s first beginnings. What difference does it make, after all, that you see a man already advanced in years? He has come to the vineyard at the ninth hour, to receive an equal wage.

**13.** We remind Your Charity of a Christian feast day, although it cannot have been erased from your hearts by forgetfulness. We commend it to you because pagans and the impious also appear to celebrate this same day for certain other reasons of their own, which are gradually passing away. They celebrate it badly, wickedly, unhappily; but how many are being freed from this is before your eyes. Those customs will grow old and pass away, but do not support them. Set yourselves apart from them; seek divine things. We are going to celebrate the day of Saint John—John the Baptist, the Lord’s forerunner, the bridegroom’s friend—with all chastity and all sobriety. As they marvel that you are different from them in your rejoicing, they gradually follow you; and all those things will grow old and perish. Hear the prophet and see the fulfillment; see what was foretold being accomplished: *Listen to me, you who know judgment.* It is the prophet Isaiah, and God speaking through him: *Listen to me, you who know judgment, my people, in whose hearts is my law. Do not fear the reproaches of men, and do not be overcome by their slander; nor consider it a great matter that they despise you. For like a garment they will be worn away by time, and like wool they will be eaten by moths. But my righteousness remains forever*. Be confident, then, brothers and sisters; be entirely confident. They are growing old and diminishing; they will come to an end, either by believing or by dying. However much noise they make, however much they devote themselves to fleshly pleasure, however much they chatter obscenities and dance in opposition to Christ’s divine songs, they are fewer today than yesterday. So, brothers and sisters, tomorrow, as I said, we will celebrate in the Lord’s name the feast of Saint John the Baptist. Seven days from now, that is, on Saturday, we will also celebrate the heavenly birthday of the holy martyrs Peter and Paul.
