# Sermon 114b

**1.** The gospel reading, dearly beloved, which we have just now heard—not indeed procured by us, as is wont to be done, but yet by the appointment of the Lord, who governs our acts—accords vehemently with this psalm of which we have determined to speak to Your Charity. For the Lord was speaking of his last coming and the end of the age, and had already recalled many terrible things which human affairs must suffer, as the end draws near [^1]. Then, rebuking those who want to live secure, not in the region of security, he terrified them dreadfully; saying that the coming of the Son of man to that judgment—a thing to be trembled at by all, but yet even to be desired by the devout faithful—would be thus; saying, then, that his coming would be thus, as it was in the days of Noah, he gravely terrified every heart, if faith is there: *For as*, he says, *in the days of Noah, they ate and drank, married, took wives, bought, sold, while the ark was being built by Noah, and the flood came and destroyed them all* [^2]. For they were living a ruinous security, all worldly joys, and in these they delighted, until Noah entered the ark, and the flood found them naked and destitute. So saying this, even now he terrified every soul, but we have a time of waking up. The day of judgment is not yet, the flood is not yet; still the incorruptible timbers are being cut from the wood, still the ark is being built [^3].

**2.** For if those were fools, and by the merit of their own madness and contempt perished, who did not say in their heart: "Not without cause does this servant of God, just, accepted by God and wise, with so great effort, with so great works, build so great an ark, except because he knew something to threaten the age. In a way the building of this ark is a herald crying: Turn to God." For if they thought this and changed their life and, turning themselves from impiety to God, made satisfaction to him for their offenses, groaning to his mercy, they would not have perished. For God was not merciful in Nineveh [^4] and would be cruel to the whole human race, if it turned itself. *In three days Nineveh will be overthrown* [^5], said Jonah, on account of their excessive sins. What so brief as three days? Yet they did not, in so great a narrowness of time, despair of God's mercy: to bend his clemency they believed even three days' lamentation and tears to suffice. If, then, so great a city had a space of three days to bend the mercy of God, through the space of a hundred years, in which the ark was built, if those men changed their ways and conduct, by appeasing God offered the sacrifice of a contrite heart [^6], would they not without any doubt escape unharmed that destruction by the mercy of him whom they had bent? So the three days of the Ninevites convict the hundred years of the ark being built. But there is another greater than Noah: see how great a time it is, see, from which he builds this ark. I think, brothers, that if the years are reckoned from which Christ began, by cutting incorruptible timbers from the wood of the nations, to set up and build the framework of this ark—that is, the Church—more are found than a hundred and than two hundred and than three hundred. And behold, so many years have passed, and still it is being built, still Noah cries out, still the building itself cries out. Nothing will destroy men, except unbelief. Let them change their ways; let them believe God promising so much, threatening so much, deceiving in nothing.

**3.** It is long to say many things from here, and on account of the narrowness of the time and our common fragility we hasten to the treatment of this psalm. But I would wish someone to tell me briefly, why he does not change his evil ways and make them good. What does he lose by it? If he believes, let him do it, because it will be true; if he doubts, let him do it, lest it be true. For believers it is firm, for doubters it is doubtful. I enumerate so many things from the beginning of the age to this day, which happened according to the Scriptures, so that we read absolutely nothing in the Scriptures of God which we do not already see fulfilled in great part. Very few things remain: are those alone false? Will the few things that remain be false? Brothers, what great thing is it? We desire a work of a middling heart. Number the many past things that happened, and believe the few that remain. Let the faithful man consider, that it is most firmly true; or at least let him consider this, lest perhaps it be true.

**4.** For example, you had a journey by a short way, and you had proposed to go by the better road. Someone reported to you, absolutely anyone, that that road was beset by robbers. This road, which was reported to you to be beset by robbers, is level, easy, pleasant, hospitable, delightful; but someone reported to you that it was infested by robbers and so beset, that either it could not be passed or could be passed only with the greatest difficulty and peril. There is another road: it has much labor, difficulty, hardness, narrowness; delights are not only not found there, but scarcely fitting hospitality is shown. Does not your heart say to you at once, wanting to gain a few days of this life and looking ahead to a life one day to end: "We go better this way; even with labor and difficulty and want, even with the wearing down both of ourselves and of our beasts, yet we go better this way"? Why better? Because safer. But now someone wanting to lead you by that delightful road says to you: "And have you so quickly believed the one who said this road was beset by robbers?"; if you know the man to be faithful—and perhaps so known to you that he never deceived you—what will you answer? "It cannot be that that man deceives me; I know the man, I have proved him a serious man, I hold many proofs of him: he always told me the truth, he never lied." But you knew the man to be such: let us set another, who did not know him. Would not this one too say: "I indeed do not know the man and am ignorant how truthful he is: it can be that he tells the truth, it can be that he lies; yet on account of that very doubt, lest perhaps what he says be true, why do I not rather endure the laborious expense, than undergo the delightful road with peril?"

**5.** Come now, my brothers, we are Christians; we all want to make a journey; even if we do not want to, we make a journey: no one is allowed to remain here, the rolling of the times compels all coming into this life to pass on. There is no place for sloth: walk, lest you be dragged. There meets us, as we make our journey and are set, as it were, at a certain fork in the road, a certain man, not a man, but God, on account of men a man; he said to us: "Do not go this way; this passage seems indeed easy and light and delightful, worn by many and broad, but the end of this road has destruction. Since to stand and to dwell here you are neither permitted nor is it expedient for you, you must go, but go this way: through certain difficulties you are to walk, but the difficulty quickly ended you will come to a great breadth of joys, and you will escape those ambushes which no one escapes who wants to walk that way." This one said this—I think, because he is known to us, if faith is in us. Or does it still please us to explore the faith of this one? Let us recall past times and the ancient Scriptures. Is not this man the Word of God? Was not the *Word* afterward *made flesh and dwelt among us* [^7]? But before it was made flesh and dwelt among us, did it not itself speak through the patriarchs and prophets? See what they announced to the human race.

**6.** God spoke, surely by his Word, to Abraham, that his nation would first come to be—when he himself, to whom it was said, by reason of old age now was not—first there would come to be a nation from the aged Abraham and from Sarah, an old and barren woman: it was believed, it was done [^8]; that this very nation, that is, the people thence born according to the flesh, would serve in Egypt and through how many years: it was done; that it would be freed from that captivity: it was freed; that it would receive the land of promise [^9]: it received it. Many things were said through the prophets; and it was said to Abraham himself that he should attend not only to that people, but *in your seed*, he says, *all the nations will be blessed* [^10]. Near things were said, things far in the future were said: the near things were done; the things that were far in the future are now being done. The word of God spoke through the prophets that that nation would sin, would be handed into the hands of its enemies, because it offended the Lord: all things were done; that it would come into the captivity of Babylon: this too was done; that thence a king, Christ, would come: Christ too came, Christ was born; because he himself foretold himself to be going to come, Christ too came. It was said that the Jews would crucify him: they crucified him. It was foretold that he would rise again and be glorified: it was done, he rose again, ascended into heaven. It was foretold that in his name all the earth would believe, it was foretold that kings would persecute his Church: these things were done. It was foretold that kings would believe in him: we already hold the faith of kings, and do we doubt about the faith of Christ? The cleavings of heresies too were foretold: do we not see them too, and groan amid them, clamoring on every side? It was foretold that the nations would press the Church on account of the idols: and it was done; it was foretold that the idols themselves were to be destroyed through the Church and the name of Christ: and this too we see fulfilled. Scandals were foretold in the Church itself, weeds were foretold, chaff was foretold: all these we both behold with our eyes and, with whatever fortitude bestowed by the Lord we can, we tolerate. In what did this one deceive you, who said to you: "Go this way"? Say securely, if you are faithful, taking so many proofs of this one who speaks to you—prove him by deeds, because he deigned so to be proved. Say: "He tells me the absolute truth, I hold all his sayings as true, in nothing did he lie. Such I have known him, he is the word of God. Through the mouths of his servants he spoke, and did not deceive: can what he says by his own mouth deceive?" But the one to whom he is not yet known, who still doubts about Christ, let him too say: "I will go this way, lest perhaps he tell the truth, in whom now the whole world has believed"; and yet it will so come about as he foretold.

**7.** Brothers, many who do not believe will be found on the last day as that multitude was found in the days of Noah. None escaped, except those who were in the ark. Such men, then, accommodate yourselves: the craftsman's hands are here, Christ builds the ark. Fit yourselves to it, give yourselves to his hands, be squared, be joined together; let no one refuse the fingers of this craftsman. His grace knows how to compose you of itself; only do not you be the rotten wood of perverse pride. So it will be; yet, my brothers, many mock these things.  
I see the narrowness of the time withdrawing from me the exposition of the psalm. For I do not want to leave unfulfilled the place of the Gospel undertaken, as far as the Lord suggests. For the present, then, if it please Your Charity, let us defer the psalm. It is not drawn out long: soon there will be the gift, as they call it. We too have a presenter, to whom we may assemble. If the peoples run from that broad road [^11], mocking and jeering that faithful shower of the way, to watch a gift which they are not to receive—and whoever would receive it would deceive himself; yet to that which they are not to receive, they run, they flow together—how much more eagerly ought we to assemble, who are to receive what we are to watch! If from me, that you may not assemble, nothing is poorer than I; but if from him from whom I too am, nothing is richer than he, nothing richer than that poor one for our sake [^12]. From him let us all receive, in him let us all rejoice. And if perhaps he shows you through me what he deigns to give, love the presenter's servant too, but on account of the presenter, because I too, brothers, in him and on account of him love you. For apart from him we all are nothing.

**8.** So briefly what of the gospel reading is perhaps obscure to anyone I will not keep silent about, with the Lord's help. Let men fear, lest they be found thus on that last day. Let us fear, my brothers. We now exult, rejoice, acclaim. I ask you: let that day find us ready. He does not lie who says it, he never lied; if you still doubt, beware lest perhaps it be true. But one of you says to me: "It is necessary, then, that it sink into the heart of the faithful"; for I am not going, merely by speaking these things now, to make all such as the Lord said: *If anyone does not take up his cross and follow me* [^13], or to make all such as the Lord said: *If you want to be perfect, go, sell all your goods, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven, and come, follow me* [^14]. Brothers, is that way then to be feared, where the leader says: *Follow me?* I know that I am not going to make all to whom I speak, or certainly very many, suddenly such. Now, then, because it has been heard from the thunder of the Gospel—if there are faithful hearts that take fright—because it was said: *In the days of Noah it was thus, they ate, drank, married, took wives, bought, sold, until Noah entered the ark, and the flood came and destroyed them all* [^15], many say to themselves: "We are bidden to await that day, and not to be found such as those were found outside the ark who perished in that flood. Certainly the word of God terrifies us, the gospel trumpet terrifies us. What do we do, if a wife is not to be taken?"—a young man says this, some youth—"is there to be no eating, no drinking, must one always fast?" Many say these things. And one who perhaps wanted to buy something says to himself: "Is something now no longer to be bought, lest I be found in the number of those who perished?"

**9.** What then do we do, if it is so? It must be wept over, as the apostles were saddened over the human race, when they heard the Lord saying what perfection was: *Sell all your goods and come, follow me* [^16], because the one to whom it was said departed saddened [^17]. And when he called him a good master, of whom he was seeking counsel of eternal life [^18], he seemed a good master, so long as he should say what was asked. He said it, and saddened him. But as that man departed sad, the Lord followed and said: *How hard a rich man enters the kingdom of heaven!* [^19] Hard, but yet at least hard. But there was added a following example by the Lord, and what he had called hard he pronounced impossible. *It is easier*, he says, *for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle, than a rich man into the kingdom of heaven* [^20]. As though he shut it against the rich. What will happen, what will happen? It is shut: *Knock and it will be opened to you* [^21]. "With what," he says, "shall we knock?" With what, except with hands? "What is: with what, except with hands?" With what, except with works? Let us see, brothers, whether the Lord offered the rich too a place for such works. Let us say this from the Scriptures, lest we be found not so much announcers as flatterers. First the same Lord in that very place: *Sell all your goods and come, follow me*. For the disciples were saddened, because the Lord said this. And saddened surely not about themselves, for they themselves had left all and were following the Lord [^22]. And saddened, what did they say? *Who then can be saved* [^23]? Now here I question the apostles: O chief members of Christ, O pillars of that resurrection to be made firm, why so, why *who then can be saved?*; has despair been made about the rich? The rich are few, thousands of the poor will be able to be saved. What did the Lord say? *It is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle, than a rich man into the kingdom of heaven*. Did he say: "It is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle, than a man into the kingdom of heaven"? He said: a rich man. Quickly may I say it, brothers—we all indeed wish well, because this is commanded and this is hoped, but yet I attend in the Scriptures to a coming heap of chaff to be consumed by fire [^24], and I can say: Would that as few went into the fire, as few as the rich are! Behold, brothers, in this whole people that hears these things, how many are rich? I did not say this, that they themselves should go into the fire; but however many are to go, and those who are to go, would that they could be as few, as few as the rich are in the human race! But now both from the number of the rich many are to go into the kingdom of heaven, and from the number of the poor many are to go into eternal fire. Until I open this in few words, attend.

**10.** The Lord said that it was easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle, than a rich man into the kingdom of heaven [^25]. And the disciples, saddened, said: *Who then can be saved* [^26], attending to so many thousands of the poor and in the human race so few rich. But what? They attended prudently, not who was rich in means, but who burned with greed. Someone is called and appears rich by abundance of all things. But this man holds all those things for nothing, despises them and truly holds them as a possessor, is not held, as it is written: *His hope is in the Lord his God* [^27]; not arrogant, not boastful, not overbearing to oppress the poor, not greedy, not gaping after the goods of others, not badly guarding and hoarding his own, but truly rich toward God [^28] and not reckoning those riches except the giver of riches, such a one is both rich and enters the kingdom of heaven. For to the saddened disciples the Lord said: "*What is hard for men is easy for God* [^29]. You were moved by the difficulty, because I named a camel through the eye of a needle, and what is truly hard and impossible for men is most easy for God. If he wills, even that most enormous beast, which is called a camel, he passes through the eye of a needle." And he deigned to do this of himself, and therefore even a rich man can enter the kingdom of heaven, because for his sake the camel entered through the eye of a needle. What is this? Let us see, whether it appears. For not without cause did John the Baptist too, the herald of that same Lord, have a garment of camel's hair [^30], because, with that judge to come after him before whom he went, he had, as it were, a cloak from him. I clearly recognize, the camel being named, a certain figure of my Lord; I recognize the great one, and yet with a lowly neck, I recognize the great one, whom no one would burden with sufferings, unless he set himself down on the earth. I see too the eye of the needle, through which that so great one entered. In the needle I understand the pricking, in the pricking the passion, in the eye the straits. Now then the camel has entered through the eye of the needle: let not the rich despair, who will follow into the kingdom of heaven.

**11.** But what kind of rich? Behold, someone off to the side, covered in rags, exulted and laughed, when it was said that a rich man does not enter the kingdom of heaven. "I," he says, "will enter. These rags will procure this for me; they will not enter who do us wrongs, who oppress us." Plainly such will not enter, but you too see whether you will enter. What if you are poor and greedy, what if you are pressed by want and burn with greed? If, then, you are such, whoever you are who are poor, you did not refuse to be rich, but you could not. *Peace*, he says, *on earth to men of good will* [^31]. God therefore does not regard your means, but your will. Attend to what your heart is full of, not to what your chest is empty of. So if you are such, of an evil life, of an evil wish, withdraw from the number of God's poor; you will not be among those of whom it is said: *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven* [^32]. Behold I find such a rich man—from comparison with whom you boasted and dared to aspire to the kingdom of heaven—I find one poor in spirit, that is, humble, devout, following the will of God and, if perhaps he loses something of that store of things, saying at once: *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away: blessed be the name of the Lord* [^33]. O meek rich man, not resisting the will of God, truly rejoicing from that land of the living [^34]! *For blessed are the meek, because they will possess the earth by inheritance* [^35]. But you perhaps are insolent, you have nothing in your cellar, and out of the phantasy of your desires you dream most vain treasures. This one rather, this one rich man will enter; against you the kingdom of heaven will be shut, because it will be shut against the greedy, it will be shut against the proud and covetous. "But poor was that widow who put two mites into the treasury!" [^36] Yes indeed: she poor, but Zacchaeus rich. Did the widow enter, and was Zacchaeus shut out? Rather, for this reason it is truly the kingdom of the free, because it is given equally to both the poor and the rich. In that kingdom Zacchaeus will not be richer than that widow, even though here he gave more than she. For this one gave half his goods to the poor [^37], she gave two mites. Their means were unequal, but they had equal love.

**12.** So the rich man enters the kingdom of heaven. What kind is described, hear; how the way is made for him, how he is called to enter, hear, you who have the substance of the world, hear and do something before the flood, hear the Apostle writing to Timothy: *Charge the rich of this world* [^38]. And as though he asked what the Lord commanded: "Sell all you have, give to the poor and you will have treasure in heaven, and come with me, follow the Lord"—the Lord had already commanded this: let him hold Christ the commander, who holds the promiser. Who wills, let him do it; let him undertake the things heard from the Lord. To certain others the Apostle says—and through the Apostle the Lord: *Or do you want proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?* [^39] What then? *Charge*, he says, *the rich of this world*, what is the chief of evil, what is feared in riches, *not to be high-minded* [^40]. Let us briefly describe this. A rich man easily says: "Wicked servant"; he is, as it were, high-minded and, unless he says it, perhaps does not govern his house. For very often he governs more by a terrible word than by an atrocious lash. He says this, perhaps the necessity of governing the house compels him to say it: let him not say it within, let him not say it in the heart, let him not say it in the eyes and ears of God; let him not think himself better for that reason, because he is rich; let him consider the fragility of his flesh, with his garments laid aside. What shall I say, brothers, what shall I say? Let him think, if all those outer ornaments are taken away, what a rich man can be, because he is flesh and blood [^41], because together he came from that mass from Adam and Eve, but the rich man cannot consider that: with difficulty you withdraw all the ornaments from him. Nor is it to be wished that they be withdrawn, it was to be wished that they be thrown away. Therefore with difficulty is he persuaded what kind he is, whom all these things surround. Let him think of himself in his mother's womb, once naked and destitute [^42], like that poor man too; one thing received him being born; what received him will so remain here, as it was not brought here. Thinking these things the rich man within is poor in spirit [^43], that is, he blows away pride, lowers himself. And if he has a terrible face toward those who must be governed by him, yet within there is a humble heart under the eyes of God, and he knows with what conscience he beats his breast. But what follows, see whether it appears in them. For he did not say this: *Not to be high-minded*, and shut it; for every rich man would answer you: "God knows I am not high-minded; and if perhaps I shout, and if I say something harsh, God knows my conscience, that out of the necessity of governing I say these things, not that I lift myself above others"—as though more powerful, because richer. Within God sees these works. What follows, let us see. *Not to be high-minded*, he says, *nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches* [^44]. Still here too he may say it; and whether he says the truth, God alone can see, whether he does not presume on the things he has, whether he does not set his hope there; for there follows: *But in the living God, who furnishes us all things to enjoy* [^45].

**13.** "And then what?", he says. *Let them be rich in good works*. This now comes out even to the eyes of men, there is no hiding it. Either it is done and appears, or it is not done and there is no way to lie about it. *Let them be rich in good works, let them readily give, share* [^46]. There humility appears. You have: let it be common to you and to the one who does not have. *Let them share*. And for what good is this? *Let them lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may obtain the true life* [^47]. If the rich are such, let them be secure: when the last day comes, they will be found in the ark, they will be in the building, they will not belong to the destruction of the flood; let them not take fright, because they are rich. And if a young man cannot contain himself, he is allowed to take a wife. Will the last day, then, find him among those of whom he said: *They took wives*? It will not find him so, if it finds such a one as the Apostle says. Behold how he joins together in the ark those who feared. For the Apostle says: *For the rest*, brothers, *the time is short*. And what follows? *It remains that those who have wives be as though not having them, and those who buy as though not buying, and those who weep as though not weeping, and those who rejoice as though not rejoicing, and those who use this world be as though not using it: for the figure of this world passes away. I want you to be without anxiety* [^48]. Brothers, if you want to be secure, do not set your happiness in these things. And if either some duty or the necessity of weakness compels you to use these things, do not trust in them, do not cling to them, count them among the transitory and temporal. For all these things slip away in a certain river of things. You see that all those adversities, which press human affairs, cut down all these superfluous things.

**14.** Brothers, daily there is murmuring against God: "Evil times and hard times." Trifles are flogged, of which we were speaking: Evil times, hard times, troublesome times; and yet gifts are presented. They are evil, they are hard: let them correct men. Do you call the time hard? How much harder you, who are not corrected by the hard time! So great still flourishes the madness of pageants, after so many superfluous things men gape! Greed has no end, not even when cut down. By what things, I ask, by what doings do they wish for prosperity, what are they going to do when they wish for security? Let some security be given: we shall see how many diseases come forth into the open, how much luxury will overflow, greater than now. Security and quiet, for the sake of theaters and organs and flutes and pantomimes! You want to use badly what you wish for, therefore you do not receive it. Hear, hear the apostolic voice, much freer than mine—for I know how many I offend; consider me more timid: I do not dare to wound your weakness—hear from the Apostle what you do not want: *You desire, and do not have*—they are apostolic words—*you kill and are envious, and cannot obtain; you quarrel, you war, and do not have; you ask, and do not receive, because you ask badly, that you may spend it in your desires* [^49]. Surely he flattered no one; in many rotten parts the iron reached even to the living. Let us be healed, brothers, let us be corrected, let us be corrected: he is to come who has come and was mocked and, because he came, is still mocked; he is to come, and there will be no leisure to laugh. My brothers, let us correct ourselves: behold, there will be better times, and behold they will be now. What do you hope for here? Change your place, change your dwelling: "Lift up your heart." What do you hope for here? The human race arose, came to a certain youth—these things flourished in the world—it sinks and declines into old age, it is now nearly decrepit. What do you hope for here? Seek something else. Do you seek rest? You seek a good thing: seek it in its own region. There is one place from which he came down to you, another to which he bids you ascend. Do not hope for times, except such as are read in the Gospel; I do not say such or such: daily the Lord's books are for sale, the reader reads; buy for yourself and you too read when you have leisure, rather make it so that you have leisure: for it is better to have leisure for this than for trifles. Read what kinds of things are foretold unto the end of the age, and believe yourself, do not flatter yourself. Not because Christ came are things evil, but because they were hard and evil, he came who would console.

**15.** Attend, my brothers: it had to be that the times be such, troublesome and hard. What would we do, if so great a consoler were not present? The human race was to be gravely sick. As a physician taking on one great sick man, from Adam to the end, that is, taking on the whole wounded human race—for, from the time we were born here, from the time we were sent out of paradise, surely there is sickness, but at the end it would be greater, and perhaps near to health for some, near to death for some. Since, then, the human race was sick, that great physician, taking on the sick man, lying on a certain great bed, the whole world, like a most skilled physician, attended to the times of the sick man, and saw and foresaw what would be, since indeed he himself procured that same sickness, on account of his justice, for the vengeance of our sin. So in the gentler times of our sickness, first that physician sent his servants to inspect us, he sent the prophets. They spoke, they preached; he cured some through them and healed them. They foretold a certain great worsening to come in the final sickness, and a certain great tossing of this sick man, for whose healing it would be needful that the physician himself be presented and himself come. So our physician said this: "In the last time the sick man will be more strongly and more violently tossed, for taking up whose healing it is needful that I come at that time; I will refresh, I will console, I will exhort, I will promise, I will heal the believer." So it was done. He came, was made man, a sharer of our mortality, that we might be able to be made sharers of his immortality. And still the sick man is tossed. And when he pants in the fever and is greatly burning, he says to himself: "Since this physician, I suffer sharper fevers, I am more gravely tossed. O enormous burnings, whence did it come to me? I think he did not enter the house with a good foot." So say all who are still sick of vanity. Why are they still sick of vanity? Because they refuse to receive from him the draught of sobriety. You see those wretches tossed by their cares and various tribulations and terrors of the world, and saying: "Since Christ came, we suffer these times; since there are Christians, in all things the world fails." O foolish sick man, not because the physician came has your sickness become graver, but for this reason the physician came, because your sickness was to be graver: he foresaw it, he did not make it; but he came that he might console you and you might be truly well.

**16.** For what is taken from you, what is removed, except the superfluous? For you were gaping after harmful things, the things you were gaping after were not useful for your fever. Is the physician harsh, because he snatches harmful fruits from the hand of the sick man? What does he take from you, except the evil security which you desired to drink up unto the ruin of your inner parts? And this, that you groan and murmur, pertains to his medicine. Wanting to cure, lest you be tormented unwilling. It is necessary that the times be harsh. Why? Lest earthly happiness be loved. Surely it is fitting—it is medicinal—that this life be disturbed, that the other life be loved. Behold, if with such sloth men still cling to earthly things, still rave in the theaters, what if everything smiled on vanity, what if your trifles were nowhere struck? Behold so many bitternesses are mingled, and still the world is sweet. Come now, my dearly beloved brothers, I beseech you by the Lord, by his cross, by his blood, by his love, by his humility, by his loftiness I beseech and adjure, do not hear these things in vain, do not think that we truly stand in this place as it were for a spectacle. His mercy knows, under whose eyes we so tremble, that we are led by the office of love to say these things to you, and compelled by that fear by which we know that we ourselves are to render an account of all things to the Lord himself [^50].
