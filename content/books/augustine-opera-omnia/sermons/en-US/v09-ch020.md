# Sermon 313/F: Delivered in the Basilica of the Blessed Martyr Cyprian in the Mappalia on the Day of His Feast

**1.** First let me answer my brother and colleague. This morning I said that love ought to be restless, not lazy; but, because he wished it, let us obey, both him, and God through him, and you, and may he give obedience in you. We have sung: *I have hoped in the mercy of God*[^1]. Let us say a few things about our hope. And the words of our sermon will indeed have an end suited to the time; but hope itself, of which we speak, ought to endure, and not cease with our sermon. Let us speak, and let us cease; but it always cries out to God. Yet even hope itself—it will be hard, perhaps, what I say, but it will not offend if I show why I say it, and I believe it will not offend—but even hope itself will not be everlasting. For when the reality comes, hope will not be; for it is called hope only so long as the reality is not yet held, as the Apostle says: *But hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^2]. If, then, *hope that is seen is not hope,* because *what a man sees, why does he hope for it?* and it is called hope for this reason, because *we hope for what we do not see;* when there comes what we may see, hope will not be, because the reality will be. Nor will it then be a curse to be without hope; but now to be without hope is a curse and a reproach to anyone. And woe to him who is now without hope! For it is bad to be without hope, because one is not yet with the reality; then let hope cease to be, when the reality will be held.

**2.** But what is that reality itself which will be held? What is it that will succeed hope? For we see now that men hope for many earthly things, and according to the world the very life of no man is without hope; and, until he dies, each one is not without hope; there is hope in boys, that they may grow, be educated, learn something; hope in young men, that they may take wives, beget sons; hope in the parents of sons, that they may rear them, educate them, see grown the children they fondled as little ones; to name above all the very life of human hope, which is, as it were, more natural, and more excusable, and more common. For there are many common hopes, much to be criticized; but let us hold to this one, which is civil and natural. For each one is born for this, that he grow, be married, beget sons, educate them, and even be called a father of sons. What more does he seek? And the hope is not yet ended: he wishes to join wives to his sons, and still hopes. And when he has obtained this too, he wishes for grandsons; and when he has these, behold there is now a third succession, and the old man is loath to give place to the boys: still he seeks for himself something to wish, seeks what to hope, and seems benevolent. Would, he says, that that boy might call me grandfather, and that I might hear it from his very mouth, and die! The boy grows, calls him grandfather, and he does not yet acknowledge himself a grandfather; for surely, if he is a grandfather. If he is an old man, why does he not acknowledge that he ought now to move on, that those born may succeed? And when he has heard the name of honor from the little one's voice, he himself wants to educate him. Surely there remains for him to hope for a great-grandson too? Thus he dies, and hopes; and he hopes for one thing after another, when he has received what he was hoping for. But by receiving what he was hoping for, he is not satisfied, he gapes after other things. Why had it come, what you were hoping for? Surely now that you might make an end; you go on: the end is not extended. And how many this hope deceives, this common hope! First, it does not satisfy when it comes, and to how many it does not come! How many hoped for wives, and could not take them! How many hoped for those with whom it would go well, and took those who would torment them! How many wished for sons, and could not have them! How many groaned over the bad ones they had! So all things. Someone hoped for riches: either, not having obtained them, he was tortured by desire, or, having obtained them, racked by fear. And there is no one who ceases to hope, no one is satisfied, so many are deceived, and they do not rest from the hope of the world.

**3.** Let our hope at last be not deceptive, but satisfying, and something so good that there can be nothing more. What, then, is the reality that we hope for, which when it comes hope will cease, because the reality will succeed? What is it? Is it earth? No. Something that is born on earth, like gold, silver, a tree, a crop, water? None of these. Something that flits in the air? The soul shrinks from it. Is it perhaps heaven, so beautiful, and adorned with lights? For what among these visible things is more delightful, what more beautiful? Nor is it this. And what is it? These things delight, these things are beautiful, these things are good; seek who made them, he is your hope. He is now your hope, he will be afterward your reality; he is the hope of the believer, he will be the reality of the seer. Say to him: *You are my hope*[^3]. For now you say well: *You are my hope;* for you believe, you do not yet see; it is promised to you, you do not yet hold it. As long as you are in the body, you are a pilgrim away from the Lord; you are on the road, not yet in the homeland. The very ruler and founder of the homeland became the way, to lead you through; so say to him now: *You are my hope.* What afterward? *My portion in the land of the living*[^4]. What is now your hope, will afterward be your portion. Let your hope be in the land of the dying, and your portion will be in the land of the living. Turned to the Lord.

[^1]: Ps 52:8 (51:10)
[^2]: Rom 8:24-25
[^3]: Ps 142:5 (141:6)
[^4]: Ps 142:5 (141:6)
