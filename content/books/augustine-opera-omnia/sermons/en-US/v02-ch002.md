# Sermon 20/A

**1** In all the things of this world, both the good and the bad, in both there is temptation. For the good things of this world flatter in order to deceive; the bad things threaten in order to break. Since, then, there is temptation in both—that is, in both the good and the bad of the present world—the Christian is not secure. With his whole heart let him say and do to God what we have just sung: *Have mercy on me, O Lord, have mercy on me, for in you my soul has trusted*[^1]. This utterance neither casts down the poor man nor exalts the rich; it makes the downcast hope and does not allow the upright to be puffed up. For whoever's soul has trusted in God is neither exalted by good things nor broken by bad. It knows that all these things pass like a shadow, but that he does not pass to whom it has said: *In you my soul has trusted*[^2].

**2** "In you," however, the servant says to the Lord, the creature to the Creator, the made to the maker, the captive to the redeemer, the bound to the liberator, and, to put it more briefly, man to God. Now two things are set before man, that he should both acknowledge God and acknowledge himself: God, in whom he may trust; himself, that he may not trust in himself. For men who give themselves much and despise others say in vain, "I do not trust in him; far be it from me to presume upon him." For sometimes humility says these things, sometimes pride. It is good that you do not trust in man, but only if you do not trust in yourself. For he who trusts in himself either trusts in a man or is not a man. There remains, then, one free utterance: *Have mercy on me*[^3]. But what merit of mine is there, that you should have mercy on me, that I can recount? Not my righteous deeds, not my riches, not my strength. Not on account of my merits, then, but *because in you my soul has trusted*[^4]. It obtained a reward because it offered a sacrifice. And what did it offer? Not a bull, not a goat, not a ram, not the incense of Arabia, not golden ornaments, not anything bought dear and very precious, but what is dearer than anything—itself. For nothing is dearer to God than the image of God.

**3** God therefore makes all things to be under man, and man to be under himself. Do you want whatever God has made to be under you? Be yourself under God. For you demand most shamelessly that the lower creation be under you, when you do not acknowledge the one who created it and who is higher. God, then, so ordered what he created that he set his own image under himself, and the rest under that image. Take him up, and you will tread man underfoot. Refuse to despise him, and let whoever wishes despise you. For what harm does he do you who despises you, whom God does not despise? He laughs at the one who despises you, because he crowns you. "He despises me." Christ was despised. The one to whom it said, *In you my soul has trusted*[^5], came to be despised for you, and, despicable, redeemed you. You would not have been redeemed if he had not been despised. But how was he despised? Because he took up the garment of a servant, your form. For one thing lay hidden, another appeared. God lay hidden, man appeared. The man was despised and was glorified by God.

**4** He, then, who made himself the way for us, did not want to have all the things that men here sought as something great, though he had all things—he whose were heaven and earth, through whom heaven and earth were made, whom the angels served in the heavens and above the heavens, who put demons to flight, who drove off fevers, who opened the ears of the deaf and the eyes of the blind, who commanded the sea and the winds and the storms, who raised the dead. He who could do so much, so much did the one he himself had made prevail against him. The creator of man was made subject to man, because he appeared as man, the liberator of man. He was made subject to man, but in the form of man, God lying hidden, man appearing, the man despised, God found—yet not found except first despised. For he did not wish to give you glory unless he first taught you humility.

**5** Every man desires high things. And what is high on earth? If, then, you desire high things, desire heaven, desire heavenly things, desire things above the heavens. Long to be a citizen of the angels, court that city, sigh for it, where you will not lose a friend, will suffer no enemy, where you will not find anyone who has been redeemed, because no one can lead anyone away from there as a captive. For that city is eternal, where no one is born, no one dies, where there is perpetual and true health, because that very health is called immortality. If you long to be there, you have truly sought high things. You see where; but see by what way. For there is no one who does not seek to be a citizen of the angels, to rejoice in God, about God, under God, to abide always, to be changed by no decay, to be worn down by no old age, to be weakened by no weariness, to be ended by no illness, no death. A great thing, a high thing, a desirable thing! You long for where you may arrive; see by what way you come.

**6** Indeed those two disciples of our Lord, the holy and great brothers James and John, the sons of Zebedee, as we read in the Gospel, longed that our Lord God would grant that one of them might sit in the kingdom at his right hand and the other at his left. These men did not want to be kings on earth, did not long from the Lord God for perishable honors, to be adorned with riches, to parade with a household, to be honored by clients, to be deceived by flatterers; but plainly they sought something great and solid, to have seats in the kingdom of God to which no one succeeds. What they longed for is great, and yet they are not repressed in their desire but called back to order. The Lord saw in them a desire for loftiness, and he deigned to teach them the way of humility, as if to say: "You see where you are reaching, you see who I am to you, and yet I who made you have come down to you, I for your sake have humbled myself." But these words I am saying are not read in the Gospel; nevertheless I am stating the sense of the words that are read in the Gospel. The words that are read in the Gospel I remind you of, that you may see that what I have said is born from them, so that they are the root and our words the branches. When, then, the Lord had heard this from them, he said to them: *Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?*[^6] You desire to sit at my sides. Tell me first what I ask you: *Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?*[^7] Is the bitter cup of humility not bitter for you who seek the seats of loftiness?

**7** Yet where the command is hard, there is great consolation. For the cup of suffering, the cup of humiliation, men do not want, they do not want to drink it. Do they seek high things? Let them love the lowly. For from the lowly one rises to the high. No one sets up a lofty building unless he has first laid a foundation in the depths. Consider all these things, my brothers, and from them be instructed, from them be built up to faith, that you may see the way by which one must arrive at the things you desire. For I know and am aware: there is not one of you who does not desire immortality, eternal exaltation, and fellowship with God. All these things we all desire. Let us see by what way we may come, for we love the place to which we would come. This, then, I have said: someone is about to build a temporary hut of hay—he does not dig a foundation. But if he plans the structure of a lofty building, heavy with masses, meant to last a long while, he does not first lift his eyes to see how high it will rise, but first directs his plan to the lower parts, how deep to dig. And the higher the summit of the building will be, the lower will be the trench of the foundation. Who does not want to see tall crops? For tall crops to come, you first work lowliness into the seed with the plow. He who plows cuts the depths. He who plows goes down into the furrow that the crop may go up. The taller the trees are, the lower they have their roots, because all height rises up from lowliness.

**8** But you were afraid, O man, to suffer the insults of humility! To drink the most bitter cup of suffering profits you. Your insides are swollen, your heart is bloated. Drink the bitterness, that you may keep your health. The healthy physician drinks. Does the sick invalid refuse to drink? For this is what he said to the sons of Zebedee: *Are you able to drink the cup?*[^8] He did not say to them, "Are you able to drink the cup of insults, the cup of gall, the cup of vinegar, the most bitter cup, the cup filled with poison, the cup of all sufferings?" If he said these things, he would frighten rather than rouse them. Where there is sharing, there is consolation. Why do you disdain it, servant? The Lord drank that cup. Why do you disdain it, sick man? The physician drinks. Why do you disdain it, feeble one? The healthy one drinks. *Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?*[^9] Then those men, eager for loftiness, not knowing what they could do, promising what they did not yet have, said: *We are able*[^10]. And he: *You will indeed drink my cup*[^11], because I grant that you should drink it, I who will make you strong, strong from being weak, who grant you the grace of endurance, that you may drink the cup of humility; *but to sit at my right hand or at my left is not mine to grant to you*, but *it has been prepared for others by my Father*[^12]. If not to them, then to what others? If the apostles do not deserve it, who deserves it? But who are the others? Among these two was that John. Which John? That one, brothers, whom the Lord loved beyond the rest, who reclined on the Lord's breast, who drank from his breast what he poured forth in the Gospel. He is that John who said: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*[^13]. A great outpouring, but first there was a great drinking. Does what he pours forth delight you? See whence he drank. He was reclining on the Lord's breast, at that banquet he had drunk all these things which he poured forth in the Gospel with great happiness. So great, then, was he who reclined on the Lord's breast, to whom too was said what your Charity has heard: *It is not mine to grant to you; it has been prepared for others by my Father*[^14]. To what others, O Lord? If John did not receive it, who reclined on the Lord's breast—if he did not receive it who reclined on the Lord's breast, if he did not receive it who passed beyond the sea, the air, the heaven, and arrived at the Word, who passed beyond such things and reached you as you are, equal to the Father—if what he asked he did not receive, who will receive it? The Lord knew what he said: *it has been prepared for others*[^15]. What does "for others" mean? For the humble, not the proud; therefore for you too, if you become others, if you lay aside pride and are clothed with humility.

**9** Therefore, my brothers, we have learned, we are learning, we sing, let us do: *Have mercy on me, O Lord, have mercy on me, for in you my soul has trusted*[^16]. O happy soul! Are you poor? Trust in him, because you have nothing greater in which to trust. Are you rich? Trust in him, because *all flesh is grass, and the glory of flesh is like the flower of grass. The grass withers, the flower falls, but the word of the Lord abides forever*[^17]. Are you poor? Like the ulcerous Lazarus, trust in him. Lazarus was poor, Abraham rich. When we hear in the Gospel that the poor ulcerous man died and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom, all the beggars, the ulcerous, the disabled, the abandoned—when they hear this reading, what do they say? "He spoke of us." Perhaps a poor, needy man, barely sufficient for himself, or perhaps a beggar, notices someone rich standing in the house of God with clothing suited to his honor. When he hears this reading he says: "He spoke of me. I, when I die, will be carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom." He hears the Gospel say there of the rich man: And when he had died, he began to be tormented in the underworld. When the poor man hears this, he says to himself: "That was said of me, that of him." Do not be mistaken, poor man. Do not fear, rich man. It does not speak of you, poor man, if you are a drunkard. It does not speak of you, rich man, if you are devout. He crowns devotion, not poverty. For God will not say in his judgment, "Let the senator approach me, let the commoner withdraw." But neither will he say this: "Let the commoner approach, let the senator withdraw." He will not say, "Let the emperor approach me, let the soldier withdraw." But neither will he say that: "Let the soldier approach, let the emperor withdraw." But: "Let the just approach, let the unjust withdraw." Therefore, poor man, hold to devotion if you wish to arrive. For do you want to know that the Lord chose devotion, not condemned riches? *The poor man was carried up*[^18]. But to where? *Into Abraham's bosom*[^19]. Read what Abraham was, and you find a rich man. The rich man went ahead to prepare lodging and a home for the poor man. See how you have it in the psalm: *The rich and the poor together as one*[^20].

**10** Let us glorify God and our Lord Jesus Christ in our good works, and let us say from the heart: *Have mercy on me, O Lord, have mercy on me, for* not in gold, not in silver, not in honor, not in wealth, not in a great friend, not in a crowd of clients, not in a parade of servants, but *in you my soul trusts*[^21].
