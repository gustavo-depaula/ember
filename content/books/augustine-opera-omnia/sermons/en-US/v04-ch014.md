# Sermon 105a

**1.** The holy Gospel, which we have just heard as it was read, exhorts us to prayer. It gives great hope, because he who asks, seeks, and knocks does not go away empty from the Lord. For he did not say that some ask and do not receive, but *Everyone*, he says, *who asks will receive, and the one seeking will find, and to the one knocking it will be opened* [^1]. And he set forth a likeness by way of contrast: if a friend comes to a friend, and asks for three loaves because a guest has come to him, and asks at that hour when it is already troublesome to rise and give; the other answers that he cannot give because he is now resting and his children are with him in the bedroom; yet the first does not cease to ask: *I tell you*, he says, *that, not because of friendship, but because of the annoyance he suffers from him, he will rise and give him as many as he needs* [^2]. If the one who is overcome by annoyance does not refuse, how does the one refuse who exhorts you to ask? For this the likeness was set forth. If, to one asking three loaves, the one who is a friend does not refuse, and gives not because of friendship but lest he suffer trouble, will God, who is the Trinity, not give us himself when we ask? But I think that friend would not give his friend three loaves so that one might be white, another common, another of barley. Since, then, God Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, gave great hope of obtaining when he exhorted us to pray, we ought to know what we should pray for. For who does not ask God? But what one should ask is to be considered. The giver is ready to give, but the petitioner must be amended. You rise and ask God that you may have riches. Ought the sons of God to ask this of God as a great thing? For this reason God himself willed to give riches even to the worst of men, that his sons might not ask them of their Father as a great thing. For by his very works God in a way addresses us, and says to us: Why do you ask riches of me? Is this the whole of what I shall give you as a great thing? Consider to whom I have given them, and blush to ask this. The faithful man asks what an actor has. The Christian matron asks what a harlot has. Do not ask these things in your prayers. Let her give them if she wills; let her not give them if she wills. For we must believe him who says: *For a man's life does not consist in abundance* [^3]. Why? Riches have harmed many. But I do not know whether anyone can be found whom they have profited. Perhaps we find one whom they have not harmed. I do not know whether one can be found whom they have profited. Perhaps someone says: Did riches, then, not profit the one who used them well by feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, receiving pilgrims, redeeming captives? Whoever does this acts so that riches may not harm him. For what if he did not have those riches with which he did these things, and yet were such as to do them if he had them? God does not weigh most opulent means, but most devout wills. Were the Apostles rich? They left only their nets and their little boat, and followed the Lord [^4]. He left much who left the hope of the world, like that widow too with the two mites which she put into the treasury [^5]. No one, he says, gave more; and many rich men gave much; they did not give more than that widow *into the gifts of God*, that is, *into the treasury. Many rich men were putting in much*, and *he was watching*—not because they were putting in much. But she came in with two mites. Who deigned even to see her? He saw her who does not regard the full hand but the heart. He both saw her and pointed her out; and pointing her out, he said that no one put in so much. For no one put in so much as she who left herself nothing. If, then, you have little, you will give little; if you have more, you will give more. Yet, when from your little you give a little, will you have less, or receive less because you gave less? If the things given are examined, some are great, some small, some many, some few. But if the hearts of those who give are examined, you will find sometimes in the many a needy heart, in the few an opulent heart. For you regard the many things given, and do not regard how much he kept for himself who gave much, how much in the end he doled out, how much of others' property he took away, who gives something of it to the poor, as though to bribe God the judge. This, then, you do by giving: that riches may thus not harm you, not that they may profit you. For even if you were poor, and from your little gave a little, as much would be reckoned to you as to the rich man giving much, or even more, as to that woman. For let us suppose that the kingdom of heaven is, as it were, for sale at the price of alms. There has been set before us for purchase a certain fertile and most opulent possession, which, when we have acquired it, when we have come to it, we shall not, departing, leave to our successors, but shall always possess it, never to leave it, never to migrate from it entirely. A great possession, a possession to be bought! It remains for you to ask how much it is worth, lest perhaps you be not worth as much as it is worth, and cannot buy it, although you long to have it. Lest you think you cannot attain it, I tell you its price: it is worth as much as you have. I add another thing for you to rejoice at, if you are not envious. When God has made you the possessor of this possession to be bought, you will not exclude another buyer. The Patriarchs bought it; did they exclude the most blessed Prophets? The Prophets bought it; did they not admit the Apostles to the buying? The Apostles bought it, and yet other buyers, the martyrs, came to them. In the end, so many bought it and it is still for sale. Let us see, then, whether the rich could buy it and the poor could not buy it. Let us see more recent examples, let us omit the most ancient buyers of this possession. Having given half his goods to the poor, when he was very rich, Zacchaeus bought it, *a chief of the publicans* [^6], who had acquired much. But publicans were so called not as commoners, but as those who farmed the taxes. The holy Gospel explains this to you when a certain apostle was called of whom it is written: *He saw a certain man sitting at the tax booth, Matthew by name* [^7]. This man whom he called from the tax booth is in another place called *Matthew the publican* [^8]. So that Zacchaeus, after the Lord had entered to him, whom he received most unexpectedly—for he greatly desired to see him; because he was small in stature, he could not in the crowd; he climbed a tree and from there saw him passing; that he might see him who for his sake was to hang on the wood, he suspended himself on the wood—so then, when the Lord had entered to him, he is filled with joy, because his heart had already entered before: *Half my goods I give* [^9]. But he kept much for himself. Note why he kept the other half: *And if I have taken anything from anyone*, he says, *by fraud, I restore fourfold* [^10]. Much was kept, not that it might be possessed, but as something from which plunder might be restored. The great buyer gave much. A little before rich, suddenly poor. Was it because he bought from so great a store, that Peter the poor man did not buy from his nets and little boat? For each it was worth as much as each had. After these, that widow too bought. She gave two mites and bought it. Is there anything cheaper? There is. I find for this so great possession a cheaper price than those two mites were. Hear the seller himself, the Lord Jesus. *If anyone*, he says, *gives a cup of cold water to one of the least of mine, amen I say to you, he will not lose his reward* [^11]. What cheaper than a cup of water, and that cold, lest he be forced even to buy wood? I do not know whether you suppose that anything can be found cheaper than this trifling price. And yet there is. He does not have as much as Peter had, much less does he have as much as Zacchaeus had, nor does he find two mites. Is a cup of cold water lacking for the moment? *Peace on earth to men of good will* [^12]. Let that diversity of prices be examined no further. If we understand, if we truly think on it, good will is the price of that possession. It bought in Peter, it bought in Zacchaeus, it bought in that widow, it bought in the one who holds out a cup of cold water. It alone buys, if one has nothing besides it.

**2.** Why have we said these things? What had we proposed? That we ought to learn what we should ask from the passage of the Gospel where the Lord gave us great hope, saying: *Ask and you will receive, seek and you will find, knock and it will be opened to you, for everyone who asks receives, and the one seeking will find, and to the one knocking it will be opened* [^13]. Because he gave great hope, we ought to know what we should ask. Hence it came about that I admonished you that, when you pray, you should not as a great thing ask, seek, knock for riches. He who knocks wants to enter. Narrow is that by which you enter. Why do you carry much? You ought, then, to send ahead what you carry, that you may be able through the narrow way easily, without a burden, to enter. Do not, then, ask of the Lord God riches as a great thing. Why do you fear lest you have less and not buy that possession? Did I not tell you it is worth as much as you have? And if you have nothing, you yourself will be its price, because, even if you have much, you will not buy it unless you give yourself too. You answer me perhaps: What, then, ought we to ask of God? Do not ask even for the death of your enemies. These are malevolent prayers. I do not know whether you are heard for your good when you rejoice at an enemy's death. For who is not going to die? Who knows when he is going to die? You rejoice that another has died. How do you know that, while you rejoice, you may not yourself expire in the end? Learn to pray that your enemy—let the very enmities die. A man is your enemy. There are two names: man and enemy. Let the man live; let the enemy die. Do you not recall how Christ the Lord struck his enemy Saul, the bitterest persecutor of his members, with one voice from heaven, laid him low, killed him [^14]? He utterly killed him, for the persecutor died, the preacher was raised up. If you do not believe me that he died, ask himself; let him be heard, let him be read. Hear his voice in his letter: *But I live, now not I* [^15]. I live, he says, not I. So he himself died. And how did he speak? *But Christ lives in me* [^16]. Pray, then, as much as you can, that your enemy may die, but see how he may die. For if he has died and has not departed in body, you have only lost an enemy and acquired a friend. Do not, then, pray and ask of God even the visible deaths of your enemies. What, you will say, are we to ask? The honors of the world? They are smoke passing away. In the lowly place you were safer. In the lofty do you plan to be in peril? And honors indeed none gives but God, like riches; but that you might despise riches, he admonished you also to whom they are given; they are given to the good, lest you think them some evil; they are given also to the wicked, lest you think them a great good. So it is also with honors: the worthy receive them; the unworthy also receive them, lest the worthy hold them for a great thing. Now, then, say, you ask, what we should ask. I do not send you off with the many exclamations, when I recalled the gospel testimony: *Peace on earth to men of good will* [^17]. Ask for that very good will. Riches, honors, and whatever such things there are—do they make you good? Even if they are goods, they are the least of goods, which the good use well, but the wicked badly. Good will makes you good. Are you not, then, ashamed if you want to have good things and be wicked? You have many goods: gold, silver, gems, estates, households, herds, flocks. Blush before your goods. Be good yourself too. For what is more unhappy than you, if your farm be good, your tunic, your sheep, in short your sandal, and your soul will be bad? Learn, then, to ask for a good that, so to speak, makes good—that is, a good that makes men good. If you have goods which the good use, ask for a good from which you may be good. Good will makes you good. For those are goods too, but they do not make men good. That you may know they are goods, among them are those which the Lord recalled: bread, fish, egg [^18]; that you may know they are goods, he himself says: *If you, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your sons* [^19]. You are evil and you give good things. Ask that you may be good. For he therefore admonished and said: If you, *though you are evil*, that he might admonish what they should ask, that is, that they should not be evil, but be good. Let him himself, then, teach us what we should ask. There, in that very passage of the Gospel, hear his following words. If you, he says, *though you are evil, know how to give good gifts* to your *sons*, and yet you are to remain evil; therefore, that you may not remain evil, hear what follows: *How much more will your Father from heaven give the good Spirit to those who ask him* [^20]. Behold the good from which you may be good! The good Spirit of God makes good will in men. The price of that possession, which possession is called eternal life, is God himself. Eternal life—what will be richer for us? What, I say, will be richer for us, when our possession will be God? Have I perhaps done wrong because I said God will be our possession? I have not. I learned what I said. I found a holy man praying and saying: *Lord, the portion of my inheritance* [^21]. Stretch out the bosom of your desire, O greedy man, and find something greater than God, find something more precious than God, find something better than God. What will you not have when you have him? But gather to yourself gold, silver, as much as you can. Shut out your neighbors; possess by enlarging your possession; reach the ends of the earth. The lands acquired, add the seas. Let what you see be yours; let what you do not see, under the waters, be yours. When you have all these, what will you have if you will not have God? If, then, by having God a poor man is rich, and by not having God a rich man is a beggar, do not ask of him anything besides himself. What will he not give when he gives himself? What will he give, if he does not give himself? Ask, then, for the good Spirit; let him dwell in you, and you will be good. *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God* [^22]. And what follows? *But if sons, then heirs—heirs indeed of God, but coheirs of Christ* [^23]. What is it that you were desiring riches? An heir of God, then, will be poor? An heir of some most opulent senator, you would be rich; and an heir of God will you be poor? A coheir of Christ, will you be poor? When your inheritance will be the Father himself, will you be poor? Ask for the good Spirit, since you ask for the good Spirit already from the good Spirit himself. For you have something of that Spirit so as to ask for that Spirit. For if you had nothing of it, you would ask nothing. But because you do not have as much as suffices, you both have and ask, until what is written comes about: *Who satisfies your desire with good things* [^24], until what is written in another place comes about: *I shall be satisfied when your glory is made manifest* [^25]. *Blessed*, then, *are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness*—not this earthly bread, not earthly water, not earthly wine, but righteousness—*for they shall be satisfied* [^26].
