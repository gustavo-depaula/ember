# Sermon 283: On the Heavenly Birthday of the Massilitan Martyrs

**1. 1.** Let us admire the courage of the holy martyrs in their suffering in such a way that we proclaim the Lord’s grace. For they too wished to be praised not in themselves but in him to whom it is said, *My soul will be praised in the Lord.* Those who understand this do not grow proud. They ask with trembling and receive with joy; they persevere and no longer lose what they have received. Because they are not proud, they are meek. And so, after saying, *My soul will be praised in the Lord,* he added, *Let the meek hear and rejoice*. What would weak flesh be, what would a worm and decay be, unless what we sang were true: *My soul will submit to God, for my patience comes from him*? For the virtue by which the martyrs endured all evils for the faith is called patience. There are two things that either entice or drive people into sins: pleasure and pain. Pleasure entices; pain drives. Against pleasures, continence is necessary; against pains, patience. For this is how the human mind is urged to sin. Sometimes it is told, “Do this, and you will have that”; sometimes, “Do this, so that you will not suffer that.” Pleasure is preceded by a promise, pain by a threat. Thus people sin to obtain pleasure or avoid suffering pain. So against these two things, one consisting in an alluring promise, the other in a terrifying threat, God has deigned both to promise and to frighten: to promise the kingdom of heaven, and to frighten with the punishments of hell.

**2. 1.** Pleasure is sweet, but God is sweeter. Temporal pain is bad, but eternal fire is worse. You have something to love in place of worldly loves—or rather, unclean loves. You have something to fear in place of the world’s terrors.

**2. 2.** But being instructed is not enough unless you obtain help through prayer. The psalm we have just sung has taught us that our patience in the face of pain comes from God. Where do we find that our continence, which is necessary against pleasures, also comes from him? You have the clearest testimony: *And when I knew,* it says, *that no one can be continent unless God grants it—and this itself was a matter of wisdom, to know whose gift it was*. So if you have something from God and do not know from whom you have it, you will not be rewarded, because you remain ungrateful. If you do not know from whom you have it, you do not give thanks; by failing to give thanks, you lose even what you have. *For to the one who has, more will be given.* What does it mean to have something fully? To know where you have it from. *But from the one who does not have*—that is, who does not know where he has it from—*even what he has will be taken away*. Thus, as the same writer says, *This itself was a matter of wisdom, to know whose gift it was.* The Apostle Paul says the same when commending to us God’s grace in the Holy Spirit.

**3. 3.** As though asked, “How do you distinguish?” he went on to add, *So that we may know the things that God has given us*. Thus the Spirit of God is the Spirit of love; the spirit of this world is the spirit of self-exaltation. Those who have the spirit of this world are proud and ungrateful to God. Many have his gifts but do not worship him from whom they have them; that is why they are unhappy. Sometimes one person has greater gifts, another lesser ones—for example, understanding and memory. These are God’s gifts. Sometimes you find a person of the keenest intelligence, with a memory so remarkable as to seem unbelievable; you find another with little understanding and a weak memory, endowed with both in small measure. Yet the first is proud and the second humble; the second gives thanks to God for his little gifts, while the first credits himself with his greater ones.

**4. 3.** The person who thanks God for a little is incomparably better than the one who exalts himself over much. For God grants greater things to the one who gives thanks for a little; but the one who does not give thanks for great things loses even what he has. *For to the one who has, more will be given; but from the one who does not have, even what he has will be taken away.* How does he not have it, if he has it? Someone who does not know where he has it from has it without having it. For God takes away what is his own, and the man is left with his own iniquity. So *no one* is *continent unless God grants it.* You have a gift against pleasures, because *this itself,* he says, *was a matter of wisdom, to know whose gift it was. No one* is *continent unless God grants it.* You have a gift against pains: *For from him,* he says, *comes my patience.* Therefore, *hope in him, all the assembly of the people.* Hope in him; do not trust your own strength. Confess your evils to him; hope for your good things from him. Without his help you will be nothing, however proud you may be. Therefore, so that you may be able to be humble, *pour out your hearts before him.* And lest you remain unhappily within yourselves, say what follows: *God is our helper*.

**5. 4.** The blessed martyr whom we admire, whose feast we celebrate today, had this helper so that he could conquer. Without him he would not have conquered. Even if he had overcome pain, he would not have overcome the devil. For sometimes those conquered by the devil overcome pain; they have not patience but stubbornness. That helper was therefore present to give him true faith, provide him with a good cause, and grant him patience for that good cause. For it is patience only when a good cause comes first. Nor does anyone other than God give faith itself. The Apostle briefly commended both things: that the cause for which we suffer and the patience by which we endure evils come to us from God. Encouraging the martyrs, he says, *For it has been granted to you for Christ.* Here is a good cause, because it is for Christ—not for sacrilege against Christ, or for heresy and schism against Christ. For Christ says, *Whoever does not gather with me scatters*. Therefore, he says, *It has been granted to you* *for Christ, not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for him*. This is true patience. Let us therefore love this patience and hold fast to it; and if we do not yet have it, let us ask for it. Then we rightly sing, *My soul will submit to God, for my patience comes from him*.

SERMON 283, expanded  
Sermon of Saint Augustine  
On the heavenly birthday of the holy Maxulitan martyrs

**1. 1.** Let us admire the courage of the holy martyrs in their suffering in such a way that we proclaim God’s grace. For they too wish to be praised not in themselves but in him to whom it is said, *My soul will be praised in the Lord.* Those who understand this do not grow proud. They ask with trembling and receive with joy; they persevere and do not lose what they have received. Because they are not proud, they are meek. And so, after saying, *My soul will be praised in the Lord,* he added, *Let the meek hear and be glad.* What is weak flesh, what is it but worms and decay? Or what would it be anywhere, unless what we sang were true: *My soul will submit to God, for my patience comes from him*? For the virtue by which the martyrs endured everything for the faith is called patience. There are two things that either entice or drive people into sins: pleasure and pain. Pleasure entices; pain drives. Against pleasures, continence is necessary; against pains, patience. For this is how the human mind is urged to sin. Sometimes it is told, “Do this, and you will have that”; sometimes, “Do this, so that you will not suffer that.” Pleasure is preceded by a promise, pain by a threat. Thus people sin to obtain pleasures or avoid suffering pain. And so against these two things, one consisting in an alluring promise, the other in a terrifying threat, God has deigned both to promise and to frighten: to promise the kingdom of heaven, and to frighten with the punishments of hell. Pleasure is sweet, but God is sweeter. Temporal pain is bad, but eternal fire is worse. You have something to love in place of worldly loves—or rather, unclean loves; you have something to fear in place of the world’s terrors.

**2. 2.** But being instructed is not enough unless you obtain help through prayer; this is what the threat is meant to bring about. Against all pleasures the law cries out to you, *You shall not covet.* You have heard it: it is a divine utterance; God has spoken. No believer has doubted that he has commanded rightly and warned truthfully. But see what the Apostle says: *The law came, and sin came to life again.* For before you were told, *You shall not covet,* you thought you could lawfully sin; it was not considered a sin when it was not forbidden. Because sin was forbidden, it became known. Now that it is known, then, avoid it, if you were seeking the law’s help. You have heard. What more do you want from the law? *You shall not covet.* The letter of the law has lodged in your mind; you have an opponent to wrestle with, but you will be defeated unless you have help. Where does help come from? From grace. For *love* *has been poured into our hearts,* not by ourselves, but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us.* Take away this grace, remove this help: *the letter kills.* The world already had you as a sinner; when the letter comes without help, it will also have you as a transgressor. Therefore *the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life,* because *God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us.* The law terrifies for this purpose: to compel us to ask for help. *The law,* as the same Apostle says, is like *a guardian.* The guardian does not teach but leads to the teacher. Once someone has been taught and equipped by the teacher, he will no longer be under the guardian. For the letter will no longer terrify you when grace helps you.

**3. 3.** We have said this because of what is written: *You shall not covet.* For this commandment seems to be directed against sins committed through the lure of pleasure, and the Apostle set it down as a general command, as though it alone were the law’s voice: *You shall not covet.* Does this commandment have any force against fears of pain as well? Perhaps it does. How so? A person does not want to suffer pain: he covets health. Someone afraid to die covets life. Against this disordered desire for bodily health where it is not needed, and against the fear of bodily death, which will come whether you wish it or not, extend the commandment *You shall not covet,* and say with the prophet, *I have not desired the day of men; you know.* This was the martyrs’ voice. They did not desire the day of men, lest they fail to reach the day of God. They did not desire a day that soon ends, so that they might reach the day without end. What did they despise, and what did they receive? Temporal losses cannot in any way be compared with those gains. One hour of labor wins eternity.

**4. 4.** Consider, brothers and sisters, the martyr’s day. It is good for us to dwell on encouragement to that same patience. Consider the labors of soldiers, of those who bear arms: what dangers they undergo, what harsh and arduous things they endure, in cold and heat, hunger and thirst, wounds and death. Every day they face dangers, keeping before their eyes not the soldier’s labor but the veteran’s leisure. “Look,” they say, “the labor will end. After a few years, leisure will follow; we will be well off, our expenses will be provided for, we will enjoy exemption from taxes, we will not be summoned to any civic duties, and after the burden of military service no one will impose his own burden on us.” With this reward before them, they labor toward an uncertain outcome. For when a soldier says, “The labor will end,” how does he know that he himself will not come to an end before the labor does? Perhaps even after the labor is over and he has obtained his leisure, he dies at once. Though he had resolved to labor somewhat longer for the sake of leisure, he is not allowed long enjoyment of the very leisure he reached through labor. People labor amid these uncertainties, yet certain labor is undertaken even for uncertain leisure. Wake up, Christian hearts! Serve God as soldiers: with him, labor cannot be in vain; with him, danger cannot be fruitless. For the world’s soldier loses his reward through death in battle; Christ’s soldier finds his reward through death. Finally, after labor lasting a short time, what is received is rest lasting not a long time, but no time at all. For we rest where there will be not a long time, but no time whatsoever. Our age will be eternity, where there is neither growth nor aging, nor will a day arrive, because no day will depart. If, then, you were told, “Labor for twenty years so that you may rest for forty,” who would not want twice as much rest for a single measure of labor? Yet scarcely any veteran, even if he reaches old age, has the chance to enjoy leisure for as long as he labored. But what has been said of us? And how does our commander, who will render us an eternal reward, [who] does not give merely a daily wage, encourage us through the Apostle? *For what is,* he says, *the temporary lightness of our tribulation produces for us, in an incredible measure, an eternal weight of glory*.

**5. 5.** What words, brothers and sisters! How he has diminished what we suffer, how he has commended what we hope for! *What is,* he says, *the temporary lightness of our tribulation, in an incredible measure . . .* For in *an incredible* *measure, eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered the human heart, what God has prepared for those who love him.* Thus he tells the faithful, *It produces for us, in an incredible measure, an eternal weight of glory.* He tells the faithful that their tribulation is light and temporary. *In an incredible measure:* they are commanded to believe what is incredible. Faithful soldier, believe the incredible, because nothing is impossible for God. He said *weight* so that you may become weighty, not lightweight. *Among a weighty people,* he says, *I will praise you.* He said *weight* so that the weight of love may make you firm and the wind of temptation may not carry you away. Consider the threshing floor: love weight and fear lightness. Chaff is there, and wheat is there; both are tossed by the winnowing fan, but not both are carried away by the wind. One remains because of its weight; the other flies away because of its lightness.

**6. 6.** This, then, will happen in us when we endure every adversity for faith and righteousness, not for just any kind of rest. *Blessed,* he says, as we have heard, *are those who have suffered persecution for righteousness.* By this addition he distinguished us from adulterers, robbers, murderers, killers of their parents, the sacrilegious, sorcerers, and heretics. For they too suffer persecution, but not *for righteousness.* If you want to avoid temptation, notice the distinction. Choose your cause, so that you need not dread the punishment. Choose your cause, so that you do not suffer punishment in vain. And when you have chosen your cause, commend that too to God and say to him, *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from an unholy nation.* Your cause is distinguished by him from whom your patience comes. For he gives true patience. In a bad cause it is stubbornness, not patience. Whatever has hardened in the body and lost sensation is harder to heal. For all praiseworthy virtues have neighboring vices by which the unwary are deceived; they also have opposites. It is easy for a person to take care not to rush into the opposite. Avoiding the neighboring vice is difficult, for the proximity of vices gives them a certain appearance and shadow of virtues. I will illustrate what I mean with a few examples, leaving you, once reminded, to understand the rest.

**7. 7.** Take the patience we are discussing: impatience is contrary to this virtue. Patience is the endurance of evils for righteousness; impatience is the inability to endure evils for any cause. This endurance is contrary to the lack of endurance. Endurance as a vice is stubbornness. For stubbornness imitates patience but is not patience. Take care, then, that its proximity does not deceive you, that you are not perhaps stubborn while thinking yourself patient. For just as someone who is &lt;not&gt; driven to evil even by suffering evil is better than others, so someone who is not recalled from evil even by suffering evil is worse than others. Pain is an evil; iniquity is an evil. A reward is promised for committing iniquity, and you do not consent: you have overcome the desire for rewards. You were being tested from the other side. Pains will be set before you: having overcome the pleasure of rewards, overcome the bite of pains as well. Someone who promises you a reward to lead you into iniquity entices you to evil by an apparent good. Someone who threatens you with evil to compel you to iniquity presses you toward evil through evil, but through a small evil toward a great one. You will not, however, be great merely by despising the one who makes promises; you will be greater if you despise the one who threatens and overcome the one who rages. That is why I said: just as someone who is not compelled to evil even by suffering evil is better than others, so someone who is not recalled from evils even by suffering evil is worse than others. You warned a man not to be an adulterer; he disregarded your warnings and was not recalled from evil by good. But if you begin to threaten pains, inflict blows, and impose various hardships, and even then he does not restrain himself from evil, how much worse he is, since even evil could not recall him from evil! See the disastrous condition of stubbornness, which lies so close to patience. For stubbornness is present in the hearts of the impious precisely so that neither the threat nor the infliction of punishments recalls them from evil deeds. The very thing you boasted of makes you worse. “I endured,” you will say, “I overcame, I did not give way, I was not bent.” I would praise this if I recognized patience in it. As it is, I detest stubbornness. &lt;. . . patience&gt; from stubbornness. “Do not question my words, but my cause. Let the cause itself answer you.” The man is a robber; he endures punishments for his robbery. He is tortured and does not confess what he knows in his conscience to be true. What shall we say: “Great patience”? Rather, let us say, “Detestable stubbornness.” But if he does this for a crime, what will you do for the faith? In him, refusing to confess seems remarkable; for you, confessing is glorious. For he suffers for a crime that must be disowned; you suffer for Christ, who must be confessed. Sometimes, however, stubbornness is so great that a man even admits his evil and suffers evils for confessing that very iniquity, so that he will not fall away or withdraw from evil deeds. He is ready to be tortured for Donatus, and does not conceal this by denying it, but confesses it without shame: he boasts of iniquity. If only he would conceal himself while remaining what he appears to be! You even dare to expose the wound you do not wish to have healed. This is not health with sensation, but hardness without sensation.

**8. 8.** Let us love patience, let us hold fast to patience, and, if we do not yet have it, let us ask for it. For our patience comes from the same one as our continence. From him comes our continence against pleasures; from him comes our patience against pains. The psalm we have just sung has taught us that our patience comes from him—our patience against pains, of course. Those who have the spirit of this world are proud and ungrateful to God. They have many of his gifts but do not worship him from whom they have them; that is why they are unhappy.
