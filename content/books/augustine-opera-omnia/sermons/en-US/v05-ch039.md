# Sermon 176

**1.** As to what the Lord deigns to admonish from the divine readings, hear attentively, brothers, he giving, I ministering. We heard the first reading, of the Apostle: *A faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the first. But for this reason I obtained mercy, that in me Christ Jesus might show all his long-suffering, for the instruction of those who would believe in him unto eternal life*[^1]. This we received from the apostolic reading. Then we sang the Psalm, exhorting one another, with one voice, one heart, saying, *Come, let us adore and prostrate ourselves before him, and weep before the Lord who made us*: and there, *let us come before his face in confession, and in psalms let us shout for joy to him*[^2]. After these, the gospel reading showed us ten lepers cleansed, and one of them, a foreigner, giving thanks to his cleanser. These three readings, as far as we can for the time, let us treat, saying a few things of each; and, as far as we can strive, with the Lord's help, not so lingering on any of them as to bring hindrance to the other two.

**2.** The Apostle sets before us the knowledge of thanksgiving. Remember what the last gospel reading sounds, how the Lord Jesus praises the one giving thanks, reproves the ungrateful—cleansed in skin, lepers in heart. What, then, does the Apostle say? *A faithful*, he says, *saying, and worthy of all acceptance*. What is this saying? *That Christ Jesus came into the world*. For what? *To save sinners*. What of you? *Of whom I am the first*[^3]. He who says either: I am not a sinner, or: I was not, is ungrateful to the Savior. No one of men in that lump of mortals which flows from Adam, no one at all of men is not sick, none healed without the grace of Christ. What of little children, if they are sick from Adam? For they too are carried to the Church; and if they cannot run there on their own feet, they run on others' feet, that they may be healed. Mother Church lends them others' feet that they may come, others' heart that they may believe, others' tongue that they may confess: so that, since they are weighed down because they are sick by another's sinning, so, when these are healthy, by another confessing for them they may be saved. Let no one, then, whisper to you foreign doctrines. This the Church always had, always held: this it received from the faith of the elders; this it perseveringly guards to the end. Since those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick[^4]. What, then, did the infant have need of Christ for, if he is not sick? If he is healthy, why does he seek the physician through those who love him? If, when infants are carried, they are said to have no sin of inheritance at all, and they come to Christ; why is it not said to those who bring them, in the Church: Take away from here these innocents: those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick; Christ came not to call the righteous, but sinners[^5]? It has never been said: but neither will it ever be said. Let each one, then, brothers, speak what he can for the one who cannot speak for himself. The patrimonies of orphans are commended to bishops as a great thing; how much more the grace of little ones? The bishop guards the orphan, lest, his parents being dead, he be oppressed by strangers. Let him cry out the more for the little one, for whom he fears lest he be killed by his parents; let him cry out with the Apostle: *A faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world*, for no other cause, but *to save sinners*[^6]. He who comes to Christ has something to be healed in him: he who does not have it, there is no cause why he should be offered to the physician. Let the parents choose one of two things: either let them confess that sin is healed in their little ones, or let them cease to offer them to the physician. This is nothing else than to wish to offer a healthy person to the physician. What do you offer? One to be baptized. Whom? An infant. To whom do you offer? To Christ. Surely to him who *came into the world*? Yes, he says. Why did he come into the world? *To save sinners*. Therefore the one you offer has something in him to be saved? If you say: He has, by confessing you blot it out; if you say: He does not have it, by denying you keep it.

**3.** *To save sinners*, he says, *of whom I am the first*. Were there not sinners before Paul? Surely at least Adam himself before all, and the earth full of sinners destroyed by the flood, and afterward how many. Whence is it true, *I am the first*? He said himself first, not by the order of sinners, but by the greatness of his sin. He attended to the greatness of his sin, whence he called himself the first of sinners; just as among advocates, for example, the first are spoken of: this one is first, not because he has more years since he has been pleading cases; but because, from when he began, he surpassed the rest. Let the Apostle say in another place, then, whence he is the first of sinners: *I am*, he says, *the last of the apostles, who am not worthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the Church of God*[^7]. No one fiercer among the persecutors: therefore no one first among the sinners.

**4.** *But I obtained*, he says, *mercy*. And why he obtained mercy, he sets forth the cause: *That in me*, he says, *Christ Jesus might show all his long-suffering, for the instruction of those who would believe in him unto eternal life*[^8]. Christ, he says, going to give pardon to sinners converted to him, even to his enemies, chose me first, a fiercer enemy; that, when he healed me, no one among the rest might despair. Physicians do this: when they come to those places where they are unknown, they first choose the desperate cases to cure; that in them they may both exercise benevolence and commend their skill; so that each one in that place may say to his neighbor: Go to that physician, be secure, he heals you. And he: He heals me? Do you not see what I suffer? I know something similar: what you suffer, I too have suffered. So Paul says to each sick man, and to one wishing to despair of himself: He who cured me sent me to you, and said to me: Go to that desperate one, and tell him what you had, what I healed in you, how quickly I healed it. I called from heaven, with one voice I struck and cast down, with another I raised up and chose, with a third I filled and sent, with a fourth I freed and crowned[^9]. Go, tell the sick, cry out to the desperate: *A faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners*. What do you fear? why do you tremble? *Of whom I am the first*. I, he says, speak to you, healthy to the sick, standing to those lying down, secure to the despairing. *For this reason I obtained mercy, that in me Christ Jesus might show all his long-suffering*. He long bore my disease, and so took it away; like a good physician he patiently tolerated the frenzied man, bore me striking him, granted me to strike for his sake. *All his long-suffering*, he says, *he showed in me, for the instruction of those who would believe in him unto eternal life*.

**5.** Do not, then, despair. You are sick, come to him, and you are healed: you are blind, come to him, and you are enlightened. And you who are healthy, give him thanks: and you who are sick, run to him to be healed; all of you say, *Come, let us adore and prostrate ourselves before him, and weep before the Lord who made us*[^10]—both men, and saved. For if he made us men, but we ourselves made ourselves saved; we did something better than he. For a saved man is better than just any man. If, then, God made you a man, and you made yourself a good man; what you did is better. Do not exalt yourself above God: submit yourself to God, adore, prostrate yourself, confess to him who made you: because no one re-creates but the one who creates, no one remakes but the one who made. This too in another Psalm: *He made us, and not we ourselves*[^11]. Indeed, when he made you, you had nothing to do; but now that you are, you too have something to do; that you may run to the physician, implore the physician, who is everywhere. And that you might implore him, he roused your heart, and granted you to be able to implore; *For it is God*, he says, *who works in you both to will and to work, for his good will*[^12]. Because, that you might have a good will, his calling went before. Cry out, *My God, his mercy will go before me*[^13]. That you might exist, that you might perceive, that you might hear, that you might consent, his mercy went before you. It went before you in all things: do you too go before in something—his wrath. In what, you say, in what? Confess that you have all this from God—whatever good you have, from yourself whatever evil. Lest in your goods you despise him, praise yourself; lest in your evils you accuse him, excuse yourself: this is the true confession. He who in such great goods went before you is going to come to you, and to inspect his gifts and your evils; he inspects how you have used his good. Therefore, because in all these gifts he went before you, see in what you may go before the face of him who is to come; hear the Psalm: *Let us come before his face in confession. Let us come before his face*: before he comes, let him be appeased; before he is present, let him be reconciled. For you have a priest through whom you can appease your God, and he himself is, with the Father, God to you, who is man for your sake. So you will shout for joy in the Psalms, coming before his face in confession. Shout for joy in the Psalm: coming before his face in confession, accuse yourself; shouting for joy in the Psalm, praise him. By accusing yourself, and praising him who made you; he will come who died for you, and will give you life.

**6.** Hold this, persist in this. Let no one be variegated, let no one be a leper. Inconstant doctrine, not having one color, signifies the leprosy of the mind: and this Christ cleanses. Perhaps you were variegated in something, and you looked, and changed your opinion for the better; and what was variegated was made of one color. Do not ascribe it to yourself, lest you be among the nine who did not give thanks. One gave thanks, the rest were Jews; that one was a foreigner, he signified the foreign nations, that number gave Christ his tithe. To him, then, we owe what we are, that we live, that we understand: that we are men, that we have lived well, that we have understood rightly, to him we owe. Nothing is ours but the sin we have. *For what do you have that you have not received*[^14]? You, then, most of all who know what you hear—the heart to be cured of disease, cleansed of variegation—set it on high, and give thanks to God.
