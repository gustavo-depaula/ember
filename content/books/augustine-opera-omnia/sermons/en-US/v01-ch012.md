# Sermon 11: A Treatise of Saint Augustine

On Holy Elijah and on the Patience of Job

**1.** The Lord our God, not wishing any of us to perish, tending his Church as his own field, seeking fruit from his trees before the time of the axe comes, when it will be necessary to cut down the unfruitful trees, does not cease to admonish us that, while it is the time and while by God's help it stands in our power, we should do good works. For when the time of working well has passed, there remains nothing but receiving. No one will say to you after the resurrection of the dead in the kingdom of God, *Break your bread to the hungry*[^1], because you will find no one hungry. No one will say, "Clothe the naked," where the tunic of all will be immortality. No one will say, "Take in the stranger," where all will live in their own homeland, for now we are strangers from there. No one will say, "Visit the sick," where there is everlasting health. No one will say, "Bury the dead," where death dies. All these offices of devotion will not be necessary in eternal life, where there will be only peace and everlasting gladness. But in this time, that we may know how greatly God commends to us the works of mercy, he has made even his own saints to be in need, so that, when they here make friends of the mammon of iniquity, those friends too may receive them into the eternal dwellings. That is, so that, when the devout servants of God, while they are continually at leisure for God, sometimes are in need, those who have the riches of the world may bestow alms. As they make them partakers in earthly substance, so they will deserve to have a portion with them in eternal life.

**2.** This I said on account of the reading of Kings, which we heard first. Had God failed to feed his servant Elijah? Did not birds minister to him, because men were lacking? Did not a raven bring him bread in the morning and meat in the evening? God showed, then, that whence he wished and how he wished he can feed his servants. And yet, that a devout widow might be able to feed him, he made him to be in need. The want of the holy soul was turned into the abundance of the devout soul. Could not Elijah, out of God's mercy, give himself what he gave to the little jar? You see, surely, and it is manifest, that sometimes the servants of God do not have, so that those who have may be proved. And yet that widow had nothing. What was left to her was finished, and she was about to die with her sons. She went out, then, to make herself bread, to gather two sticks, and then Elijah saw her. The man of God saw her then, when she was seeking those two sticks. That woman bore the type of the Church. And because two sticks make a cross, she who was about to die was seeking that whence she might live forever. The mystery being thus foreshadowed, Elijah speaks to her what he heard. She tells her plight, says she is about to die when she has consumed what is left. Where, then, is what the Lord had said to Elijah, *Go to Zarephath of Sidon; for there I have commanded a widow to feed you*[^2]? You see how God commands, not in the ear but in the heart. Do we read that some Prophet was sent to that woman, and it was said to her, "Behold—the Lord says—my hungering servant is coming to you, minister to him of what you have; do not fear want; I will supply what you give"? We do not read that this was said to her. Nor do we read that an angel was sent to her in dreams and foretold that Elijah was coming hungry, and that someone admonished that woman about feeding him. But God commands in wonderful ways, who speaks by thought. We say that God commanded by speaking in the heart, by suggesting what was needful, by persuading what was useful to the rational soul, the widow woman. So too in the Prophets we read that the Lord commanded a worm to gnaw the root of the gourd. What is "He commanded," except "He prepared the heart"? By the Lord's inspiration, then, that widow woman had her heart prepared to obey. Such she had come, such she spoke with Elijah. He who was in Elijah to command, was himself in the widow to obey. *Go,* he says, *make for me first*[^3] out of your want, your riches will not fail. For the widow's patrimony was a little flour and a little oil. This little did not fail. Who has such an estate? Gladly the widow fed the hungering servant of God, because her patrimony hung on a peg. What is happier than this poverty? If here she receives such things, what will she hope for at the end?

**3.** I said this, that we may hope for the reward of our sowing, not in this time in which we have sown. For here we sow the harvest of good works with labor, but in the future we shall gather its fruit with joy, according to what is written: *Going, they went and wept, casting their seeds; but coming, they will come with exultation, carrying their sheaves*[^4]. For that was done for a sign, not for a gift. For that widow received here what she received for feeding the man of God. It is not great what she sowed, because she did not reap a great crop. It is temporal what she received, flour that did not fail and oil that was not diminished, until God should give rain upon the earth—as though she then began to be more in need, when God deigned to send rain. For then she was about to labor, to await the fruits of the field, to gather. But when it did not rain, her food came easily. This sign, which God bestowed on her for a few days, was a sign of the future life, where our reward knows no failing. Our flour will be God. As those did not fail through those days, so he will not fail forever. Let us hope for such a reward when we do good works, lest perhaps any of you be tempted by such a thought and say, "I will feed some hungering servant of God, that my jar may not fail, or that I may always find wine in my cask." Do not seek this here. Sow secure, your harvest will come later, will come more slowly, but when it comes, it will have no end.

[^1]: Isa 58:7
[^2]: 1 Kgs 17:9
[^3]: 1 Kgs 17:13
[^4]: Ps 126:6 (125:6)
