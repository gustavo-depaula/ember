# Sermon 165

**1. 1.** We have heard the Apostle, we have heard the Psalm, we have heard the Gospel; all the divine readings agree, that we place our hope not in ourselves, but in the Lord. *I ask*, says the Apostle, *that you not be weakened in my tribulations for you, which is your glory*[^1]. *I ask*, he says, *that you not be weakened*—that is, that you be not weakened, when you hear that I suffer tribulations for you; for this is your glory. He asks them, then, not to be weakened; which he would not do, unless he wished to rouse their will. For if they answered: Why do you ask of us what we do not have in our power? would they not seem to themselves to have given a just answer? And yet the Apostle, unless he knew there was in them a consent of their own will, where they too did something, would not say, *I ask*. And if he said, I command, unless he knew that they could apply their will to his command, in vain would this word proceed from his mouth. But again, knowing that without God's help the will of man is weak, not only lest they say: We have no choice of will, did he say, *I ask*; but also lest they say: The choice of will is enough for us, see what he added: *For this reason*. For what reason, but that which he had said above, *I ask that you not be weakened in my tribulations for you, which is your glory*? Because, then, you have the choice of will: *I ask*. But because the choice of will is not enough to fulfill what I ask: *For this reason I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named. That he may give you*[^2]. What: *give you*? What I ask of you, I ask that he give you. For I ask of you, on account of the choice of will: I ask that he give you, on account of the help of his majesty.

**2. 2.** But we have anticipated the Apostle's words. Perhaps you still await to hear—you who do not hold in memory the text of this reading—whether the Apostle really bends his knees to the Father for them, that he may give them what he said to them: *I ask*. Remember, then, what he asked of them. *I ask that you not be weakened in my tribulations for you*: this he asks of them. Now see what he asks for them: *I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, that he may give you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power*. What else is this, but not to be weakened? *To be strengthened with power*, he says, *through his Spirit*[^3]. This is the Spirit of grace. See what he asks. This he asks of God, which he demands of men; because, that God may be willing to give, you too ought to fit your will to receiving. How do you wish to receive the grace of the divine goodness, you who do not open the bosom of your will? *May he give*, he says, *you*. For you do not have it, unless he give it you. *May he give you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit*. For if he gives you to be strengthened with power, there he will give you not to be weakened. *That Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts, in the inner man*. May he give you all this. *Rooted and grounded in love, that you may be able to comprehend with all the saints*[^4]. To comprehend what? May he give you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit, and that Christ may dwell in your inner man through faith, and so, rooted and grounded in love, you may be able to comprehend with all the saints: what? *What is the breadth, the length, the height, and the depth*[^5]. "Height" indeed in the Latin language signifies both: both what is upward has the name of "height"; and what is deep downward has the name of "height." Therefore the translator rightly rendered: for what is high upward, to say "height"; for what is high downward, to say "depth."

**3. 3.** What is it, then, my brothers? I will expound this to you. Perhaps if it were easier for anyone—what then? Because the breadth, length, height, and depth, these four things which the Apostle names, I am less fit either to comprehend or to set forth, shall I pass over this? Or shall I perhaps knock, and, that I may set forth something wholesome for you, be helped by your prayers? Why do you range in heart, O Christian man, through the breadth of the earth, the length of times, the height of heaven, the depth of the abyss? When do you comprehend these things, either with the mind or with the body? That is, either by thinking or by gazing with the eyes of the flesh, when do you comprehend these things? Hear the Apostle himself saying to you, *But far be it from me to glory, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ*[^6]. And let us too glory in it, or because we lean upon it. In it let us all glory, O good brothers, in it let us glory. There perhaps we shall find the breadth, the length, the height, and the depth. For by these words of the Apostle the cross is in a way set before our eyes. For it has breadth, in which the hands are fixed; it has length, in that the wood is led from there down to the earth; it has also height, in that, from the very crossbeam in which the hands are fixed, it rises somewhat, where the head of the crucified is placed; and it has depth, that is, what is fixed in the earth and is not seen. See the great sacrament. From that depth which you do not see rises all that you see.

**4. 4.** Where, then, is the breadth? Turn yourself to the life and morals of the saints, who say, *Far be it to glory, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ*. We find in their morals the breadth of love; whence the Apostle himself admonishes them, saying, *Be enlarged, do not be drawing the yoke with unbelievers*[^7]. And because he himself was broad, who exhorted them to breadth, hear what he says: *Our mouth is open to you, O Corinthians; our heart is enlarged*[^8]. Breadth, then, is love, which alone works well. Breadth makes God love a cheerful giver[^9]. For if one suffers straits, he will give sadly: if he gives sadly, what he gives perishes. There is need, then, of the breadth of love, lest whatever good you do perish. But since the Lord says, *Where iniquity abounds, the love of many will grow cold*[^10]; give me also the length. What is the length? *He who perseveres to the end, this one will be saved*[^11]. This is the length of the cross, where the whole body is stretched out; where, as it were, one stands, by which standing one perseveres. If, then, you seek—you who glory in the cross—to have the breadth of the cross, have the virtue of working well. If you wish to have the length of the cross, have the long-suffering of persevering. But if you wish to have the height of the cross, know what you hear, and where you hear: Lift up your heart. What is, Lift up your heart? There hope, there love: from there seek virtue, there await the reward. For if you work well, and give cheerfully, you seem to have breadth. If in those same good works you persevere to the end, you seem to have length. But if you do not do all these things on account of the heavenly reward, you will not have height; and that will then be neither breadth nor length. For what is it to have height, but to think of God, to love God; and to love freely God himself the helper, himself the spectator, himself the crowner, himself the bestower of the reward; finally, to reckon him himself the reward, to await nothing else from him but himself? If you love, love freely: if you truly love, let the one you love be himself the reward. Or are all things dear to you, and is he cheap who founded all things?

**5. 5.** That we might be able to do these things, the Apostle bent his knees for us, surely for this reason, that it might be given to us. For the Gospel too terrifies: *To you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom, but to them it is not given. For he who has, to him it will be given*. But who has, to whom it will be given, but the one to whom it has been given? *But he who has not, even what he has will be taken from him*[^12]. But who has not, but the one to whom it has not been given? Why, then, was it given to that one, and not given to that one? It does not irk me to say it: this is the depth of the cross. From some depth of the judgments of God, which we cannot scrutinize and contemplate, comes all that we can. From some depth, I say, of the judgments of God, which, unsearchable, we cannot contemplate, we cannot scrutinize, comes all that we can. What I can, I see: whence I can, I do not see; except that even this I see thus far, that I know it is from God. But why this one, and not that one; it is too much for me, it is an abyss, it is the depth of the cross; I can cry out in wonder, I cannot demonstrate by disputation. What can I cry out about this depth? *How magnified are your works, O Lord*[^13]! The gentiles are enlightened, the Jews are blinded. Some little children are washed in the sacrament of Baptism, but some little children are left in the death of the first man. *How magnified are your works, O Lord! Your thoughts are made exceedingly deep*! And it follows, *The imprudent man does not know, and the fool does not understand these things*[^14]. What does the fool and imprudent man not understand? That it is even deep. For if the fool does not understand, and the wise man understands, it is not exceedingly deep. But if the wise man understands that it is deep, the fool does not understand that it is even deep.

**5. 6.** Therefore many, seeking to render a reason for this depth, have gone off into fables of vanity. Some have said that souls sin above in heaven, and according to their sins are directed to bodies according to their deserts, and are shut up there as it were in fitting prisons. They went after their own thoughts; wishing to dispute about the depth of God, they were drowned in the depth. For the Apostle meets them, wishing to commend grace, and he chose those twins in the womb of Rebecca, and says, *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil*[^15]. See how he took from vain men the fancies of a sojourn of souls before the body in heaven. For if they had already sojourned there, they had already done something good or evil, and were thrust down to earthly bodies according to their deserts. If it please, let us contradict the Apostle, who said, *Not yet born, nor having done anything good or evil*. But because, on account of the Apostle's evident sentence, the catholic faith rejects this—that souls live and sojourn first in the heavens, and there take on the deserts of the bodies they are to receive—these newcomers now do not dare to say it.

**6. 7.** But what do they say? Some of them, as we have heard, dispute thus: Plainly, they say, all men die according to their own deserts, because they have sinned; for there would be no death but coming from sin. It is indeed most well and truly said: There would be no death but coming from sin. But I, when I hear this, praise it for this reason, because I look to that first death and the sin of that first man. For I hear the Apostle: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^16]. *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^17]. For all were one. Do I hear you, then, saying that the death of man is from sin? No, he says. And what do you say? God now creates every man immortal. A wonderful novelty. What do you say? Plainly, he says, God creates every man immortal. Why, then, do little infants die? For if I say, Why do grown men die? you will say to me: They sinned. So I will not dispute about the age of the older: the infancy of little ones I will cite as a witness against you. They do not speak, and they convict; they are silent, and they prove what I say. Behold, infants are surely innocent in their own works, having with them nothing but what they drew from the first man; for whom therefore the grace of Christ is necessary, that they may be made alive in Christ, who died in Adam; that, because they were stained by generation, they may be cleansed by regeneration. These very ones, then, I will cite as witnesses. Answer me: Why do they die, if all men are born immortal, and because they sin, therefore they die? What do you suppose could be said? What ears could bear it? They too sinned. Where did they sin? I ask you, when did they sin? how did they sin? They do not know what good and evil is. Do they receive sin who do not grasp a precept? Prove to me that infants are sinners; what you said, truly because you forgot what you were, prove to me the sins of infants. Or do they sin because they weep? Because by movements as of dumb animals they repel discomforts, take pleasures, do they therefore sin? If those movements are sins, they become greater sinners in Baptism; because when they are baptized, they struggle most vehemently. Why is sin not imputed to them in so great a struggle, except because there is as yet no choice of will?

**6. 8.** But I say something else: These, because they were born, as you reckon, sinned. For if they did not sin, you say, they would not die. What do you say of those who die in the womb? O strait! They too, he says, sinned, therefore they die. Do you lie, or are you deceived? The Apostle contradicts you: *Not yet born, nor doing anything good or evil*[^18]. I hear the Apostle rather than you: I believe the Apostle rather than you: *Not yet born, nor doing anything good or evil*. But if you refute this testimony, go rather to those wanderings, and say: Because they sinned in heaven, and from there are cast down into bodies. I do not say it, he says. Why do you not say it? Because the Apostle says, *Not yet born, nor doing anything good or evil*. If, then, you do not accuse them in heaven, why do you accuse them in the womb? To both the Apostle answers, and answers those who say, They sinned in heaven; and answers those who say, They sinned in the womb, because those words avail for both, which say that, before they were born, they did nothing either good or evil. Why, then, do they die? And here too am I to hear you, and not rather the Teacher of the Gentiles?

**7. 9.** Tell me, Paul the apostle, why do they die? *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^19]. Behold, the first man made the whole lump damnable: let him come, let him come, our Lord, the second man; let him come, let him come; let him come by another path, let him come through a virgin; let him come living, let him find the dead: let him die, that he may help the dying, transfer the dead to life, redeem the dead from death, keep life in death, slay death from death. This grace alone is for the little ones, alone for the grown; alone it frees the small with the great. Why this one, and why that one; why not this one and that one; do not seek of me. I am a man: I notice the depth of the cross, I do not penetrate it; I am terrified, I do not scrutinize it. His judgments are unsearchable, his ways untraceable[^20]. I am a man, you are a man; a man was he who said, *O man, who are you to answer back to God*[^21]? A man said it, said it to a man. Let man hear, lest man perish, on whose account God became man. In this depth of the cross, then, in this so great obscurity of things, let us hold to what we have sung: let us not presume on our own strength, let us not arrogate anything in this question to the powers of our little wit: let us say the Psalm, with the Psalm let us say, *Have mercy on me, O God, have mercy on me*[^22]. Why? Because I have virtue by which I may earn you? No. Why? Because I bear the choice of will, whence my merit may go before your grace? No. But why? *Because in you my soul trusts*[^23]. A great knowledge, this confidence. Let us turn to the Lord . . .
