# Sermon 313/A: Delivered at Carthage at the Table of the Blessed Martyr Cyprian, on His Feast, the Eighteenth of the Kalends of October

**1.** The holy solemnity of the most blessed martyr, which has gathered us in the name of the Lord, demands that something be said of the merits and glory of so great a martyr: but nothing can be said worthily; for the human tongue could perhaps suffice for his virtues and glory, if he had been willing to praise himself. Nevertheless let us too praise him by devotion rather than by ability, indeed let us praise the Lord in him; the Lord in him, and him in the Lord. For what he would be without the Lord, the voice of the martyrs from the Psalm was just now heard when it was read: *Our help is in the name of the Lord*[^1]. If the help of all of us is in the name of the Lord, how much more the martyrs'! Where there is a greater fight, there a greater help is necessary. For there are two things that make the narrow way of Christians: the rejection of pleasure, and the endurance of suffering. You conquer, whoever contends. If you conquer what pleases and what terrifies. You conquer, I say, Christian, whoever contends, if you conquer what pleases and what terrifies. One is what pleases, another what terrifies. We treat of the glory of the martyrs. It is easy to celebrate the feasts of the martyrs; it is hard to imitate the passions of the martyrs.

**2.** The narrow and strait way of Christians, as I had begun to say, two things make: the contempt of pleasure, and the endurance of suffering. So whoever contends, let him know that he contends with the whole world; and contending with the whole world, let him conquer these two, and he conquers the world. Let him conquer whatever flatters, let him conquer whatever threatens; for the pleasure is false, the punishment passing. If you want to enter by the narrow gate, close the gates of desire and fear; for by these that tempter tempts to overthrow the soul. The door of desire tempts by promising; the door of fear tempts by threatening. There is something you may desire, so that you do not desire these things; there is something you may fear, so that you do not fear these things. Let desire not be taken away, but changed; let fear not be extinguished, but transferred to something else. What did you desire, you who yielded to the flattering world? What did you desire? The pleasure of the flesh, the desire of the eyes, the ambition of the age. Some three-headed dog of hell, I know not what, is this. But hear the apostle John, who reclined on the breast of the Lord, and belched forth in the Gospel what he drank at Christ's banquet; hear him saying: *Do not love the world, nor the things that are in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him; for all that is in the world is the desire of the flesh, and the desire of the eyes, and the ambition of the age*[^2]. So "world" is called this heaven and earth. He does not blame the world itself, who says: *Do not love the world;* for he who blames this world blames the maker of the world. Hear "world" named twice in one place under different senses. Of the Lord Christ it was said: *He was in this world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^3]. The world was made through him: *Our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth*[^4]. The world was made through him: *I lifted up my eyes to the mountains, from where help will come to me. My help is from the Lord, who made heaven and earth*[^5]. This world was made by God, *and the world did not know him.* What world did not know him? The lover of the world, the lover of the work, the despiser of the maker. Let your love migrate: break the ropes from the creation, bind yourself to the Creator. Change your love, change your fear; for nothing makes good and bad conduct but good or bad loves. This is a great man, someone will say, he is good, he is great. From what, I ask? He knows many things. I ask what he loves, not what he knows. *Do not,* then, *love the world, nor the things that are in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him: for all that is in the world*—surely in the lovers of the world—the things that are in the lovers of the world, *are the desire of the flesh, and the desire of the eyes, and the ambition of the age.* In the desire of the flesh is pleasure; in the desire of the eyes is curiosity; in the ambition of the age is pride. He who conquers these three, has nothing at all left in desire for him to conquer. Many branches, but a threefold root. How many evils the appetite of fleshly pleasure has, how many evils it does! From it adulteries, fornications; from it luxuries, drunkennesses; from it whatever titillates the senses unlawfully, and penetrates the mind with a pestilent sweetness, binds the mind to the flesh, casts the ruler down from the citadel, subjects the commander to a servant. And what right thing will a man be able to do, perverse in himself?

**3.** What evils base curiosity does, the vain desire of the eyes, the eagerness for trifling spectacles, the madness of the racecourses, the contests of games with no reward! The charioteers contend for some reward; for what reward do the people quarrel over charioteers? But the charioteer delights, the hunter delights, the actor delights. Does baseness then delight what is honorable? Change also the desire of spectacles; the Church offers to your mind more honorable and venerable spectacles. Just now the passion of blessed Cyprian was being read; with the ear we were hearing, with the mind we were watching, we saw him contending, in a way we feared for him in danger, but we hoped for the help of God. Indeed do you want to know quickly what is the difference between our spectacles and those of the theaters? We, as far as a sound mind thrives in us, desire to imitate the martyrs whom we watch; we, I say, desire to imitate the holy martyrs whom we watch contending. Honorable spectator, when you watch in the theaters, you are mad if you dare to imitate the one you love. Behold, I watch Cyprian, I love Cyprian. If you are angry, curse me, and say: May you be such! I watch, I am delighted, as far as I can I embrace with the arms of the mind: I see the contender, I rejoice in the victor. Be angry, as I said, and say to me: May you be such! See whether I do not embrace it, see whether I do not wish it, see whether I do not desire it, see whether I cannot say myself unworthy, yet cannot flee or turn away. Do you watch, be delighted, love. Do not be angry, if I say: May you be such! But I spare you, I do not say it; recognize a friend, change your spectacles with me. Let us love such men, of whom we need not blush; let us love such, whom we may wish to imitate, as far as we are able. But that man who is watched is disreputable; is the one who watches honorable? Let the desire of the buyer cease, and there will be nothing shameful for sale. By watching you confirm the infamy. Why do you provoke what you accuse? I marvel if the infamy of the one you love does not splash you. But let it not splash you, let honor remain unstained, if it can, the spectatress of lusts, the buyer of shameful pleasures. Do I dare forbid spectacles? I dare forbid, I dare plainly; this place gives me confidence, and he who appointed me in this place. The holy martyr was able to endure raging pagans; shall I not dare to instruct listening Christians? Shall I dread silent offenses, when he despised open furies? I will say it outright: surely I shall be refuted in the hearts of my hearers, if I say what is false. Excellently did it act, altogether excellently, the ancient Roman discipline, which assigned every kind of actor to a place of infamy. To them no honor in the senate-house, not even in the plebeian tribe; everywhere removed from the honorable, and set out for sale to the honorable. What have you removed from the senate-house for the sake of dignity, and set for yourself in the theater for the sake of pleasure? Let your pleasure be in harmony with your dignity. And those very wretches are bound over to the voices of the watchers, to the desires of the watchers, to the maddening pleasures of the watchers. Remove all these, and they are freed; he does mercy upon them who refuses to watch.

**4.** Let these things be said about the desire of the eyes. How much evil the ambition of the age has! There is all pride: and what is worse than pride? Hear the sentence of the Lord: *God resists the proud, but to the humble he gives grace*[^6]. So the ambition of the age is malignant. Someone will say: Without it the powers of the age cannot exist. They certainly can. Some author of theirs, I know not who, says: *Each transfers his own fault upon his occupation*[^7]. They certainly can. A ruler is set in power; let him rule himself, and he has ruled. But does the human mind proceed to exaltation? Let exaltation be bridled: let him know that he is a man, who judges concerning a man. The dignity is unequal, but the very frailty is common. He who thinks of this piously and holily, both has power, and does not proceed to exaltation. Cyprian conquered all these things. For what did he not conquer, who despised life itself, overflowing with all trials? The judge threatened him with death; he confessed Christ, ready to die for Christ. When death has come, no ambition will remain, no curiosity of the eyes, no appetite for filthy and carnal pleasures; one life despised, all are overcome.

**5.** So let the blessed one be praised in the Lord. When could he do this, if the Lord had not helped? When would he conquer, if the spectator, who was preparing the crown for the victor, did not supply strength to the laborer? He too rejoices indeed, he rejoices for us, not for himself, when he is praised in the Lord. For he is very gentle, and it is written: *In the Lord my soul will be praised; let the meek hear and be glad*[^8]. He was gentle: he wants his soul to be praised in the Lord. Let his soul be praised in the Lord. Let his body too be honored, because *precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^9]. Let it be celebrated holily, as celebrated by Christians. For we do not set up an altar, as to a god, to Cyprian, but we have made Cyprian an altar to the true God.
