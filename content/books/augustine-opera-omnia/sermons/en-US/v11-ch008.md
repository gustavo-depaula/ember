# Sermon 29/B: (Dolbeau 8)

**1.** The divine word exhorts us and says: *Give praise to the Lord, for he is good*[^1]. *Give praise,* it says, *to the Lord.* And as if you sought the reason, it adds: *For he is good.* Let not the guilty man fear the severity of the judge: the security of the confession is the goodness of the hearer. You delay before a man who takes cognizance, if you confess; you delay before God who has foreknowledge, if you do not confess. A man, when he hears you, waits to learn from your confession what kind of case you have. But God judges even while you are thinking. Confess, then, that you may have God favorable. For you who deny cannot be hidden. God, when he admonishes you to confess, does not expect from you contempt of himself, but your humility. Now confession in the holy Scriptures is wont to be spoken and understood in two ways: one is of your punishment, the other of the praise of God. Of your punishment, I say, when you repent. For everyone who repents punishes himself, lest he be punished by the Lord. That confession, then, is spoken in two ways—that is, that there is not only a confession of sins, but also of the praise of God—let us first prove, and afterward we will discuss both these kinds of confession, as the Lord grants.

**2.** The confession of sins is customary and known, nor is there need to prove it, but only to admonish concerning it. We ought rather to seek how we can prove confession in praise of God. For so accustomed are men to call confession that which concerns their own sins, that almost always, when "Confess" is heard from the mouth of the reader, breasts are at once beaten, and a murmur resounds as conscience is admonished. This almost always; yet confession is not always said of sins; for sometimes it is also of praise, as Scripture says in a certain place: *Give praise to the Lord, and you shall say this in confession: all the works of the Lord are exceedingly good*[^2]. When you hear: *You shall say this in confession: all the works of the Lord are exceedingly good,* it is manifest that this confession is of the praise of God, not of your iniquity. For you confess that all the works of the Lord are good, not your own evil deeds. You have another instance, whence likewise there can be no doubt. The Lord Jesus surely had no sin, and yet he says in the Gospel: *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth.* Then follows the praise: *Because you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones. Yes, Father, for so it was pleasing before you*[^3]. This is the confession of one praising God, not of one accusing himself. Since, then, he who confesses either accuses himself or praises God, attend a little to what usefulness each confession has.

**3.** He who accuses himself because he is evil is displeased with himself. Insofar as he is displeasing to himself as evil, he now begins to be good, because the evil man is not pleasing to him. This is the beginning of the joining of our heart with the law of God, that what he punishes you too may punish, what is displeasing to him may be displeasing to you also; you now begin with God to hate sin, and you begin with him to hate yourself, that he may begin to love you. For sin cannot be unpunished. You do not wish him to punish it: punish it yourself. For sin cannot be left unpunished. Either you punish it and he frees you, or you overlook it and he punishes it. For whence was the publican justified rather than that Pharisee, whence did he merit to be spared, but because he did not spare himself? He cast his eyes to the ground and had his heart above, he beat his breast and healed his conscience. What more? *He went down justified, rather than that Pharisee.* If you seek the reason, *because he who exalts himself shall be humbled, and he who humbles himself shall be exalted*[^4].

**4.** About to come before the judge, be a judge to yourself. Anticipate the one who judges, and you will find one who frees. What is "anticipate"? Before he punishes, you punish. For surely you have read: *Let us anticipate his face in confession*[^5]. Two words seem contrary to each other: to acknowledge and to overlook. You wish him to overlook: you acknowledge. See what the one who accuses himself in repentance says in the psalm: *Turn away your face.* But from what? He does not say: "From me." For in another place he says: *Do not turn your face from me*[^6]. From what, then, he says? *Turn your face from my sins*[^7]. By what merit does he wish God to turn his face not from himself but from his sins, so that by not seeing them he may see him? By what merit? Attend, and imitate. For he says in the same psalm: *For I acknowledge my iniquity.* Therefore do you overlook it. *And my sin,* he says, *is always before me*[^8]. He touched those who put others' sins before them, their own behind them, others' before them which they reproach by biting, their own behind them which they defend by carrying.

**5.** Therefore the Lord, since those who put their sins behind them are unwilling to see them and pretend not to acknowledge them, threatens the sinner: *You did these things, and I kept silent.* What is *I kept silent?* I did not avenge, I did not punish, I did not send into hell. You did it and lived, and did it again, because you mocked the one who spared you. *You suspected iniquity, that I would be like you*[^9], that is: you thought me like yourself, as though iniquity pleased me as it pleases you. You see, then, how much it profits you, if your iniquity displeases you. For from this you begin to be like God, lest, being perverse, you wish God to be like you. For see how perverse you are: God made you to his likeness, and you wish to bring God to your likeness. Therefore *you did it, and I kept silent,* that is: I did not avenge. *You suspected iniquity, that I would be like you.* All the evil, the wicked, the lost, the blasphemers and criminals say this: "Truly, if these things we do displeased God, we would not be alive." What is "if these things displeased God, we would not be alive"? Do you say this? Then these things please God. *You suspected iniquity:* these things do not please God, God will not be like you. Correct yourself rather, and you will be like God. But you are unwilling, you put yourself behind you; you do not do what is written: *And my sin is before me,* but you put yourself behind you. Hear what follows: *I will reprove you and set you before your face*[^10]. What you are unwilling to do, he says, I do: I set you before yourself, I punish you out of yourself. Therefore do it, lest he do it; set yourself before yourself and say securely: *For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my sin is always against me*; let it not be before you, because it is before me; *turn away your face,* from which I do not turn away; overlook what I acknowledge. Therefore do not fear to die, confess lest you die.

**6.** See now the confession of praise: because in the confession of sin you were displeased with yourself, in the confession of praise God will please you. Let what you did in yourself displease you, let him who made you please you. For sin is your work, you are God's work. God hates your work in his own work. Turn, then, to him, confess to him, and by accusing yourself and praising him you will then be upright. For perverse men do the contrary: they praise themselves, they accuse God. See, and if you recognize it in yourself, amend it, because, even if you are not now what I am about to say, you once were. This is the way of perverse men. Blasphemers, when they do anything good, wish themselves to be praised; when they do anything evil, they wish to accuse God. "Ungrateful one," says the inflated and arrogant spirit, "ungrateful one, I bestowed this and that on you, and conferred these and those things on you." There it sounds, as if with puffed cheeks: "I, I!" On the other hand, if a sin is caught out, a theft, an adultery, or something of the kind, when you begin to be accused in the presence of the governor, you say: "My evil fate," or if you do not name fate: "If God did not wish it, would I have done it?" Whether you accuse God by a shortcut, or by going around through fate—since in fate you accuse the stars, but the stars are God's works—in whatever way you wish to find fault with God, to defend yourself. Correct it, and let what you were putting above be below; what you had put below, lift above. You sinned: accuse yourself. You did well: praise God.

**7.** In your sin say the word of the psalm: *I said: Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^11]. Here say "I." Where you say *I have sinned against you,* there say "I." Why do you withdraw yourself from there, where God will find none but you? Attend and learn. *I,* he says, *said: Lord,* I *have sinned against you. I said.* Remember that *I said,* not fate, not fortune, not you, not, finally, the devil himself, because, unless I had willed it, I would not have consented. For both to accuse the devil and to excuse oneself is not good. For God commanded pardon to be given to those who confess. You have not done it: pardon is not given you, because you have done nothing. You ask pardon: confess your fault, lest you find punishment. But the devil himself wishes to be the object of your anger; when he is accused, he rejoices utterly, provided he takes confession from you. Therefore when you sin, say "I"; but when you do well, say what the apostle says: *It is not I who live, but the grace of God with me*[^12]. You will be corrected by confessing both your sins and the praise of God: your evil works, his good ones. And you confess *to the Lord, because he is good;* and when you confess, he has mercy not for a time, as in this transitory life, *for his mercy is forever*[^13]. Turned to the Lord, etc.
