# Sermon 241: In the Days of Easter, on the Resurrection of Bodies, Against the Gentiles

**1.** The proper faith of Christians is the resurrection of the dead. This—that is, the resurrection of the dead—Christ our head showed in himself, and gave us an example of faith; so that the members might hope for in themselves what went before in the head. Yesterday we suggested to you that the wise men of the gentiles, whom they call philosophers, those very ones who were most excellent among them, scrutinized nature, and from the works came to know the craftsman. They did not hear the prophets, they did not receive the law of God: but God, as it were silent, was speaking to them by the works of the world itself, and the forms of the world invited them to seek the craftsman of things: nor could they bring themselves to think that heaven and earth stood without an author. Of these the blessed apostle Paul speaks thus: *The wrath of God*, he says, *is revealed from heaven against all impiety.* What does it mean, *against all impiety?* Not only against the Jews, who received the law of God and offended the giver of the law; but also against all the impiety of the gentiles is the wrath of God revealed from heaven. And lest anyone should say, Why, since they themselves did not receive the law? he went on to add: *And against the unrighteousness of those who hold the truth in iniquity.* Now you answer: What truth? For they did not receive the Law, they did not hear a prophet. Hear what truth: *Because what is known of God*, he says, *is manifest among them.* From where manifest? Hear further: *For God has manifested it to them.* If you still ask, How did he manifest it to those to whom he did not give the law? hear how: *For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that have been made. For his invisible things*, that is, the invisible things of God: *from the creation of the world*, that is, from when he established the world: *are clearly seen, being understood through the things that have been made*, those things are understood through these. *Also his everlasting*—I quote the Apostle's words, I weave them in—*also his everlasting power and divinity*, you supply: *are clearly seen, being understood. So that they are without excuse. Why* without excuse? *Because, knowing God, they did not glorify him as God, or give thanks*[^1]. He did not say: Not knowing God; but: *Knowing* him.

**2.** From where *knowing?* From the things he made. Question the beauty of the earth, question the beauty of the sea, question the beauty of the wide and diffused air, question the beauty of the sky, question the order of the stars, question the sun making the day bright with its splendor, question the moon tempering the darkness of the following night with its brightness, question the animals that move in the waters, that abide on the land, that fly in the air; the hidden souls, the visible bodies; the visible things to be ruled, the invisible things ruling: question these things. They all answer you: See, look, we are beautiful. Their beauty is their confession. Who made these beautiful changeable things, except the unchangeable Beautiful One? In man himself, finally, that they might be able to understand and know God the creator of the whole world; in man himself, I say, they questioned these two things, body and soul. They questioned what they themselves bore: they saw the body, they did not see the soul. But they did not see the body except through the soul. For they saw through the eye, but within was the one who looked out through the windows. Finally, when the inhabitant departs, the house lies still: when the one who ruled departs, what was ruled falls: and because it falls, it is called a corpse. Are not the eyes there whole? Even if they are open, they see nothing. The ears are present; but the hearer has departed: the organ of the tongue remains; but the musician who moved it has gone away. They questioned, then, these two things, the body that is seen, the soul that is not seen: and they found that the one that is not seen is better than the one that is seen; the hidden soul better, the visible flesh worse. They saw these things, they gazed on them, they examined both, and found both, in man himself, changeable. The body changeable through ages, through corruptions, through nourishment, through restorings, through failings, through life, through death. They passed to the soul, which they had surely grasped to be better, and they marveled at it even as invisible: they found it too changeable; now willing, now unwilling; now knowing, now not knowing; now remembering, now forgetting; now fearing, now daring; now going into wisdom, now failing into folly. They saw it too changeable, they passed beyond it too; for they sought something unchangeable.

**3.** Thus, then, they came to knowing God who made these things, through the things he made. *But they did not honor him as God, or give thanks*: the Apostle himself says. *But they became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened. Saying that they were wise, they became fools*[^2]. By arrogating to themselves what they had received, they lost what they were holding. Saying that they were great, as it were, they became fools. And where did they arrive? *And they changed*, he says, *the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of the image of corruptible man.* He means idols. And it was too little to make an idol after the form of man, and to consign the craftsman to the likeness of his own work: this was too little. But what besides? *And of birds, and of four-footed beasts, and of serpents*[^3]. For all these mute and irrational animals, those, as it were, great wise men, made gods for themselves. I rebuked you when you adored the image of a man: what shall I do with you, when you adore the image of a dog, the image of a snake, the image of a crocodile? They reached down even to these. As high as, seeking, they were carried up to the heights, so far, falling, they were plunged into the depths. For what falls from on high is plunged the deeper.

**4.** These, then, as I admonished you yesterday, sought what afterward, that is, what after this life. They sought as men: but how could they find, since they were men? They did not have the teaching of God, they did not hear the prophets: they could not find, they made guesses. I reported to you yesterday their guesses. The bad souls go out, they say; and because they are unclean, they are at once turned again into other bodies: the souls of the wise and the just go out; and because they lived well, they fly to heaven. Well done, prettily you have found them a place: flying, they reach heaven. And what there? There they will be, they say, and will rest with the gods: their seats will be the stars. You have found them no bad dwelling: at least leave them there, do not cast them down. But, they say, after long ages, having utterly forgotten their old miseries, they begin to wish to return into bodies; and it will delight them to come, and again they come to suffer these things, to endure these things, to forget God, to blaspheme God, to follow the pleasures of the body, to fight against lusts. They come to these miseries: from where, and to where? Tell me, why? Because they forget. If they forget all evils, let them forget also the delight of the flesh. This alone they remembered, to their harm, the place from which they fell. They come: why? Because it delights them to dwell again in bodies. From where the delight, except through memory, because they once dwelt there? Wipe out all memory, and perhaps you will make the remaining wisdom: let nothing remain to call them back.

**5.** *Virgil does not approve the doctrine of the return of souls into bodies. That souls cannot be blessed in that opinion.* A certain author of theirs shuddered at it, to whom it was being shown, or who brought in a father showing it to his son among the dead. For nearly all of you know this; and would that few of you knew it. But few of you know it in the books, many in the theaters, that Aeneas went down to the underworld, and his father showed him the souls of the great Romans about to come into bodies: Aeneas himself was terrified, and said:  
*Father, must we believe that any souls rise from here  
to heaven, only to return again to sluggish  
bodies?*  
Is it to be believed, he says, that they go to heaven, and return again?  
*What dreadful longing for the light possesses these wretched souls?*  
The son understood better than the father expounded. He rebuked the desire of the souls wishing to return again into bodies. He called it a dire desire, he called them wretched; nor did he blush for them. To this, philosophers, you have brought it, that souls are purified, reach the highest cleanness, and through that very cleanness forget everything, and through forgetting their miseries return to the miseries of bodies. Tell me, I beseech you: even if these things were true, would they not better be unknown? Even if they were true, I say—which without doubt are false, because they are foul—would they not better be unknown? Or perhaps you are going to say to me: You will not be wise, if you do not know these things. Why should I know them? Can I be better now than I will then be in heaven? If in heaven, when I will be better and more perfect, I will forget everything I learned here, and there, being better, I am not going to know these things, allow me now not to know them. You say that the one dwelling in heaven forgets everything: allow me to be on earth not knowing all these things. Then, I ask you, do these souls in heaven know that they are going to suffer again the miseries of this life, or do they not know? Choose what you will. If they know they are going to suffer such great miseries, how are they blessed, thinking of their future miseries? how are they blessed, where they are without security? But I see what you will choose: you are going to say, They do not know. So you praise there this ignorance, which you do not now allow me to have, teaching me on earth what you say I am not going to know in heaven. They do not know, you say. If they do not know, and do not think they are going to suffer, by being deceived they are blessed. For what they are going to suffer, they think they are not going to suffer: and to think what is false, what is it but to be deceived? They will, then, be happy by deception; they will be blessed; not by eternity, but by falsehood. Let the truth free us, that we may be able to be truly blessed: for the word of our Redeemer is not empty: *If the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^4]. For he himself said: *If you remain in my word, you will truly be my disciples; and you will know the truth, and the truth will free you*[^5].

**6.** Then hear something else worse, something to be lamented, or rather laughed at. Here, wise man, here, philosopher, that is, on earth (for example, Pythagoras, Plato, Porphyry, and I know not who else of them), why do you philosophize? For the blessed life, he says. When will you have this blessed life? When I have left this body, he says, in the earth. So now a wretched life is led, but there is hope of a blessed life: there a blessed life is led, but there is hope of a wretched life. Therefore the hope of our unhappiness is happy, and that of our happiness unhappy. Let us throw these things away, and either laugh because they are false, or grieve because they are thought great. For these things, my brothers, are the great ravings of great learned men. How much better do we hold the great mysteries of great saints? Out of love of bodies, they say, the purified, cleansed, wise souls return; the purified souls, out of love of bodies, return to bodies. Does a purified soul, then, love so? Is not this love a great filth?

**7.** *But every body is to be fled.* Their great later philosopher Porphyry, a most bitter enemy of the Christian faith, who lived already in Christian times; but yet, blushing at those very ravings, in some measure corrected by the Christians, said, and wrote: *Every body is to be fled.* He said *every*, as if every body were a wretched chain of the soul. And, to be sure, if a body of any kind is to be fled, you cannot praise the body to it, and say how, by God's teaching, our faith praises the body: because even the body that we now have, although from it we have punishment for sin, and the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul; yet this body has its own form, the arrangement of its members, the distinction of senses, an upright stature, and the rest of the things at which those who consider them well are amazed. Yet that body will be wholly incorruptible, wholly immortal, wholly nimble and easy for movement. But Porphyry says: In vain do you praise the body to me; whatever kind of body it is, if the soul wishes to be blessed, every body is to be fled. This the philosophers say: but they err, but they rave. I prove it quickly: I do not wish to argue at length; because that which is predicated must have a subject. For there are two things joined to each other, predicate and subject. God surpasses all things: to him all things are subject. And the soul, if it has any honor with God, must have something subject to it. But I do not wish to argue at length from this; I read your books: you say that this world is an animal, that is, heaven, earth, seas, all the huge bodies that exist, the immense elements everywhere; all this, and the whole body that consists of all these elements, you say is a great animal, that is, it has its own soul, but does not have bodily senses; because outside there is nothing that could be perceived: yet it has understanding, it clings to God: and that very soul of the world you call Jupiter, or call Hecate, that is, as it were the universal soul ruling the world, and making it one single animal. And you say that this same world is eternal, will always be, will have no end. If, then, the world is eternal, and the world remains without end, and the world is an animal; that soul is always held in the world: surely every body is to be fled? What was it you were saying, Every body is to be fled? I say that blessed souls will always have incorruptible bodies. You who say, Every body is to be fled, kill the world. You say that I should flee from my flesh: let your Jupiter flee from heaven and earth.

**8.** What of the fact that we find this same Plato, the master of all of them, in a certain book of his which he wrote on the constitution of the world, bringing in God the fashioner of gods, making, that is, the heavenly gods, all the stars, the sun and the moon? He says, then, that God is the maker of the heavenly gods: he says that these very stars have intellectual souls, which understand God, and visible bodies which are seen. I say it, that you may understand: This sun that you see would not be seen, unless it were a body: this is true. No star or the moon would be seen, unless it were a body: he speaks the truth. Therefore the Apostle too says: *And heavenly bodies, and earthly bodies.* And it follows: *One is the glory of the heavenly, another of the earthly.* And again, speaking of the glory of heavenly bodies, the Apostle added and said: *One is the glory of the sun, another the glory of the moon, another the glory of the stars. For star differs from star in glory: so also is the resurrection of the dead*[^6]. You see that brightness is promised to the bodies of the saints, and a diversity of forms of brightness, because the merits of love are diverse. But what do they say? These stars that you see are indeed bodies, but they have their own intellectual souls, and they are gods. Meanwhile, about the bodies, because they are bodies, they speak the truth: but whether they have their own souls, why should I discuss it? Let us now come to the point. God is brought in by Plato himself addressing the gods, whom he made of corporeal and incorporeal substance, and among other things saying to them: *Since you have come to be, you cannot be immortal and indissoluble*. Already at this voice they could tremble. Why? Because they desired to be immortal, and were unwilling to die. So, to take away their fear, he went on to add and said: *Yet you will not be dissolved, nor will any fates of death destroy you, nor will they be stronger than my counsel, which is a greater bond for your perpetuity than those by which you have been bound together.* See, God gives security to the gods made by him: he gives them the security of immortality; he gives them the security that they will not leave the globes of their bodies. Surely every body is to be fled? As far as I judge, they have been answered, as you can understand: just as we too can speak, as far as the hour of the sermon permits, as far as your capacity allows, they have been answered. But as for what they themselves also say about the resurrection of bodies, as if cleverly, so that we cannot, as they think, answer them too—it is much for me to tell you today. But since I once promised you that during these days this question about the resurrection of the flesh was to be handled, prepare both ears and hearts for tomorrow, with the Lord's help, for the things that remain.
