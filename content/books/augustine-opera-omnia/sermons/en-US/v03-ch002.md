# Sermon 52: On the Trinity

**1. 1.** The Gospel reading has set before us something from which to speak to your love, as if by the Lord's command—and truly by the Lord's command. For my heart waited from him for, as it were, an order to bring forth the sermon, so that from this I understood that he willed me to speak of what he himself had willed to be read. Let your zeal and devotion hear, then, and aid my labor before our Lord God himself. For we see, and as if by a divine spectacle set before us we behold, at the river Jordan our God commended to us in Trinity. For when Jesus came and was baptized by John—the Lord by the servant (which he did as an example of humility; for in that very humility he showed justice to be fulfilled, when, as John said to him, *I ought to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?* he answered: *Let it be so now, that all justice may be fulfilled*[^1]): when, then, he had been baptized, the heavens were opened, and the Holy Spirit descended upon him in the form of a dove; then a voice followed from above: *This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased*[^2]. We have, then, the Trinity in a certain way distinguished: in the voice the Father, in the man the Son, in the dove the Holy Spirit. This indeed had to be mentioned, for to see it is very easy. For this Trinity is evidently, and with no scruple of doubt, commended, since the Lord Christ himself, coming to John in the form of a servant, is surely the Son: for it cannot be said that he is the Father, nor can it be said that he is the Holy Spirit. *Jesus came*, it says[^3]: surely the Son of God. Of the dove who would doubt; or who would say, What is the dove? when the Gospel itself testifies most plainly: *The Holy Spirit descended upon him in the form of a dove*[^4]? And about that voice likewise there is no doubt that it is the Father's, when he says: *You are my Son*[^5]. We have, then, the Trinity distinguished.

**2. 2.** And if we consider the places, I dare to say (though I say it timidly, yet I dare) the Trinity as if separable. When Jesus came to the river, from one place to another; the dove descended from heaven to earth, from one place to another; the voice of the Father itself sounded neither from the earth nor from the water, but from heaven: these three are as if separated by places, separated by offices, separated by works. Let someone say to me: Show the inseparable Trinity. Remember that you speak as a Catholic, that you speak to Catholics. For our faith, that is, the true faith, the right faith, the catholic faith, gathered not by the opinion of presumption but by the testimony of reading, not made uncertain by heretical rashness but founded on apostolic truth, conveys this; this we know, this we believe. Though we do not see it with our eyes, nor yet with the heart, as long as we are being cleansed by faith; yet by that very faith we most rightly and most firmly hold that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are an inseparable Trinity, one God, not three gods. Yet so one God that the Son is not the Father, that the Father is not the Son, that the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son, but the Spirit of the Father and the Son. This ineffable divinity, abiding in itself, renewing all things, creating, re-creating, sending, recalling, judging, freeing: this Trinity, then, we know to be at once ineffable and inseparable.

**2. 3.** What, then, do we do? Behold, the Son came separately in the man, separately the Holy Spirit descended from heaven in the form of a dove, separately the voice of the Father sounded from heaven: *This is my Son*[^6]. Where is the inseparable Trinity? God has made you attentive through me. Pray for us, and as it were opening your bosom, may he himself grant whence what you have opened may be filled. Labor together with us. For you see what we have undertaken; not only what, but indeed who has undertaken it; from what condition we wish to speak, where we are placed, how placed, in a body that is corrupted and weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that ponders many things[^7]. When, then, I draw this mind away from the many things, and gather it to the one God, the inseparable Trinity, that I may see something to say; do you think that in this body which weighs down the soul, I shall be able to say something worthy to speak to you: *Because to you, O Lord, I have lifted up my soul*[^8]? May he help me, may he lift it up with me. For I am weak before him, and it is heavy to me.

**3. 4.** This question is usually raised by the most zealous brothers, usually discussed in the conversation of the lovers of the word of God, usually much knocked over before God for, with men saying: "Does the Father do something that the Son does not do; or does the Son do something that the Father does not do?" Let us speak meanwhile of the Father and the Son. But when from this our effort has been delivered—we to whom we say, *Be my helper, do not forsake me*[^9]—it is understood that the Holy Spirit too in no way departs from the operation of the Father and the Son. Hear, then, brothers, about the Father and the Son. Does the Father do anything without the Son? We answer, No. Or do you doubt? For what does he do without him through whom all things were made? *All things*, it says, *were made through him.* And, plying it abundantly upon the slow, the hard, the quarrelsome, he added: *And without him was made nothing*[^10].

**3. 5.** What then, brothers? *All things were made through him*[^11]; we understand, surely, that the whole creation was made through the Son, that the Father made it through his Word, God through his Power and Wisdom: are we going to say, "All things, indeed, when they were created, were made through him, but now the Father does not govern all things through him"? Far be it. Let this thought withdraw from the hearts of the faithful, let it be driven from the sense of the devout, from the understanding of the godly. It cannot be that he created through him, and does not govern through him. Far be it that what exists should be governed without him, when through him it was made to exist. But let us teach this too by the testimony of Scripture, that not only were all things made and created through him, as we recalled from the Gospel: *All things were made through him, and without him was made nothing*; but that through him also the things that were made are governed and ordered. You acknowledge, then, Christ as the Power of God and the Wisdom of God: acknowledge what is said of Wisdom too: *She reaches from end to end mightily, and orders all things sweetly*[^12]. Let us not doubt, then, that all things are governed through him, through whom all things were made. Nothing, therefore, does the Father do without the Son, nothing the Son without the Father.

**3. 6.** A question occurs, which in the name of the Lord and by his will we have undertaken to resolve. If the Father does nothing without the Son, and the Son nothing without the Father, will it not, as it were, follow that we should say the Father too was born of the Virgin Mary, the Father suffered under Pontius Pilate, the Father rose again and ascended into heaven? Far be it. We do not say this, because we do not believe this. *For I believed, therefore have I spoken: and we believe, therefore we also speak*[^13]. What is in the faith? That the Son was born of the Virgin, not the Father. What is in the faith? That the Son suffered under Pontius Pilate, and died, not the Father. It escaped us that certain ones who understand wrongly are called Patripassians, who say that the Father himself was born of a woman, that the Father himself suffered, that the Father himself is the one who is the Son, that there are two names, not two realities. And the catholic Church has removed these from the communion of the saints, lest they deceive anyone, so that, separated, they quarrel apart.

**3. 7.** Let us, then, bring back the difficulty of the question to your senses. Let someone say to me: You said that the Father does nothing without the Son, nor the Son without the Father; and you brought testimonies from the Scriptures, that the Father does nothing without the Son, because all things were made through him[^14]; nor is what was made governed without the Son, because he is the Wisdom of the Father, reaching from end to end mightily, and ordering all things sweetly[^15]. Now you tell me, as if speaking against yourself: The Son was born of the Virgin, not the Father; the Son suffered, not the Father; the Son rose again, not the Father. Behold, I hold something that the Son does which the Father does not do. Either, then, confess that the Son does something without the Father; or confess that the Father too was born, suffered, died, rose again. Say either the one or the other: choose one of the two. I choose neither, I say neither this nor that. I do not say that the Son does anything without the Father; for I lie, if I say this: nor do I say that the Father was born, suffered, died, rose again; for I lie no less, if I say this. How, he says, will you extricate yourself from these straits?

**4. 8.** The question set forth pleases you: may God help, that its solution may please too. Behold what I say, that it may free both me and you. For we stand in one faith in the name of Christ, and live in one house under one Lord, and are members in one body under one head, and are quickened by one Spirit. So that the Lord may free both me who speak and you who hear from these straits of a most troublesome question, I say this: The Son indeed, not the Father, was born of the Virgin Mary; but that very birth of the Son, not of the Father, from the Virgin Mary, both the Father and the Son wrought. The Father indeed did not suffer, but the Son: yet the Son's suffering both the Father and the Son wrought. The Father did not rise again, but the Son: yet the Son's resurrection both the Father and the Son wrought. From this question we now seem to be extricated; but perhaps by my words—let us see whether also by the divine words. It pertains to me, then, to show by the testimonies of the holy books that both the Father wrought the birth of the Son and the Son did; so the suffering; so the resurrection: so that, although the birth and suffering and resurrection are of the Son alone, yet these three which pertain to the Son alone were done neither by the Father alone nor by the Son alone, but by the Father and the Son together. Let us prove them one by one, you who hear are the judges, the case has been set forth, let the witnesses come forward. Let your judgment say to me what is usually said to those pleading cases: Prove what you promise. I prove it plainly with the Lord's help, and I recite a reading of the heavenly law. You have heard the one proposing attentively; hear now more attentively the one proving.

**4. 9.** Concerning the birth of Christ I must first show how both the Father wrought it and the Son wrought it, although what the Father and the Son wrought pertains only to the Son. I cite Paul, a competent counselor in divine law. For advocates today have Paul too as one who dictates the laws of litigants, not of Christians. I cite, I say, Paul dictating the laws of peace, not of strife. Let the holy Apostle show us how the Father wrought the birth of the Son. *But when*, he says, *the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law*[^16]. You have heard, and because it is plain and open, you have understood. Behold, the Father made the Son be born of a virgin. For when the fullness of time had come, *God sent his Son*, namely the Father sent Christ. How did he send him? *Made of a woman, made under the Law.* The Father, then, made him of a woman under the Law.

**4. 10.** But does it perhaps trouble you that I said "of a virgin," and Paul says *of a woman*? Let it not trouble you, let us not linger over it; for I am not speaking to the untaught. Scripture says both, both "of a virgin" and "of a woman." How "of a virgin"? *Behold, a virgin shall conceive in her womb, and bear a Son*[^17]. But "of a woman," as you have heard; it is not contrary. For the property of the Hebrew idiom calls "women" not those whose virginity has been corrupted, but females. You have the evident Scripture of Genesis, when Eve herself was first made: *He formed her into a woman*[^18]. Scripture also says in another place that God commanded the women to be set apart who had not known the bed of a man[^19]. This, then, ought now to be known; and let it not detain us, that we may, with the Lord's help, explain the other things which will deservedly detain us.

**4. 11.** We have proved, then, that the birth of the Son was wrought by the Father; let us prove that it was wrought by the Son too. What is the birth of the Son from the Virgin Mary? Surely the assumption of the form of a servant. Is being born anything else for the Son than receiving the form of a servant in the womb of the Virgin? Hear that the Son too did this: *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal with God; but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^20]. *When the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman; who was made for him of the seed of David according to the flesh*[^21]: we see, then, the birth of the Son wrought by the Father; but because the Son himself *emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*, we see the birth of the Son wrought also by the Son himself. This has been proved; let us pass on from here: attentively receive another point, which follows in order.

**4. 12.** Let us prove the suffering of the Son too, both wrought by the Father and wrought by the Son. Let the Father bring about the suffering of the Son: *Who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^22]. Let the Son too bring about his own suffering: *Who loved me, and handed himself over for me*[^23]. The Father handed over the Son, the Son handed over himself. This suffering was done to one, but was done by both. As with the birth, then, so the suffering of Christ neither the Father did without the Son, nor the Son without the Father. The Father handed over the Son, the Son handed over himself. What did Judas do here, except sin? Let us pass on, and come from here to the resurrection.

**4. 13.** Let us see the Son indeed, not the Father, rising again, but both the Father and the Son bringing about the resurrection of the Son. Let the Father work the resurrection of the Son. *For on this account he exalted him from the dead and gave him a name that is above every name*[^24]. The Father, then, raised the Son, by exalting him and rousing him from the dead. Does the Son also raise himself? He plainly raised himself. As a figure of his body, he said of the temple: *Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^25]. Finally, as laying down the soul pertains to the suffering, so taking up the soul again pertains to the resurrection: let us see whether the Son indeed laid down his soul, and whether the Father restored his soul to him, not he himself to himself. For that the Father restored it is manifest. For the Psalm says of this: *And raise me up, and I will repay them*[^26]. But that the Son too restored his soul to himself, what do you await from us? Let him say it himself: *I have power to lay down my soul*[^27]. I have not yet said what I promised. I said *to lay down*: but already you have cried out, because you fly ahead. For trained in the school of the heavenly Master, like ones intently hearing the lessons, devoutly rendering them, you are not ignorant of what follows. *I have power*, he says, *to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again. No one takes it from me; but I lay it down of myself, and take it up again*[^28].

**5. 14.** We have discharged what we promised: we have proved our propositions, as I think, by the most firm proofs of the testimonies. Hold what you have heard. I recapitulate briefly, and commend to your minds a most useful matter, as I judge, to be lodged there. The Father was not born of a virgin: yet both the Father and the Son wrought that birth of the Son from the virgin. The Father did not suffer on the cross: yet both the Father and the Son wrought the suffering of the Son. The Father did not rise from the dead: yet both the Father and the Son wrought the resurrection of the Son. You have the distinction of persons, and the inseparability of operation. Let us not say, then, that the Father works something without the Son, or the Son something without the Father. Or do the miracles that Jesus did perhaps trouble you, lest he did some that the Father did not do? And where is: *But the Father abiding in me, he does his works*[^29]? These things we have said were plain; they only had to be said: there was no need to labor that they be understood, but care to be taken that they be remembered.

**5. 15.** I want to say something more, where indeed I require both your keener attention and your devotion before God. For only bodies are held or occupied by bodily places. Divinity is beyond bodily places. Let no one seek it as in a space. Everywhere it is present, invisible and inseparable: not greater in one part, lesser in another; but everywhere whole, nowhere divided. Who sees this? Who grasps this? Let us restrain ourselves; let us remember who we are, from what condition we speak. Whatever it is that God is, let it be devoutly believed, holily thought; and as far as it is granted, as far as it is possible, ineffably understood. Let words be still, let the tongue cease; let the heart be roused, let the heart be lifted there. For it is not such a thing as ascends into the heart of man, but that to which the heart of man ascends. Let us attend to the creature: *For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made*[^30]; lest perhaps in these things that God made, in which we have a certain familiarity of habit, we find some likeness whereby we may prove that there are certain three things, which three may be brought forth separably yet operate inseparably.

**6. 16.** Come, brothers, be present with your whole mind. See first what I promise; lest perhaps I find in the creature, because the Creator is exalted above us. And perhaps one of us, whose mind the flash of truth strikes with a certain lightning, can say those words: *I said in my ecstasy.* What did you say in your ecstasy? *I am cast away from the sight of your eyes*[^31]. For it seems to me that this man who said this had lifted up his soul to God, and poured out his soul over himself, when it was said to him daily: *Where is your God?*[^32] had arrived by a certain spiritual contact at that unchangeable light, and was unable to bear it by the weakness of his vision; and had fallen back again into his sickness, as it were, and his languor, and had compared himself to it, and had felt that the edge of his mind could not yet be tempered to the light of the wisdom of God. And because he had done this in ecstasy, snatched away from the senses of the body and caught up into God, when he was in some way recalled from God to man, he says: *I said in my ecstasy.* For I saw I know not what in ecstasy, which I could not long bear; and returned to my mortal members, and to the many thoughts of mortals, from the body that weighs down the soul, I said. What? *I am cast away from the sight of your eyes.* You are far above, I am far below. What, then, shall we say, brothers, of God? For if you have grasped what you wish to say, it is not God: if you have been able to comprehend it, you have comprehended something else in place of God. If you have been able as it were to comprehend, you have deceived yourself by your thought. This, then, is not God, if you have comprehended it: but if it is God, you have not comprehended it. What, then, do you wish to speak, which you could not comprehend?

**6. 17.** Let us see, then, whether perhaps in the creature we may find something whereby to prove that certain three things are both shown separably and operate inseparably. Where shall we go? To heaven, to discourse of the sun and moon and stars? To the earth, to discourse perhaps of the thickets, the trees, the animals filling the earth? Or of heaven itself, or of the earth itself, which contain all that is in heaven and on earth? How long, O man, do you go round about the creature? Return to yourself, look at yourself, examine yourself, scrutinize yourself. In the creature you seek certain three things to be both shown separately and to operate inseparably: if you seek in the creature, first seek in yourself. For you are not no creature. You seek a likeness. Are you going to seek it in a beast? For you were speaking of God when you sought a likeness. You were speaking of the Trinity of the ineffable Majesty; and because you failed in things divine, and confessed your weakness with due humility, you came to things human; there examine. Do you seek in a beast, do you seek in the sun, in a star? For which of these was made to the image and likeness of God? Surely you seek something of these more familiarly and better in yourself. For God made man to his own image and likeness. Seek in yourself, lest perhaps the image of the Trinity have some trace of the Trinity. And what? An image made, far distant: yet a likeness and image far distant, not as the Son is the image of what the Father is. For the image in a son is one thing, in a mirror another. There is much difference. In a son, your image is yourself. For a son is by nature what you are. By substance what you are, in person other than you. Man, then, is not the image as the only-begotten Son is, but made to a certain image and to a certain likeness. Let him seek in himself something, if he can find it, even certain three things which may be pronounced separably, yet operate inseparably. Let me seek, seek with me. Not I in you, but you in yourselves, and I in myself. Let us seek in common, and let us handle in common our common nature and substance.

**7. 18.** See, O man, consider whether what I say is true. Do you have a body, do you have flesh? I do, you say. For whence am I, whence am I in a place, whence am I moved from place to place? Whence do I hear the words of a speaker, except through the ear of the flesh? Whence do I see the mouth of a speaker, except through the eyes of the flesh? You have one, it is established, nor need we be long busy about a manifest matter. See something else, see what operates through the flesh. For you hear with the ear, but you do not hear from the ear. There is another within who hears through the ear. You see through the eye; gaze upon him himself. Have you recognized the house, and neglected the dweller? Does the eye see by itself? Is it not another who sees through the eye? I do not say, The eye of a dead man does not see, of which it is established that the dweller has departed from the body; but the eye of one thinking of another matter does not see the face of one present. Look, then, to your inner man. For there rather is to be sought some likeness of certain three things shown separately, operating inseparably. What does your mind have? Perhaps if I seek, I find many things; but there is something near at hand, which is more easily understood. What does your soul have? I call to mind within, recall. For I do not ask that what I am about to say be believed of me: do not accept it, if you do not find it in yourself. Gaze, then; but first let us see what had slipped away: whether man is the image not only of the Son, or the image only of the Father, but of the Father and the Son; and indeed, consequently, of the Holy Spirit too. Genesis speaks: *Let us make*, it says, *man to our image and likeness*[^33]. The Father, then, does not make without the Son, nor the Son without the Father. *Let us make man to our image and likeness. Let us make*; not, "let me make"; or, "make"; or, "let him make": but, *let us make. To the image*; not, "yours"; or, "mine"; but, *to ours.*

**7. 19.** So I ask, I speak of a dissimilar thing. Let no one say: Behold what he has compared to God. I have already spoken, and forewarned, and made you cautious, and been cautious myself. These things are far distant, the lowest from the highest, the changeable from the unchangeable, the created from the creating, the human from the divine. Behold, first I commend this, that what I am about to say is far distant; let no one slander me. So that I may not perhaps seek ears, while another readies his teeth, I promised I would display this: certain three things shown separately, operated inseparably. How similar or dissimilar these are to the almighty Trinity I do not now treat: but in the very creature, lowest and changeable, we find certain three things which can be shown separably and operate inseparably. O carnal thought, and conscience stubborn and unbelieving! Why do you doubt of that ineffable Majesty in a matter which you have been able to find in yourself? Behold, I say, behold, I ask: Man, do you have memory? If you do not, how have you held what I said? But perhaps already you have forgotten what I said a little before. This very thing that I say, I said; you would not hold these two syllables, except through memory. For whence would you know there are two, if, when the second sounded, you had forgotten the first? Why, then, longer? Why am I so pressed? Why am I so compelled to convince you? It is manifest, you have memory. I ask another thing: Do you have understanding? I do, you say. For if you did not have memory, you would not hold what I said: if you did not have understanding, you would not recognize what you held. You have this too. You recall your understanding to what you hold within, and you see, and by seeing you are formed, so that you are said to know. I seek a third: You have memory, by which you hold what is said; you have understanding, by which you understand what is held; concerning these two I ask of you, willingly did you hold and understand? Willingly indeed, you say. You have, then, a will. These are the three which I had promised I would speak to your ears and minds. These three things which are in you, which you can number, and cannot separate. These three, then—memory, understanding, and will—these three, I say, notice are pronounced separately, operate inseparably.

**8. 20.** The Lord will be present, and I see that he is present: from your understanding I understand that he is present. For from these voices of yours, by how you have understood, I notice; I presume he will help, that you may understand all things. I promised that three things would be shown separably, and operate inseparably. Behold, I did not know what was in your mind; you showed me by saying, Memory. This word, this sound, this voice went forth to my ears from your mind. For this, which is memory, you were thinking silently, and not saying. It was in you, and had not yet come to me. But that what was in you might be brought forth to me, you said the very name, that is, Memory. I heard: these four syllables in the name of memory I heard. It is a name of four syllables, it is a voice, it sounded, it went forth to my ear, it conveyed something to the mind. What sounded passed away; what was conveyed remains. But this I ask: when you said this name "of memory," you see surely that this name pertains only to memory. For the other two have their own names. For one is called understanding, not memory; another is called will, not memory; but one is called memory. But that you might say this, that you might produce these four syllables, from what did you produce them? This name, which pertains only to memory, both memory wrought in you, that you might hold what you were saying; and understanding, that you might know what you held; and will, that you might bring forth what you knew. Thanks be to the Lord our God. He has helped us, both in you and in us. Truly I say to your love, I had approached this discussion and conveyance most fearfully. For I feared lest perhaps I might gladden the talent of the capable, and make a grievous tedium for the slower. But now I see that you, by your attention in hearing and your quickness in understanding, have not only perceived what was said, but flown ahead of what was to be said. Thanks be to the Lord.

**9. 21.** See, then—now secure I commend what you have understood; I do not press the unknown upon you, but repeating I commend what has been perceived. Behold, of those three, one thing was named, the name of one thing was said: Memory is the name of one of those three, and yet the name of one of those three the three themselves wrought. "Memory" alone could not be said, except by will, understanding, and memory operating. "Understanding" alone cannot be said, except by memory, will, and understanding operating. Nor can "will" alone be said, except by memory and understanding and will operating. The things promised have, I think, been unfolded: what I pronounced separately, I thought inseparably. One of these names the three wrought: but yet this one thing which the three wrought pertains not to three, but to one. The three wrought the name "of memory": but this pertains only to memory. The three wrought the name "of understanding": but it pertains only to understanding. The three wrought the name "of will": but it pertains only to will. So the Trinity made the flesh of Christ: but it pertains only to Christ. The Trinity made the dove from heaven: but it pertains only to the Holy Spirit. The Trinity made the voice from heaven: but the voice pertains only to the Father.

**9. 22.** Let no one, then, say to me, let no slanderer try to press me, the weak: What, then, of these three things which you have shown to be in our mind or in the soul, which of these three pertains to the Father, that is, as to a likeness of the Father; which of these to the likeness of the Son, which of these to the likeness of the Holy Spirit? I cannot say, I cannot unfold it. Let us leave something for those who reflect, let us grant something to silence too. Return to yourself, and remove yourself from every noise. Look within yourself, whether you have there some sweet inner chamber of your conscience, where you do not make a racket, where you do not quarrel or prepare lawsuits, where you do not plot dissensions and obstinacy. Be meek to hear the word, that you may understand. Perhaps you are about to say: *To my hearing you will give exultation and gladness, and the bones shall exult*, but *humbled*[^34], not "exalted."

**10. 23.** It suffices, then, that we have shown that certain three things are shown separably, and operate inseparably. If you have found this in yourself, if this in man, if this in a certain person walking on earth, still bearing the fragile body that weighs down the soul: believe that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit can, through certain individual visible things, through certain forms of an assumed creature, both be shown separably and operate inseparably. This suffices. I do not say, The Father is memory, the Son is understanding, the Spirit is will: I do not say this, however it may be understood; I do not dare. Let us reserve greater things for those who grasp more, weak ones to the weak what we can. I do not say these things are to be equated, as it were, to that Trinity, as if to be directed to an analogy, that is, to a certain ratio of comparison: I do not say this. But what do I say? Behold, in you I have found three things shown separably, operated inseparably; and each one of those three a name made by the three, which yet pertains not to three, but to some one of these three. Believe now there what you cannot see, if here you have heard and seen and held. For what is in you, you can know: what is in him who made you, whatever it is, when can you know? And if you will be able, you cannot yet. And yet when you are able, will you be able so to know God as God knows himself? Let it suffice, then, for your love: we have said what we could; we have rendered the promises to those exacting them: the rest that must be added, that your understanding may profit, seek from the Lord.
