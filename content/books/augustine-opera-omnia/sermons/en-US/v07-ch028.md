# Sermon 254: In the Days of Easter

**1.** So it stands, my brothers, so it stands with the misery of our condition and the mercy of God, that the time of gladness is preceded by the time of sorrow; that is, that first comes the time of sorrow, afterward the time of gladness; first the time of labor, afterward of rest; first the time of calamity, afterward of happiness. So it stands, as we have said, with the misery of our condition and the divine mercy. For the time of sorrow, labor, misery, our sins brought forth for us; but the time of gladness, rest, happiness will come not from our merits, but from the grace of the Savior. We deserve one thing, we hope for another; we deserve evils, we hope for good things. This the mercy of him who created us does.

**2.** But in the time of our misery, and, as the Scripture says, *in the days of our vanity*[^1], we ought to know from what this sadness ought to come. For sadness is like dung. Dung not put in its place is uncleanness. Dung not put in its place makes a house unclean; put in its place it makes a field fertile. See the place provided for dung by the farmer. The Apostle says: *And who is there to gladden me, except the one who is saddened by me?*[^2] And in another place: *Sadness*, he says, *that is according to God works repentance unto a salvation not to be repented of*[^3]. He who is sad according to God is sad with repentance over his sins. Sadness over one's own iniquity gives birth to righteousness. Let what you are first displease you, that you may be able to be what you are not. *The sadness that is according to God*, he says, *works repentance unto a salvation not to be repented of. Repentance*, he says, *unto salvation. What kind of salvation? Not to be repented of.* What does it mean, *not to be repented of?* One that you can in no way repent of. For we had a life that we ought to have repented of; we had a life to be repented of, but we cannot reach the life not to be repented of, except through repentance of the bad life. Surely, brothers, as I had begun to say, will you find dung in a cleaned mass of wheat? Yet to that brightness, to that beauty and loveliness one comes through dung; the foulness was the way to a beautiful thing.

**3.** Rightly too the Lord in the Gospel says of a certain unfruitful tree: *See, it is now three years that I come to it, and find no fruit on it; I will cut it down, lest it encumber my ground.* The gardener intercedes; he intercedes when the axe is already threatening the unfruitful roots and about to strike; the gardener intercedes, as Moses interceded with God; the gardener intercedes and says: *Lord, leave it this year too; I dig around it and apply a basket of dung: if it bears fruit, well; if not, you will come and cut it down*[^4]. This tree is the human race. The Lord visited this tree in the time of the patriarchs, as it were in the first year. He visited it in the time of the Law and the Prophets, as it were in the second year. See, in the Gospel the third year has dawned. Now as it were it ought to be cut down: but the merciful one entreats the merciful one. For he who wished to show himself merciful set up an intercessor against himself. Let it be left, he says, this year too; let it be dug around—the digging is a sign of humility; let a basket of dung be applied, in case perhaps it bears fruit. Rather, because it bears fruit, and in part does not bear fruit, its Lord will come and *divide* it. What does it mean, *divide*? Because there are good, there are bad, but now as it were established in one assembly, in one body.

**4.** Therefore, my brothers, as I said, the suitable place of dung produces fruit, but the unsuitable place makes a place unclean. Someone or other is sad, I find someone or other sad. I see the dung, I look for the place. Tell me, friend, why are you sad? I have lost money. An unclean place, no fruit. Let him hear the Apostle: *The sadness of the world works death*[^5]. Not only no fruit, but even great ruin. So too about the other things pertaining to worldly joys, which it would be long to enumerate. I see another grieving, groaning, weeping. I see much dung, and there I look for the place. And when I saw him sad, weeping, I looked and saw him also praying. Sad, groaning, weeping, praying—this brought me some good signification; but still I look for the place. For what if this man, praying, groaning, and with great weeping, begs death for his enemies? Even so, even so he now weeps, now begs, now prays. An unclean place, no fruit. There is more that we find in the Scriptures. He begs that his enemy may die, he falls into the curse of Judas: *Let his prayer become sin*[^6]. I looked at another again groaning, weeping, praying. I recognize the dung, I look for the place. I turned my ear to his prayer, and I hear him saying: *I said: Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^7]. He groans over sin, I recognize the field, I await the fruit. Thanks be to God. The dung is in a good place, it is not idle there, it gives birth to wheat. This is truly the time of fruitful sorrow, that we grieve over the condition of our mortality, the abundance of temptations, the stealthy approaches of sins, the adversities of desires, the brawls of the lusts that always make tumult against good thoughts; over this let us grieve, over this let us be sad.

**5.** This time of our misery and of our groaning, if there is anyone who has such a groaning of hope, those forty days before Easter signify; but the gladness, which will be later, of rest, happiness, eternal life, the kingdom without end, which is not yet, is signified by these fifty days, on which praises are said to God. For two times are signified to us: one before the Lord's resurrection, the other after the Lord's resurrection; one in which we are, the other in which we hope to be. The time of mourning, which the days of Lent signify, we both signify and have; but the time of gladness and rest and the kingdom, which these days signify, we do indeed signify by *Alleluia*, but we do not yet have the praises; rather now you sigh *Alleluia.* What is *Alleluia?* "Praise the Lord." Why are the praises of God frequently used in the Church during these days after the resurrection? Because for us too there will be perpetual praise after our resurrection. The Lord's passion signifies our time, in which we here weep. The scourges, bonds, insults, spittle, crown of thorns, gall-mixed wine, vinegar on a sponge, mockeries, reproaches, finally the cross itself, the sacred limbs hanging on the wood, what does it signify to us, except the time we now pass, the time of mourning, the time of mortality, the time of temptation? Therefore a foul time, but let this foulness of dung be in the field, not in the house. Let mourning be over sins, not over cheated desires. A foul time, but, if it is well used, a fertile time. What is fouler than a dunged field? The field was beautiful, before it had a basket of dung. The field was first brought to foulness, that it might come to richness. The foulness of this time, then, is a sign, but let this foulness be for us a time of fertility. And let us see with the prophet who says: *We saw him.* Of what kind? *He had no form or comeliness*[^8]. Why? Ask another prophet. *They numbered all my bones*[^9]. The bones of the one hanging were numbered. A foul appearance, the appearance of the crucified, but that foulness gave birth to beauty. What beauty? Of the resurrection, because he is *beautiful in form beyond the sons of men*[^10].

**6.** Let us, then, praise the Lord, brothers, because we hold his faithful promises, we have not yet received them. Do you think it a small thing, that we hold the promiser, so that we may now demand of the debtor? God the promiser became a debtor. By his goodness he became a debtor, not by any prerogative of ours. What did we give him, that we should hold him a debtor? Or perhaps because you heard in the Psalm: *What shall I repay to the Lord?* First, when he says: *What shall I repay to the Lord?* these are the words of a debtor, not of one demanding a debt. It was furnished to him: *What shall I repay to the Lord?* What does it mean: *What shall I repay?* I will pay back. For what? *For all that he has repaid to me*[^11]. What has he repaid you? First I was nothing, and he made me; I had perished, he sought me; seeking he found me; captive he redeemed me; bought he freed me; from a servant he made me a brother. *What shall I repay to the Lord?* You have nothing to repay. When you await everything from him, what have you to repay? But wait. I do not know what he means, when he asks: *What shall I repay to the Lord for all that he has repaid to me?* Looking around everywhere for what to repay, he as it were found something. What did he find? *I will take the cup of salvation*[^12]. You were thinking of repaying, you were still seeking to receive. See, I beg you. If you still seek to receive, you will still be a debtor. When the repayer? If, then, you will always be a debtor, when will you repay? You will not find what to repay. You will have nothing, except what he gives.

**7.** See, when you said: *What shall I repay?* it pertains to that which you said: *Every man is a liar*[^13]. For whoever wishes to say that he has repaid something to God is a liar. All things are to be hoped for from him. From ourselves, apart from him, nothing, except perhaps sin and a lie, because he who speaks a lie speaks of his own. Truly, man fully abounds with what is his own. He has utterly a lie there, and his heart is a treasury of lies. He lies as much as he can, he does not fail. Let him invent whatever he can, he lies whatever he can. Why? Because what he has freely—for he has it from himself, he did not buy it. But when one comes to the truth, if he wishes to be truthful, it will not be of his own. Peter was a liar of his own. Where a liar? The Lord promised us the passion, and he says: *Far be it from you, this shall not happen.* *Every man is a liar.* From where a liar? Hear the Lord himself: *You do not savor the things that are of God, but the things that are of man*[^14]. But Peter truthful, when? *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^15]. From where this truth to a lying man? See, a man says: *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.* Who says it? Peter. What was Peter? A man who said the truth? Surely, *every man is a liar.* See, see his tongue, see the truth from his tongue, how is *every man a liar?* Hear that *every man is a liar.* For that man is truthful, because not of his own. Therefore *every man is a liar*, because of his own. So from where is Peter truthful? Hear Truth himself: *Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jona.* From where blessed? From your own? Far be it. *Because flesh and blood did not reveal it to you, but my Father who is in heaven*[^16].

**8.** Let us, then, praise the Lord who is in heaven, beloved. Let us praise God. Let us say *Alleluia.* Let us signify by these days the day without end. Let us signify, in place of mortality, the time of immortality. Let us hasten to the eternal home. *Blessed are those who dwell in your house; forever and ever they will praise you*[^17]. The Law says it, Scripture says it, Truth says it. We are going to come to the house of God, which is in heaven. There we will praise God not for fifty days, but, as it is written: *Forever and ever.* We will see, we will love, we will praise; neither will what we will see fail, nor will what we will love perish, nor will what we will praise be silent; the whole will be everlasting, it will be without end. Let us praise, let us praise, but not with voices alone, let us praise also with conduct. Let the tongue praise, let the life praise, lest the tongue quarrel with the life, but let them have infinite love. Turning to the Lord, etc.
