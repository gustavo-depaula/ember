# Sermon 293/A: On the Birthday of Saint John

**1.** Since the Lord wished today to restore our voice and presence to Your Charity, and did this not according to our arrangement but according to his will, we give him thanks with you and offer you the service of a sermon. This is our ministry, in which it is both our duty and fitting for us to serve you. Your part, beloved, is to receive with love the stewardship of God's servants, such as it is, and to thank with us the one who granted us to spend this day together with you.

**2.** What, then, should we speak about today except the one whose birthday is today? Holy John, born of a barren woman, the forerunner of the Lord born of a virgin, became the one who greeted his Lord from the womb and announced him after the womb*.* The barren woman was not capable of giving birth; the virgin had no means of giving birth. The barren woman bore the herald, the virgin the judge. But the Lord Jesus Christ himself, about to come to human beings from a virgin's womb, had sent many heralds ahead. All the prophets were sent ahead by him, but he who came after them was himself speaking in them. Since the Lord sent many heralds before himself, what did this man merit so greatly? What greater excellence did he possess, whose birthday is commended to us today? For it was not without a sign of some greatness that his birthday should be known, just as his Lord's birthday is known. We do not know when the other prophets were born; we were not permitted to be ignorant of John's birth. This too appeared as his great merit: the others foretold the Lord, desired to see him, and did not see him. Even if they saw him in spirit, they saw him as future; they were not here to see him present. The Lord himself says of them to his disciples that *many prophets and righteous people wanted to see what you see and did not see it, and to hear what you hear and did not hear it.* Was he not himself sending them? Yet all had the desire, if it were possible, to see Christ here in the flesh. But because they preceded him in dying as they preceded him in being born, Christ did not find them here; nevertheless, Christ redeemed them for eternal life. To know how great was everyone's desire to see Christ here, recall that old man Simeon, to whom God's Spirit announced as no small blessing that he would not depart from this age before seeing Christ. Christ was born: Simeon recognized that infant in his mother's hands, received him, and held in his hands the one by whose divinity he himself was carried. Holding the infant Word in his hands, he blessed God, saying: *Now, Lord, you dismiss your slave in peace, because my eyes have seen your salvation.*

**3.** Therefore, the other prophets did not see him; Simeon saw him as an infant; John recognized and greeted him at his conception, and announced and saw him as a young man. Thus John was more excellent than all the others. Hear the Lord's testimony about him: he placed himself before him, no one else. Very great was the man above whom no one but Christ could be placed. Accordingly, the Lord himself says: *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist.* And to place himself before him: *But the one who is lesser* *in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.* He called himself both lesser and greater: lesser in being born, greater in ruling; lesser in age, greater in majesty. For the Lord was born after him, but in the flesh and from a virgin; before him, however, *in the beginning was the Word.* A great thing: Christ after John, and yet John through Christ. For *everything* *was made through him, and without him nothing was made.* Why, then, did John come? To show the way of humility, so that human presumption might diminish and God's glory increase. John therefore came great, commending the great one; John came as the measure of a human being. What is the measure of a human being? No human being could be more than John; whatever was more than John was already more than human. For if the measure of human greatness had reached its limit in John, you could no longer find a greater human being; yet you found someone greater. Confess as God the one you could indeed find greater than a human being. John is human, and Christ is human; but John is only human, Christ is God and human. Insofar as he is God, he made John; insofar as he is human, he was born after John.

**4.** Yet see how much this forerunner of his Lord, God and man, humbles himself. The one than whom no greater had arisen among those born of women is asked whether he himself is the Christ. He was so great that people could be mistaken. There was doubt about whether he himself was the Christ, doubt such that he was questioned. Now if he were a son of pride rather than a teacher of humility, he would have offered himself to those mistaken people. He would not have had to make them think it, but simply accepted what they already thought. Perhaps wanting to persuade people that he himself was the Christ would have been too much for him? If he tried to persuade them and were not believed, he would have remained rejected and cast down, despised among people and condemned before God. But he had no need to persuade people; he already saw them thinking it. He could accept their error and increase his honor. But far be it from the bridegroom's faithful friend to want the bride to love him in the bridegroom's place. He confessed that he was not what he was not, lest he lose what he was. John was not that bridegroom; for when questioned, he said: *The one* *who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the bridegroom's friend stands and hears him, and rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom's voice*. *I indeed baptize you in water for repentance, but the one who comes* *after me is greater than I.* How much greater? I am not worthy *to untie his sandal strap.* See how much lesser he would be even if he were worthy, how much he would humble himself if he said, *He is greater than I,* whose *sandal strap* I am worthy to untie. For he would have said he was worthy to bend down to his feet. But now how exalted he proclaimed him, when he called himself unworthy even of his feet, indeed of his sandals! Therefore, he came to teach the proud humility and to announce the way of repentance.

**5.** The voice came before the Word. How did the voice come before the Word? What is said of Christ? *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; he was in the beginning with God*. But to come to us, *the Word became flesh* to dwell among us. Therefore, since we have heard that Christ is the Word, let us hear that John is the voice. When he was asked, *Who* *are you?* he answered: *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness*. If, then, Christ is the Word and John the voice, John was taken up as a voice so that the Word might be spoken to us; and so that the Word might come to us, the voice went before. Therefore, Christ existed eternally before John; yet John had to be born first, so that the voice would precede the Word toward us. There will therefore be a time when we see the Word as the angels see him; meanwhile, let us advance in the Word, so that we may remain with him forever.

**6.** But *he* *must increase, and I must decrease*. This also appeared through the births themselves of the Word and the voice. The Word was born on December 25, from which the day begins to increase; the voice was born before God's Word, when the day begins to decrease. *He,* he says, *must increase, and I must decrease.* Their sufferings themselves showed this too: John was diminished by beheading; Christ increased, lifted upon the cross. Therefore, brothers, let us celebrate the voice's birthday in honor of the Word: *Not in drunkenness and revelries,* as the Apostle says, but whatever you do, do everything in God's name, *and the God of peace will be with you*.

Sermon 293/A, Expanded  
Sermon of Saint Augustine  
on the birthday of Saint John the Baptist  
and on the voice and the word

**3. 3.** Hear also the Lord's testimony about him: he placed himself before him, no one else. Very great was the man above whom no one but Christ could be placed. Accordingly, the Lord himself says: *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist.* And to place himself before him: *But the one who is lesser in the kingdom of heaven,* he says, *is greater than he* *.* He called himself both lesser and greater: lesser in being born, greater in ruling. For *everything* *was made through him, and without him nothing was made* *.* Why, then, did John come? To show the way of humility, so that human presumption might diminish and God's glory increase. John therefore came great, commending the great one; John came as the measure of a human being. What is the measure of a human being? For if the measure of human greatness had reached its limit in John, you could no longer find a greater human being; yet you found someone greater. Confess as God the one you could find greater than a perfect human being. John is human, and Christ is human; but John is only human, Christ is God and human. Insofar as he is God, he made John; insofar as he is human, he was born after John.

**4. 4.** Yet see how much this forerunner of his Lord, God and man, humbles himself. The one than whom no greater had arisen among those born of women is asked whether he himself is the Christ. He was so great that people could be mistaken. There was doubt about whether he himself was the Christ, doubt such that he was questioned. Now if he were a son of pride rather than a teacher of humility, he would not oppose those mistaken people. He would not have had to make them think it, but simply accepted what they already thought. Perhaps wanting to persuade people that he himself was the Christ would have been too much for him? If he tried to persuade them and were not believed, he would have remained rejected and cast down, despised among people and condemned before God. But he had no need to persuade people; he already saw them thinking it. He could accept their error and increase his honor. But far be it from the bridegroom's faithful friend to want the bride to love him in the bridegroom's place. He confessed that he was not what he was not, lest he lose what he was. For John was not that bridegroom. When questioned, he said: *The one who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the bridegroom's friend stands and hears him, and rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom's voice* *. I indeed baptize you in water for repentance, but the one who comes after me is greater than I.* How much greater? *I am not worthy to untie his sandal strap* *.* See how much lesser he would be even if he were worthy, how much he would humble himself if he said, 'He is greater than I, whose sandal strap I am worthy to untie.' For he would have said he was worthy to bend down to his feet. But now how exalted he proclaimed him, when he called himself unworthy even of his feet, indeed of his sandals! Therefore, he came to teach the proud humility and to announce the way of repentance.

**5. 5.** The voice came before the word. How did the voice come before the word? What is said of Christ? *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; he was in the beginning with God* *.* But so that the Word might come to us, *the Word became flesh* to dwell among us. Therefore, we have heard that Christ is the Word; let us hear that John is the voice. When he was asked, *Who are you?* *,* he answered: *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness* *.* Therefore, beloved, let us discuss voice and word briefly, briefly, as much as the Lord grants. Christ is the Word, a word that does not sound and pass away, for what sounds and passes away is a voice, not a word. Therefore, God's Word, through whom everything was made, is our Lord Jesus Christ; *the voice of one crying in the wilderness* is John. Which comes first, voice or word? Let us see what a word is and what a voice is, and there we will see which comes first.

**6. 6.** What do you think a word is, brothers? Let us leave aside that Word of God and speak a little about our own words, to see whether we can take some steps of likeness from the lowest things to the highest. Who comprehends God's Word, through whom everything was made? Who is worthy even to think of him, much less to speak of him? Therefore, let us set aside for a little while his majesty, his ineffable eternity and coeternity with the Father. Let us believe what we do not see, so that by believing we may deserve to see. See, let us discuss this word as something continually present in our hearts, ears, or mouths. What is a word? Do we now suppose the word is what sounds in your ears? The word is what you want to say. You have conceived something in your heart to say; that conception has already become a word in your heart. But this word—that is, what you want to say, the conception formed in your heart, what you have undertaken to express—you already know, and it has been spoken within you. May that Word who is God's Son assist me, so that I may fittingly express to your ears what he himself has deigned to give our heart to conceive. But if, unequal and weak before the greatness of the subject, I perhaps fail and do not express it worthily, you have someone to turn to from me. May that very Son of God, God's Word, preside over your hearts and, speaking with you inwardly, fulfill there what I, as a human being, could not fulfill in your ears. Nevertheless, help my effort by your attention and by prayer for me, that I may be able to speak, and for yourselves, that you may deserve to hear.

**7. 7.** Therefore, let the word be, as we said, what you conceive in your heart to say. Let the thing itself you want to say, conceived in the heart to be spoken, be called a word. When you have conceived the thing you want to say and that very thing, that thought itself, has become a word in your heart, you consider whom you are addressing and to whom you want to say it. If you see a Greek, you seek a Greek voice in which to bring the word out; if you see a Latin speaker, a Latin voice in which to utter the word; if you see a Hebrew, a Hebrew voice; if a Punic speaker, a Punic voice—if you know these languages. But if you do not know them, when you see standing before you someone who knows only the language you do not know, you lack not the word but the voice. Therefore, the word you had conceived in your heart preceded all voices and was before all voices—before Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Punic. That conception preceded every language and every voice throughout the world. The soul's offspring was carried by the soul laboring to give birth, and a way to bring it forth was sought, because what was already held in the heart could not be conveyed to another except through some voice. But unless that voice were distinct, how could it be recognized? The voice had to be distinguished by differences of language, so that you spoke Greek to a Greek, Hebrew to a Hebrew, Punic to a Punic speaker. But the word you had conceived before all voices was neither Latin nor Greek nor Punic nor anything of that sort. Therefore, see a great mystery. If you were altogether silent, would the word in your heart therefore not be alive? And if there were no one to speak to, would what you conceived in your heart not be apparent within you? It would be apparent in simple knowledge, without any difference of language.

**8. 8.** Let us say something by way of example to make this clearer. God is a certain reality above everything he made, if indeed he should be called a reality. Therefore, God is something surpassing everything he made, from whom, in whom, and through whom everything exists. Is all that I have said, what God is, two syllables? Is all that immense power confined within these two syllables? God existed even before I conceived this in my heart. How can I express this very thing by saying 'Deus'? What is called 'Deus' in Latin, 'theos' in Greek, and 'ylim' in Punic—I have named three languages—what I conceived in my heart was none of those languages. But when I wanted to express what I had conceived in my heart about God, if I met a Punic speaker, I said 'ilim'; if a Latin speaker, 'Deus'; if a Greek, 'theos.' Before I met any of them, what was in my heart was neither Greek nor Punic nor Latin. Therefore, what I conceived to express was the word; what I employed to express it was the voice.

**9. 9.** We have discussed voice and word: the word before all languages, the voice in some language. Which, then, is first, word or voice? Within me the word comes first. For unless I conceived a word in my heart, I would not employ a voice by which to express the word. Therefore, the word was conceived before the voice and employed the voice as a kind of vehicle through which it might come to you, not in which it might exist within me. For I know what I am going to say even if I do not say it. See, before I speak I have employed no voice, and the word is within me. But to make it known to you, I employ a voice so that when you hear the voice, the word may also be in you. Therefore, within me, for teaching, the word precedes and the voice follows. But for you, for learning, the voice precedes and the word follows. Attend carefully and understand, with the Lord's help. For I know I am speaking of hidden things placed in the secrecy of mysteries, but such as I am, I speak to faithful Christians whose faith anticipates what I am about to say. Therefore, within me the word preceded, and the voice was employed for the preceding word. For you my voice preceded, and then you understood the word that was in my heart.

**10. 10.** Therefore, if Christ is the Word and John the voice, Christ the Word preceded with God, but the voice preceded toward us so that the Word might come to us. Therefore, the Word was with God, and John the voice did not yet exist. Surely before John was the voice, the Word already existed with God? He was there; but so that the Word might be spoken to us, John was taken up as a voice. And so that the Word might come to us, the voice went before. Therefore, Christ existed eternally before John; yet John had to be born first, so that the voice would precede the Word toward us. Blessed is the Lord our God, because I have spoken as I could, and you have understood what you could. May he increase and multiply your understanding, and may the Word who sent the voice ahead become clear to you.

**11. 11.** But see, my brothers: the voice sounds and passes away; the word remains. See what I mean. Take my saying 'Deus': first I conceived in my heart what to say; then those two syllables sounded and passed away. Did what I conceived in my heart pass away with them? Again, when I said 'Deus,' it brought about in your heart the thought of God. In my heart that thought preceded my speaking, and in your heart the thought of God arose when you heard these two syllables. These two syllables fulfilled their ministry and passed away; nevertheless, what I had conceived in my heart did not pass away. It was in me and remained in me after these two syllables were spoken. And the thought that arose in your heart when these two syllables touched your ear remained in your heart after they passed away. Therefore, brothers, the ministry of the man John was going to pass away like a voice. Rightly he also received a baptism, but John's baptism was transitory and was called John's baptism. There were Christ's baptism and John's baptism, but John's baptism was transitory like a voice, while Christ's baptism remains and remains forever, as the word remains.

**12. 12.** The more we advance in God, the more voices diminish and the word grows in us. Why do we have voices except to understand something? If fullness of understanding were in us, there would be no need of voices. If we could see our thoughts, would we need a tongue to speak to one another? Therefore, a time will come when we see the Word as the angels see him, and your voices will not be needed. For there will be no need to preach the Gospel then, when we see the Word himself. All temporal things will pass away, because the voice is from flesh, from grass; but *the glory* *of flesh is like the grass's flower: the grass dried, the flower fell, but the Lord's word remains forever* *.* Therefore, because the more we advance toward understanding, the less we will need the voices through which we are led to understanding, John himself also said: *He must increase, but I must decrease* *.* As the word increases, the voice decreases. What does the word's increasing mean? The Word himself does not increase, but we grow in him, advance in him, and increase in him, so that we no longer need voices. This also appeared through the births themselves of the Word and the voice. The Word was born on December 25, from which the day begins to increase; the voice was born before God's Word, when the day begins to decrease. *He must increase, but I must decrease.* Their sufferings themselves showed this too: John diminished through beheading; Christ increased, lifted upon the cross.

**13. 13.** Therefore, let us celebrate the voice's birthday in honor of the Word, and neither heed nor be deceived by the subtleties of empty people who do not understand what they say. For John had a baptism, and in the Acts of the Apostles we find that those having only John's baptism were baptized. Certain disciples were found who had John's baptism and were commanded by the Apostle Paul to be baptized, because they had only a transitory baptism, the baptism of the voice, not yet of the Word. For now you look for John's baptism and do not find it: the voice sounded and passed away, but Christ's baptism remains today. Therefore, because for the sake of this very mystery the Apostle Paul commanded those having John's baptism to be baptized, heretics have wanted to draw from this an argument for rebaptism. We grieve over their error and rejoice over their deliverance. Therefore, let us answer something briefly about this.

**14. 14.** You think a person who received Christ's baptism should be rebaptized because the Apostle Paul commanded people who had John's baptism to be baptized, and you argue this way: 'If after John the Baptist, of whom the Lord said, *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist* *,* if after him the Apostles baptized, how much more should people be baptized after heretics!' I answer: You think John the Baptist is wronged because people were baptized after him, if they are not baptized after heretics. I too deplore that wrong, but I reply to you this way: If people were baptized after John, ought they not to have been baptized after Optatus? What will you say to me here? Who was John? '*Among those born of women* no one will arise *greater than John the Baptist.*' There is some presbyter among you who is at least a drunkard—I do not want to say a moneylender or an adulterer. For now, let us take what is common and done publicly: there is at least a drunken presbyter among you. 'Very well.' Why do you not rebaptize after him? If you baptize after John, who drank no wine, ought you not to baptize after a drunkard? Here he is certainly troubled and has nothing to say. What, then? Hear it from me.

**15. 15.** Paul commands the baptism of those who had John's baptism and did not have Christ's. But why do you not baptize after a drunkard? Because he gave no baptism except Christ's. For it is Christ's baptism: whether a sober man gives it or a drunkard gives it, it is Christ's, neither the sober man's nor the drunkard's. Peter gave this baptism: it is Christ's. Judas gave it: it is Christ's. For it is not Peter's baptism because Peter gave it. Why? Just as that was called John's baptism, this was not called Peter's baptism in those Peter baptized, Paul's baptism in those Paul baptized, John the Evangelist's in those he baptized, or Judas's in those Judas baptized. It was not called Peter's baptism, Paul's baptism, or Judas's baptism. Rather, in those baptized by Peter, Paul, John, and Judas, it is Christ's baptism. But the baptism given by John's disciples was John's, because John had received this stewardship and preliminary ministry: the voice before the word. Therefore, you do not want to baptize after a drunkard, nor do I after a heretic.

**16. 16.** And if perhaps you think that a heretic does not enter the kingdom of heaven but a drunkard does, the Apostle Paul made the matter clear, saying: *The works of the flesh are manifest: fornications, impurities, extravagance, service of idols, sorcery, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkenness, revelries, and* *things like these. I warn you about them, as I warned you before, that those who do such things will not possess God's kingdom* *.* He placed heresies where he placed drunkards, and concluded: *Those who do such things will not possess God's kingdom.* Set a heretic and a drunkard before me. If the heretic perseveres in heresy, he does not enter the kingdom of heaven; likewise, if the drunkard perseveres in drunkenness, he does not enter the kingdom of heaven. Tell me: whose baptism do both give? Both are outside the kingdom of heaven, but they give something belonging to the kingdom of heaven. A herald pronounces the sentence but does not free the innocent. The judge frees him, having instructed the herald what to say. Sometimes the herald is wicked, and an innocent person is freed through him. The wicked herald says, 'I have ordered him released,' and through a wicked person an innocent one is freed. Why? Because the herald's voice is the judge's sentence. A drunkard baptizes: it is a ministry. A heretic has baptized: it is a ministry. Baptism's gift is almighty God's gift. Certainly, if he baptized in Donatus's name, the person must be baptized again. But if I recognize Christ's baptism there, if I recognize the Gospel's words, if I recognize my king's form and mark, then even if you were a deserter and could be convicted and put to death by that mark, come to the camp with the Lord's mark. You can deserve pardon; your mark cannot be changed.
