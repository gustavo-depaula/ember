# Sermon 151

**1. 1.** The divine reading that was recited from the Epistle of the apostle Paul, whenever it is read, makes us fear lest, being misunderstood, it give occasion to people who are looking for occasion. For people are prone to sin, and scarcely hold themselves back. So when they hear the Apostle saying, *For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I hate, that I do*[^1], they do evil, and, as though displeased with themselves for doing evil, they suppose themselves to be like the Apostle, who said, *For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I do not will, that I do*. So sometimes it is read, and then it lays upon us the necessity of discoursing, lest people, taking the wholesome food amiss, turn it into poison. Let your Charity, then, be attentive, until I tell you what the Lord shall grant; so that where you perhaps see me laboring in the difficulty of some obscurity, you may help me by the affection of your devotion.

**1. 2.** First, then, recall what by God's favor you are accustomed to hear: that the life of the righteous man in this body is still warfare, not yet triumph. But of this warfare there will one day be a triumph. So the Apostle uttered both the voices of warfare and the voices of triumph. The voices of warfare we have just heard: *For I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do. But if I do what I hate, I agree with the law, that it is good. To will lies near at hand for me, but to accomplish the good I do not find. But I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*[^2]. When you hear "fighting against," when you hear "taking captive," do you not recognize warfare?

**2. 2.** The voice of triumph, then, is not yet here; but because it is to come, the same Apostle teaches you, saying, *This corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. But when this corruptible has put on incorruption, and this mortal has put on immortality*—there is the voice of triumph—*then shall come to pass the saying that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory*. Let those who triumph say, *Where, O death, is your struggle*[^3]? We shall say it, then; one day we shall say it; and that "one day" will not be far off. For there does not remain as much of this age as has already been taken away. This, then, we shall say one day. But now, in this warfare, lest this reading should be a trumpet of the enemy, not ours, by which he may be roused, not by which he may be conquered—attend, I beseech you, my brothers, and you who are fighting, fight. For those who are not yet fighting will not understand what I say; those who are already fighting will understand it. My voice is in the open, yours will be in silence. First recall what he wrote to the Galatians, by which this can well be expounded. For he says, speaking to the faithful, speaking to the baptized, to whom surely in the holy washing all sins had been forgiven—yet speaking to these, but speaking to those who are fighting, he says, *But I say: Walk by the Spirit, and do not accomplish the desires of the flesh*. He did not say, "do not have them," but, *do not accomplish them*. Why this? It follows, and he says, *For the flesh desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh. For these are opposed to one another, that you may not do what you will. But if you are led by the Spirit, you are no longer under the law*[^4]—surely, but under grace. *If you are led by the Spirit*; what is it to be led by the Spirit? To consent to the Spirit of God commanding, not to the flesh desiring. Yet it desires and resists; and it wills something, and you do not will it; persevere, that you may not will it.

**3. 3.** Yet your desire toward God ought to be such that there be not even that desire which you resist. See what I have said: your desire toward God, I say, ought to be such that there be no desire at all which you must resist. For you resist, and by not consenting you conquer; but it is better not to have an enemy than to conquer one. This enemy will one day not exist. Turn your mind to the voice of triumph, and see whether it will exist. *Where, O death, is your struggle*? It will not be. *Where, O death, is your sting*[^5]? You will seek its place and not find it. For this is not—what you most ought to hear—this is not, as the Manicheans rave, as it were another nature. It is our weakness, it is our defect. It will not be elsewhere, set apart, but it will be nowhere, healed. Therefore, *do not accomplish the desires of the flesh*. It would indeed be better to fulfill what the law said, *You shall not covet*[^6]. This is the fullness of virtue, the perfection of righteousness, the palm of victory: *You shall not covet*. Since this cannot now be fulfilled, let at least that be fulfilled which holy Scripture likewise says, *Do not go after your desires*[^7]. It is better not to have them; but since they are there, do not go after them. They do not want to go after you; do not go after them. If they should want to go after you, they will not be; for they will not rebel against your mind. They rebel; rebel back. They fight; fight. They assail; assail back. Only see to this: that they do not conquer.

**4. 4.** See, I will set forth something from this, from which you may understand the rest. You know that there are sober people; they are fewer, but they exist. You know too that there are drunkards; they abound. A sober man has been baptized; so far as concerns drunkenness, he has nothing to fight against; he has other desires to fight against. But that you may understand about the rest, let us set in the midst the contest of one enemy only. A drunkard has been baptized; he heard, and heard with fear, that among the other evils by which the kingdom of God is shut off from people living badly, drunkenness too was mentioned; for where it was said, *Neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor sodomites, nor thieves*, there was added, *nor drunkards, etc., shall possess the kingdom of God*[^8]. He heard, and feared. He was baptized; all the things in which he was drunk are forgiven him; there remains the adversary, habit. So the reborn man has something to fight against. All his past vices are forgiven him; let him watch, be vigilant, fight, lest at some time he become drunk. So that desire for drinking rises up, tickles the mind, brings dryness into the throat, lies in wait for the senses; it even wants, if it can be done, to penetrate the wall itself, to reach the one shut within, to drag him captive. It fights; fight back. Oh, if only it did not exist! If it came in by an evil habit, it will die by a good habit; only do not satisfy it, do not glut it by yielding, but kill it by resisting. Yet as long as it exists, it is an enemy. If you do not consent to it, and are never drunk, it will be less and less every day. For your submission is its strength. For if you yield to it and become drunk, you give it strength. Is it against me, and not against you? I admonish from the higher place, I speak, I proclaim; what evil is going to befall drunkards, I announce beforehand. There is no reason for you to say, I did not hear; there is no reason for you to say, God requires my soul at the hand of the one who was silent to me. But you labor, because by an evil habit you yourself made for yourself a strong adversary. You did not labor to nourish it; labor to conquer it. And if you are less than fit against it, ask God. Yet if it has not conquered you—though your own evil habit has wrestled with you, if it has not conquered you—you have done what the apostle Paul says, *Do not accomplish the desires of the flesh*. The desire arose by its tickling, but it was not accomplished by drinking.

**5. 5.** What I have said of drunkenness, this I say of all vices, this of all desires. For with some we are born, others we have made by habit. Now on account of those with which we are born, infants are baptized, that they may be loosed from the guilt of their origin—not of an evil habit, which they did not have. Therefore we must always fight, because that very desire with which we are born cannot be ended as long as we live; it can be lessened daily, it cannot be ended. On account of it this body of ours is called the body of death. Of it the Apostle says, *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man. But I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*[^9]. This law was born when the first law was transgressed. Then was this law born, I say, when the first law was despised and transgressed. What is the first law? The one the human being received in paradise. Were they not naked, and not ashamed[^10]? Why were they naked and not ashamed, except because there was not yet a law in the members fighting against the law of the mind? The human being did a deed to be punished, and found a motion to be ashamed of. They ate against the prohibition, and their eyes were opened. What then? Did they before wander in paradise with closed or blind eyes? Far from it. For how did Adam impose names on the birds and beasts, when all the animals were brought to him[^11]? On those on which he imposed names, if he did not see them? Then it is said, *The woman saw that the tree was pleasing to the eyes to look upon*[^12]. So they had open eyes; and they were naked, and were not ashamed. But their eyes were opened to something they had never felt, something they had never dreaded in the motion of their body. Their eyes were opened to gaze upon it, not to see; and because they felt something shameful, they took care to cover it. *They sewed together*, it says, *fig leaves, and made themselves girdles*[^13]. What they covered, there they felt it. See whence original sin is drawn; see whence no one is born without sin. See why the Lord did not wish to be conceived in this way, whom a virgin conceived. He loosed it who came without it; he loosed it who did not come from it. Hence one and one: one to death, one to life. The first man to death, the second man to life. But why was that man to death? Because he was only man. Why is this man to life? Because he is God and man.

**6. 6.** The Apostle, then, does not do what he wills; for he wills not to covet, and yet he covets; so he does not do what he wills. Did that evil desire drag the Apostle, subjugated, to fornication and adultery? Far from it. Let no such thoughts rise into our heart. He wrestled, he was not subjugated. But because he did not want even to have this against which he wrestled, he therefore said, *I do not do what I will*. I do not wish to covet, and I covet. So I do not do what I will; yet I do not consent to the desire. For otherwise he would not say, *Do not accomplish the desires of the flesh*, if he himself accomplished them. But he set before your eyes his own struggle, that you might not fear your own. For if the blessed Apostle had not said this, when you saw a desire stirred in your members, to which you did not consent—yet, when you saw it stirred, you would perhaps despair of yourself and say, If I belonged to God, I would not be moved thus. See the Apostle fighting, and do not make yourself despair. *I see another law*, he says, *in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*. And because I do not want it to fight—for it is my flesh, it is my very self, it is a part of me—*I do not do what I will, but the evil that I hate, that I do*; because I covet.

**7. 7.** What good, then, do I do? Because I do not consent to the evil desire. I do good, and I do not accomplish the good; and the desire, my enemy, does evil, and does not accomplish evil. How do I do good and not accomplish good? I do good when I do not consent to the evil desire; but I do not accomplish good, so as not to covet at all. And again, how does my enemy do evil and not accomplish evil? It does evil, because it stirs an evil longing; it does not accomplish evil, because it does not drag me to evil. And in this warfare is the whole life of the saints. What now shall I say of the unclean, who do not even fight? Subjugated, they are dragged along; nor are they dragged, since they follow willingly. This, I say, is the battle of the saints; and in this warfare a person is always in peril, until he dies. But in the end—that is, in the triumph of that victory—what is said? Rather, what does the Apostle say, already foretasting the triumph? *Then shall come to pass the saying that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your struggle*? The voice of those who triumph. *Where, O death, is your sting? But the sting of death is sin*[^14]; by whose stinging death was wrought. Sin is like a scorpion: it stung us, and we died. But when it is said, *Where, O death, is your sting*?—by which sting you were made, not which you made; when, therefore, it is said, *Where, O death, is your sting*?—surely it will not be, because sin will not be. *But the sting of death is sin*. Against sin the law was given. *But the power of sin is the law*[^15]. How is *the law the power of sin*? *It came in, that the offense might abound*[^16]. How is this? Because before the law the human being was a sinner; the law being given and transgressed, he became also a transgressor. People were held guilty by sin; the law being given, they were made more guilty by transgression.

**8. 8.** Where is hope, but in what follows: *Where sin abounded, grace abounded the more*[^17]? Therefore that soldier, in a way most practiced in this warfare—so practiced that he was even a leader—when he labored in this warfare against the enemy, and said, *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*, a foul law, a wretched law, a wound, a rottenness, a sickness—added, *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death*[^18]? And help came to him as he groaned. How did help come? *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord* will free you from the law of this death—that is, from the body of this death—*the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^19]. When will you have a body in which no desire will remain? *When this mortal puts on immortality, and this corruptible puts on incorruption*, and it will be said to death, *Where, O death, is your struggle*? and it will not be: *Where, O death, is your sting*? and it will be nowhere[^20]. What now? Hear: *So then I myself serve the law of God with the mind, but the law of sin with the flesh*[^21]. *I serve the law of God with the mind*, by not consenting; *but the law of sin with the flesh*, by coveting. And with the mind the law of God, and with the flesh the law of sin. And I delight in this, and there I covet; but I am not conquered; it tickles, lies in wait, knocks, tries to drag: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death*? I do not wish always to conquer; but I wish at some time to come to peace. Now then, brothers, keep this measure: serve the law of God with the mind, but the law of sin with the flesh—but out of necessity, because you covet, not because you consent. Sometimes this desire so lies in wait for the saints that it works in those asleep what it cannot in those awake. Whence did you all cry out, but because you all recognized it? It is shameful to linger here, but let it not be irksome to pray to God about it. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
