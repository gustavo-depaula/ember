# Sermon 283: Augm. (Dolbeau 15)

**1.** Let us so marvel at the fortitude of the holy martyrs in their passion that we proclaim the grace of God. For neither do they themselves wish to be praised in themselves, but in him to whom it is said: *In the Lord shall my soul be praised*[^1]. Those who understand this are not proud. With trembling they ask, with joy they receive; they persevere, they do not lose it. For because they are not proud, they are meek. And therefore, when he had said: *In the Lord shall my soul be praised,* he added: *Let the meek hear, and rejoice*[^2]. What is the weak flesh, what is it but worms and rottenness? Or wherever anything might be, unless what we sang were true: *My soul will be subject to God, for from him is my patience*[^3]? For that the martyrs might endure all things for the faith, their virtue is named patience. For there are two things that either entice or drive men into sins: pleasure and pain. Pleasure entices, pain drives. Against pleasures, continence is necessary; against pains, patience. For in this way it is suggested to the human mind, that it may sin. Sometimes it is said: "Do this, and you will have this"; but sometimes: "Do this, lest you suffer this." A promise precedes pleasure, a threat precedes pain. Therefore, that men may have pleasures, or may not suffer pain, they sin. And therefore God, against these two things, one of which is in an alluring promise, the other in a terrible threat, deigned both to promise and to terrify: to promise the kingdom of heaven, to terrify with the punishments of hell. Pleasure is sweet, but God is sweeter. Temporal pain is evil, but eternal fire is worse. You have what to love against the loves of the world, indeed against unclean loves; you have what to fear against the terrors of the world.

**2.** But it is too little to be admonished, unless you obtain help, which pertains to the threat. Against all pleasures the law cries out to you: *You shall not covet*[^4]. You have heard: it is a divine oracle, God said it. That he commanded well and admonished truthfully, no one of the faithful has doubted. But see what the apostle says: *The law came, and sin revived*[^5]. For before it was said to you: *You shall not covet,* you thought you sinned lawfully, nor was it thought a sin, when it was not forbidden. Because sin was forbidden, it was known. Known, therefore, let it be avoided, if you were seeking the help of the law. You have heard. What more do you desire from the law? *You shall not covet.* The letter of the law has stuck in your mind, you have something with which to struggle, but you will be conquered if you have no help. Whence the help? From grace. For *love has been poured out in our hearts,* not by ourselves, but *by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^6]. Take away this grace, remove this help: *the letter kills*[^7]. The world held you at first a sinner; with the letter coming without help, it will hold you also a transgressor. Therefore *the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life*[^8], because *the love of God has been poured out in our hearts, by the Holy Spirit who has been given to us.* To this end the law terrifies, that it may compel us to ask for help. The *law,* as the apostle himself says, is as it were a *tutor*[^9]. The tutor does not instruct, but leads to the master. Instructed and fortified by the master, one will no longer be under the tutor. For the letter will no longer terrify you, whom grace helps.

**3.** We have said this on account of what is written: *You shall not covet.* For this precept seems set against those sins which are committed through the enticement of pleasure, and the apostle set this down as it were generally, as if it were the sole voice of the law: *You shall not covet.* Does this precept avail anything against the fears of pain as well? Perhaps it avails. For what then? He does not wish to grieve: he covets health. He who fears to die covets life. Against this depraved coveting of bodily health, where there is no need, and of bodily death, which, like it or not, is to come, extend the precept: *You shall not covet,* and say with the prophet: *And the day of men I have not coveted: you know*[^10]. This voice was in the martyrs: they did not covet the day of men, lest they not arrive at the day of God. They did not covet the day with a brief end, that they might arrive at the day without end. What did they despise, and what did they receive? For temporal losses are in no way to be compared with those gains. One labors for one hour, and eternity is acquired.

**4.** Consider, brethren, the day of the martyr; it is good for us to dwell in the very exhortation of his patience. Consider the labors of soldiers, of those who bear arms, what perils they undergo, what hard and arduous things they endure, in cold, in heat, hunger, thirst, wounds, deaths. Daily they live amid perils, setting before their eyes not the labor of the soldier, but the leisure of the veteran. "See," they say, "the labor ends, after a few years leisure will follow, it will be well with us, our means will not be lacking, immunity will be there, we shall be summoned to no public duties of the cities, no one after the burden of military service will lay his own on us." With this reward set before them, one labors toward an uncertainty. For he who, soldiering, says: "The labor ends," whence does he know that he himself may not end before the labor ends? Perhaps even when the labor is ended, leisure now attained, he dies at once. And he for whom it had been set, on account of the promised leisure, to labor somewhat longer, is not permitted to enjoy for long the very leisure to which by labor he came. In these uncertainties one labors, and yet for uncertain leisure a certain labor is undertaken. Awake, Christian hearts; soldier for God, with whom labor cannot be void, with whom peril cannot be fruitless. For the soldier of the world will by death lose his reward in battle, the soldier of Christ by death finds his reward. Finally, after a labor of brief time, not rest of long time is received, but of no time. For there we rest, not where there will be long time, but where there will be no time at all. For our age will be eternity, where there is neither growth nor aging nor will a day be added, because no day will depart. If, then, it were said to you: "Labor twenty years that you may rest forty," who would not wish, for a single labor, a double rest? Yet scarcely does it befall any veteran, even if he grows old, to enjoy leisure for as long a time as he labored. But of us, what is said? And our commander, who is to render us an eternal reward, not a daily wage, how does he exhort us through the apostle? *For what is,* he says, *the momentary lightness of our tribulation, works for us beyond an incredible measure an eternal weight of glory*[^11].

**5.** What words, brethren! How he made light of what we suffer, how he commended what we hope for! *What is,* he says, *the momentary lightness of our tribulation, beyond an incredible measure . . .* For the *incredible measure eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him*[^12]. To the faithful, then, he says: *Beyond an incredible measure it works for us an eternal weight of glory;* light and momentary tribulation he calls it to the faithful. *Beyond an incredible measure:* we are bidden to believe what is incredible. Faithful soldier, believe the incredible, for to God nothing is impossible. He said *weight* by which you may become heavy, lest you be light. *In a weighty people,* he says, *I will praise you*[^13]. He said *weight,* that the gravity of love may confirm you, that the wind of temptation may not carry you off. Consider the threshing floor and love gravity, fear lightness. There is chaff, there is wheat: both are shaken by the winnowing fan, but not both are snatched by the wind. The one remains through gravity, the other flies through lightness.

**6.** This, then, will be done in us, when for the sake of faith and justice we endure all adversities; for not for the sake of any rest whatever. *Blessed,* he says—which we have heard—*are those who have suffered persecution for justice's sake*[^14]. By this addition he distinguished us from adulterers, robbers, murderers, parricides, the sacrilegious, sorcerers, heretics. For they too suffer persecution, but not *for justice's sake.* If you wish to avoid temptation, see the distinction. Choose the cause, lest you shudder at the penalty. Choose the cause, lest you suffer the penalty in vain. And when you have chosen the cause, commend it too to God and say to him: *Judge me, O God, and distinguish my cause from a nation that is not holy*[^15]. By him your cause is distinguished, from whom is your patience. For he grants true patience. For in a bad cause it is hardness, not patience. Whatever has hardened in the body, sensation lost, is healed with more difficulty. For all praiseworthy virtues have neighboring vices, by which the incautious are deceived; they have also contrary ones. It is easy for a man to take care not to rush into the contrary. To avoid the neighboring one is hard. For the neighborhood of the vices has a certain appearance and shadow of the virtues. What I say, having set forth certain examples, the rest recalled, I leave to be understood.

**7.** See, we speak of patience itself: to this virtue impatience is contrary. Patience is the endurance of evils for justice; impatience is the non-endurance of evils for any cause whatever. This endurance is contrary to non-endurance. Endurance in vice is hardness. For hardness imitates patience, but is not patience. See, then, lest perhaps it deceive you by its neighborhood, lest perhaps you be hard and seem to yourself patient. For just as he is better than the rest who is not driven to evil even by evil, so he is worse than the rest who is not even called back from evil by evil. Pain is an evil, iniquity is an evil. A reward was promised that you might do iniquity, you do not consent: you have conquered the desire of rewards. From another side you were assailed. Pains will be set before you, conquer also the bite of pains, you who conquered the pleasure of rewards. He who promises you a reward to lead you to iniquity, as it were by a good entices you to evil. He who threatens you with evil to compel you to iniquity, by an evil drives you to evil, but by a small evil to a great evil. But you will not be great, if you despise the one who promises; you will be greater, if you despise the one who terrifies, if you conquer the one who rages. Therefore I said: Just as he is better than the rest who is not driven to evil even by evil, so he is worse than the rest who is not called back from evils even by evil. You admonished a man not to be an adulterer, he despised your admonitions, he was not called back from evil by good. But if you begin to threaten pains, to inflict blows, to impose certain troublesome things, if not even then he restrains himself from evil, how much worse is he who could not even by evil be called back from evil. See to what calamity hardness, neighbor to patience, is subject. For to this end is hardness in the hearts of the impious, that they not be called back from evil deeds, neither by the proposal nor by the infliction of punishments. Whence you boasted, you are worse. "I endured," you say, "I conquered, I did not yield, I was not bent." I would praise this, if I recognized patience. But now I detest hardness . . . patience from hardness. "Do not question my voice, but my cause. Let the cause itself answer you." He is a robber: he suffers penalties for his robbery. He is tortured, and what he knows to be true in his conscience he does not confess. Shall we say: "Great patience"? Rather let us say: "Detestable hardness." But if he does this for a crime, what do you do for the faith? In him not to confess seems wonderful, in you to confess is glorious. For he suffers for a crime which must be denied, you for Christ who must be confessed. But sometimes the hardness is so great that one both confesses the evil and for the very confession of his iniquity suffers evils, lest he fall or depart from evils. He is ready to be tortured for Donatus, nor does he cover this by denying it, but he confesses it, nor does he blush: he boasts of his iniquity. Would that he were hidden from himself, while there is what appears. The wound you do not wish to be healed, you even dare to lay bare. This is not health with sensation, but hardness without sensation.

**8.** Let us love patience, let us hold patience, and, if we do not yet have it, let us ask for it. For from him is our patience, from whom is continence. From him is our continence against pleasures, from him is patience against pains. But the present psalm which we sang taught us that from him is our patience, surely against pains. Whence do we find that from him is also our continence, which is necessary against pleasures? We have a most evident testimony: *And when I knew,* he says, *that no one can be continent unless God grant it; this very thing too was wisdom, to know whose gift this was*[^16]. Therefore if you have anything from God, and do not know from whom you have it, you will not be rewarded, because you remain ungrateful. If you do not know from whom you have it, you do not give thanks: by not giving thanks, even what you have, you will lose. *For to him who has, it shall be given*[^17]. What is it to have fully? To know whence you have it. *But he who does not have,* that is, does not know whence he has it, *even what he has shall be taken from him*[^18]. Finally, just as he says: *This very thing too was wisdom, to know whose gift this was,* so too the apostle Paul, when he commended to us the grace of God in the Holy Spirit, said: *We have received not the spirit of this world, but the Spirit which is from God.* And as if it were said to him: "Whence do you distinguish it?", he went on and said: *That we may know the things given to us by God*[^19]. Therefore the Spirit of God is the Spirit of love; the spirit of this world is the spirit of pride. Those who have the spirit of this world are proud, are ungrateful to God. They have many of his gifts, but they do not worship the one from whom they have them: therefore they are unhappy. Sometimes one man has greater gifts, another has lesser: for example, intelligence, memory. They are gifts of God. You will sometimes find a man most keen in talent, with a memory of incredible wonder; but you will find another of small understanding, of no tenacious memory, but endowed with both small: yet the one proud, the other humble; the one giving thanks to God for small things, the other attributing the greater things to himself. Incomparably better is he who gives thanks to God for a small thing than he who exalts himself over a great one. For the one who gives thanks for a small thing, God sometimes admits to great things; but he who does not give thanks for great things loses even what he has. *For to him who has, it shall be given; but he who does not have, even what he has shall be taken from him.* How does he not have, if he has? He who does not know whence he has it has without having. For his thing is taken away by God, and his iniquity remains to him. Therefore *no one is continent unless God grant it.* You have a gift against pleasures: since *this very thing too,* he says, *was wisdom, to know whose gift this was: no one is continent unless God grant it.* You have a gift against pains: *For from him,* he says, *is my patience.* Therefore *hope in him, all the assembly of the people. Hope in him,* do not trust in your own strength. To him confess your evils, from him hope for your good things. Without his help you will be nothing, however proud you have been. Therefore, that you may be humble, *pour out your hearts before him.* And lest you remain badly in yourselves, say what follows: *God is our helper*[^20].

**9.** This helper, that he might conquer, the blessed martyr had, whom we admire, whose solemnity we celebrate today. Without him he would not conquer. And if he conquered pains, he would not conquer the devil. For sometimes those conquered by the devil conquer pains: not having patience, but hardness. That helper, then, was present, that he might grant him true faith, make him a good cause, and for the good cause grant patience. For there is patience when a good cause precedes. For even faith itself none but God grants. The apostle briefly commended both, that the cause for which we suffer, and the patience by which we endure evils, are from God for us. For, exhorting the martyrs, he says: *Because it has been granted you for Christ's sake*[^21]. See, a good cause, because *for Christ's sake!* For sacrilege is against Christ; for heresy and schism, against Christ! For Christ says: *He who does not gather with me scatters*[^22]. Therefore: *To you,* he says, *it has been granted for Christ's sake, not only that you believe in him, but also that you suffer for him*[^23]. This is true patience. This patience, then, let us love, this let us hold; and if we do not yet have it, let us ask for it. And rightly we sing: *My soul will be subject to God, for from him is my patience.*
