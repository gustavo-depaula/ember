# Sermon 294: Delivered in the Basilica Maiorum, on the Birthday of the Martyr Guddenis,

**June 27**

**[*On the Baptism of Infants, Against the Pelagians*]**

**1. 1.** On Saint John's birthday, among other things that seemed fitting to say, our sermon was led to the baptism of infants. Because it was already lengthy and we were thinking of ending it, we did not say as much about so great a question as concerned people ought in so great a danger. What concerns us is not the doctrine itself, long established in the Catholic Church by the highest authority, but the arguments of certain people, which are now striving to spread and overturn many minds. Therefore, with the Lord's help, we have decided to speak about this today. We celebrate a martyr's solemn day, certainly, but the cause of all believers is greater than that of martyrs alone. For not all believers are also martyrs, but martyrs are martyrs because they are believers. Therefore, let us see what they propose and what moves them, because we ought to think not so much of refuting them as of healing them.

**1. 2.** They concede that infants ought to be baptized. Therefore, the question between us and them is not whether infants should be baptized, but the reason why they should be baptized. Let us therefore hold without any dispute with them to what they concede. No one doubts that infants should be baptized. Let no one doubt it, since even those who oppose us in some respect do not doubt this.

**2. 2.** But we say they will not otherwise have salvation and eternal life unless they are baptized in Christ; they say baptism is not for salvation or eternal life but for the kingdom of heaven. Pay attention briefly while we explain as we can what this means. They say an infant, even if not baptized, by the merit of innocence—because he has no sin whatever, either personal or original, neither from himself nor drawn from Adam—must have salvation and eternal life, even without baptism. But he must be baptized to enter also God's kingdom, that is, the kingdom of heaven. If this must be examined, it must certainly be examined for their sake, not ours. For they are our brothers, disturbed by the question's depth, but they ought to have been guided by authority's rudder. When they say infants should be baptized not to receive salvation and eternal life but only for the kingdom of heaven and God's kingdom, they indeed confess baptism is necessary, but not for eternal life, only for the kingdom of heaven. What of eternal life? 'They will have it,' they say. Why will they have it? 'Because they have no sin and cannot belong to condemnation.' Is there, then, eternal life outside the kingdom of heaven?

**3. 3.** This is the first error to turn away from our ears and uproot from our minds. It is new in the Church and previously unheard of that there is eternal life apart from the kingdom of heaven, eternal salvation apart from God's kingdom. First see, brother, whether you ought perhaps to agree with us here: whoever does not belong to God's kingdom undoubtedly belongs to condemnation. When the Lord comes to judge the living and dead, as the Gospel says, he will make two divisions, right and left. To those on the left he will say: *Go into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels*; to those on the right: *Come, blessed by my Father, receive the kingdom prepared for you from the world's beginning.* Here he names the kingdom, there condemnation with the devil. No middle place is left where you can put infants. Judgment will be made concerning the living and dead: some will be on the right, others on the left. I know no other place. You who introduce a middle place, step out of the middle; do not obstruct the one seeking the right. I admonish you too: step out of the middle, but not onto the left. Therefore, if there will be right and left, and we know no middle place in the Gospel, see, on the right is the kingdom of heaven. *Receive,* he says, *the kingdom.* Whoever is not there is on the left. What will be on the left? *Go into the eternal fire.* On the right, to the kingdom, certainly eternal; on the left, into eternal fire. Whoever is not on the right is undoubtedly on the left; therefore, whoever is not in the kingdom is undoubtedly in eternal fire. Surely, you say, someone unbaptized can have eternal life? He will not be on the right, that is, will not be in the kingdom. Do you reckon everlasting fire to be eternal life? Hear more explicitly about eternal life itself, because the kingdom is nothing other than eternal life. First he named the kingdom on the right and eternal fire on the left. Then in the final sentence, to teach what the kingdom and eternal fire are, he said: *Then* *these will go into eternal burning, but the righteous into eternal life*.

**4. 3.** See, he has explained to you what the kingdom is and what eternal fire is, so that when you confess the little one will not be in the kingdom, you acknowledge he will be in eternal fire. For the kingdom of heaven is eternal life.

**4. 4.** The Apostle Paul says nothing different. When he was frightening people—not infants or unbaptized people, but the wicked, criminals, the defiled, and the lost—he did not frighten them by saying they would be in everlasting fire, where they will undoubtedly go unless corrected. He frightened them only by saying they would not be in the kingdom, so that on seeing themselves lose hope of the kingdom, they would see that nothing followed except the punishment of eternal fire. *Do not,* he says, *be mistaken: neither fornicators nor idol worshipers, neither adulterers nor effeminate men nor men who lie with males, neither thieves nor the greedy, neither drunkards nor the abusive nor robbers will possess God's kingdom.* He did not say, 'These and those, people of this or that kind, will be tormented in eternal fire,' but *they will not possess God's kingdom* *.* With the right removed, nothing remains but the left. How do they escape everlasting fire? Only because they will be in the kingdom. He continues: *And such indeed you were.* How are they no longer such? *But you were washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ and in our God's Spirit. In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ*. *For there is no other name under heaven in which we must be saved*—all of us, small and great. If we must be saved in this name, undoubtedly without this name there will be no salvation, the salvation promised to infants without Christ. With their permission I say it: whoever promises anyone salvation without Christ, I do not know whether he himself can have salvation in Christ.

**5. 5.** Next we ask them: What if someone says that infants, by the merit of their innocence, as you say, and their freedom from every offense, will have not only salvation and eternal life but also God's kingdom? How is it defined and certain among you that infants will not have God's kingdom without baptism, so that you divided things for them at your own discretion—not helpers of little ones but oppressors of the miserable—dividing things at your own discretion and giving them salvation and eternal life apart from the kingdom of heaven? Someone more benevolent and merciful than you and, as you think, more just, will give them everything, both eternal life and the kingdom of heaven. How will you defeat him? Since human reasoning sometimes delights you in opposition to the most evident authority, bring forth your rule of reasoning and assert with all your strength how to defeat the person who, because of infants' merited innocence and, as you say, absence of fault, that is, original sin, would give even the unbaptized not only eternal life but also the kingdom of heaven. Defeat him. Without prejudging the matter, I take his part for a little while and will say what I myself do not think; but I warn you to see a sharper adversary.

**5. 6.** See, someone comes forward and says: An infant with no sin whatever, neither contracted in his own life nor drawn from the first parent's life, will have both eternal life and the kingdom of heaven.

**6. 6.** Answer; defeat the person resisting you who divide things differently. For you say: This unbaptized child will indeed have eternal life, but will not have the kingdom of heaven. He replies: On the contrary, both life and the kingdom of heaven. For why do you snatch the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven from an innocent person? Someone who does not obtain the kingdom of heaven is certainly deprived of a great good. What justice is this? Tell me why. What offense has the unbaptized infant committed, having no fault, neither his own nor one drawn from a parent? Tell me what offense he committed, that he should not enter the kingdom of heaven, should be separated from the saints' lot, and exiled from the angels' fellowship. You seem merciful to yourself because you do not take life from him; yet you condemn the one you separate from the kingdom of heaven. You condemn him: you do not strike him, but send him into exile. For exiles live too, if healthy; they are not in bodily pain, not tormented or afflicted by a prison's darkness. Their only punishment is not being in their homeland. If the homeland is loved, this is a great punishment; if not loved, the heart's punishment is worse. Is it a small evil in a human heart not to seek the saints' fellowship or desire the kingdom of heaven? If he does not desire it, there is punishment from perversity; if he desires it, there is punishment from frustrated love. But suppose, as you wish, the punishment is small. Even that small punishment is great if there is no fault. Here defend God's justice. Why is even a small punishment inflicted upon an innocent person in whom no sin whatever is found? Speak against this adversary who, with greater mercy and justice than you, wants to give unbaptized infants not only eternal life but also the kingdom of heaven. Answer if you can, but bring an argument, for you delight in boasting of reason.

**7. 7.** I feel this question's depth and do not recognize my powers as adequate to search its bottom. Here too I wish to cry out with Paul: *O the depth of the riches!* An unbaptized infant goes to condemnation, for these are the Apostle's words: *From one unto condemnation*. I do not find a sufficiently worthy explanation, because I do not find it, not because it does not exist. Therefore, where I do not find the depths within the depths, I ought to consider human weakness, not condemn divine authority. I certainly cry out, and am not ashamed: *O the depth of the riches of God's wisdom and knowledge! How inscrutable his judgments and unsearchable his ways! For who has known the Lord's mind? Or who was his counselor? Or who gave to him first, to be repaid? For from him, through him, and in him are all things; to him be glory forever and ever*. I fortify my weakness with these words and, surrounded by this safeguard, stand walled against the arrows of your reasonings. But you, warrior—that is, powerful reasoner—answer the one who tells you: An utterly innocent infant, free of every sin, personal and original, will have not only eternal life but also the kingdom of heaven. This is just. Why should someone who has no evil lack any good? 'But I know,' you say. How do you know? 'Because the Lord said it.' At last you have arrived. Therefore, not because you reason it out, but because the Lord said it. I certainly praise this; it is sound. As a human being you found no explanation; you flee to authority. I approve, certainly I approve. You do well. You find no answer; flee to authority. I do not pursue you there or expel you from there; rather, I welcome and embrace you as you flee.

**7. 8.** Therefore, bring forth the authority; let us stand in it together against our common enemy. For both you and I say that an unbaptized infant does not enter the kingdom of heaven. Let us both resist that common adversary who says an unbaptized infant will enter the kingdom of heaven, and bring forth faith's shield against his most treacherous darts.

**8. 8.** Let the conjectures of human reason withdraw briefly; let divine weapons be taken up. *Take up,* the Apostle says, *God's armor*. See, let us say together to this person: Are you a Christian? 'A Christian,' he says. Hear the Gospel, you who want to send unbaptized infants into the kingdom of heaven; hear the Gospel: *Unless someone is born again from water and the Holy Spirit, he will not enter God's kingdom.* This is the Lord's sentence; only a non-Christian resists it. He has been repelled; my contest with you remains. Perhaps the very means by which you defeated him for his good will defeat you for yours. For if the one you defeated is not stubborn, you have taught him. Therefore, do not be stubborn yourself. Meanwhile, let us hold this sentence together: *Unless someone is born again from water and the Spirit, he will not enter God's kingdom.* 'Therefore,' you say, 'I cannot promise God's kingdom to an unbaptized infant against the Lord's clear sentence. This is why I say they will not have God's kingdom; this is why I say they must be baptized to have God's kingdom.' Is that why you say it? 'That is why,' he says. But consider, in view of what we said above, whether you will find eternal life apart from God's kingdom. For the statements about those two divisions, right and left, are very clear; he gave no middle place for life without the kingdom. Does this correct you too little, warn you too little? Attend briefly with me to the very reading from which you brought this sentence.

**8. 9.** For you said you were unwilling to promise the kingdom of heaven to unbaptized infants because the Lord's sentence is clear: *Unless someone is born again from water and the Spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of heaven.* Did you not notice there, when Nicodemus asked how these things could happen—that is, how a person could be reborn, born anew, since he certainly cannot enter his mother's womb again and be born anew—what he heard from the Lord, what he heard from the good teacher, what error heard from truth?

**9. 9.** For among other things, showing how it happens, he also gave a comparison. But first he said: *No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven*. He was on earth and said he was in heaven; what is greater, he said the Son of man was in heaven, to demonstrate one person in both natures. He was God's Son equal to the Father, God's Word, God with God in the beginning; and he was the Son of man, taking a human soul and human flesh, clothing himself in humanity and coming out to human beings. For in these two there are not two Christs or two Sons of God, but one person, one Christ the Son of God, and the same one Christ, not another, the Son of man: God's Son according to divinity, the Son of man according to the flesh. Which of us, paying little attention or understanding little, would not prefer to distinguish them this way: God's Son in heaven, the Son of man on earth? But lest we divide them this way and by dividing introduce two persons, he says: *No one has ascended into heaven* *except the Son of man who descended from heaven.* Therefore, the Son of man descended from heaven. Was not the Son of man made on earth? Was not the Son of man made through Mary? But he says: O human being, do not separate what I wish to unite. It is too little that the Son of man descended—for Christ descended, and the same one is Son of man who is Son of God. The one walking on earth sits in heaven. He was in heaven because Christ is everywhere, and the same Christ is both God's Son and the Son of man. Because of the unity of person God's Son is on earth; because of that same unity of person we have proved that the Son of man is in heaven from these words of the Lord: *The Son of man,* he says, *who is in heaven.* Because of the unity of person, does not Peter tell the one present and visible on earth: *You are the Christ, the living God's Son*?

**9. 10.** Therefore, let Nicodemus learn how what seemed incredible and almost impossible to his limited understanding happens: *No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended from heaven.*

**10. 10.** All who are reborn certainly ascend into heaven; absolutely none of the others do. All who are reborn ascend into heaven through God's grace, and *no one ascends into heaven except the one who descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven.* How is this? Because all who are reborn become his members; Christ alone, born of Mary, is one Christ, and the head together with his body is one Christ. Therefore, he meant to say: *No one ascends except the one who descended.* Thus no one ascends except Christ. If you want to ascend, be in Christ's body; if you want to ascend, be Christ's member. *For just as we have many members in one body, and all the body's members, though many, are one body, so also is Christ*, because head and body are Christ. Let us still inquire how this happens. The question remains hidden; that depth rises high.

**10. 11.** Christ has no sin: he neither drew original sin nor added his own. He came apart from the pleasure of fleshly lust; there was no marital embrace. From the virgin's body he took not a wound but a remedy, not something to heal but something through which to heal—I speak with regard to sin. Therefore, he alone is without sin. How will those, none of whom is without sin, be his members? How? Hear the comparison that follows: *Just as Moses lifted the serpent in the wilderness, so the Son of man must be lifted up, so that everyone believing in him may not perish but have eternal life*. Why did it seem to you that sinful human beings could not become Christ's members, members of the one who had no sin whatever? You were troubled by the serpent's bite. Therefore, Christ is crucified; therefore, Christ pours out his blood for the forgiveness of sins, because of sin, that is, the serpent's poison. *Just as Moses lifted the serpent in the wilderness,* so that those bitten by serpents in that desert might be healed—they were commanded to look at the lifted serpent, and whoever looked was healed—*so the Son of man must be lifted up, so that everyone believing in him,* that is, looking at the lifted one, not ashamed of the crucified one, glorying in Christ's Cross, *may not perish but have eternal life. May not perish:* how? By believing in him. How may he not perish? By looking at the lifted one; otherwise, he would have perished. For this is the meaning: *Everyone believing in him may not perish but have eternal life.*

**11. 12.** You bring me an infant and tell him to look at the lifted one, yet deny that he has the serpent's poison. On the contrary, if you favor him, if the innocence of his own life moves you, do not deny that some guilt has been drawn from the first life, not his own but his first parent's. Do not deny it; confess the poison so as to ask for the remedy. He is not healed otherwise. Or why tell him to believe? For this is the response made by the person carrying the infant. He is healed through another's words because he is wounded through another's deed. The question is asked: Does he believe in Jesus Christ? The answer: He believes. An answer is given for the one who cannot speak, who is silent, who cries and by crying somehow prays to be helped, and it has force. Is that serpent perhaps trying to persuade us that it has no force? Far be it from the hearts of Christians, such as they are. Therefore, the answer is given and has force. Through a kind of shared breathing the spirit participates; he believes in another because he sinned in another. Did the one whom weakness bore find this age's life, while the one whom love bore fails to find the coming age's life?

**11. 13.** Therefore, just as Moses lifted the serpent in the wilderness so that everyone struck by a serpent might gaze at the lifted one and be healed, so the Son of man had to be lifted up so that everyone poisoned by the serpent might gaze at the lifted one and be healed. Adam first received the serpent's bite with its poison. Therefore, the one born in sinful flesh is saved in Christ through the likeness of sinful flesh.

**12. 13.** For *God sent* *his Son,* not in sinful flesh, but, as the writer continues, *in the likeness of sinful flesh*, because he came not from a marital embrace but from a virgin's womb. *He sent him in the likeness of sinful flesh:* why? *So that from sin he might condemn sin in the flesh*: sin through sin, the serpent through the serpent. For who doubts that sin is called by the serpent's name? Therefore, sin through sin, the serpent through the serpent, but through a likeness, because there was no sin in Christ, only the likeness of sinful flesh. Therefore, the serpent was lifted up, but a bronze one; the likeness of sinful flesh was lifted up to heal the origin of sin. For *God sent his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh.* Not in the likeness of flesh, for it is true flesh, but *in the likeness of sinful flesh,* because it is mortal flesh without any sin whatever. *So that from sin,* because of the likeness, *he might condemn sin in the flesh,* because of true iniquity. True iniquity was not in Christ, but mortality was. He did not take on sin, but took on sin's punishment. By taking on punishment without fault, he healed both punishment and fault. See how these things happen, about which Nicodemus had said in wonder: *How can these things happen?* For we are healed in this way, not because we merit it. See how these things happen. Now where do you put infants? You still say, 'They are wounded by no poison.' Remove them from the sight of the lifted serpent. But if you do not remove them, you say they need healing and confess they are poisoned.

**13. 14.** Moreover, did you not hear what the Lord himself said in that same discourse to Nicodemus when that reading was read today? *The one believing in him is not judged; but the one who has not believed has already been judged*. Here too you seek a middle place, man of the middle, and dispute, and are listened to without yourself listening: *The one believing in him is not judged; but the one who has not believed has already been judged.* What does *has already been judged* mean? Has been condemned. For you know judgment is often put for condemnation; the Scriptures bear witness, especially in that one most evident testimony that no one can contradict. Speaking of the resurrection, the Lord said: *Those who have done good* *to the resurrection of life; those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment*. Certainly he put judgment for condemnation. And do you dare expound or believe otherwise? *The one who does not believe has already been judged.* Elsewhere: *The one believing in the Son has eternal life,* which you were promising to unbaptized infants. *The one believing in the Son has eternal life.* 'But,' he says, 'an infant who does not believe has it too, although he does not have God's kingdom.' But see what follows: *The one disbelieving the Son does not have life, but God's wrath remains upon him*. Where do you put baptized infants? Certainly among believers. For according to the Church's ancient, canonical, most firmly established custom, baptized infants are called faithful. We ask about them thus: Is this infant a Christian? The answer: A Christian. A catechumen or a member of the faithful? Faithful—certainly from faith, and faith from believing. Therefore, you will count baptized infants among believers and will not dare judge otherwise in any way unless you want to be an open heretic. Thus they have eternal life because *the one believing in the Son has eternal life.*

**14. 14.** Do not promise them eternal life without this faith and this sacrament of faith. *The one disbelieving the Son,* the one who does not believe in the Son, *does not have life, but God's wrath remains upon him.* He did not say, 'It will come upon him,' but *it remains upon him.* He looked to origin when he said: *God's wrath remains upon him.* Looking to this too, the Apostle said: *We too were once by nature children of wrath*. We do not accuse nature. God is nature's author. Nature was established good by God, but through evil will it was corrupted by the serpent. Therefore, what in Adam belonged to fault, not nature, has now become nature for us born from him. From this defect of nature with which a person is born, only the one born without defect delivers. From this sinful flesh only the one born without sin, through the likeness of sinful flesh, delivers. From this serpent's poison only the lifting of the serpent delivers. What do you say to these things? Is this enough?

**14. 15.** Pay brief attention to one very subtle point they bring forward. When they begin to be pressed by the Apostle's words, *Through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin; and so it passed to all people, in whom all sinned*—words I do not know who could fail to understand, words for which I do not know whether anyone needs an interpreter—they try to answer that the Apostle said this because Adam sinned first, and those sinning afterward sinned by imitating him. What is this but an attempt to pour darkness over clear light? *Sin entered through one man, and death through sin; and so it passed to all people, in whom all sinned.* You say this is because of imitation, because Adam sinned first.

**15. 15.** I answer plainly: Adam did not sin first. If you seek the first sinner, look at the devil. But the Apostle, wishing to show the mass of the human race poisoned from its origin, therefore named the one from whom we were born, not the one we imitated. Certainly the one you imitate is also called your father: *My children,* he says, *whom I bear again*. The same man says: *Be imitators of me*. Because of this very imitation, the ungodly are told: *You are from your father the devil*. For it is established in the Catholic faith that the devil neither begot our nature nor created it. In him there is only the seduction of one going before, and in the follower imitation. Finally, as it was said of Adam, *In whom all sinned,* let someone read to me anywhere, 'All sinned in the devil.' Sinning with him preceding and deceiving is one thing; sinning in him another. Because according to fleshly propagation we were all in Adam before we were born, we were there as in a parent, as in a root. Thus this tree in which we were was poisoned. For because origin does not belong to the devil, the prince of sin and truly the first sinner, but imitation does, Scripture said of him: *Through the devil's envy* *death entered the world, and those belonging to his side imitate him*. By imitating him they become his partisans. Was it said, 'They sinned in him'? But when speaking of Adam, because of origin, posterity, and propagation from his body, he said: *In whom all* *sinned.* For if Adam was established as first because he sinned first, as though an example rather than an origin, why is Christ sought against Adam at so great a distance, after so long a time? If all sinners belong to Adam because he was the first sinner, all righteous people ought to belong to Abel because he was the first righteous person. Why is Christ sought? Wake up, brother. Why is Christ sought, except because birth was condemned in Adam, and rebirth is sought in Christ?

**16. 16.** Therefore, let no one deceive us. Scripture is evident, authority most firmly established, the faith most catholic. Everyone born is condemned; no one is delivered unless reborn. Therefore, now instructed, beloved, answer another of their tricks when they say, troubling the little ones: If sinners are born from a sinner, why are righteous people not born from a baptized believer whose sins have all been forgiven? Answer quickly: A righteous person is not born from someone baptized because he begets not from what made him reborn, but from what made him born. Of Christ it was said: *Put to death in flesh, made alive in spirit*. So of a human being it can be said: Wasted in flesh, justified in spirit. *What is born from flesh is flesh.* You ask for a righteous person to be born from a righteous person, although you see that no one can be righteous unless reborn. You do not attend to the Lord's sentence, which you yourself have on your lips: *Unless someone is born again from water and the Spirit*. I suppose this did not happen in intercourse. You wonder why a sinner is born from a righteous person's seed; does it not please you to wonder why a wild olive is born from an olive's seed? Take another comparison. Set the baptized righteous person as a cleaned grain. Do you not notice that from a cleaned grain wheat is born with chaff, without which it was sown? Moreover, since in the propagation of those born there is fleshly birth, and in the propagation of the reborn spiritual birth, you want someone baptized to be born from someone baptized, although you see that a circumcised person is not born from someone circumcised. Certainly that birth is fleshly and circumcision fleshly, yet no circumcised person is born from someone circumcised. So too someone baptized cannot be born from someone baptized, because no one is reborn before being born.

**17. 17.** Another supposedly very sharp argument of theirs—but what sharp thing is not blunted by truth's shield?—is this; see what they say. 'If Adam harms those who have not sinned, then Christ ought also to benefit those who have not believed.' You certainly see how sharp this is against truth; hear how much it helps truth. For the person saying this says only that Christ does not benefit unbelievers. This is true. Who would not accept it? Who would not agree that Christ does not benefit unbelievers but benefits believers? But tell me, I beg you: does Christ benefit baptized infants at all, or not? He must say he benefits them; he is pressed by Mother Church's weight. Perhaps they would like to deny it, for their reasonings seem to force them there. But they are restrained by the Church's authority, lest, I will not say, they be buried under people's spittle, but swept away as by a river by the infants' own tears. For if they say Christ does not benefit baptized infants, they say nothing other than, 'Infants are baptized unnecessarily.' But so that they are not baptized unnecessarily, because they do not dare say that, they confess Christ benefits baptized infants.

**18. 17.** If he benefits the baptized, I ask whom he benefits: believers or unbelievers? Let them choose what they wish. If they say unbelievers, where is your accusation that Christ cannot benefit unbelievers? See, you confess he benefits infants who nevertheless do not believe. Whatever the benefit—although you do not think it is eternal life or eternal salvation—Christ certainly benefits baptized infants toward receiving the kingdom of heaven itself. Does he therefore benefit unbelievers? But far be it from me to call infants unbelievers. I have already argued above: the one who sinned in another believes in another. It is said, 'He believes,' and it has force, and he is counted among baptized believers. Mother Church's authority holds this; the established rule of truth maintains this. Whoever rams against this strength, this impregnable wall, breaks himself. Therefore, Christ benefits baptized infants in some way, and, as I say and the whole Church says with me, he benefits believers, the faithful. Choose what you wish. Certainly I want you to choose what is truer and say with us that he benefits believers. But if you say, 'He benefits unbelievers,' you have spoken against yourself. If you say, 'He benefits believers,' you have spoken with me. Choose whether to say something false against yourself or something true with me. For a little earlier you said Christ does not benefit unbelievers, wanting to establish that Adam likewise did not harm those who had not sinned, just as Christ does not benefit those who have not believed. See, now you acknowledge that Christ benefits baptized infants who do not believe. But if you say he benefits believers, you speak well, with me: infants too believe.

**19. 17.** By what do they believe? How do they believe? By their parents' faith. If they are purified by their parents' faith, they were defiled by their parents' sin. The body of death in their first parents begot them as sinners; the spirit of life in their later parents regenerated them as believers. You assign faith to one who does not answer, and I assign sin to one who does nothing.

**19. 18.** 'Holy people,' he says, 'ought to be born from holy people, because the Apostle said: *Otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*.' How do you take this? How do you understand someone born from believers as so holy that he ought not to be baptized? Take this holiness however you wish. For there are many kinds of holiness and many kinds of sanctification. Not everything sanctified is sent to the kingdom of heaven. Of our food the Apostle said: *It is sanctified through God's word and prayer*. Because our food is sanctified, do we not know where it is sent? Therefore, learn that there is some kind, some faint shadow, of sanctification that does not suffice for receiving salvation. It differs, and what the difference is God knows. Nevertheless, let people run to baptism with a believer's child. Parents must not err by thinking him already born a believer. They can call him born, not reborn. To know how you should understand believers' children as sanctified—without now investigating the manner of this sanctification, which would take too long—where you have an unbelieving husband, you also have a believing wife. *The unbelieving husband,* he says, *is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving woman is sanctified in the brother*. Because perhaps some kind of sanctification exists there, by which an unbelieving husband is sanctified in a believing wife, should he therefore be assured already that he will enter the kingdom of heaven without being baptized, reborn, or redeemed by Christ's blood? Therefore, just as the unbelieving husband is sanctified in his wife and nevertheless perishes unless baptized, so believers' children, though sanctified in some manner, nevertheless perish unless baptized.

**20. 19.** I ask you to remain attentive a little. I am only reading. It is Saint Cyprian whom I have taken in hand, an ancient bishop of this see. Hear briefly what he thought about infants' baptism, or rather what he showed the Church always thought. For it is not enough that these people discuss and argue for some ungodly novelties; they also try to accuse us of saying something new. Therefore, I read Saint Cyprian so that you may see the canonical understanding and catholic meaning in the words I discussed a little earlier. He was asked whether an infant should be baptized before the eighth day, because under the Old Law an infant could not be circumcised except on the eighth day. The question arose about the day of baptism; there was no question about sin's origin. Therefore, the question that arose was solved from the matter about which there was no question. Saint Cyprian said, among other things that I mentioned above: *Therefore, we think no one should be prevented from receiving grace by the law already established, nor should spiritual circumcision be hindered by fleshly circumcision, but absolutely everyone should be admitted to Christ's grace, since Peter too speaks in the Acts of the Apostles and says*: *God told me that no human being should be called common and unclean*. *Moreover, if anything could prevent people from obtaining grace, it would rather be the more serious sins of adults, the advanced in years, and older people. But if even the most serious offenders, who previously sinned greatly against the Lord, are given forgiveness of sins when they afterward believe, and no one is barred from baptism and grace, how much less should an infant be barred, who, newly born, has sinned in nothing except that, born according to Adam in the flesh, he contracted the contagion of ancient death in his first birth? He comes all the more easily to receive forgiveness of sins precisely because the sins forgiven him are not his own but another's.* See how, doubting nothing about this matter, he solved the matter in doubt. He took this from the Church's foundation to strengthen a wavering stone.

**21. 20.** Therefore, if we can, let us obtain from our brothers that they not call us heretics besides. Perhaps, if we wished, we could call those arguing such things heretics, yet we do not. Let their mother bear them with devout affection to be healed, carry them to be taught, lest she mourn them dead. They go too far; it is much, scarcely bearable, and still borne with great patience. Let them not abuse the Church's patience; let them be corrected: it is good. We exhort as friends, not quarrel as enemies. They slander us; we endure it. Let them not slander the rule or the truth; let them not oppose holy Church, laboring daily for the forgiveness of infants' original sin. This matter is established. A disputant erring in other questions not carefully worked out or yet confirmed by the Church's full authority must be borne; there error must be borne. It should not go so far as to strive to shake even the Church's foundation itself. This is not beneficial. Perhaps our patience is not yet blameworthy, but we ought to fear that our negligence may also be blamed. Let this suffice for Your Charity. Those of you who know them, deal with them as friends and brothers, peacefully, lovingly, sorrowfully. Let piety do whatever it can, because afterward impiety must not be loved.
