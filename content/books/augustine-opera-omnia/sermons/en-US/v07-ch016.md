# Sermon 243: In the Days of Easter, on the Resurrection of the Lord According to John

**1. 1.** The narrative of the resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ according to the Evangelist John began to be read today. For this you know, and I had commended it to you, that during these days the resurrection of the Lord is recited according to all four Evangelists. In these things, then, that we have heard, this alone is wont to disturb: why the Lord Jesus said to the woman seeking his body, and now recognizing him as alive: *Do not touch me; for I have not yet ascended to my Father*[^1]. But I told you, and you ought to remember, that not all say everything; but things are said by some that are left out by others. Yet not so that they are to be believed to be at variance with one another, if contentiousness is absent, and the devotion of one who understands is present. For, as it is read in the Evangelist Matthew, after he rose, he met two women, among whom this one too was; and he said to them: *Hail. But they approached, and held his feet, and adored him*[^2]: and surely he had not yet ascended to the Father. How, then, is it now said to this woman: *Do not touch me; for I have not yet ascended to my Father?* For these words seem to sound as though Mary could then touch him, when he had ascended into heaven. If she does not touch him set on earth, who of mortals can touch him sitting in heaven?

**2. 2.** But that touch signifies faith. He touches Christ who believes in Christ. For that woman too who was suffering from a flow of blood said within herself: *If I touch the hem of his garment, I will be saved*. By faith she touched, and the health which she presumed followed. Finally, that we might know what it is truly to touch, the Lord at once said to his disciples: *Who touched me?* And the disciples said: *The crowds press you, and you say, Who touched me?* And he: *Someone touched me*[^3]. As if to say: The crowd presses, faith touches. This Mary, then, to whom the Lord said: *Do not touch me; for I have not yet ascended to my Father*, seems to bear the person of the Church, which then believed in Christ, when he had ascended to the Father. See, I ask you, when you believed; I ask the Church spread through the whole world, whose person was in one woman: and with one voice she answers me, I believed then, when Jesus had ascended to the Father. What does it mean, I believed then; except, I touched then? Many carnal men thought Christ only a man, they did not understand the divinity hidden in him. They did not touch well, because they did not believe well. Do you wish to touch well? Understand Christ where he is coeternal with the Father, and you have touched. But if you think him a man, and think nothing more, he has not yet ascended to the Father for you.

**3. 3.** The Lord Jesus, then, presented the appearance of his body to human senses, to confirm the resurrection of the flesh. He wished to teach us nothing else, by showing himself alive in the body after his resurrection, than that the resurrection of the dead should be believed by us. Since, then, everything is to be restored whole, a difficult question about the use of the members is wont to be asked by those who desire to know, and again to be raised by those who desire to quarrel. For they say that our body has all its members, and that it is evident which members are necessary for which works. For who does not know, who does not see, that we have eyes for seeing, ears for hearing, a tongue for speaking, nostrils for smelling, teeth for chewing, hands for working, feet for walking; and also those members that are called the private parts, for generating? Moreover, the inner organs too, which, lest they should be horrible to the sight, God willed to be covered; our inner parts, and what are called the intestines, for what uses they serve, both many men, and physicians better, have come to know. They argue, then, and say to us: If we will have ears to hear, eyes to see, a tongue to speak; why will we have teeth, if we will not eat, the throat, lungs, stomach, intestines through which foods pass and are changed to suit the balance of our health; finally, those very members that are called the private parts, why, they say, will we have them, where there will be no generation, no digestion?

**4. 4.** What shall we answer them? Are we going to say that we will rise without intestines, after the likeness of statues? For about the teeth it is easily answered. For the teeth help us not only to chew, but also to speak; striking our tongue, as the plectrum the strings, so that it may sound out syllables. The rest of our members, then, will be for appearance, not for use; for the commendation of beauty, not for the need of necessity. Will they, because they will be idle, therefore be unseemly? And indeed now, because we are unskilled, and ignorant of the causes of things, if our inner parts are seen, they are shuddered at rather than loved. For who knows how the members are connected to one another, and by what proportions they are fitted together? Hence it is even called a harmony; which word is taken from music: where surely we see in the lyre the strings stretched. If all the strings sound alike, there is no melody. A different stretching produces different sounds; but different sounds joined by reason produce, not for those who see, a beauty, but for those who hear, a sweetness. Whoever has learned this reason in the human members marvels so much, delights so much, that this reason is preferred by those who understand to all visible beauty. Now we do not know it; but then we will know it: not because they will be laid bare, but because, even covered, they will not be able to lie hidden.

**5. 5.** Someone will answer me, and say: How, if they are covered, will they not be able to lie hidden? Will our hearts not lie hidden, and our inner parts lie hidden? Our thoughts, my brothers, the thoughts that now no one sees except God, all will see one another's in that society of the saints. No one there wishes what he thinks to be covered, because no one there thinks evil. Hence the Apostle says: *Do not judge anything before the time*: that is, Do not rashly judge, when you do not see with what heart anyone acts. If something is done that can also be done with a good heart, do not blame it: do not arrogate to yourself more than humanity requires. To see the heart belongs to God: but it does not belong to men, except to judge of the things that are manifest. *Do not, then*, he says, *judge anything before the time.* What does it mean, *before the time?* It follows, and he says: *Until the Lord comes, and illumines the hidden things of darkness.* What darkness he meant, he plainly shows by the following words: *He will illumine*, he says, *the hidden things of darkness.* What is this? Hear what follows: *And he will make manifest the thoughts of the heart*[^4]. This is to illumine the hidden things of darkness, to make manifest the thoughts of the heart. Now, then, our thoughts are in the light to ourselves, to each one individually, because we know them: but to our neighbors they are in darkness, because they do not see them. There, what you know you are thinking, another too will know. What do you fear? Now you want to hide, now you fear to have your thoughts made public: for perhaps you sometimes think something evil, perhaps something base, perhaps something vain. Nothing but good, nothing but honorable, nothing but true, nothing but pure, nothing but sincere, when you are there, will you think. As you now want your face to be seen, so then you will want your conscience to be seen.

**6. 5.** For the very recognition too, beloved, the very recognition—will it not be the recognition of us all? Do you think that, because you know me, you are going to recognize me, but you are not going to recognize my father, whom you do not know, or some bishop or other who was in this church many years ago? You will all know one another. Those who will be there will not recognize one another because they will see faces: there will be a recognition of one another by a greater knowledge. So all will see, and far more excellently, in the way the prophets are accustomed to see here. They will see divinely, when they will be full of God. There will be nothing to give offense, nor anything to lie hidden from the one who knows.

**6. 6.** There will be there, then, whole members, even those that here are private parts, but there they will not be private parts. There will be no anxious concern for the honor of modesty, where there will be no disgrace of lust. See, even here, where, in a way, necessity is the mother of all our works—which necessity then will not be—yet we find some things that God placed in bodies for no uses, but for adornment alone.

**7. 6.** A while ago I was running through the members: and now let us go over them a little more carefully. We have eyes for seeing, ears for hearing, nostrils for smelling, the mouth and tongue for speaking, teeth for chewing, the throat for swallowing, the stomach for receiving and digesting, the intestines for passing the food down to the lower parts, and those that are called the private parts, either for excretion or for generation; hands for working, feet for walking. What is the use of a beard, except that it is beauty alone? Why did God create a beard on a man? I see its appearance, I do not seek its use. It is clear why women have breasts, surely that they may suckle little ones: why do men have nipples? Ask the use, there is none: ask the appearance, a chest with nipples becomes men too. Take the nipples away from a man's chest, and see how much beauty you have removed, how much ugliness you have introduced.

**8. 7.** So then, beloved, so believe, so hold, that there will be no use for many of the members there, but no adornment of any will be lacking. Nothing will be unseemly there, there will be the highest peace, nothing discordant, nothing monstrous, nothing that offends the sight; in all things God will be praised. For if now, in this weakness of the flesh and the delicate working of the members, so great a beauty of the body appears, which entices the lustful, and rouses to inquiry both the studious and the curious; and if there is found in the body a reasoning of numbers, and no other craftsman of these is found than the craftsman of the heavens, but the same one is the creator of the lowest things and of the highest: how much more there, where there will be no lust, no corruption, no deformed crookedness, no troublesome necessity, but unending eternity, beautiful truth, the highest happiness?

**9. 8.** But you say to me: What am I going to do? there will be no use of the members there, what am I going to do? Does no activity seem to you to be there—to see, to love, to praise? See, these holy days, which are celebrated after the Lord's resurrection, signify the life to come after our resurrection. For as the days of Lent before Easter signified the laborious life in this mortal trouble: so these joyful days signify the life to come, where we will be going to reign with the Lord. The life that is signified by Lent before Easter is now had: the life that is signified by the fifty days after the Lord's resurrection is not had, but hoped for, and by hoping loved; and in that very love God who promised these things is praised, and those very praises are the Alleluia. For what is Alleluia? It is a Hebrew word, Alleluia, Praise God. Allelu, Praise; ia, God: so when we sound Alleluia we say Praise God, and we rouse one another to praise God: with hearts in concord, better than with the strings of a lyre, we say praises to God, we sing Alleluia. And when we have sung, because of weakness we withdraw, to refresh our bodies. Why do we refresh them, except because we grow faint? Then so great is the weakness of the flesh, so great the trouble of this life, that any great thing whatever comes into distaste. How we desired these days to come round each year, when they were just departing; and with what eagerness we came to them through the interval of time? If it were said to us, Say Alleluia without ceasing, we would excuse ourselves. Why would we excuse ourselves? Because, being weary, we could not, because even at the good itself we would grow tired with distaste. There there is no failing, no distaste. Stand, praise, *you who stand in the house of the Lord, in the courts of the house of our God*[^5]. What do you seek, what you will do there? *Blessed*, he says, *are those who dwell in your house, O Lord; forever and ever they will praise you*[^6].
