# Sermon 348/A: , Augmented.

**1.** The cause of the coming and incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ is that, when he came, he found all to be sinners. The Apostle says most plainly that this was the cause of his coming, in this way: *It is a human saying*, he says, *and worthy of all acceptance, that Jesus Christ came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first*[^1]. No cause, then, brought the Son of God, God and eternal God, coeternal with and equal to the Father, down from heaven to earth to take flesh and to die for us, except that there was no life in us. The physician would not come down except to the sick; life would not come down except to the dead. Today, when the Apostle was being read, you who were attentive heard: *God commends his love in us*, he says, *since, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us; much more now, justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him*[^2]. This is *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^3], which first the prophets, then he himself by his own mouth, then the apostles after the presence of his flesh, then the whole Church holds, confesses, proclaims and commends and worships. This is *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*.

**2.** Whence your love ought first to know, or rather to recall what you know and what you have always heard, that no man can be saved by his own merits and strength. For man was ready to wound himself, just as concerning the very life of our flesh it is easy for anyone to kill himself: but is he able to raise himself up again? In order to fall, then, we needed no help; rather, because we let go the help of God, therefore we fell. But in order to rise from our ruin, let us ask his help, lest we remain in our sins. *Christ died for us*—you have heard the Apostle—not for himself, but *for us*. Why not for himself, but *for us*? Because he had no cause of death, he in whom there was no sin[^4]. Death is the punishment of sin[^5]. For Adam too, unless he had sinned, would surely not have died, nor would we be born mortal from his stock. But that one came, without sin, who would loose all sins: for one liable could not loose the bound, nor one guilty free the condemned. He took flesh from a virgin without the lust of a man; the flesh he assumed was not a wound, but the medicine of the wound. *Christ died for us*.

**3.** What else shall we seek from him? What is Christ? You have heard: when he questioned his disciples, it was said by Peter: *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^6]. He is Son by nature, we by grace; he is the only one, we are many, because he is begotten, we are adopted. Since, then, God had one and only Son, his own only Son, as the Apostle says, *he did not spare, but handed him over for us all*[^7]. What greater medicine could the human race ask or hope for, than that the only Son should be sent, not to live with us, but to die? But that he might die for us, he received flesh in which he might die, because, being the Word, God with God the Father, he had nothing whereby he could die. *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^8]. Where is visibility and tangibility in the Word, where is suffering or death in God? Only by the mind can be seen what cannot be seen. But even the mind itself was covered with darkness, blinded by sins—the whole man weak, wholly languishing, wholly wounded, I dare say wholly dead and extinguished, from where could he see the One present everywhere, not having the inner eye healthy, from where the invisible things are seen?

**4.** Nothing in us, then, was healthy: the curer of body and soul came down, because he is the Savior of body and soul. For if those physicians can cure what they did not create, by these medicines and herbs which they did not create—if, then, a man physician cures by means of God's creation, how much more does God cure by means of his own? But the physician cures one who will die; God cures one who will live forever. And this very thing, that he willed to die for us, was our medicine. Great is that mercy of our physician, brothers, that he willed to cure us not from his medicine-chest, but from his blood. *Much more*, he says, *now justified*. By what? *In his blood*, not in our strength, not in our merits, but *in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him*, not through ourselves, but *through him*. He has bound us to the cross: indeed, if we wish to live, let us not cling to death. He who clung to himself, clung to death. For there is no life in the dead. What does the dead man presume of himself? He was able to die of his own; he cannot come back to life of his own. To sin through ourselves we both could and can, yet through ourselves we shall never at any time be able to rise. Let our hope be in none but God. Let us groan toward him, let us presume in him; as for what pertains to us, let us strive by the will, that we may merit by prayer.

**5.** These things, then, being so, brothers, I will tell you more openly, because there is nothing for us to conceal: a certain new heresy, lurking and creeping secretly and widely, as much as we could, until it should break out, we tolerated in silence; we always refuted the error itself; we kept silent about names, lest perhaps, while we were refuting the error, men might be corrected; we kept silent about names: for nothing is better, nothing more desirable than that they, hearing what was preached by us according to the most ancient foundation of the Church, should fear to preach their errors and be healed in silence, turned to him who heals all who call upon his name. This we did for a long time. For concerning impiety of this kind we even wrote some things, and they were already in the hands of readers, and yet the names of those concerning whom we had written had not yet been brought to your notice. Some of them were here, and some have been corrected, over whose salvation, in the name and mercy of the Lord, we rejoice. For of those who have been corrected from that error, very many asked us to compose writings concerning that very error.

**6.** But now we have heard: that this very man, who is the chief and author of this pernicious teaching, was said to have been absolved in episcopal proceedings in the eastern regions and pronounced catholic. Therefore the things charged against him he denied to be his, and what others seemed to spread by his teaching he denied that he held, but even anathematized it. The proceedings indeed have not yet reached us. Nevertheless, because we are accustomed to write to him familiarly as to a servant of God, as he to us, last year, when my son the presbyter Orosius, who is with us, a servant of God from Spain, had gone to the East with my letters, I wrote through him to that same Pelagius, not branding him in my letters, but exhorting him to hear from the presbyter what I charged. But the presbyter found that very place where he was already most disturbed by his preachings and by the dissensions of the brothers; from there he brought back to me a letter of the holy presbyter Jerome, much to be venerated by us according to the merit of his age and holiness and learning, known to all. But this presbyter Jerome had already written even against him on free choice, which was brought to us also. But that man, as I said, was absolved in ecclesiastical proceedings, confessing the grace of God which he seemed to deny and assail by his disputations.

**7.** But afterward, a few days ago, there came to us from there our fellow citizen, the palatine deacon, son of Gattus, of Hippo—many recognize him, and more know even his name; his father is present; he is here among the deacons, he stands, he hears me, it is he. He brought me a certain short book of Pelagius himself against the things charged against him, not as a part of the proceedings, but a defense made and composed by him, just as he perhaps defended himself even in the episcopal proceedings, which, as I said, could not yet reach our hands. And he charged the deacon to give me this same defense of his to read; yet he did not send me his letter. Whence I am anxious lest afterward he deny even that he sent what he sent. I wished, then, to dispute nothing about it, until we should read the proceedings, in which the ecclesiastical and episcopal authority seems to be contained. But why did I wish to convey this to your faith? Because I know not what great disturbance has been made at Jerusalem and has been announced to us full of sadness, so that even by a popular tumult two monasteries in Bethlehem are said to have been burned. Which it would not have been needful to tell you, were it not that I learned it had already reached some of you. It is better, then, that you heard the whole from me, than that you should be wounded by hidden rumors.

**8.** What evil, then, this heresy has, receive briefly, that you may beware, and whomever you hear either secretly whispering such things or noisily clamoring them in open disputations, do not conceal from us. For we fear lest the cancer creep when it is spared, and suddenly we find as many such men as we can scarcely or never heal. What evil, then, this heresy has, hear. What a little while ago I was saying and commending to you, *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^9], this grace that heresy assails with its pestiferous disputations. How? you say. They say that human nature can do so much, that the free choice of the will can do so much, that, just as through ourselves we were made sinners, so through ourselves too we can be justified. And since a just man is better than a man—for the name "man" is of nature; the name "justice," of happiness and blessedness—since, then, a just man is better than any man whatever, they say that God made the man, but the man made himself just, so that it may seem that man gives himself more than God had given him.

**9.** Let your love, then, attend: through the malign disputations of these men our prayers too are assailed. For they so act, so dispute, that we seem to pray without cause. For the Lord taught us how to pray, lest perhaps in our prayers we ask for carnal and temporal things, as is to ask that your head not ache, that you not die, that you not carry out a son for burial, that you not suffer loss, that you not be oppressed by someone and cast into prison, and whatever similar things here are temporal and worldly. These they grant us to pray for; but that which the Lord taught they take away, not because they dare to deny it, but because they dispute such things by which it is taken away. For when one says to you: "You suffice yourself for doing justice; if you wish, you do it; if you wish, you do not do it; you have need of no help of God for fulfilling what he commanded, because the grace of God is not this which helps you not to sin, but that grace which made you with a free will." When, then, they say these things, they call it the grace of God by which we were made, which grace we have in common even with pagans. For it is not the case that we were created and they were not created, or that we came forth from the workshop of another craftsman than they: we and they have one God as author, one founder, one creator, *who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^10]. This they call the grace of God; another they will not call, not that by which we are men even with the pagans, but that by which we are Christians. You know what grace they deny; hear it more evidently.

**10.** You know that the apostle Paul set before our eyes our struggle with the flesh, so that we may live piously and justly, the very contest in which we labor, saying: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man. But I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin—and of death—which is in my members*[^11]. In this difficulty he cried out and said: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?* and as though an answer was given: *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^12]. This grace they do not deny, but they hear that you wrestle with the flesh and with the evil habit of your sins: "You suffice yourself to conquer. Why do you ask for help? By your own virtue you can fulfill this." But the Apostle gave way. He confessed his weakness, that he might obtain healing: *I see*, he says, *another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin*. What does it profit me that as the inner man I delight in mind in the law of God? Behold, I am fought against, behold, I am dragged, behold, I am oppressed, behold, I am taken captive. See whether, as if from great oppression looking up to God, he did not cry out. If he said: *Who will free me from the body of this death*, except my own virtue?, he would seem to have spoken proudly; but perhaps we would understand even this, that he could not have said it except of God, to whom the psalm says: *I will love you, O Lord, my strength*[^13]. Did he then say: *Who will free me*, except my nature, except my will, except the strength of my choice and power? He did not say it. He humbled himself, that he might be exalted[^14]: *The grace of God*, he said, *through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^15].

**11.** On account of this grace the Lord commended to us what we should pray: *Hallowed be*—and what?—*your name*[^16]. Is not the name of God holy? What is *hallowed be*, except in us? Now you, if of free will and of the proper strength of your nature you can hallow the name of God in yourself, why do you pray, why do you ask of the supreme majesty what you have in your power? Why say more? {(1.) Those two petitions: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^17], and: *Lead us not into temptation*[^18]—when they are put to them, what do you think they answer? I shuddered, my brothers, when I heard it. I indeed did not hear it with my own ears, but my holy brother and fellow bishop, our Urbanus, who was a presbyter here and is now bishop of Sicca, when he had returned from the city of Rome and had there contended with a certain man holding such views, or related that he had contended, told me that this man said this, when he was being pressed by the weight of the Lord's Prayer. For he was pressing him and saying: "If it is in our power not to sin, and it is in our power to overcome all the temptations of sins by our wills alone, why do we say to God, lead us not into temptation?" What do you think he answered? "We ask," he said, "God not to lead us into temptation, lest we suffer some evil which we do not have in our power: lest I fall from my horse and break my foot, lest a robber kill me, and anything of this kind. For these things," he said, "I do not have in my power. But to conquer the temptations of my sins, if I wish, I can, even without the help of God."

**12.** (2.) You see, brothers, what a malign heresy! You see how you all shudder: beware lest you be captured! For I know the cleverness and the evasions of impious men turned away from the truth, and, because they have already fallen into their own opinions, unwilling to be conquered. See, I beseech you. For behold, he found something to say, that we say *Lead us not into temptation* for this reason, lest something befall us which we do not have in our power, according to the temptation of the body. Was it for this, then, that the Lord said: *Watch and pray, lest you enter into temptation*[^19]? Did he say this: *Watch and pray*, lest you break your foot, or lest your head ache, or lest you run into some loss? He was not saying this, but what was he saying? What he said to Peter: *I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail*[^20]. *I have prayed*, he says, for you. God says to a man, the Lord to a servant, the master to a disciple, the physician to the sick: *I have prayed for you*. What? *That it may not fail.* What? Your hand? your foot? your eye? your tongue, by some paralysis, that is, by a dissolution of the members? No, but *that your faith may not fail*. According to these men, we have it in our power that our faith may not fail.

**13.** (3.) Why is God asked for us? That he may grant us what these men say we ought not to ask of the eternal majesty, but to have in our power. Our blessings, my brothers, our blessings which we make over you, they empty out, they drain, they shatter. You have heard me, I believe, my brothers, when I say: "Turned to the Lord, let us bless his name. May he grant us to persevere in his commandments, to walk in the right way of his instruction, to please him in every good work, and other such things." "Altogether," they say, "this whole thing is established in our power." Then we wish such things for you in vain. Let us defend ourselves and you, lest both we bless without cause, and you subscribe "Amen" without cause. My brothers, your "Amen" is your subscription, "Amen" is your stipulation, your consent. Lest perhaps some of them condemn both us and you, let us defend ourselves from the apostle Paul: let us see whether he wished such things for his people as we pray over you. Hear what he said in a certain place. I say a brief thing. What do you say, O new heretic, whoever you are who hear me, if you are present? what do you say? "That we have it so in our power not to sin, that we can fulfill this without the help of divine grace." Do you say this? "This," he says. Then we have it in our power not to sin without the help of God? "Plainly," he says, "our free choice suffices us for this." What is it, then, that the Apostle says, writing to the Corinthians: *But we pray to God, that you may do no evil*[^21]? You have attended, you have heard, you have received; and because it is most manifest, without doubt you have understood what the Apostle prayed. *We pray*, he says, *to the Lord, that you may do no evil*. He could have said: We admonish you, that you may do no evil; we teach you, that you may do no evil; we command you, we charge you. Which indeed, if he said it, he would rightly say, because our will too does something; for our will does not do nothing, but it alone does not suffice. Yet he preferred to say: *We pray*, that he might commend grace itself, that they might understand that, when they do no evil, it is not by their own will alone that they avoid evil, but by the help of God that they fulfill what is commanded.

**14.** (4.) Therefore, brothers, when something is commanded, recognize the choice of the will; when what is commanded is prayed for, recognize the benefit of grace. For you have both in the Scriptures: both it is commanded and it is prayed for; what is commanded, this is prayed for. See what I say. It is commanded that we understand. How is it commanded that we understand? *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^22]. You have heard that it is commanded: ask, that you may be able to fulfill what is commanded. "How," you say, "do I ask?" Hear Scripture. What is commanded you? *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*. Because it is commanded, you have recognized the will. Hear that it is prayed for, that you may recognize grace. *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments*[^23]. It is commanded that we have wisdom. Because it is commanded, I read it. "Where do you read it?" he says. Hear: *You who are unwise among the people, and foolish, be at some time wise*[^24]. Now what does that one say? "You see how God commanded us to be wise." Then is wisdom in our power? I have already said, I have heard the precept, I have recognized the will: hear the prayer, that you too may be able to recognize grace. It is about wisdom, which is commanded us. Let us hear what the apostle James says: *But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all abundantly*[^25]. Continence is commanded us. Where is it commanded? The Apostle to Timothy: *Keep yourself continent*[^26]. It is a command, it is a precept: it must be heard, it must be done. But unless God helps, we are left behind. We try indeed to do it by will, and the will strives at something. Let not the power presume, unless the weakness be helped. For surely it is commanded: *Keep yourself continent*. Hear another place of Scripture: *And when I knew*, he says, *that no one can be continent unless God grants it, and this very thing was a matter of wisdom, to know whose gift this was*. "And what," he says, "did I do?" *I approached the Lord and besought him*[^27]. What need is there to run through many things, my brothers? Whatever is commanded us, it must be prayed that it be fulfilled. But not so that we let ourselves go and lie on our backs like the slothful and say: "Let God rain food upon our faces, so that we wish to do nothing at all"; and when the food has rained upon our mouth, let us also say: "Let God swallow for us." We too ought to do something, we ought to be eager, we ought to try, and to give thanks in that wherein we have been able, to pray in that wherein we have not been able. When you give thanks, you take heed lest you be condemned as ungrateful; but when you ask for what you do not yet have, you take heed lest you remain empty, because you are hindered.}

**15.** (5.) Consider these things, then, my brothers: whoever approaches you and says to you, "What, then, do we do? We have nothing in our power, unless God gives all. Then God will not crown us, but will crown himself"—you now see that he comes from that vein. It is a vein, but it has poison. For it has been struck by the serpent, it is not healthy. For this Satan now does daily, how he may cast out of the Church by the poisons of heretics, just as then by the poisons of the serpent he cast out of paradise. Let no one say that that man was absolved by the bishops. It is absolved, but the confession, as a kind of correction, is itself absolved, because the things he said before the bishops seemed catholic; but what he wrote in his books the bishops who absolved did not know. And perhaps he has been corrected: for we ought not to despair of a man, who perhaps preferred to be joined to the catholic faith and fled to its grace and help. Perhaps this has happened: yet it is not the heresy that has been absolved, but a man denying the heresy.} But when we have read the proceedings, when they reach our hands, whatever more openly we learn about this evil, or perhaps about his correction, we ought, with the Lord helping, to announce it to your love.
