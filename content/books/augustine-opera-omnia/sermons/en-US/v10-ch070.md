# Sermon 383: On the Anniversary of Episcopal Ordination

**1.** Every day indeed, and every hour, and with altogether continuous care, most beloved, a bishop ought to think how great a burden of stewardship he bears, what account of it he is to render to his Lord. Yet when the anniversary of our ordination dawns, then especially the weight of this office is attended to, as though then for the first time imposed. But it makes a difference that on the day on which we first undertook it, we only thought how it was to be borne; whereas on the following days, and especially on that on which its festival is held, we not only provide, with cautious foresight, for its future, how thereafter it ought to be borne; but also recall with anxious memory the things past, how they were done: so that we may imitate ourselves in good deeds, and, if anything blameworthy has passed, may take care that it not be repeated, and pray that it be pardoned; and may flee the accusation of the devil, where we can, by diligence in acting rightly; but where we cannot, may conquer it by the piety of confessing. For just as future sins are committed by neglecting righteousness, so past ones are made firm by defending injustice. As, therefore, love foresees that they not happen, so humility blots out what has been done: so that what can no longer, by acting rightly, be kept from being committed, may at least, by not being proud, be dismissed. For we have learned to say to our Father who is in heaven: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^1]. And that we may be able to say this truthfully, we must indeed love even our enemies: whom, however, we ought not to have, unless justice compels it. For if men are our enemies because of our evil deserts, we must not take care to forgive them their debts, but rather fear that we may pay them. For if they hate us by the merit of our iniquity, we are their debtors, not they ours.

**2.** On this solemn day, then, of my episcopate, I will first address in a few words my debtors, who, without knowing it, plead for me before God, while they incur debts which I may forgive, so that I too may deserve to have my debts forgiven me. To you, therefore, I say—whether present or absent—to whom I am made an enemy by preaching the truth, to whom by counseling I seem burdensome, whose advantage I seek and am compelled to offend your will: *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^2]. For these beasts of burden too most attack, with hoof and bite, those by whom they are cared for, when their wounds are handled to be cured. You do not spare, I do not spare; you oppose, I oppose; you resist, I resist. The struggle pairs us, but the cause separates us. You are an enemy to the physician, I to the disease; you to my diligence, I to your pestilence. *They repaid me*, he says, *evil for good; but I prayed*[^3]. What did he pray, except: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^4]? *When they*, he says, *revile you, and say all manner of evil against you for righteousness' sake, rejoice and exult, for your reward is great in heaven*[^5]. You, however, correct your perversity, recognize our love; render love for love: we do not want a greater reward with your perdition. Let these few words, for now, suffice to my debtors, whom I forgive, that I may be forgiven.

**3.** Next I must address briefly those too to whom I am a debtor. For as the Apostle says: *To Greeks and barbarians, to the wise and the unwise, I am a debtor*[^6]. For such a debtor I too am, according to the smallness of my strength and the little portion of my stewardship, not to certain ones, but to all. But now I speak of those debts which I desire to be forgiven me, not exacted of me. For I am not so lifted up by the swelling of a vain mind as to dare to say that, from the time I bear the burden of this office, no man has been wrongly harmed by me. This is, I will not say impossible, but certainly difficult, for any man occupied and stretched by so many and so troublesome affairs: how much more for me, who know my weakness, which, with your prayers and on my behalf, day and night I offer to be healed by the Lord our God? Disturbed, then, by the heats and difficulties of various cares, if perhaps I have not heard someone as he asked, if I have looked at someone more sternly than was needful, if I have uttered against someone a word harsher than I ought, if by an unsuitable reply I have disturbed someone bruised in heart and needing help, if perhaps, intent on something else, I have either passed over, or put off, or even saddened with a harsh look some poor man importunately pressing me; if against someone—as a man against a man suspecting—I have been indignant more bitterly than was just, if anyone did not recognize in his own conscience what I suspected of him in human fashion: you, to whom for these and such offenses I confess myself a debtor, at the same time believe me to be your lover. For a mother often, in tight quarters, treads on the chicks she warms, but not with the whole weight of her foot, nor does she therefore cease to be a mother. Forgive, that you may be forgiven. Forgive the debts of difficulty to one who loves you, you who ought not even to hold against your enemies the debts of cruelty. In sum, I beseech you all: commend to the Lord his care for you that is mine; for from this I justly ask of you yours for me: that whatever there is of my offenses in the past, he may graciously pardon, not severely take note. And whatever time of mine there will be hereafter under this burden, may he govern me as I journey, and make me pleasing to his eyes and useful to you; so that his gaze may find you not my horror and punishment, but my joy and my crown.

[^2]: Ps 32:9 (31:9)
[^3]: Ps 35:12-13 (34:12-13)
[^4]: Luke 23:34
[^5]: Matt 5:11-12
[^6]: Rom 1:14
