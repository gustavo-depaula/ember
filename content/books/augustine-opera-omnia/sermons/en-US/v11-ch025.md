# Sermon 360/B: (Dolbeau 25)

**1.** The word of God does not cease to exhort and console us by faithful promises and by healthful threats. For neither not to love nor not to fear is expedient for us. But as God the promiser is to be loved, so the threatener is to be feared. In neither does he deceive the hearer, in neither does he deceive the believer. Let no one say in his mind: "He promises true things, but threatens false ones." Both are true. Love and fear. Without any doubt he is going to come who has already come. But he came to teach you patience, he is going to come to crown patience. He will surely crown when he comes what he taught when he had come. And what he threatened when he had come, he will inflict when he comes. But there are these two things: the promise of God, eternal life; the threat of God, eternal punishment. If you do not yet know how to love what he promises, begin to fear what he threatens. For so it is written: *The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^1]. But the apostle John says: *Fear is not in love, but perfect love casts out fear*[^2]. When, then, we have heard: *The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord,* let us begin to fear. But because fear has the torment of the heart, you will not be long in torment, if in you love has grown and been perfected. Nor yet can it be begun in you, unless by fear you have furnished your heart for sowing it. But love, born, the more it grows, the more fear is diminished. And if, as it grows, fear is diminished, when it is consummated, fear is consumed.

**2.** For what God promises, dearly beloved, exceeds not only our words, but even the thoughts of all men. For so too it is commended: *What eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him*[^3]. If it were some color like the light which the bodily eyes know, it would not be said: *Nor has eye seen;* if it were some sweet sound such as human ears are wont to be delighted by, like that of instruments and any musical things, it would not be said: *Nor has ear heard.* And because men cannot turn over good things in their minds, except those which they have perceived by the bodily senses, it was added: *Nor has it ascended into the heart of man.* For you, O man, cannot think of a good, except such as you are wont to see or hear or touch by some such sense. Whatever has not entered through the sense of your body cannot even be thought by your soul.

**3.** And so, when it has been said to us that we will be in paradise, we think of some pleasant garden. And if we think of a larger one than we are wont to see, yet we enlarge this very kind of thing. If we are wont, for example, to see small trees, we think of them large; and if we are wont to see such or such apples or fruits, we think of larger ones. If we are wont to see meadows of some extent, we turn over in our mind immense ones without end: yet we enlarge by thinking the same things we know by the eyes. Again, when we hear: God *dwells in inaccessible light*[^4], from this light which we know by the bodily eyes we measure that one and amplify it into an immense measure, yet by enlarging what we know, although that light is of a far other kind: for that light is not of the eyes, but of minds. And just as the eye of the flesh must be cleansed that it may be able to bear this corporeal light, which either shines down from heaven or flashes from the nocturnal lights, and if the eyes are wounded and disturbed by some internal humor or by some thing rushing in from without, they will have that very light by which they were wont to be quickened as a thing of pain and will be tortured by that in which they were wont to rejoice, so too for that intelligible and immortal light, not the eye of the flesh, but the eye of the heart must be cleansed. For just as phlegm, which causes bleariness, disturbs the eye of the flesh, so iniquity disturbs that one. It too has its own weakness, has its own uncleanness, coming not from dust, but from sin. As, then, this eye of the body must be cleansed to see its corporeal light, so too that inner eye must be cleansed to see that light which *neither eye has seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man.*

**4.** Why *does it not ascend into the heart of man?* For it is beheld by the heart, when it is beheld. But why *does it not ascend into the heart of man?* Because of man. What "because of man"? Those who know the Scriptures understand and anticipate by thought what I am about to say. Sometimes, by a certain definite signification, our Scripture calls those "men" who are still wise according to the flesh. For they are men, that is, they are Adam. But you know that Adam sinned, and thence draws the origin of carnal concupiscence whoever is mortally born. He carries with him, then, a wound of the eye, and as long as he is a man, as long as there is in him that which was wounded and disturbed by the first sin. Whence a certain one cries out in the psalms and says, sighing and groaning to God: *And the light of my eyes is not with me*[^5]. As long, then, as a man is so, perceiving carnally, he cannot think nor grasp with the mind that light, and therefore it is said: *What eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man.* What is *of man?* Of one perceiving carnally. What is *of man?* Of one still carrying Adam. Whence those who were men, what did he wish to make them, to whom it was a reproach that they were men? For he says to them: *For when each one of you says: I am of Paul, but I of Apollos, but I of Cephas*[^6]. For they had divided among themselves the ministers of God and made themselves parts of the Church of Christ, the evil of schisms beginning, which were afterward confirmed by the error of men. These things they said, perceiving carnally and placing their hope not in God but in man, not singing with the heart what we sang a little before: *In you our fathers hoped*[^7].

**5.** Rebuking these, then, as saying such things, the Apostle says: *Are you not men and do you not walk according to man?*[^8] Again in the psalm it is said in the person of God speaking: *I said: You are gods and all sons of the Most High. But you shall die like men and shall fall like one of the princes*[^9]. As you know, by "one of the ancient princes" he means the devil. For though he was an angel, fallen by pride he was made the devil. He, then, who, fallen then, envied the one standing, he himself even now envies the one returning. Men were therefore made mortal, that by a greater affliction of penalty they might be schooled to humility, and, scourged in a manner by their mortality and considering that they cannot live here longer and, if they lived longer, yet would not always live and this life would at some time have to be ended, they might humble themselves to God and try to grasp the future, the present fleeing. For what flees or slips away cannot be held. Or can any of us now, while either we stand and speak or you stand and hear, hold his ages, that either boys not grow up or young men not grow old? You see that, from the time we speak, it is time. And if, from the time we speak, it is time, and by the length of time it comes about that we incline into old age, even now, in this very time in which we have spoken, we have aged somewhat. But these our changes are grasped by reason, but cannot be seen by the eyes. For neither are your hairs seen when they grow, and yet unless they grew continually, you would not after a few days seek the barber: for what the barber is going to take off tomorrow does not grow in a single night. But just as the hairs both grow now and cannot be seen, so age itself both grows old now and cannot be grasped by the eyes.

**6.** Men, then, love the present life, which they cannot hold as it flees, and, as those things increase and these fail, it slips away. How much better, then, do they hold something firm, to which, these things ended, they are going to come. To this is added that, since the life of man is short, it is also uncertain. For if old age itself were certain for every man, he ought to consider that life is short, even if it were permitted to all to be led to its bounds. For what is long that is ended? To this, then, is added that death is the companion of mortality, and as it walks with you in a manner on the way, when it may seize you, you surely do not know. Since, then, both life itself is short, and death is uncertain to every age, men thinking thus ought to be humbled to God, to entreat the Creator, to confess, to groan in their sins, to allege their sickness to the physician, that they may be healed within and change that eye whence that light can be seen, which is not seen as long as the inner eye of man is still of man. Let them wake up, then, when they hear from God: *I said: You are gods and all sons of the Most High.* What is *I said?* I call to this, I wish to do this. Hear the Gospel: *He gave them power to become sons of God*[^10]. When, therefore, I say: *You are gods and all sons of the Most High, but you shall die like men,* and not even your very mortality profits you to correct you, but as though you were immortal, so *you fall like one of the princes,* that is, you grow proud just as the angel dared to grow proud. But if pride cast down an angel, what will it do to a man? You shall, then, be gods. And if you do not worship false gods, you shall be gods. And how shall you be? By the making of him who made men too. For he who made us men wishes to make us gods, not that we be worshiped in his place, but that he himself be worshiped in us.

**7.** There is, then, as I had begun to say, dearly beloved, an inner eye which sins, carnal concupiscences, and earthly desires wound and disturb, so that the very sinning man heard: *You are earth and into earth you shall go*[^11]. If, then, the proud and unjust man deserved to hear: *You are earth and into earth you shall go,* why should not the humble and pious man hear: "You are heaven and into heaven you shall go"? For by humility and piety one becomes a seat of God. Now when he has been made a seat of God, is he not heaven? It is said in the Scriptures: *Heaven is my seat, but the earth is the footstool of my feet*[^12]. If, then, heaven is the seat of God, be heaven that you may carry God. When you have begun to carry God, you will be heaven. But that you may carry him fully, he himself will cleanse you, when he begins to dwell in you, until he brings that eye of the heart to such cleanness that it can see his face, in whom unseen it believed. Believe, then, before you see, that by faith, the heart cleansed, you may merit even to see what you believed. For light is promised you, which *neither eye has seen,* because it is not a color, *nor ear heard,* because it is not a sound, *nor has it ascended into the heart of man,* because the man who is properly called man, carnal, weak, animal, cannot think of anything but what he has drawn from the senses of the body. Not such is that light. Let the soul not dare to form anything for itself, as in a fantasy, about God: the one it wishes to find, let it first learn not to find.

**8.** What is it that I said: let it first learn not to find? That, when perhaps it thinks of God and there begins to occur to it what it has seen, the beauty of the earth occurs, let it spit it out from its mind; the appearance of waters occurs, the tranquility of the serene air occurs, let it spit this out too by its thought; let it say to itself: "This is not my God, it is the work of my God." This is not, I say, my God, it is the work of my God: what was made, you think of. O my soul, seek him who made it. But when your thought has come even to the heavenly bodies, let not the heavenly light entice you, not even that which is highest. For the highest heavenly light is in the sun. The primacy of brightness of the heavenly bodies is found in the sun, which suffices for the day. Do not think your God to be anything such, nor increase that very brightness into something greater and wander off for yourself through the spaces of your fantasy; neither is this God which is figured in your soul such as is wont to shine to the eyes of the body. This is not God.

**9.** Come to the soul itself, because it too is not seen. The soul is not seen, and great is the power of an incorporeal nature. For the soul is not a body, it is something invisible, and something great: it cannot be seen, but from its works marvel at what you do not see. What delights you in human affairs? Look around at the order of things, the beauty of cultivated fields, of forests cleared, of fruitful trees grafted, and whatever we see and love in the fields, the very order of the commonwealth, the masses of buildings, the varieties of arts, the abundance of tongues, the depth of memory, the richness of eloquence. All these are works of the soul. How great works of the soul you see, and you do not see the soul itself! When, therefore, a certain such nature has begun to occur to you, is it now your God whom you were seeking? It is not seen, it is now something incorporeal, something spiritual, something great, which quickens even the mortal members, which restrains and holds the flowing, in a manner, rottenness of the body. But this even the soul of a beast can do. Something great indeed is even the soul of a beast, something invisible is this too. But transcend to the soul of man: behold where man was made to the image of God. For to the image of God he was made not in the body, but in the mind, in that thing by which he governs all these, in that thing by which he surpasses the beasts. For how many beasts surpass us both in firmness of body and in keenness of carnal senses! Both in speed and in strength, in many corporeal endowments we are surpassed by beasts. Whence are we better than the beasts, but because we understand, because we have reason by which we can even tame a wild beast? But by a wild beast we cannot be tamed. But just as a man is fit to tame a beast, so to tame a man none is fit but God. When, therefore, something such occurs to you, that you think of the human mind even as free from corporeal bonds, dare to suspect God to be nothing such. Now indeed you seem to be near, but at a long interval. Near, in such a way that there is nothing higher that approaches God, but yet between your mind and God who created your mind there is very much difference. No nature or place lies between, but by unlikeness it is far off, since this was made, he made; nor can what was made in any way be compared to the maker. Yet the image of your God is in some measure in your mind.

**10.** Transcend, if you can, even your mind, if indeed you have arrived by cognition at your mind. Consider, brethren, what I say. For perhaps, even when you think of your mind, you think of something corporeal by the custom of the carnal senses, so that your mind seems to you to be either air or fire or this light which you behold. You think of something such, when you think of your mind. Do not think of anything such. As soon as that which you understand has occurred to you, "what is it that I understand?", say to yourself. For surely, unless a certain light were there, you would not understand. For so you behold a certain light within, as you behold a certain light without. For the light of your body is your eye. But the light being absent, what does your open eye profit? You have indeed whole eyes, but you are aided by another light that you may see. So too when you understand, you have something or other of the kind, which can enjoy the inner light, which is not this which you see with the eyes. Think your mind to be something such, if you can; but if you cannot, what is that which is above your mind, which terrifies your mind, which exhorts your mind, which forms your mind? You cannot think the mind worthily: whence can you him, whom you are to see with a pure mind? Therefore if you cannot do even this, then not the earth, not the heaven, not the air, not this starry light, not the so wonderful power and nature of the rational soul itself can rightly be called your God, but everywhere you ought to say: "This is not my God." You cannot, then, know what he is, unless you have first learned what he is not. Therefore first think what he is not, that you may find what he is.

**11.** This is what I said a little before: learn to be ignorant of God, that you may merit to find him. For you learn to be ignorant of him by a better ignorance than a false knowledge. For better is an ignorance that does not err, than a knowledge that is so called and is not. For you will say to me: "I know God." I ask what God is. You begin to wish to explain, and first by this very thing you are ignorant, in that you suppose you can explain what you cannot think. You are going to tell me, then, your thoughts, you are going to tell me those that have ascended into your heart. But see that you are a man, and that what you are going to tell me has ascended into the heart of man. But he who promises himself to be enjoyed by those who love him surely does not promise what eye has seen nor what ear has heard nor what has ascended into the heart of man. But how do they love him whom they do not see, except because they believe before they see? To those who love him, then, what does he promise? *What eye has not seen nor ear heard.* Can it perhaps be thought? Do not deceive yourself: *nor has it ascended into the heart of man.*

**12.** What then? How do you prepare yourself? Say. "I wish to see my God." Say to him: "I wish to see," say to him who says: *Ask and you will receive, knock and it will be opened to you*[^13]. Knock, set before the door, knock vehemently. He who closes does not drive away: he wishes to exercise the knocker. Knock, then, knock, not with a corporeal hand, but with the affection of the heart. Say to the Lord your God what you sing in the psalms: *To you my heart has said: I have sought your face, your face will I seek*[^14]. Say too what is said in another psalm: *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, that I may contemplate the delight of the Lord*[^15]. Desire to contemplate and say to him: "I wish to see you." But whence shall I see? If with eyes of flesh, then you are a corporeal light. Now my heart already reports to me that you, my God, are not a corporeal light. What, then, are you? I have transcended all those things, I have now arrived at my mind, and even that is not my God. And yet the nature of the mind transcends all corporeal things whether terrestrial or heavenly, but it is not yet my God. For my mind is changeable: but my God is unchangeable. I seek something unchangeable, when I seek my God. But whence do I recognize the mind to be changeable? Now it remembers, now it forgets; now it is wise, now it is foolish; now it wills, now it does not will; now it is angry, now it is calmed. I seek something unchangeable, when I seek my God. So my God has spoken to me in the Scriptures, that I may in some way think what I believe, not yet have what I see. I seek something which always remains unchangeable.

**13.** But whence shall I see? The Gospel will answer you: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^16]. If, then, *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God,* but we carry hearts unclean from the weight of sins, what shall we do? Whence shall we cleanse our inner eye, by which the face of our God can be seen? Whence shall we cleanse it? You have this too in Scripture: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^17]. Let us, then, hold these two testimonies: one from the Gospel, the other from the Acts of the Apostles. What from the Gospel? *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* You have looked at yourself, you have found a certain uncleanness of your heart; desiring to see God and hearing that he is not seen except by clean hearts, inflamed by the desire of seeing, you surely seek to cleanse your heart. Whence, then, shall you cleanse it? Attend to the one saying in the Acts: *Cleansing their hearts by faith.* Hold these two: one in the promise, the other in the action. In the promise what? *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* In the action what? *Cleansing their hearts by faith.* Believe, then, before you see, that you may be able to rejoice when you see.

**14.** Let not a vain thought ascend into your heart: "What is this that the Christians say: Believe, believe?" The physician says this, who knows what is being done in your eye. Now resist also the hands of the physician and say: "I do not believe, unless you show me." The physician will answer you: "There is no one to whom I may show it; I wish to heal in you that by which you can see what you now desire me to show." Suppose a physician to say to a man blinded by some mist, and perhaps from the beginning of his life, so that he does not now even know what is seen by the seeing: "There is what I may show you; behold, by a certain sense you know yourself to be blind, but others to be seeing: since you need a guide which they do not need, there is surely some difference between them and you. They see, then, something which you do not see; which, if you saw, you would greatly rejoice." He rouses in him a desire of seeing what he does not know, wishing to cure him, that he may be able to see what he does not see. But if he is so absurd and averse from all reason of truth that he says to the physician: "You shall not cure me, unless you demonstrate to me what I am to see," what do you suppose the physician will answer? "You must be cured that you may see, not see that you may be cured. You are perverse, you are preposterous. Let that be done first which you do not wish, that you may be able to arrive at that which you wish. If you had eyes by which I could show you what I say, there would be no need for you to be cured." Perhaps he will answer: "And what am I to do? Cure me as you wish." And the physician: "I am going to apply certain sharper salves, which may wipe away your mist, of whose sharpness you are going to feel some pain. But you must bear the healthful pain patiently and not, anxious and impatient of pain, push away my hands. For I know what I am doing in your eyes, that there may be eyes which now are not even to be called eyes. I know what I am doing, and therefore I foretell to you that you are going to suffer something troublesome, with the fruit of enlightenment." Perhaps he, terrified lest he be bitten by those medicines which the physician is going to apply, returns again to that worn-out and rejected: "Am I to suffer such great pains, which you are going to lay on me? Unless I first see what you are going to demonstrate, I will not bear them." On the contrary, he: "You cannot. This is what I seek; I beg you: to be cured. You will see, the mist will be driven out, that light will shine again for you which you hear those who see name and you do not see. For you hear: light, color, brightness; these names you hear. These names are of certain things, the things themselves you do not see. Those who see are more blessed than you. Bear, then, some pain in compensation for great joys." If he consents, he will be cured and will see. If he does not consent, wishing first to see rather than to be cured that there may be that whence he may see, most absurd and an enemy of his own salvation, he will forsake the physician.

**15.** Attend now: the healthful physician, our Lord Jesus Christ, came to us, found the blindness of our heart, promised the light which *eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man.* This the angels see, of it they rejoice. For just as those healthy men see what the blind man does not see, so the angels see what man does not see. Why does man not see? Because he still wishes to be a man. Let the man himself, then, begin to be cured, that from a man he may become one among the sons of God, because *he gave them power to become sons of God*[^18]. This is: *he gave them power,* that they may be cured, that the mist of the heart may be wiped away, because *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* And hear that what is said elsewhere is in the Gospel too: *Cleansing their hearts by faith.* When the Lord had said: *He gave them power to become sons of God,* he at once subjoined: *to those believing in his name*[^19]. If, then, he gave to believers the power to become sons of God—and the sons of God will be able to see what does not ascend into the heart of man—he cleanses their hearts by faith that they may be able to be pure in heart, because they shall see God.

**16.** Blessed, then, are you, brethren, who believe; pray for those who do not believe, that they too may merit to see. Blessed are you who believe: you do not see, but you believe; you are not yet healthy, but you are still being cured. Your salvation is in hope, not yet in reality. Persevere under the hands of the physician, bear his precepts like sharp salves, abstain from the ruinous pleasures of the world: let not the unlawful things of the nations lead you, not the theatrical trifles, not the luxury of drunkenness, not the poison of unlawful curiosity. From all these abstain yourselves. But you have been wont to rejoice in these things, and when you begin to abstain from them, the greed of a broken habit will grieve. But these are the sharp salves by which the eyes are cured. Receive the precepts of the physician. Whatever he lays on you to bear, he himself first bore. And in him indeed there was nothing to be cured, because he was sick with nothing, but by the office of healing he bore what he laid on the sick. He gave a certain bitter cup to one swelling and inflated with pride; he, coming humble, suffered all humble things from proud men.

**17.** The humility of Christ is the medicine of your pride. Do not mock that by which you are cured. Deign to be humble, on account of him for whom God became humble. For he judged that you were to be healed by this medicine, who well knows both whence you are sick and whence you are to be healed. In all the members of the body good physicians seek the cause whence are drawn all those things that are most difficult to bear. And therefore many unskilled men, curing the accidental causes and not curing the original one, seem for a time to heal, but, the source as it were of the diseases remaining, what persists in the origin flows out again into streams of calamity. Hear, then, whence man is sick, whence he has not only eyes, but no members healthy. Hear whence he is sick, take it from the Scriptures where the art of the physician is written. For he is not more sure to you who reads to you of disease from Hippocrates, than he who demonstrates to you from the divine Scripture whence you are inwardly sick. Hear, then, the Scripture saying: *The beginning of all sin is pride*[^20]; and yet you run for the health of your body, for the soul you are slothful. If a straw rushes into your eye, it is not deferred that you remove it. Iniquity presses the eye of your heart, and you do not run to the physician. Although, while you could not run to the physician, the physician himself came to you and—what is more grievous—this very thing, that he came to you, you mock, you hold his mercy for nothing. He came, he wishes to help, he knows what to apply. Therefore God came humble, lest he be not imitable by man. For how would you imitate the lofty? Without imitating him, how would you be healed? He came, then, humble, because he knew what kind of cup he was giving you. Somewhat bitter indeed, but healthful. But you mock the one bringing the cup and still say to yourself: "What kind of God shall I have? Born, suffering, smeared with spittle, crowned with thorns, hung on a cross?" Unhappy soul! You see the humility of the physician, you do not see the swelling of your pride. Therefore the humble one displeases you, displeases your pride, the medicine which the physician gives you displeases your disease.

**18.** If you still mock, you are frenzied. The frenzied even often strike physicians, and the merciful not only are not angry with those who strike, but even seek the health of those who strike. But sometimes they prevail so as even to be able to kill the physician. But he vehemently avoids being killed by the frenzied, because he cannot rise again and heal the frenzied. But that physician of ours neither feared to be killed by the frenzied, and from his very death he prepared medicines for the frenzied. For he died and rose again. And see that the true physician is not angry with the frenzied who strike him, but rather pities them and from what he suffers wishes to heal the raging. Hear the physician hanging on the cross; the crowd of raging frenzied ones surveyed, he says: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^21]. Nor was his voice in vain. For after he rose and was glorified in the eyes of his disciples, so that he showed them even the scars of that same flesh raised again, and offered himself not only to be seen but also to be handled, he ascended into heaven, sent the Holy Spirit: miracles began to be done in the name of the slain one, in the name of the crucified one, and they were then more pricked who had killed him than when they saw him hanging on the cross. For they considered that such things were done in the name of him who was thought slain among their hands. They felt him living, whom they had mocked dying. *They were pricked at heart,* as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles; many of those very Jews who crucified the Lord sought counsel from the apostles; they received counsel too, because not in vain did he say, hanging: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.* The apostle said to them: {*Do penance, and let each one be baptized in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and your sins are forgiven you*[^22]. It was done: they were baptized and believed} in him whom they crucified. This is what I said, brethren, that even from his very death he made medicines for the frenzied.

**19.** Thence it went to the nations. The apostles were sent to the nations: they found the whole world given over to images. The disciples themselves too of that physician, in whom the physician himself presided, because they had been made heaven and carried God, began, then, to preach him who was crucified and *died for our offenses and rose again for our justification*[^23]. With signs and so great miracles bearing witness with them, the world began to be filled. And first, that it might be filled, the disciples of the physician too were slain, just as the physician himself had been slain. But when would they fear to be killed, when in their head they beheld even the flesh risen again? For whom would they fear, souls never to die, when in body they had already risen in the Lord? But he made all believers as it were a body to a head, so that he himself was their head, but all those who believe in him cohered as it were as members. But from the beginning of the world to the end, Christ has been believed in and will be believed in, because even before he was born of the Virgin Mary, many believed that he would come, just as now they believe in him who has already come. And through that very faith all are healed, nor is there any other salve for that mist of the spiritual eye, except as it is written: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^24]. He made, then, all the saints a body for himself, of which body he himself is the head. Nor would he be the head of this body, unless he had something from that body. For whence did he take on flesh, which could die in him? For if the soul of man cannot die, when could the divinity of the Word die? Thousands of martyrs too, then, were slain, and as it were their blood being sown, the harvest of the Church arose throughout the whole world.

**20.** Therefore, brethren, thousands of years ago these things were foretold and, as they were foretold, so they are seen expressed and displayed. Few remain which we read and believe, for more we already read and see. But from these which we read and see, it is no great thing to believe the fewer that remain. It was a great thing to believe for those who saw none of these things which we see. Now it is no longer praiseworthy to believe, but damnable not to believe. Let those at length wake up and be cured who still were unwilling to be cured. Let them believe: they will see. Let them not be so perverse as to say to us: "Let me first see and then I will believe." What is "let me see and then I will believe"? {For he who sees, does he believe? He believes who does not see. It is one thing to believe, another to see. Believe what you do not see, that by believing what you do not see you may merit to see what you believe. The merit of vision is faith. The reward of faith is vision. Why do you seek the reward before the work? Believe, then, and walk in faith: your salvation is in hope. For the best physician has begun to cure you, to whom no disease is incurable. Do not fear your past crimes, however immense, however incredible, which you perhaps committed: the diseases are great, but the physician is greater. Do not, then, care about the past: in one moment of the sacrament they will be forgiven, and absolutely all will be forgiven}. Nothing of the past will remain that may distress your care. You will be secure not in your own strength, but in the hand of the physician. Be, then, secure under him, because he will also heal what remains: that very weakness of our mortality, whence indeed lesser sins, while we live here, creep in. He will heal the whole, he will cleanse the whole. All the mist will be taken away, but let such an eye of the heart be given that you may be blessed when you see, because believing you have heard: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* Let those who have not yet believed behold, O my brethren, let them behold how great things God has displayed. All the things that are seen done throughout the whole world in the name of Christ were foreseen, foretold, prescribed. In our hands are the books, before our eyes the deeds.

**21.** And if we consider the truth, brethren, he did a more difficult thing than what he is going to do. What is it that is more difficult? He justified {the impious, from an idolater made a believer, from a drunkard a sober man, from a wanton man a frugal one, from a greedy man one giving away his goods, not giving to hunters with the devil applauding, but giving to the poor with Christ crowning, and acquiring for himself that which cannot pass away. The thing he did was more difficult. For he who from an impious man made a pious one, will he not render a reward to the pious one? Attend, my brethren. What is more incredible? To make a pious man out of an impious one, or to make an angel out of a pious one? The impious and the pious are contraries. The pious and the angel are not contraries. Will he not fill you from the neighboring thing, who changed you from the contrary? For now, when you have begun to be pious, you begin to imitate the life of an angel. But when you were impious, you were removed from the choir of angels. But faith came and justified you. You are humbled to God, who blasphemed God. And you who had turned to the creature now desire the Creator. See what he has bestowed on you: he has displayed his Church in the world; as he promised it, he displayed it. Idolatry was foretold to be at some time slain and taken away: our forebears read these things and did not see them, but we read and see them}. Heresies were foretold, schisms were foretold: these too exist. Whence Christians are not troubled when they see heresies and schisms. For they hope more surely that the things promised will come to pass, when the things foretold come to pass.

**22.** Flee, then, the crimes of all heresies and schisms, flee the sacrileges of the nations, the curiosities of demons, the adorations of idols, the sacrileges of remedies, the consultations of astrologers. Flee these things, brethren. You have nothing to hope for in them. For the future life they have never promised; for the present, if they say it, they lie to you. Let someone arise who says: "I know the astrologer told me the truth, and I know the soothsayer told me the truth, and I had that remedy, and it availed." Let your love know this alone, which can easily be seen. The happiness and unhappiness of the present time, which is neither true happiness nor full unhappiness, is mingled and scattered through all men. If only those were happy according to time who do these things, you, brethren, who have believed, ought, on account of future happiness, to despise the present. But since you see, mingled, both healthy those who do them and those who do not do them, mingled, dying both those who do not do them and those who do them, mingled, rich and poor both those who do them and those who do not do them, mingled, honored and lowborn both those who do them and those who do not do them—since, then, you see temporal happiness or unhappiness mingled in the human race, why do you not beware eternal unhappiness, when it will be said: *Go into the eternal fire,* and procure true happiness, when it will be said: *Receive the kingdom?*[^25]

**23.** "Juno," he says, "bestows on those giving birth, and Mercury on hunters or men of letters, and Neptune on those sailing." These things are false. For if they are true, let not the women bear well who blaspheme Juno. What great thing is it, my brethren, to open the eyes to see these things? Did the prophets perhaps foretell these things too? Question yourselves: let the human race answer. Are all shipwrecked who do not worship Neptune? Do all merchants suffer loss who mock Mercury? But if these things are false, they have never promised you future life, they profit nothing for the present life. Why are they worshiped, except to bind the feet of those going the way of the Lord, lest they seek immortality and some rest after the labors and difficulties of this life? For that devil sets himself against them with his angels, and as it were makes himself necessary, that he may reduce you to servitude. Use rather your liberty; greater is he who redeemed you than he who assails you. Whoever consent to this one will be condemned with him; whoever believe in Christ will condemn with him. These things are to come, but from those which have already come conjecture those which remain.

**24.** Persecutions to come for Christians from the kings of the world were foretold: they came to pass, the slaughter of the martyrs was made, and those who did these things thought they could finish off the Christians by killing them. The Church grew by its own blood, the persecutors were conquered, those who suffered persecution conquered. This too was foretold. The kings themselves too we find in the holy Scriptures going to submit their necks to the yoke of Christ, by whom at first persecution of the Church seemed as it were to be feared. And this came to pass, brethren: now the cross of Christ is on the foreheads of kings, kings adore what the Jews mocked. And because it was said thus: *God chose the weak things of this world that he might confound the strong, and the lowborn things of this world he chose, and the things that are not as though they were, that the things that are might be emptied out*[^26], our Lord Jesus Christ came for the salvation not only of the poor, but also of the rich, not only of plebeians, but also of kings. Yet he was unwilling to choose kings into discipleship, he was unwilling to choose the rich, the noble, the learned, but he chose the poor, the uneducated, the lowborn, fishermen, where his grace might more shine forth. For he came to give a cup of humility and to heal pride. And if he first called a king, the king would say his rank was chosen in him; if he first called a learned man, he would say his learning was chosen. Those who were called to humility had to be called through humble men. And so Christ did not gain a fisherman from an emperor, but an emperor from a fisherman.

**25.** Now kings come to Rome. A great thing, brethren, how it has been fulfilled. When it was being said, when it was being written, none of these things existed. It is wonderful: attend and see, rejoice. Let those be curious who are unwilling to attend to it; for these things we wish them to be curious: let them forsake the crimes and trifles of vain curiosities, let them at some time be curious about the divine Scriptures, let them find that the things they now see were foretold so long before. For they wonder that in the name of the crucified one the human race runs together and flocks, from kings to those clothed in rags. No age has been passed over, no sect, no teaching. For it is not that the unlearned believed and the learned did not believe, or the lowborn believed and the noble did not believe, or women believed and men did not believe, or boys believed and old men did not believe, or servants believed and the free did not believe. Every age has been called to salvation, every age has now come, every dignity, every abundance and human means. Now let all be within. Now few have remained outside and still argue; let them at length wake up at least to the noise of the world: the whole world cries out.

**26.** Kings come, as I had begun to say, to Rome. There are the temples of the emperors who by their pride exacted divine honors for themselves from men and, because they could—for they were kings and rulers—extorted rather than merited them. From whom could a fisherman extort any such thing? There is the tomb of the fisherman, there is the temple of the emperor. Peter is there in a tomb, Hadrian is there in a temple. The temple of Hadrian, the memorial of Peter. The emperor comes. Let us see where he runs, where he wished to bend his knees: in the temple of the emperor, or in the memorial of the fisherman? His diadem laid aside, he beats his breast where is the body of the fisherman, whose merits he considers, whose crown he believes, through whom he desires to come to God, by whose prayers he feels and finds himself helped. See what that crucified one, mocked on the cross, did; see whence he laid the nations low, not with the raging sword, but with the mocked wood. Let the proud, then, drink the cup of humility, with Christ humbled. Let them deign to be humble, let them now know their medicine, let them come and believe.

**27.** Exhort them, brethren, not only with words, but also with your conduct, and we exhort them now not to delay. For perhaps some think and say: "Tomorrow I will be a Christian." If tomorrow it is good, today too it is good. For that he may be a Christian, he is not to ask an astrologer for a day. God made every day. That is a good day for you on which you have done something good. If, then, it is good to believe in Christ, that by faith the heart may be cleansed and that eye healed which is to see so great a light, why is it deferred, why has the crow's voice remained in men? "Tomorrow, tomorrow," says the crow, which, sent from the ark, did not return; the dove returned. The crow cries "tomorrow," the dove daily groans. Let there not, then, be in you the voice of one deferring, but the groan of one confessing. Whoever are wearied by hearing, let them pardon the eager. Whoever still wish to hear, let them pardon the wearied, because we too are pressed by time to finish the sermon. For we see your eagerness in Christ to be so great that you could hear more, but we cannot hold the time. Whoever are here who have not believed, see, we are here, see, here is the Church: if they will, let them believe. If they think it should be deferred, which, I think, they ought now not to think, let them make room for those about to perform the divine mysteries.  
And after the pagans had gone out:

**28.** Now, brethren, both yesterday we said to you and now we say and always we ask, that by living well you may gain those who have not yet believed, lest you too have believed in vain. We ask you that, as the word of God pleases you, so it may please also in your conduct, not in the ear alone, but in the heart, not in the heart alone, but in your life, that you may be the family of God, fit and pleasing in his eyes for every good work. Altogether, brethren, we do not doubt that, if you live worthily of God, soon none of those who have not yet believed will remain in unbelief.
