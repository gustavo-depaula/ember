# Sermon 159/B: (Dolbeau 21)

**1.** The divine readings, which feed us spiritually, admonish us to dispense to you, waiting and attentive, and as it were from the Lord's storeroom, of which we are the stewards, to set something before the hungry. The apostolic reading has been announced to us in these words, which your holiness recalls with us: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How incomprehensible are his judgments, and how unsearchable his ways! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor, or who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid him? For from him and through him and in him are all things; to him be glory forever and ever, amen*[^1]. But that the Apostle might cry out and, as it were terrified by a certain depth of the judgments of God, say: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God,* he had said above: *God has shut up all under sin, that he might have mercy on all*[^2]. After this sentence, then, where he says: *God has shut up all under sin, that he might have mercy on all,* because truly there is something deep, I know not what, in men being first made manifestly guilty in their own conscience, that they might, by confessing, be helped, he cried out: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God.* Where is the *depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God?* In this, that *God has shut up all under sin, that he might have mercy on all.* Under what sin? Of unbelief. For he used this word: *God has shut up all,* he says, *under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.* Therefore let the Lord our God himself be present, whose riches the apostle praising cries out, and let him deign to impart to us something of those very hidden and deep riches of his, that, what we feel to be inexplicable, we may in some way say, not that we may explain it, but that we may commend things inexplicable. For the apostle seems, by a certain human weakness, as it were to have failed in explaining what he rejoiced in beholding. He saw something, I know not what, that the tongue could not explain; he beheld with the heart what he was less fit to express in words, and he did not find how to make us attentive to that which he saw, except by crying out and lifting up our hearts, saying: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God,* that our hearts, lifted up, may be directed to him whose riches, seeing, he cried out, not to the mouth of a weak steward who could not explain those riches. This, then, according to our small measure, let us have done, and let us have directed your hearts to him whose we all are and under whom, the one master, we are all fellow disciples in this school, where are the riches of God, where is the *depth of the riches,* and where are *his inscrutable judgments and his unsearchable ways,* because *he has shut up all under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.*

**2.** He who *has shut up all under unbelief* seems to be angry, but he who has mercy on all is gentle. Therefore the passage of the Apostle agrees with the psalm: *God, you have rejected us and destroyed us, you have been angry and have had mercy on us*[^3]. Hear the angry and the merciful. *God has shut up all under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.* What did the Lord our God wish to do? First to be angry, to reject, to humble, and afterward to come to aid, to call the one turned away, to hear the one converted, to help the one heard, to change the one helped, to crown the one changed. Join other testimonies of the Scriptures. The very voice of a certain man laboring on this earth says—that is, of Adam himself, of the human race—which human race, however, the second man from heaven did not abandon, that, being first earthly, they might afterward become heavenly. For because we were humbled, hence earthly; because we were set down, hence earthly; because we were rejected, hence earthly; but because he who rejected and set down and humbled had mercy on us, hence heavenly. Let us, then, hear the voice of that same man saying elsewhere: *Before I was humbled, I sinned*[^4]. Groaning in his humiliation, he acknowledged his sin; he attributed iniquity to himself, justice to God. For what does he say? *Before I was humbled*—which is the penalty God inflicted—*I,* he says, *sinned.* Lest God who humbled me seem unjust, my sin went before, my humiliation followed. The Lord my God is therefore a just judge. For I would not have come to this humiliation, unless I first sinned. And since that very humiliation of his seems indeed to sound the anger of God the judge, but pertains to mercy, hear his voice elsewhere: *It is good for me,* he says, *that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications*[^5]. Let your love attend to what he says: *Before I was humbled, I sinned.* In the penalty he seems to groan, in fetters to sigh, in this mortality and earthly weakness to seek his help by confessing, whom he had offended by sinning. For these words sound this: *Before I was humbled, I sinned,* that is: I do not charge my humiliation to you, my God: I did what is malignant, you did what is just.

**3.** With these words agree those words which we sang. For he who says: *God, you have rejected us and destroyed us,* he himself says: *Before I was humbled, I sinned.* For you have heard that God rejected; you have heard that God set down, that is, cast from a height to the ground. You have heard. Seek the cause why God did this: *Before I was humbled, I,* he says, *sinned.* You have heard your sin preceding and the justice of God following; hear that even that very justice of God, which humbled you, indicates not only the severity of a just judge, but also the clemency of a merciful one. For he says, as I said a little before: *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications.* What then, my brethren? Was God angry when he humbled, or was he merciful? If the humiliation profited us nothing, let it be reckoned to excessive severity of God; which severity, if it existed, we could not complain of his injustice. For let the sinner repay his own desert; let not the proud and unjust man flatter himself; let him first find what he is worthy of, that he may thus recognize what God has bestowed. Does the heart of any sinful man dare to promise itself anything but punishment, to promise itself anything but a most just penalty? Or if punishment has followed a man's iniquity, can it be said to a just judge: "You did wrong to condemn the sinner"? This, then, let us as sinners promise ourselves, let us so confess in our penalties both our sins and the justice of our God: for thus we will deserve, in our very penalty, to find the mercy of God. This, dearly beloved brethren, no one finds, except one who has first humbled himself. And because I am about to say it as I can, I do not think any of you will understand what I am about to say, unless he first suppress the smoke of pride, by which the eyes of the mind are darkened, lest the mercy of God in the penalty itself can be understood.

**4.** But first see this in daily life itself—for thence you can have a way to understanding, because he did not abandon the mortality of men—by certain likenesses in their very deeds, that it may be shown us that a penalty can be inflicted mercifully. What shall I say? You discipline your servant, and in giving discipline surely in the very thing in which you seem to punish, you show mercy, but I do not say "to a servant": perhaps you are so angry with a servant that you hate him. Indeed you ought not so, if you are a Christian; you ought not so, if you consider yourself to be a man; you ought not so, if you consider that "servant" and "master" are indeed diverse names, but not diverse "man" and "man." You ought not so to persecute with hatred a sinning servant. But because men are wont to do so, let us reject this likeness, let us set down a son. No one can but love his sons: for a man is not to be praised who loves his son. *For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have?* the Lord says; *do not the publicans do this?*[^6] How much more sons, whom men beget as successors to themselves. No one at all can, by the very right of nature, hate the one he begot. Nor is a man to be praised in that which is found in a beast. No one praises a man for loving his sons. You find this not only in gentle cattle: the ferocity of lions grows gentle toward their young, tigers love their young, serpents brood over their eggs and nourish their cubs. Therefore if those things that seem to be savage and harsh in creation do not keep their harshness and savagery toward those they beget, what great thing does a man do to love his son? But I have said these things, brethren, that you may see there can be a penalty of one showing mercy, from the example of sons, from that thing which no one can hate. So someone sees his son going into pride, exalting himself against his father, claiming for himself more than is fitting, wishing to dissolve into delightful trifles, wishing to squander what he does not yet possess; and that son, while he does this, is glad, laughing, rejoicing, exulting; but the father restrains him with rebuke, penalty, lashes, takes away his laughter, brings on weeping, seems to have taken away a good and brought in an evil—see what he took away: gladness; see what he brought in: groaning—and yet, if he had let that gladness go unpunished, he would be cruel; because he forced him to weeping, he is found merciful. If, then, a father who forces to weeping is found merciful, why do we not understand that our Creator too could do what we sang: *God, you have rejected us and set us down?* But why this? Is it for ruin, is it for destruction? Hear what follows: *You have been angry and have had mercy on us.* Why is he justly angry with you? Join, since we said: *Before I was humbled, I sinned.* What does it profit you that you were rejected and set down? *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications.*

**5.** Let us now bring back our mind to that apostolic saying: *God has shut up all under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.* The first sin of man was pride: so we read in Genesis, so we find in another Scripture. What do we read in Genesis? That man, created and formed, was placed in paradise, under a certain law, under a certain commandment; the commandment imposed on him showed him this: that he was so made great that he had a greater one above himself. God commanded, then, that humility be always kept by man, who was subject to him, that is, that the humility of man, set under God, be preserved. Man was indeed made to the image of God and, as it is written in another place, *he gave him the power of containing all things:* all things were under him, but above him was he who made all things. So, then, man ought to have attended to the things that were beneath him, so as the more to attend to him who was above him; for by clinging to the superior, he would more securely possess the inferior; but by withdrawing from the superior, he would be made subject to the inferior. As if we set down three men: one man having a servant, and having also a master, as it often happens that servants with property of their own have servants. Attend: he has a servant, he has a master; he is under one, he is over another; he is superior to the servant, inferior to his master. We set down the third as the servant of the servant, the first as the master of the master, the middle one as both servant and master: master of his servant and servant of his master. That third is nothing but a servant, that first is nothing but a master, that middle one is both servant and master. But he securely possesses his servant, if he does not offend his master. And we have indeed spoken of three men: all are of the same kind, all subsist of the same substance and nature. Not so those three: God and man and the creature inferior to man. For of another kind and not of the same substance are the founder and the founded, the maker and the made, the craftsman and the work, the creator and the creature. But the things that were created are indeed generally all called created, but they differ in natures and orders and merits and places. For first are the spiritual things and afterward the carnal, the very things that God founded, that God made. The first place the spiritual things have, the last the corporeal. But something spiritual is the human mind, where the likeness and image of God is imprinted; but corporeal are all the things which we perceive to lie under the senses of the body: they are known to all, they are seen, heard, smelled, tasted, touched; hard and soft, hot and cold, rough and smooth, all these are called corporeal, they are inferior. Above all these man was set, but according to the mind, according to that which in him was made to the image and likeness of God. For God is not circumscribed and shut in by a corporeal form, so that on one side he has a back, on another side has eyes, but he is a certain light, not such as we see with the eyes, nor, if you enlarge this which we see with the eyes, expanding it through the fantasy of your thoughts, and make fields of light and mountains of light and trees of light, flitting about through the vanities of your thoughts. Do you wish to understand spiritual light? Seek whence you understand.

**6.** This very light, I say, understand, by which you understand. What did I say? You see white and black things with the bodily eyes, you are aided from without by the light either of the sun or the moon or a lamp or some little fire. Furthermore, if that light from without did not aid your eyes, your lights would lie open in vain and without cause be called lights. But what lies open in you and is sound, that is, the eye, and what is applied from without as help, that is, the light, and what it is for the seeing of which you are aided, that is, colors and forms, you know and distinguish. This we have said about the eyes. You hear sounds, you know whence you hear. The eyes do not hear, but neither do the ears see. Something is lacking to the eyes for perceiving sounds, and something is lacking to the ears for perceiving colors. But to you none of these is lacking, because through the eyes you see, through the ears you hear. You know also the things that smell, and what member you apply whereby you perceive an odor, you know. For you do not apply the ear to perceive the sweetness of an odor, but you bring that which God created in you for smelling. Nor, when you wish to taste some dish, do you put it to the ears or the eye: you know that there is no sense there for distinguishing flavors. And do you wish to perceive whether something is hard or soft, cold or hot? You know that with your whole body you can perceive touch. These things you know. Well. Attend to that which is within: who is this within to whom all these senses report what men perceive? For these are as it were instruments, these are as it were placed in servitude. The perceiver is some inner ruler, I know not who, to whom these messengers report whatever they find outside. But that inner one, who distinguishes all these things, is surely superior to all these things. Therefore the eye has what to see, the ear what to hear, the nostrils what to smell, the mouth what to taste, the hand what to touch, and does the mind not have what it can behold through itself? The mind itself indeed perceives white and black, but with the eyes reporting; it itself perceives in sounds the melodious and the harsh, but with the ears reporting; it itself perceives in odors the sweet-smelling and the foul-smelling, but with the nostrils reporting; it itself perceives the sweet and the bitter, but with the mouth reporting; it itself perceives the hard and the soft, but when the hand, handling, has reported. Can it, then, perceive these so many and various things, with the body reporting: can it not by itself perceive something, with no member of the body reporting to it? Seek, then, what it perceives by itself, and you will find where the image of God is. White and black it perceived through the eyes, melodious and absurd the ears reported. And, not to run again through these things one by one which lie next to the body, the members of the body reported them. The just and the unjust—do the eyes report it? The just and the unjust the mind distinguishes and says: "This is just, this is unjust." Seek who has reported it. If justice is a color, the eyes reported it; if justice is a sound, the ears reported it; if it is an odor, the nostrils reported it; if a flavor, the mouth reported it; if it is hardness or softness, the hand reported it. If it is none of these, who reported it, but the inner light? This nature, then, this substance which you see to be excellent, of which, if I wished to speak more fully, time would not suffice, something inner, something divine in us made to the image and likeness of God, is above all corporeal things, and was so made that all corporeal creation, subject to it, served it; but yet the mind itself is not God. For if it were God, would it have sinned? For God is unchangeable. But our mind, because it was created, because it was made, is not what God is. It is changeable. We see even now its very changes. It is wise, it is foolish; it remembers, it forgets; it wills, it wills not; it is delighted, it is saddened. These changeabilities do not befall God, who is above the mind, and is the creator of the mind.

**7.** But yet all this which I have said is above the body, beneath God, beneath the master and above the servant. These are the three of which I was speaking a little before. If, then, three men, since they are all men, are ordered by a certain condition of this life, so that one of them is master only, another of them is servant only, but another of them is both servant of a master and master of a servant, how much more, do you think, and how much more easily and more distinctly is the whole creation ordered? The nature and substance of the mind set under God, the nature of the whole body set under the mind. But as I was saying: then he securely possesses his servant, if he has not offended his master; so that mind, if it did not, by a certain pride, by which it wished to be of its own power, offend its master, the whole nature of the body would always, like a servant, be subject to it. But because by pride it offended its Lord, the creature of the body, which had been given into its service, was made for it a torment of punishment, a torment of vengeance. For through the difficulty of the body the mind is now tortured, when it formerly had dominion over the whole nature of the body. As if that man—for from this you better grasp a clearer likeness, because this very thing, that we understand it with difficulty, pertains to the penalty by which we were humbled: from custom we give certain proofs. Set those three servants again before your eyes, because you scarcely understand this, though it is more distinct; for these things are more distinct in that they are diverse: for God is far other than the mind, and the mind is far other than the body. But in those three there is man, and man, and man. The nature is not diverse, but the condition makes an order; yet because those things are in our custom, we more easily understand them than those things which are more distinct. Now then understand what we say. Suppose that middle one—because he is so a servant that he is also a master; he is so a master that he is also a servant: servant of the superior, master of the inferior—suppose, then, that he offended his master. Whence did he offend? Through a certain pride. For he considered that he too had a servant, and dared to be exalted against his master, by the very fact that he seemed to have his servant in his power. He raised himself against his master; his master ordered that he be beaten by his own servant. For that master of the master was the master of both. For that servant did not have his servant in his power as much as the master of both had both. For when could he despise that master, no one's servant, so as not to beat his master, at the bidding of the more powerful master of both? Our God commanded, then, because we offended him, that we be tortured by our body; and our body was made mortal, and we began to suffer penalties thence, whence we dared to be proud against the Lord. We are therefore now beaten by our servant. We are tortured in the torments of our flesh; the Lord humbled us, that we might be beaten by a servant.

**8.** But why did he humble us, that we might be beaten by a servant? Because we first sinned: *Before I was humbled, I sinned.* Set, then, under the lash of your servant, cry out to the Lord your God, and say to him: *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications.* What *justifications of yours?* That, just as I have my body as a servant, you too have me as a servant. And just as I seek that the body obey me, so I ought to obey you. Therefore from this I learned *your justifications,* as it were with the Lord speaking to me from above and saying: "*O wicked servant,* at least now, set in this humiliation, acknowledge whom you have offended, and to whom you have been subjected. Surely you are tortured by your servant: you have a body and you wish it to obey you in all things, you wish that when you raise your hand, the hand follow; when you raise your foot, the foot follow. And although I have willed that you be beaten by your servant, still your servant serves you." For when we wish to walk and to move the body to another place, we command the feet, and they obey; we command the eye to see, when we wish to behold something: it does not contradict us, it turns, it reports to us. We apply the ear to sounds, at once it reports what sounds; we raise the hand to handle something, it does not resist. For in that the body serves us, it indicates that we are its masters; but in that it resists us, it indicates that we have a Lord. But let us see in what your body does not obey you. For example, you can walk ten miles, you wish twenty: it does not obey. You can walk fifty miles, you wish sixty: it does not obey. You wish to stay awake two nights: it obeys in part, in another part it does not obey. You wish to move the hand to lift something: you lift something; you try for something else: it does not obey. Add so many tormenting difficulties of its weakness and corruption which cannot be numbered, and attend how the body, in that it is corrupted, weighs down the soul. In that, then, it serves you, it shows that you are its master; but in that it resists you, it admonishes you to serve your Lord. Therefore say to your Lord: *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications.* How do you learn his justifications? That you may no longer disdain to serve your Lord, just as you wish your body to serve you. And you begin now to serve your Lord, and your body does not yet serve you as you wish. For you believe, who were unbelieving, you follow the precepts of your Lord, you walk the way, but justice is not yet perfected in you: therefore obedience is not yet perfected in your servant; there still remains something of bitterness, lest this world grow sweet to you, and you not desire your Lord who made the world.

**9.** Cry out from the ends of the earth to him, O Church spread through the world, say in the psalm: *From the ends of the earth I cried to you, when my heart was in anguish*[^7]. In the psalm these things are written: *On a rock you exalted me, you led me, because you have become my hope*[^8]. For God exalted us *on a rock.* On what rock? *But the rock was Christ,* says the Apostle[^9]. And how was our hope made there? Because our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we were made, is himself the Word of God, through whom all things were made. He took flesh from the mass of our mortality, and the death that pertained to the penalty of sin he took on, not sin, but by the mercy of freeing from sin he handed over that very flesh of his to death. For he was not handed over unwilling; he would not be crucified, unless he had handed himself over. For even that Judas handed him over, he handed him over willing. Yet there is not imputed to Judas the merit of the will of Christ, but the merit of his own greed. For when he handed over the Lord, he did not consider our salvation, but his own avarice and his own perfidy. For Judas handed him over, Christ handed himself over, the Father of Christ handed him over. All seem to have done one thing. They did one thing, but they did not do it with one intention. The Father handed over the Son out of mercy, the Son handed himself over by that same mercy, Judas handed over his master out of perfidy. There seems to be no difference between handing over and handing over, but between mercy and perfidy there is the greatest difference. How did the Father hand over? Hear the Apostle: *Who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^10]. How did the Son hand himself over? The same Apostle says of the Lord himself: *Who loved me and handed himself over for me*[^11]. He handed over, then, this flesh to be slain, that you might fear nothing for your flesh. He showed in his resurrection after three days what you ought to hope for at the end of the world. He leads you, then, because he has become your hope. You walk now toward the hope of resurrection, but unless our head first rose, the other members would not find what to hope for.

**10.** What then, my brethren? Although, even before the Lord suffered, the body served him as its Lord—for he was not so bound to the body as if for vengeance, as if for a penalty, that he should be beaten by a servant as we are; but if he wished to suffer anything in his body, he suffered it by will and by power, not by necessity and want, as he himself said: *I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again: no one takes it from me, but I myself lay it down*[^12]. Great, then, is the power in him; yet what he wished to suffer in the flesh, there he showed what you deservedly suffer. He suffered undeservedly, you suffer deservedly. But that you may bear what you suffer deservedly, he who suffered undeservedly consoles you. Bear, then, what you suffer, until your mortality pass away. Your kingdom comes, then, by fixed measures of time; he displays what he promised, because in himself he has already displayed that he rose. For he rose after three days; he willed to rise first and to show us what we ought to hope for at the end. We thought the flesh was going to perish; therefore he was unwilling to take flesh from anywhere else than where we too have flesh. For if he took it from elsewhere, we would say: "The flesh could rise that was taken from elsewhere. Was it taken from where we were taken?" He did not indeed admit to the mother the consort of a man, because he was the only Son of God. Because he had a Father above, on earth he sought only a mother. He showed us that what he created is not evil: he created male and female, he himself created both. But because through a woman man had been seduced, women might despair of themselves, unless through the Virgin Mary that sex were honored. He chose to be born of a woman; it was fitting that he who took on a man be born a man. But God had created not only a man, he had created a woman too. Women could, as I said, despair of themselves and say that they did not pertain to the mercy of God, because through a woman man was deceived; he deigned to be born of a woman, having taken on a man, and he honored the sex; he showed himself the founder of both sexes, and afterward the liberator. For because through a woman death was prepared for man by the serpent, to men themselves life was announced through women. For the women first saw the rising Lord and announced it to the apostles, the men. Our Lord Jesus Christ, then, showed us in his flesh what we ought to hope for at the end. Therefore he humbled us, that we might learn his justifications.

**11.** Now then let us return humbled, who were cast down proud. For the whole cause of our mortality, the whole cause of our weakness, the whole cause of all our torments, of all difficulties, of all the hardships in this world which the human race suffers, is nothing but pride. You have Scripture saying: *The beginning of all sin is pride*[^13]. And what else does it say? *The beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^14]. If pride seems a small evil to you, at least tremble to *apostatize from God.* Furthermore, if you tremble to *apostatize from God,* cast down the cause of apostatizing. For pride made man *apostatize from God.* Because, then, it is itself the head of all our diseases, for we are sick in this life. As a skilled physician, when he sees a man languishing with various diseases, does not attend to the proximate causes and leave the origin of all the causes—for if he cures the proximate causes while the source of the diseases is kept, the derivations of the calamity return, and he seems for a time to heal, but does not heal thoroughly; but that physician is found most skilled who gathers well all the causes of all the diseases, and whatever first cause he finds, whence all those diverse things seem to have arisen like branches, he cuts the root, and the whole forest of pains is cut down—so the Lord Jesus Christ—why he was called Savior, and who said: *The healthy have no need of a physician, but those who are ill*[^15], came to the sick, because the sick could not come to him; he sought those not seeking him, he turned himself to the weak, he suffered many things, he endured being killed by the blind, that from his very death he might heal their eyes—he did all these things, and because he saw pride to be the cause of all our diseases, by his humility he healed us.

**12.** Do not, then, mock the humility of Christ. For many pagans mock that Christ came humble—and would that only pagans!—and many heretics who call themselves Christians. It is foul to them that Christ was born of a woman; it is foul to them that he was fixed to the cross and wounded, and that those wounds he received were real, and those nails that were driven in were real; it is foul to them and they say: "He simulated all those things, he feigned them and did not endure them." Did truth, then, free you by a lie? Were you laboring under a lie, and were you healed by a lie? How can it be? But whoever say these things show what kind of masters they themselves are. For if the Lord rose and offered to his doubting disciple his hands to be touched and his scars to be handled, the disciple saying: "I will not believe, unless I put my fingers into his side," he showed himself not only to be seen with the eyes but also to be handled with the hands; but the disciple, when he handled the scars, found the truth made manifest and cried out: *My Lord and my God*[^16]. If, then, Christ deceived, will you tell the truth? How shall I hear you, tell me. Do you wish me to hear you as a master? "As a master," he says to me. What do you say to me, what do you teach me? "I teach you," he says, "that Christ was not born of a woman, and did not have real flesh, and that death was not real, nor those wounds real, and if not real wounds, then not real scars." And I, on the contrary, learned from the Gospel about the Lord Jesus Christ, who, when the disciple doubted, offered him his scars. Surely he could have risen without scars, who could heal the eyes of one born blind. But why did he wish to bring the testimony of the scars? Because the testimony of the scars of the body was the medicine of the wounds of the mind. What, then, will you teach me: that these things were false, and Christ simulated all these things, and the disciple, deceived by falsehood, cried out: *My Lord and my God?* If, then, he wished to make one healthy out of falsehood, how do I know whether you tell me the truth or lie? For you do not think it a crime to lie, when you try to set Christ before me as the author of a lie. For I am going to say to you: You lie, and you to me: "Far from it, I do not lie." You lie outright. "Far be it from me to lie." This you will say to me, that I may believe you. For say to me: "I am lying," I wish to know whether I am to believe you anything. But that I may believe you anything, you will say to me: "Far be it that I lie." Why did you say: "Far be it that I lie," unless you think it a crime to lie when you teach? What, then, you think a crime for yourself, this you assign to Christ? Therefore let human deceits withdraw: as it is written in the Gospel, so Christ came. Let not the humility of Christ be foul to you; that humility is foul to pride. Do not be proud, and the humble Christ will not be foul to you.

**13.** The Apostle says: *All things are clean to the clean. But to the unclean and unbelieving nothing is clean, but both their mind and conscience are polluted*[^17]. With a chaste heart say: "A woman conceived, a virgin conceived." She conceived by faith, the virgin conceived, the virgin gave birth, the virgin remained. Believe all these things, and let not those innards seem to you unclean. For even if that flesh had been altogether unclean, Christ coming to the flesh would cleanse the unclean, he would not be made unclean by the unclean. See the humility of your Lord: if it horrifies you, you are proud. Humility horrifies the proud. So, as you are proud, force yourself, that the cup of your swelling not horrify you. For when you are proud, you swell; you are not great. If you swell, drink the cup, that your innards may subside, that you may be able to be healthy. This cup the physician tempered for you, that you may drink. The physician himself tempered the chalice for you: drink the bitter chalice, if you wish to be healthy. Do you not see that you swell, do you not see that your innards are not healthy? You think yourself great, and you swell. That is not greatness, but disease. Do you wish to be free of disease, do you wish to be free of swelling? Drink the cup of humility. He tempered it for you, who came to you humble. And lest you doubt to drink, the physician drank first, not because it was necessary for the physician, but that he might take away doubt from the sick. Do not, then, despise the humility by which you are healed. The head of all diseases is pride. To heal the head of all diseases came he who deigned to become the head of the Church. The head of all diseases removed, you will be healthy. Be humbled and you will be healthy, and you will say more securely: *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your justifications.* For you raised yourself and were humbled. Humble yourself and you will be raised, because *God resists the proud, but to the humble gives grace*[^18]. Therefore *God has shut up all under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.*

**14.** Man withdrew from God, followed his own concupiscences, let go the reins: by erring, by wandering, he came even to the worship of idols. The Jewish people too, who worshiped one God, had grown proud, had grown proud and gone into iniquity. God, wishing to show them that they are weak, wishing to show them that they lie under the frailty of their own flesh, that that concupiscence which was drawn from the propagation of their parents still remained in them, gave them the law and commandments just and good and holy, as the Apostle says: *And so the law indeed is holy, and the commandment just and holy and good. Did, then, that which is good become death to me? Far from it! But sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through the good*[^19]. See how he called good that very law which had been given to the Jews. He called it good, because God had given it. And truly he had commanded all good things in the decalogue. Or was there perhaps anything evil: *You shall not steal, you shall not kill, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not bear false witness* and the rest, *you shall not covet your neighbor's thing?* For even if you have not taken it away, but only coveted it, the laws do not hold you in the forum, but God holds you in judgment. Attend, then, brethren: the law was given to the Jews, weak but proud. They began to try to do the justices of the law and to be cast down by their concupiscences, and they were made guilty, who before were unjust, but were not guilty of the law, were not transgressors. Whence the Apostle says: *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^20]. When the law is given, he who acts against the law, although he does what he was doing, yet when he did this without the law, he was a sinner, he was not a transgressor; but when he now does it, the law having been received, he is not only a sinner, but also a transgressor. Because, then, he is not only a sinner but also a transgressor, that is fulfilled which the Apostle says: *But the law entered in, that the offense might abound*[^21]. But why did the offense abound? For this is: *God, you have rejected us and set us down.* But it follows and he says: *But where the offense abounded, grace superabounded*[^22]. Because, then, *the offense abounded,* we rightly say: *God, you have rejected us and set us down, you have been angry.* But because *grace superabounded,* we rightly subjoin: *And you have had mercy on us.* Let not the Jews, then, say: "We are something." For *God has shut up all under unbelief, that he might have mercy on all.*

**15.** Let us, then, dearly beloved brethren, know our life, our Lord Jesus Christ; let us hold the medicine of our pride, the humility of our Lord Jesus Christ. Let us believe in him, let us hope wholly in his mercy, *who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all;* and when perhaps we make progress in his justifications, let us not be proud and despise others, but let us attend, on the road of justice, not to how much we have passed over, but to how much remains for us to traverse; and everywhere let us groan, and let us groan as long as we are pilgrims, because our joy will be only in the fatherland, when we have been made equal to the angels. *As long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims away from the Lord*[^23]. Why *are we pilgrims away from the Lord?* *For we walk by faith,* he says, *not by sight*[^24]. Faith is to believe what you do not see, sight is to see what you had believed. When, then, sight comes, that flame of love will be keener, because what, absent, you desired, present, you embrace; what, absent, you believed, present, you see. And if God believed is sweet, what will he be beheld? When, then, all these things are ended which still torment us on account of the remnants of our sins, then there will be the fullness of justice, then, joined to the angels, we will sing the everlasting hymn "alleluia": the praise of God will be ours without failing, nor will hunger drive us from it, because the body does not hunger, except that which is corrupted and weighs down the soul; nor will we thirst nor be weak nor grow old nor sink into sleep nor be wearied by any tiredness, but such as are the bodies of the angels, such will our flesh be in the resurrection of the dead. Do not wonder that this flesh will be heavenly bodies in the resurrection of the dead. Consider that, before we were, we were nothing, and from this believe what we will be when we have risen. Let each one consider himself: before he was born, what was he, where was he, where did he lie hidden? All this distinction of the body: ears, eyes, face, the spirit quickening the whole mass of the body, where were all these things? Surely in the secret of nature, surely where they were not seen. They came forth thence, God formed you who were not. What great thing is it for God to make an angel out of a man, who made man out of mud? What were you? and you are a man; you are a man, and will you not be an angel? An angel is made out of a man more nearly than a man is made out of what you were. He who did what is more wonderful in you, will he not do what remains?

**16.** You must believe, and let your faith not abandon Christ, not abandon the Gospel, not abandon his promises. Understand that all the things that are written are nearly accomplished, few are the things that remain. This Church, which you see spread throughout the world, a little while ago did not exist. You, a few years ago, were pagans, now you are Christians; your parents served demons, before, the temples were full of those offering incense, now the Church is full of those praising God. How suddenly did God change human affairs? Before all these things were, the Scriptures were read, they were believed and not seen; now we see the things which our forebears read. If, then, these so great things have been fulfilled, will the few that remain not come? Believe firmly that they will come, brethren, because all these things that have already come, came no otherwise than as they were written and foretold before they came. Many thousands of years ago, when it was said to Abraham: *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^25], it was said to one man: *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed;* he considered himself one, and this seed, and his wife already aged and worn out by old age itself, and it was said to him not only: "There will be seed from you"—which, if it alone were said, what more wonderful?—it was too little to say to one worn out by old age: "You will have a son," *In your seed,* he says, *all the nations shall be blessed.* God said wonderful things, he said impossible things, but easy for himself. That one man believed what he did not see, and we see it; what he believed, has been displayed to us, indeed has been repaid to us, what has been displayed in us. For of the seed of Abraham was Isaac, and of Isaac Jacob, and of Jacob the Jewish people, and of the Jewish people David, and of the seed of David the Virgin Mary, and of the Virgin Mary the Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore *in the seed* of Abraham *all the nations shall be blessed,* because all the nations are blessed in Christ. See, now there has been displayed to us what was promised to him. Therefore God, omnipotent and faithful, what he promised to one man, he displayed: what he promised to all, will he not display? My brethren, let your faith be built up, let your hope be strengthened. He did not deceive one man, will he be able to deceive the whole world? He displayed to one man the world full of Christians, he will display the world living with Christ his Son forever.

**17.** Holding these things, brethren, understand that the Church is not in a part, but is in the whole. Christ bought the whole, he gave his blood for the whole: the whole world has Christians, the unity of Christ is the Church. Without cause do the heretics quarrel with the Church of Christ: it is too little that they wished to be disinherited, they also slander the heirs. You draw them into unity, to the whole; let them not seduce you to a part. If you follow them, you will go to a part; if they hear you, they will come to the whole: by their own gain they are conquered. For Christ bought the whole, when he hung on the cross, my brethren: the commerce of Christ, the passion of Christ; there he bought us, where he was crucified. For there he poured out his blood, our price, there where it was foretold in the psalms as yet to come. See how many years ago it was foretold: *They have dug my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones; but they themselves gazed and considered me, they divided my garments among themselves and cast lots over my clothing*[^26]. All these things can scarcely be distinguished whether they are heard in the psalm or recited from the Gospel. Are they not, just as they are sung in the psalm, so read in the Gospel: *They have dug my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones?* There Christ bought us, where all his bones were numbered; where his hands and his feet were dug with nails, there he bought us. For there he poured out his blood, which is our price. In the psalm itself it is understood what he bought. Do you wish to know? Question the psalm itself. What did Christ buy, hanging on the wood? For after a few verses it says: *All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the Lord, and all the families of the nations shall adore in his sight*[^27]. Why shall they *adore?* *For the kingdom is his, and he will rule the nations*[^28]. As if it were answered why, who is this to whom *all the ends of the earth shall turn and in* whose *sight all the families of the nations shall adore?* *Because the kingdom is his,* he says, *and he will rule the nations.* Why is it *his?* Because he bought it.

**18.** Now the enemy possessor rushes in, and this under the name of Christ. He can divide some garments of Christ; that tunic no one will divide, which is *woven from above.* *They divided,* he says, *my garments among themselves and cast lots over my garment*[^29]. And the evangelist says: *There was there a certain tunic woven from above, and they said among themselves*—those who crucified the Lord—*Let us not divide it, but cast lots over it*[^30]. It was not set in the division, that tunic was apart from the division. Why was that tunic apart from the division? Because it was *woven from above.* It was signified why what was *woven from above* did not deserve to be divided. What is it that is woven from above? Whence it is said to us "hearts up." And so he who has his heart up cannot be divided into parts, because he will belong to that tunic which cannot be divided. Therefore, my brethren, that tunic fell by lot to our Lord Jesus Christ himself, because his lot is his inheritance. And since the inheritance was his, he bought it. But those who were divided can belong to the other garments of Christ, because he is clothed with all. All who believe in him, in whatever way he is clothed with them. But whoever seek earthly honors, temporal advantages, corporeal fantasies, are not woven from above, because they desire worldly things. They, then, can be divided. But that tunic which is *woven from above* cannot come into division. Rejoice that you belong to it, you who are offshoots of the catholic Church. Question your heart, whether you seek from Christ nothing but the kingdom of heaven: not vain things, not temporal things, not corporeal images, not the things that delight in this world and on this earth. When you have questioned yourselves, your conscience answers you that you have your "hearts up." And if you have your "hearts up," you are woven from above; if you are woven from above, you cannot be divided.
