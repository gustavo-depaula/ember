# Sermon 38

**1** There are two things which in this laborious life are commanded us by the Lord: to restrain and to endure. For we are bidden to restrain ourselves from those things which in this world are called goods, and to endure those evils which abound in this world. The former is called restraint, the latter endurance. Two virtues which cleanse the soul and make it capable of divinity. In bridling lusts and curbing pleasures, lest what flatters wickedly seduce us and what is called prosperous enervate us, restraint is needful for us: not to trust earthly happiness, and to seek to the end the happiness that has no end. And as it belongs to restraint not to trust the happiness of the world, so it belongs to endurance not to yield to the unhappiness of the world. Whether, then, we are in abundance of things or in straits, the Lord is to be awaited, who both gives what is truly good and sweet, and turns away from us what is truly evil.

**2** The good things of God, which he promises to the just, are kept for the end; and the evils he threatens to the impious are kept for the end. But the good and evil things that are mixed together in this world neither the good alone have, nor the evil alone. Whatever good of this world you name, the good man has it, and the evil man has it too. For example, bodily health itself both good and evil men have. You will find riches both among the good and among the evil. Success of children we see to be a gift common to both good and evil. A long life—some good men live long, some evil men live long. And whatever other goods you wish to enumerate in this world, you find them mixed among good and evil. Again, whatever harsh, whatever sad things there are, both the good and the evil suffer: hunger, disease, pains, losses, oppressions, bereavements. This is the common material of all tears. It is easy, then, to see this, both that the goods of the world are among the good and the evil, and that good and evil men endure the evils of the world. And therefore the feet of some totter on the way of God, and they try to stray. Many even deviate and stray, when they have set up and proposed in their mind to serve God for this reason, that they may abound in earthly goods and be free from evils and avoid them. For when they have proposed this to themselves, and have set this as the reward of their piety and religion, when they see themselves laboring and the unjust flourishing, then, as if they had lost their reward, as if the one who called them had deceived them, as if the one who imposed the work in vain had cheated them in the reward, they renounce God. And to where do they wretchedly turn, turning away from the one by whom they were made and clinging to the things that were made? When what was made begins to perish, where will the lover of time be, who has lost eternity?

**3** Accordingly, on account of those goods which God will give only to the good, and on account of those evils which God will give only to the evil, because at the end both will appear, God wishes to be believed. For what is the reward of faith, or what even is the very name of faith, if you now want to see what you hold? You ought not, then, to see what you believe, but to believe what you will see; to believe as long as you do not see, lest, when you see, you blush. Therefore we believe while it is the time of faith, before the time of sight comes. For so the Apostle says: *As long as we are in this body, we are away from the Lord. For we walk by faith*[^1]. Therefore by faith, as long as we believe what we do not see. We will hold sight when we see face to face, as he is. The time of faith and the time of sight the apostle John too distinguishes in his epistle, saying: *Beloved, now we are children of God, and it does not yet appear what we shall be*[^2]. This is the time of faith. See the time of sight. We know, he says, that when he appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is.

**4** Laborious is the time of faith. Who denies it? It is laborious, but this is the work whose reward is that. Do not be slothful in the work whose reward you desire. For if you yourself had hired a hired hand, you would not count out the wage before you set him at the work. You would say to him: "Do it, and receive it." He would not say to you: "Give it, and I do it." So, then, also God. You do not cheat your hired hand, fearing God; and does God himself cheat you, who commands you not to cheat your hired hand? And yet you can fail to give what you promised. And if there is no guile of falsehood in your heart, there is at least, in human frailty, a want of difficulty. What do we fear from God, who can neither deceive, because he is truth, and abounds in all things, because he made all things?

**5** Let us, then, believe God, brothers. This is the first precept, this is the beginning of religion and of our way: to have the heart fixed in faith, and, by fixing the heart in faith, to live well, to abstain from seductive goods, to endure temporal evils, and, as long as those flatter and these threaten, to have the heart unshaken against both, lest you flow away in the former, lest you be broken in the latter. Having, then, restraint, having also endurance, when the temporal goods have passed away and the evils that are inflicted will be no more, you will have God who is good, you will have no evil. Therefore in the reading what was said to us? *Son, approaching the service of God, stand in justice and fear, and prepare your soul for temptation. Humble your heart, and endure; that your life may increase in the last things*[^3]—not as now, but in the last things—*that your life may increase in the last things*. How much, do we suppose, does it increase? So as to become eternal. For now human life, as long as it is prolonged and seems to be prolonged, decreases rather than increases. Attend and see, reckon and see that it decreases. A man is born. For example, God appointed him seventy years of life. Life accrues to him, we say, by growing. Does it accrue, or does it diminish? See, of seventy years he has lived sixty, ten remain. What was set has been diminished, and the more he lives, the less remains to him. And so by living here life decreases, not increases. Hold to what God promised you, *that your life may increase in the last things*.

**6** Then there follows what was not read: *Whatever is applied to you, receive, and in grief endure, and in your humiliation have patience. For gold and silver are tested in fire, but acceptable men in the furnace of humiliation*[^4]. It seems hard, you have failed. Have you not lost what does not fail? For why? Many suffer many things for perishable money, and will you not suffer for an abiding life? You so refuse to labor for the promises of God, as though, if you do not labor for the promises of God, you would not labor for your desires. How much do robbers suffer for their iniquity, how much do the lost suffer for their crimes, the luxurious for their wickedness, the merchants for their avarice, crossing the sea, committing body and soul to winds and storms, leaving their own, running to the unknown! If a judge pronounces exile, it is a punishment. Avarice commands exile, and it is a joy. What great thing, then, does wisdom command you, that avarice could not also command? And yet when avarice commands, you do it. And when you have done what avarice commands, what will you have? A house full of gold and silver. Have you not read: *Though man walks in an image, yet he is vainly disturbed. He treasures up, and does not know for whom he gathers it*[^5]? Why, then, did you sing and say to God: *Give ear to my tears*—that is, *perceive my tears with your ears*[^6]? Why do you not perceive with your ears the words of the one by whom you want your tears perceived? If you accuse your avarice, he will invite you to his wisdom. But when you have taken up the yoke of wisdom, will it be laborious? Plainly laborious. But see to what end, with what reward. Do you not know, when you gather by wisdom, for whom you gather it? For yourself you gather. Wake up, be vigilant, have the heart of the ant. It is the season of summer; gather what may profit you for winter. When it is well with you, it is summer. Therefore when it is well with you, then learn whence you may be sustained when it is ill with you. It is well with you, it is summer. Do not be slothful; gather grains from the Lord's storehouse, the words of God from the Church of God; gather and store them within in the heart. For you know that it is well. The time will come when it is ill. To every man tribulation will come. And if all things are serene, surely when he begins to die, he passes through tribulation to another life. Who is there who says: "It will be well with me, and I do not die"?

**7** Yet, if you love life and fear death, the very fear of death is a daily winter. And the fear of death pricks most when it is well with us. For when it is ill, we do not fear death. When it is well with us, then we fear death the more. Therefore that rich man, whom his riches greatly delighted—for he had great riches and great possessions—I believe was interrupted by the fear of death, and amid his delights was wasting away. For he thought he would leave those goods behind. He had gathered, and did not know for whom, and he desired something eternal, and came to the Lord, and said to him: *Good Master, what good shall I do that I may attain eternal life?*[^7] It is well with me, but what I hold slips away. It is well with me, but suddenly what I have will be no more. Tell me whence I may have what will always be; tell me how I may come to what I shall not lose. And the Lord to him: *If you want to come to life, keep the commandments*[^8]. He asked, which commandments? He heard. He answered that from his youth he had kept them all. The Lord, the counselor of eternal life, said to him: *One thing you lack. If you want to be perfect, go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven*[^9]. For I did not say, Lose it, but keep it, *and come, follow me*. That man, who delighted in his riches, and therefore asked the Lord what good he might do to attain eternal life, because he wished to migrate from delights to delights and dreaded to leave these in which he delighted, went away sad to his earthly treasures. He refused to believe the Lord, that he can keep in heaven what is to perish on earth. He refused to be a true lover of his treasure. By holding it badly, he lost it; by loving it too much, he let it go. For if he loved it well, he would migrate to heaven, where he himself would afterward follow. God showed him a home where he might migrate, not a place where he might lose it. For there followed: *For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also*[^10].

**8** But men want to see their riches. Suppose they treasure them on earth—do they not fear that their riches will be seen? And they dug, and buried them, and covered them over. Do they see what they have? He himself does not even see it. He longs for it to lie hidden, he fears lest it be exposed. He wants to be rich in reputation, not in truth. As if it sufficed to have it in conscience, because he kept it on earth! Oh, how much greater and better will your conscience be, if you keep it in heaven! Here, when you bury it on earth, you fear lest your servant know and carry it off and flee; here you fear lest your servant carry it off from you. There you do not fear, because your Lord keeps it well for you. "But I have," you say, "a faithful servant, who both knows and would not betray it and would not take it." Compare him to your Lord. If you have found a faithful servant, when did your Lord deceive you? And even if your servant cannot take it, he can still lose it. Your Lord can neither take it nor lose it, nor does he permit it to perish. He keeps it for you, abides for you, frees you, makes you abide. He will neither lose you, nor lose what you have entrusted to him. "Come," he says, "receive what you placed with me." Far be it. God does not say this. "I," he says to you, "who forbade you to lend at interest, have been lent to by you. For you wanted to increase by interest, and you gave to a man that he might render you more, and when he received it he was glad, and when he repaid it he wept. This you wanted, and I forbade it. For I said: *Who has not given his money at interest*[^11]. I forbade you usury. I command you usury: lend to me." This your Lord says to you: "Do you want to give little and receive more? Leave the man who weeps when you demand. Find me, who rejoice when I repay. See," he says, "I am present. Give and take. At the time of repayment I will repay you. And what shall I repay? You gave little, take more; you gave earthly things, take heavenly; you gave temporal things, take eternal; you gave my things, take me myself. For what did you give, except from what you received from me? Shall I, then, not repay what you have given, I who gave you whence you might give? I who gave you yourself who give? I who gave you Christ to whom you might give, who would say to you: *When you did it to one of the least, you did it to me*?"[^12] See to whom you give. He feeds and hungers for your sake; he gives and is in need. When he gives, you want to receive; when he is in need, you do not want to give. Christ is in need when the poor man is in need. He who is ready to give eternal life to all his own deigned to receive temporally in each poor man.

**9** And he gives counsel whither you may migrate. For he gave counsel whither you ought to migrate. Migrate, then, from earth to heaven, lest you lose it. For how many have lost what they were keeping, and not even so, corrected, have learned to place it in heaven! He, then—if perhaps he said to you, "Migrate your riches from the West to the East if you do not want them to perish"—you would burn, you would labor, you would busy yourself, you would consider how great were the things you had, you would see that, by the multiplicity of your goods, you could not easily migrate to distant places. And perhaps you would weep, because you were forced to go and could not find how you might take with you what you had gathered. He bade you migrate farther, who did not say, "Migrate from West to East," but, "Migrate from earth to heaven." You burn, as though you saw yourself suffering a greater difficulty, and you say to yourself: "If I could not find beasts and ships by which to migrate from West to East, how shall I find ladders by which to migrate from earth to heaven?" "Do not," God says to you, "do not labor. I who made you rich, I who gave you whence to give, have made the poor your porters." If, for example, you found some needy man from beyond the sea, or wherever you wished to go, you found from there some citizen suffering need, you would say to yourself: "This citizen is from where I want to go. He is in need here. I give him what he may repay me there." See, this poor man is in need here. He is a citizen of the kingdom of heaven. Why do you hesitate to make a transfer? For those who do this so give that they may receive more, when they come to those places from which is the one who received. Let us do this too.

**10** This happens, if we believe, if we rouse our faith. For we are vainly disturbed. Why are we vainly disturbed? Because, while Christ slept in the boat, the disciples almost shipwrecked. Jesus was sleeping, and the disciples were disturbed. The winds raged, the waves were stirred up, the boat was sinking. Why? Because Jesus was sleeping. So you too, when the storms of temptations rage in this world, your heart is disturbed, like your boat. Why, except that your faith sleeps? For so the apostle Paul says, that Christ dwells through faith in our hearts. Rouse, then, Christ in your heart; let your faith be awake, let your conscience be calmed, and your boat is freed. Perceive that he who promised is truthful. He has not yet shown it, because it is not yet the time to show it. Yet he has already shown many things. He promised his Christ and gave him; he promised his resurrection and gave it; he promised his Gospel and gave it; he promised his Church to be spread through the whole world and gave it; the very tribulations and heaps of calamities in human affairs he foretold and showed. How many are the things that remain! The things that were promised are being fulfilled, the things that were foretold are being fulfilled. And do you totter, lest what remains not come? You ought to fear then, if you did not see what was foretold. There are wars, there are famines, there are crushings. Kingdom against kingdom, there are earthquakes, there are heaps of calamities, an abundance of scandals, the cooling of love, the abundance of iniquity. Read all these, they were foretold. Read, see that all you see was foretold, and believe that you will see what has not yet come, counting how many things have come. But will you, seeing God show what he foretold, not believe that he will give what he promised? There you ought to believe, where you have begun to be disturbed.

**11** If it is the end of the world, one must migrate from the world, the world must not be loved. See, the world is disturbed, and the world is loved. What if the world were tranquil? How would you cling to it beautiful, you who so embrace it foul? How would you gather its flowers, you who do not draw back your hand from its thorns? You do not want to leave the world; the world leaves you, and you follow the world. Let us, then, cleanse our heart, dearly beloved, and not lose endurance, but receive wisdom and hold to restraint. Labor passes, rest will come; the false delightful things pass, and the good will come which the faithful soul has desired, for which every pilgrim in the world burns and sighs: the good homeland, the heavenly homeland, the homeland with the peoples of the angels, the homeland where no citizen dies, where no enemy is admitted, the homeland where you may have the everlasting God as friend, where you may fear no enemy.
