# Sermon 86: On the Words of the Gospel, Matthew 19:21: "Go, Sell All That You Have, and Give to the Poor," and the Rest

**1. 1.** The Gospel, in the present reading, has admonished us to speak to your Charity about the heavenly treasure. For our God did not, as faithless misers think, wish us to lose our possessions: if it is rightly understood, and faithfully believed, and devoutly received, what is prescribed to us; he did not prescribe to us that we lose them, but he showed the place where we should put them. For no one can but think about his treasure, and follow his riches by a certain journey of the heart. If, then, they are buried in the earth, the heart seeks the depths; but if they are stored up in heaven, the heart will be on high. If, then, Christians want to do what they know they also profess—for not all who hear know this; and would that those who know it have not known it in vain—he, then, who wants to have his heart on high, let him place there, there, what he loves; and set on earth in the flesh, let him dwell with Christ in the heart. And as her head went before the Church, so let a Christian's heart go before him. As the members will go where their head Christ has gone before, so, rising again, a man will go where his heart now has gone before. Let us go, then, from here, by that part by which we can: the whole of us will follow where something of us has gone before. The earthly house is ruinous; the heavenly house is eternal. Where we arrange to come, let us first migrate.

**2. 2.** We have heard of a certain rich man seeking counsel from the good Teacher about obtaining eternal life. Great was what he loved, and cheap was what he refused to despise. And so, hearing with a perverse heart the one he had just called the good Teacher, by his greater love of cheap things he lost the possession of love. Unless he wanted to attain eternal life, he would not have asked counsel about having eternal life. What is it, then, brothers, that he spurned the words, drawn out for him in faithful teaching, of the one he himself had called the good Teacher? Is that Teacher good before he teaches, and bad when he has taught? Before he taught, he was called good. He did not hear what he wanted, but he heard what he ought; he had come desiring, but he went away sad. What if it had been said to him: Lose what you have? when he went away sad because it was said, Keep well what you have. *Go,* he says, *sell all that you have, and give to the poor.* Perhaps you fear lest you lose it? See what follows: *And you will have treasure in heaven*[^1]. You would perhaps have set some little servant as guardian of your treasures; the guardian of your gold will be your God. He who gave it on earth, he keeps it in heaven. Perhaps he would not have hesitated to entrust to Christ what he had; and therefore he was saddened, because it was said to him: *Give to the poor;* as though saying in his heart, If you said, Give to me, I will keep it for you in heaven, I would not hesitate to give to my Lord, the good Teacher; but now you said: *Give to the poor.*

**3. 3.** Let no one fear to give to the poor; let no one think that the one whose hand he sees is the one who receives. He receives who commanded you to give. Nor do we say this from our own heart or by human conjecture: hear him himself both admonishing you and writing you security. *I was hungry,* he says, *and you gave me to eat*[^2]. And when, at the recounting of their services, they answered: *When did we see you hungry?* he answered: *When you did it to one of these least of mine, you did it to me*[^3]. The poor man begs, but the rich man receives: you give to one who consumes, he receives who will repay. Nor is he going to repay only what he receives: he wished to lend at interest, he promises more than you will have given. Bring out now your greed, think of yourself as a moneylender. Surely if you were one, you would be rebuked by the Church, refuted by the word of God, all your brothers would curse you as a cruel moneylender, wanting to acquire from others' tears. Be a moneylender, no one forbids you. You want to give to a poor man, who, when he repays, will lament: give to the suitable one, who even urges you to receive what he promises.

**4. 4.** Give to God, and accost God. Indeed, give to God, and you will have been accosted so as to receive. Surely on earth you sought your debtor; he too sought—but where he might hide himself from your face. You had gone to the judge and said: Order that debtor of mine to be summoned. Hearing this, he departs, and does not even seek to greet you, to whom in his need you had perhaps granted health by lending it. So you have someone to pay out to. Give to Christ; he himself accosts you, of his own accord, that you may receive, and that you may marvel that he has received something from you. For to those set on his right he himself will say, of his own accord: *Come, blessed of my Father.* Where? *Come, take possession of the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.* For what reason? *I was hungry, and you gave me to eat; I was thirsty, and you gave me to drink; I was naked, and you clothed me; a stranger, and you took me in; sick and in prison, and you visited me.* And they: *Lord, when did we see you*[^4]? What is this? The debtor accosts, and the creditors excuse themselves. The faithful debtor does not want them to be cheated. Do you hesitate to receive? I received, and you do not know it? And he answers in what manner he received: *When you did it to one of these least of mine, you did it to me*[^5]. I did not receive through myself: I received through my own. What was given to them came to me: be secure, you have not lost it. You were looking at less suitable ones on earth: you have a suitable one in heaven. I, he says, received; I will repay.

**4. 5.** And what did I receive? What do I repay? *I was hungry,* he says, *and you gave me to eat,* and the rest. I received earth, I will give heaven; I received temporal things, I will restore eternal things; I received bread, I will give life. Indeed, let us even say this: I received bread, I will give bread; I received drink, I will give drink; I received lodging, I will give a home; sick, I was visited, I will give health; I was seen in prison, I will give freedom. The bread you gave to my poor is consumed; the bread that I will give both refreshes and does not fail. Let him give us bread, then, that bread which came down from heaven. When he gives bread, he will give himself.

**5. 5.** For what did you want, when you lent at interest? To give coins and receive coins; but to give fewer, to receive more. I, says God, will exchange for the better all that you have given. For if you gave a pound of silver and received a pound of gold, with how great a joy would you be seized? Look, and ask your greed: I gave a pound of silver, I receive a pound of gold. What likeness between silver and gold? Much more, then, what likeness between earth and heaven? Both the gold and the silver he was going to leave here; but you yourself are not going to remain here forever. And I will give another thing, and I will give more, and I will give better, and I will give eternal. So, then, let our greed be checked, brothers, that another greed, which is holy, may be inflamed. Anything at all that forbids you to do good speaks to you wickedly: you want to serve a bad mistress, not acknowledging the good Lord. And sometimes two mistresses occupy the heart, and they tear the bad servant—worthy to serve such mistresses—in different directions.

**6. 6.** Sometimes two contrary mistresses possess a man, greed and self-indulgence. Greed says: Save. Self-indulgence says: Spend. Under two mistresses commanding different things, demanding different things, what will you do? Both have their addresses to you. And when you begin to be unwilling to obey, and to go into your freedom, then, because they cannot command, they coax. And their coaxings are more to be guarded against than their commands. What does greed say? Save for yourself, save for your sons. If you are in want, no one will give to you. Do not live for the moment: take counsel for yourself for the future. On the contrary, self-indulgence: Live while you live, do well with your soul. You are going to die, and you know not when; you do not know to whom you are going to leave it, or whether he will possess it. You take away and withdraw from your own throat; he, perhaps, when you are dead, will not set a cup over you; or if perhaps he sets a cup, he himself will get drunk, and not a drop will come down to you. So do well with your soul, while you can, when you can. One thing greed commanded: Save for yourself, take counsel for yourself for hereafter. Another thing self-indulgence: Spend, do well with your soul.

**7. 7.** Be wearied, O you who are called into freedom; be wearied of the servitude of such mistresses. Acknowledge your Redeemer, your Liberator. Serve him: he commands easier things, he does not command contrary things. I dare to say still more. Greed and self-indulgence commanded contrary things, so that you could not obey both: and the one said, Save for yourself, and take counsel for hereafter; the other said, Spend, do well with your soul. Let your Lord come forward to say the same things, and your Redeemer will not say contrary things. If you are unwilling, his house has no need of one who serves unwillingly. Attend to your Redeemer, attend to your price. He came to redeem, he shed his blood. He held you dear, whom he had bought so dearly. You acknowledge who bought you; attend to what he redeems you from. I say nothing of the other vices proudly lording it over you—for you were a slave to countless bad masters. These two I name, commanding contrary things, dragging you in different directions: greed and self-indulgence. Tear yourself away from these, come to your God. If you were a slave of iniquity, be a slave of righteousness. The words that they said to you, and commanded contrary things, you hear those very words from your Lord, and he does not command contrary things. He does not take away their words, but he takes away their power. What did greed say to you? Save for yourself, take counsel for hereafter. The word is not changed, the man is changed. Now, if you please, compare the counselors. The one is greed, the other righteousness.

**7. 8.** Sift the contrary counsels themselves. Save for yourself, says greed. Suppose you want to obey: ask where you should save. She will show you a fortified place, a walled chamber, an iron chest. Fortify everything: perhaps a household thief will break even into the inner parts; and while you take counsel for your money, you will fear for your life. Perhaps, while you save much, one who wants to snatch it thinks also of killing. Finally, against thieves you may fortify your treasure and your garment with whatever fortification; fortify them against rust and moth. What will you do? It is not an enemy outside carrying it off, but it is something inside consuming it.

**8. 9.** Greed, then, did not give good counsel. Behold, she ordered you to save, and she found no place to give you for saving. Let her say also what follows: Take counsel for hereafter. For how long a hereafter? For a few and uncertain days. She says, Take counsel for hereafter, to a man who will perhaps not live even until tomorrow. But behold, let him live as long as greed thinks—not as long as she shows, not as long as she teaches, not as long as she trusts; but as long as she thinks: he has lived, grown old, ended; and still the bent old man, leaning on his staff, seeks gain, and hears greed saying, Take counsel for hereafter. For what hereafter? She speaks now to one drawing his last breath. For the sake of your sons, she says. Would that we had even those old men not greedy who have no sons. Even to these, even to such, even to those who excuse their iniquity by no image of devotion, she does not cease to say, Take counsel for hereafter. But perhaps these quickly blush; let us look at those who have sons, whether they are sure their sons will possess what they leave behind. Let them attend, while they are alive, to the sons of others—some losing through others' wickedness what they had, others consuming through their own worthlessness what they had possessed: and the sons of the rich remain destitute. Sparing, tenacious slaves of greed! But my sons, she says, will possess this. It is uncertain. I do not say: It is false; but what you have done is uncertain. Finally, make it certain: what do you want to leave them? What you acquired for yourself. Surely what you acquired had not been left to you, and you have it. If you could have what was not left to you, then they too will be able to have what you do not leave.

**9. 10.** The counsels of greed are refuted; but let the Lord say the same things, let righteousness now speak: the words will be the same, not the meaning. Save for yourself, says your Lord, take counsel for hereafter. And question him too, Where shall I save? *You will have treasure in heaven*[^6]; where a thief will not approach, nor moth corrupt[^7]. For how long a hereafter will you save? *Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world*[^8]. Of how many days this kingdom is, the very end of the sentence shows. When he had said of those on the left: *So will those go into eternal burning;* he said of those on the right: *But the just into eternal life*[^9]. This is to take counsel for hereafter. A hereafter that has no further hereafter. Those days without end are called days, and it is called a day. For someone says: *That I may dwell in the house of the Lord for length of days*[^10], when he was speaking of those days. And it is called a day: *Today I have begotten you*[^11]. And those days are one day; because now there is not even time: that day is neither preceded by yesterday nor shut out by tomorrow. So let us take counsel for that very hereafter: and the words are not different that greed said to you; and greed is overturned.

**9. 11.** It remains for you to say: And what am I to do about my sons? Hear counsel about that too from your Lord. If your Lord says to you, I think better, who created them, than you who begot them: perhaps you will have nothing to say. But you are going to look at that rich man who went away sad and was reproached in the Gospel; and you will perhaps say to yourself: That rich man did wrong, therefore, not to sell everything and give to the poor, because he had no sons; but I have sons; I have those for whom I should save. And in this weakness your Lord stands with you.

**10. 11.** Let me dare to say, through his mercy; let me dare to say something, not out of my own presumption, but out of his compassion. Save for your sons too: but hear me. If, as human things go, anyone loses one of his sons—attend, brothers, attend, because greed has no excuse, neither here nor in the age to come. Behold, these things are human: for we do not wish them, but we behold the examples. Some Christian has been lost, you have lost a Christian son: you have not lost him, then, but sent him ahead. For he has not departed, but gone before. Question your faith: surely you too are going where he has gone before. I say a short thing, to which I think no one will reply. Does your son live? Question your faith. If, then, he lives, why is his portion invaded by his brothers? But you will say: Is he going to return and possess it? Then let it be sent to him where he has gone before: he cannot come to his property, his property can go to him. See with whom he is. If your son were serving in the palace and became a friend of the Emperor, and said to you, Sell my portion there and send it to me: would you find anything to reply? Now your son is with the Emperor of all emperors, and with the King of all kings, and with the Lord of all lords: send to him. I do not say, He himself has need: his Lord, with whom your son is, is in need on earth. Here he wants to receive what he gives in heaven. What some greedy men are wont to do, do as a transfer: give to those abroad, what you may receive in your homeland.

**11. 12.** Finally, now nothing about you; I speak of your son. You hesitate to give your own, you hesitate even to repay another's: surely you are convicted that you were not saving for your sons. Behold, you do not give to your sons, because you will take from your sons. From this one, certainly, you will take. Why is he unworthy to receive, because he lives with a worthier one? Rightly, if the one with whom he lives were unwilling to receive, he is now rich for your house—nay, for the divine house. Far be it, then, that I say to you, Give what you have; but I say to you, Repay what you owe. But his brothers, you say, will have it. O bad teaching, teaching your sons to wish for the death of their brothers! If they will be richer from the property of their dead brother, see how they regard one another in your house. What, then, will you do? You divide the patrimony, and teach acts of parricide?

**11. 13.** But I do not want to speak of one son lost, lest I seem to threaten human misfortunes. Let us speak in a better and more prosperous way somehow. I do not say, You will have one less; reckon that you have one more. Make room for Christ along with your sons; let your Lord be added to your family; let your Creator be added to your offspring; let your Brother be added to the number of your sons. For although there is so great a difference, he deigned even to be a brother. And although he is the Only one to the Father, he wished to have coheirs. Behold how generously he gives! Why do you give so barrenly? You have two sons; reckon him the third: you have three; let a fourth be counted: you have five; let a sixth be named: you have ten; let there be an eleventh. I do not want to say more: keep for your Lord the place of one son. For what you give to your Lord will profit both you and your sons; but what you wrongly save for your sons will harm both you and your sons. But you will give one portion, which you have assigned to one son. Reckon that you have begotten one more.

**12. 14.** What is the great thing, my brothers? I give counsel; do I tie up your throat? As the Apostle says: *I say these things for your benefit, not to cast a snare upon you*[^12]. I think, brothers, that it is a small and easy thought, for the father of children to think that he has one son more, and to procure such estates as you may possess forever, both you and your sons. Greed has nothing to say. You have cried out at these words. Speak against her, let her not conquer you, let her not prevail in your hearts more than your Redeemer. Let her not prevail in your heart more than he who admonishes us to have our hearts on high. So now let us dismiss her.

**12. 15.** What does self-indulgence say? What? Do well with your soul. Behold, the Lord too says: Do well with your soul. What self-indulgence said to you, this righteousness says to you. But see here too how it is said. If you want to do well with your soul, attend to that rich man who, on the counsel of self-indulgence and greed, wanted to do well with his soul. His land yielded a crop, and he had nowhere to store his produce, and he said: *What shall I do? I have nowhere to gather it. I have found what I will do: I will destroy my old barns, and build new ones, and fill them, and say to my soul: You have many good things, be merry*[^13]. Hear the counsel against self-indulgence: *Fool, this night your soul will be taken from you: the things you have prepared, whose will they be*[^14]? And where is this soul going, which will be taken from him? This night it will be taken, and he does not know where it is going.

**13. 16.** See another rich man, self-indulgent and proud. He feasted sumptuously every day, and was clothed in purple and fine linen; and a poor man, covered with sores, lay at his gate, vainly desiring the crumbs from the rich man's table; the dogs fed on his sores, and he was not fed by the rich man. Both died: one of them was buried; what was said of the other? *He was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom*[^15]. The rich man sees the poor man—or rather now the poor man sees the rich man: he desires a drop of water from the finger upon his tongue, from the one who had desired a crumb from his table. Surely the roles are reversed. In vain does the dead rich man say this: not in vain let us hear it who are alive. For he wanted to be called back to those above, and was not permitted; he wanted someone of the dead to be sent to his brothers, nor was this granted him. But what was said to him? *They have there Moses and the Prophets*[^16]. And he: *They will not hear, unless someone goes from the dead.* Abraham said to him: *If they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they believe, even if someone goes from the dead*[^17].

**14. 17.** Concerning the giving of alms, then, and the obtaining of rest for the soul hereafter, that we may do well with our soul—what self-indulgence said perversely, Moses too said, and the Prophets said. Let us hear it while we are alive. For there he desires to hear in vain who despised these things when he was hearing them. Or do we wait for someone to rise even from the dead and tell us to do well with our soul? This too has now been done: your father did not rise, but your Lord rose. Hear him, accept good counsel. Do not spare your treasures, pay out as much as you can. The voice of self-indulgence pleaded; it has become the voice of the Lord. Pay out as much as you can, do well with your soul, lest this night your soul be taken away. You have had, I think, in the name of Christ, a sermon on the giving of alms. That voice of yours, as you praise, is then accepted by the Lord, if he sees also the hands of those who do the work.
