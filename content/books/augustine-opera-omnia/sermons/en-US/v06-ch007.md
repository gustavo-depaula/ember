# Sermon 190: On the Birthday of the Lord

**1. 1.** Our Lord Jesus, who was with the Father before he was born of a mother, chose not only the virgin of whom he would be born, but also the day on which he would be born. Erring men for the most part choose days, one for planting vines, another for building, another for setting out on a journey, and sometimes even another for taking a wife. When a man does this, he does it so that something born from it may be happily nourished. Yet no one can choose the day on which he himself is born. But he was able to choose both, who was able both to create both. Nor did he so choose the day as those choose who vainly hang the fates of men upon the disposition of the stars. For he who was born was not made happy by the day; but he made happy the day on which he himself deigned to be born. For the day of his nativity, too, holds the mystery of his light. For the Apostle says thus: *The night is far gone, the day is at hand: let us cast off the works of darkness, and put on the armor of light; let us walk becomingly as in the day*[^1]. Let us recognize the day, and let us be day. For we were night, when we lived faithlessly. And because that very faithlessness, which had covered the whole world in the manner of night, had to be diminished as faith grew; therefore, on the birthday of our Lord Jesus Christ, the night begins to suffer losses, and the day to receive increases. Let us, then, brothers, keep this solemn day; not like the faithless on account of this sun, but on account of him who made this sun. For what was the Word was made flesh[^2], that for our sake it might be under the sun. In the flesh, indeed, under the sun: but in majesty above the whole world, in which he founded the sun. But now even in the flesh he is above this sun, which those worship as God who, blind in mind, do not see the true Sun of righteousness.

**2. 2.** This day, then, Christians, let us celebrate not as that of his divine nativity, but of his human one, namely that by which he was made proportionate to us; that through the invisible made visible, we might pass from visible things to invisible. For by the catholic faith we ought to hold that there are two nativities of the Lord: one divine, the other human; that one without time, this one in time. But both wonderful: that one without a mother, this one without a father. If we do not comprehend this one, when shall we declare that one? Who could comprehend a newness so new, so unwonted, unique in the world, the incredible made credible, and incredibly believed by the whole world: that a virgin should conceive, a virgin bear, a virgin remain a virgin in bearing? What human reason does not discover, faith grasps: and where human reason fails, faith advances. For who would say that the Word of God, through whom *all things were made*[^3], could not make flesh for himself even without a mother, just as he made the first man without a father and a mother? But because he himself surely created both sexes, that is, of male and female; therefore he willed in being born also to honor both sexes, which he had come to set free. You know, surely, the fall of the first man, that the serpent did not dare to speak to the man, but to cast him down used the ministry of the woman. Through the weaker he overcame the stronger: and he who penetrated through the one triumphed over both. Therefore, that we might not shudder at our death in the woman as by an impulse of righteous grief, and believe it condemned without repair; the Lord, coming to seek what had perished, willed to honor and commend both, because both had perished. In no sex, then, ought we to do injury to the Creator: the Lord's nativity commended both for the hope of salvation. The honor of the male sex is in the flesh of Christ: the honor of the female is in the mother of Christ. The grace of Jesus Christ conquered the cunning of the serpent.

**3. 3.** Let both sexes, then, be reborn in him who was born today, and let them celebrate this present day: the day on which the Lord Christ did not begin to be, but, being always with the Father, brought forth into this light the flesh he received from his mother; bringing his mother fruitfulness, not taking away her integrity. He is conceived, born, an infant. Who is this infant? He is called an infant because he cannot speak, that is, talk. So he is both an infant and the Word. Through the flesh he is silent, through the angels he teaches. The prince and shepherd of shepherds is announced to the shepherds: and in the manger lies the food of the faithful beasts of burden. For it had been foretold through the Prophet: *The ox knows its owner, and the ass its master's manger*[^4]. Therefore he sat upon a little ass, when he entered Jerusalem with the praises of the crowd going before and following after[^5]. And let us, too, recognize him, draw near to the manger, eat the food, carry our Lord and ruler; that with him leading we may come to the heavenly Jerusalem. Weak is Christ's nativity from his mother; but ample is his majesty from the Father. In temporal days he has a temporal day; but he himself is the eternal day from the eternal day.

**3. 4.** Rightly are we kindled by his psalm, as by the voice of a heavenly trumpet, where we hear: *Sing to the Lord a new song; sing to the Lord, all the earth. Sing to the Lord, and bless his name*[^6]. Let us recognize, then, and proclaim the *day from day* who was born in the flesh on this day. The day, the Son from the day, the Father, God from God, light from light. For this is the salvation of which it is said elsewhere: *May God have mercy on us and bless us; may he make his face shine upon us: that we may know your way upon earth, your salvation among all nations*[^7]. What he said, *upon earth*, he repeated, *among all nations*: and what he said, *your way*, he repeated, *your salvation*. We remember that the Lord himself said: *I am the way*[^8]. And just now, when the Gospel was read, we heard that the most blessed old man Simeon had received a divine answer that he would not taste death unless he first saw the Christ of the Lord. When he had received the infant Christ in his hands, and had recognized the great one as a little child: *Now you dismiss*, he said, *your servant, Lord, according to your word, in peace: for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^9]. Well, then, let us proclaim the *day from day, his salvation*. Let us proclaim *his glory among the nations, his wonders among all peoples*[^10]. He lies in a manger, but contains the world: he sucks the breast, but feeds the angels: he is wrapped in swaddling clothes, but clothes us with immortality: he is nursed, but adored: he finds no room in the lodging, but makes himself a temple in the hearts of believers. For that weakness might become strong, strength was made weak. Let us, then, marvel at his fleshly nativity rather than despise it; and there let us recognize the humility of such great loftiness for our sake. From there let us kindle love, that we may come to his eternity.
