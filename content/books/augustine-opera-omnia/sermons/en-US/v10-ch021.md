# Sermon 350/D: On Almsgiving of Spiritual Things

**1.** The fruit of faith is to do good to the needy, because faith is fruitless that so believes in God that the works of mercy are neglected. For just as a barren tree is cultivated in vain, a hard flint rained upon in vain, the dryness of sand plowed in vain, so it profits a man nothing not to deny what is true, who does not wish to provide what is good. Deservedly it is written that faith without works is dead in itself, whence such people are even compared to demons; for to certain ones glorying in faith and idle from good works the apostle James speaks thus: *You believe that God is one, you do well. The demons too believe and tremble*[^1]—so that there seems to be no difference between the fear of the suffering demon and the grace of the faithful man, except that the deeds of the one are evil, but of the other good, since both proceed from the same belief, just as from the same water there sprout both the bristle of thorns and the fruit of grapes.

**2.** Mercy, moreover, is first of the faithful man toward himself; this Scripture commands, saying: *Have mercy on your soul, pleasing God*[^2]. From there, by growing, it is extended toward the neighbor, that the precept may be fulfilled: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^3]. True mercy, then, which is expended on the neighbor, is to be expended for this end, that he too may please God: to this he must be called, to this exhorted, for this instructed and trained. For even those alms themselves which are provided for bodily needs and temporal life are to be done with that mind and intention, that those for whom they are done may love God by whose gift they are done. This the Lord too admonishes, saying: *Let your good works shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^4]. The man of God, then, *a vessel sanctified unto honor, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work*[^5], whatever he does in his life, it is nothing but a work of mercy, whether toward himself or toward his neighbor. And toward himself indeed he is merciful, as we mentioned above, when he pleases God; and he pleases God when, in what he does of good, God is pleasing to him, and in what he suffers of evil, God is not displeasing to him. For the Apostle too, when he had said, speaking of his good works, *I have labored more than all of them*, at once subjoins: *yet not I, but the grace of God with me*[^6]; and Job in his temptation and tribulation said: *As it has pleased the Lord, so has it been done. Blessed be the name of the Lord*[^7]. But toward his neighbor he is merciful, when he gives as much effort as he can, that he too may likewise enjoy the sweetness of pleasing God.

**3.** I had proposed to speak of the works of mercy, whence I may now seem to some to have strayed from my purpose and to have gone off into something else, because I do not say: *Break your bread for the hungry, bring the needy and homeless into your house, if you see one naked, clothe him*[^8], and other things of the kind. For these are thought and called, as it were properly, almsgiving, as though the works of mercy pertained to these alone; which they do indeed pertain to, but so far from being the only ones that these are the least—unless perhaps men are so foolish as to think that those who provided carnal things for the apostles to reap were more merciful than the apostles themselves, who sowed spiritual things. Far be it that anyone think this who hears with understanding the Apostle saying: *If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great matter if we reap your carnal things?*[^9] But in sowing spiritual things, see what sort of dispenser he appears, where he says: *So desiring you, we are pleased to impart to you not only the Gospel of God, but also our own souls*[^10], and in another place: *But I will most gladly*, he says, *spend and be spent for your souls*[^11]. Compare now him who breaks his bread for the hungry, and him who imparts his soul to the believer; compare him who for the temporal life of the needy spends gold, and him who for the eternal life of his brother spends himself: if he is rightly merciful, both called and held to be so, who brings into his house the pilgrim lacking a roof, ministers to him fullness at his table, rest in his bed—how much more merciful is he found who, calling back the one wandering through the ways of iniquity and taking him up, brings him into the house of God, incorporates him into the members of Christ, where he may be rendered, refreshed by the refreshment of justice, secure by the remission of sins!

**4.** But these works of mercy, by which it is brought about that God is pleased, are so far set, by the truthful law of wisdom, above those works by which the necessary support is supplied to carnal need, that very often, when the former are done the more prudently, the latter are the more mercifully withdrawn. For first, a man merciful toward himself, mindful of the divine precept which says, *Have mercy on your soul, pleasing God*[^12], that he may please God often fasts; and, although he is commanded to love his neighbor as himself, he provides bread for his hungry neighbor, denies it to himself, namely chastising his body and reducing it to servitude, lest perhaps, preaching to others, he himself be found a reprobate[^13]. But perhaps to do this in himself is a matter of mercy, but in another of cruelty? What, then? When food is often not provided by parents to hungry little ones, either for reasons of health or for the severity of discipline or for the humiliation of some great fast to be observed by all, are not the parents even then the more merciful toward their children?—unless perhaps the Ninevites, when, believing God and turning to God, they not only feared him threatening but also hoped him merciful, and observed the fast appointed even by the king's command with such great humility that they restrained even the cattle from pasture and drink[^14], were cruel toward their little ones, whom that universal decree included, which had not passed over even the dumb animals? To how many surely, who through their age did not know what was being done and demanded food with weeping, was it denied, so that even from this the greater grief might more pressingly humble the afflicted minds of the elders, and, pouring out to the Lord the groan and prayer wrung out by this pressure, they might obtain salvation not only for themselves who willingly fasted, but also for their little ones whom they compelled! But this was not only no cruelty of fathers or mothers toward their children, but even great mercy; which mercy, however, did not break bread for the hungry, but withdrew it even from the weeping. Finally, what is more merciful than the Lord God himself, *who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^15], who yet not only often corrects sinners with the scourge of famine, but also exercises the patience of the just by such a test of probation? Or was he not merciful toward the apostle Paul, when he strengthened him with fatherly chastisement as he campaigned in hunger and thirst and cold and nakedness, or did he therefore not grow angry with the people of Israel because, when they had desired the care of the flesh, he heaped their camp with birds[^16]? These temporal and earthly goods, then, are often given for the punishment of those to whom they are given, and often withheld for their benefit; often God takes them away when favorable and gives them when angry, and again often gives them when favorable and takes them away when angry, according as the most wise judgment of the one governing holds. Accordingly, the Apostle came to visit the people of God not only in the gentleness of a spirit of meekness, but also mercifully with a rod[^17]; not only, moved by mercy, did he order the spirit of divination to come out from a certain woman[^18], but he also mercifully handed certain ones over to Satan himself, that they might learn not to blaspheme[^19]; because to the Apostle himself too the Lord mercifully provided not only food and covering[^20], by which the weakness of the flesh might be sustained under the strength of the mind, but also mercifully gave the angel of Satan to buffet him, that the strength of the mind might be perfected in the weakness of the flesh[^21].

**5.** Food, therefore, and drink and clothing and the whole bodily health of this mortal body are, by mercy, withdrawn at the opportune time; but that mercy by which it is brought about that we please God ought to be perpetual, continual and unbroken, varied at no time by any alternation. For just as it can be said, "Sometimes it is expedient to take food, sometimes it is not expedient," so it cannot also be said, "Sometimes it is expedient to please God, sometimes it is not expedient," nor, just as it is said, "Now break bread for the hungry because this profits him, now compel him to fast because this is wholesome for him," can it also rightly be said, "Now act with this one so that he may please God, for this is expedient for him, now so that he may displease him, because this too is sometimes expedient." Most absurd certainly and altogether to be detested is an error of this kind, and it is not to be believed that any man, however sacrilegious, could be entangled in it! That mercy, then, by which works are expended that we may please God, is itself in a certain way the cardinal one: the other things which are mercifully shown are rightly done, if they never depart from the contemplation of this.
