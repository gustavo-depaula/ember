# Sermon 43

**1** The beginning of the good life, to which even eternal life is owed, is right faith. Now faith is to believe what you do not yet see, the reward of which faith is to see what you believe. Therefore in the time of faith, as in the time of sowing, let us not fail, and let us not fail even to the end, but persevere, until we reap what we have sown. For when the human race had turned away from God and lay in its offenses, just as we needed a Creator that we might exist, so we needed a Savior that we might come to life again. The just God condemned man, the merciful God frees man. *The God of Israel, he will give power and strength to his people. Blessed be God*[^1]. But believers receive, despisers do not receive.

**2** Yet not even of faith itself ought we so to boast, as though we could do anything ourselves. For faith is not nothing, but something great; and if you have it, surely you received it. *For what do you have that you did not receive?*[^2] See, dearly beloved, whence you may give thanks to the Lord God, lest in any gift of his you remain ungrateful, and on account of being ungrateful you lose what you received. The praise of faith can in no way be unfolded by me, but it can be thought of by the faithful. Moreover, if in some part it be thought of as is worthy, who would worthily think to how many gifts of God it is itself to be preferred? And if we ought to acknowledge the lesser gifts of God in us, how much more ought we to acknowledge that which surpasses them?

**3** From God we ought to have what we are. Because we are not nothing, from whom do we have it but from God? But there are also logs, there are also stones—from whom but from God? We, then, what more? Logs and stones do not live; but we live. But still this very living is common to us with trees and shrubs. For vines too are said to live. For if they did not live, it would not be written: *He killed their vines with hail*[^3]. It lives when it is green; it withers when it dies. But this life has no sense-perception. What more have we? We perceive. The fivefold sense of the body is known. We see, we hear, we smell, we taste, and by touch too, through our whole body, we distinguish soft and hard, rough and smooth, hot and cold. There is, then, in us a fivefold sense. But beasts too have this. We have, then, something more. And yet these things which we have enumerated, my brothers, if we consider them in ourselves, how great a thanksgiving, how great a praise do we owe the Creator for them? But still, what more have we? Mind, reason, counsel, which beasts do not have, birds do not have, fish do not have. In this we were made to the image of God. Finally, where Scripture narrates that we were made, there it adds, that it may not only set us before the cattle but also set us over them—that is, that they may be subject to us: *Let us make*, it says, *man to our image and likeness, and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of the air and all the cattle and the serpents that creep upon the earth*[^4]. Whence does he have power? On account of the image of God. Whence to certain ones it is said, by way of rebuke: *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^5]. But understanding is one thing, reason another. For we have reason even before we understand, but we cannot understand unless we have reason. There is, then, an animal capable of reason—or, to say it better and more quickly, a rational animal, in which reason is by nature, and before it understands it already has reason. For it therefore wants to understand, because it goes before by reason.

**4** This, then, by which we go before the beasts, we ought above all to cultivate in ourselves and, in a way, to recarve and reform. But who will be able, unless it be the Craftsman who formed it? We were able to deform the image of God in us, we cannot reform it. We have, then—to weave it all together briefly—the very being itself with logs and stones, living with trees, perceiving with beasts, understanding with angels. We distinguish, then, colors with the eyes, sounds with the ears, odors with the nostrils, flavors with the taste, heat with touch, morals with the understanding. Every man wants to understand; there is no one who does not want to understand; not all want to believe. A man says to me: "Let me understand that I may believe." I answer: "Believe that you may understand." Since, then, such a controversy in a way has arisen between us, that he says to me, "Let me understand that I may believe," and I answer him, Rather believe that you may understand—since with this controversy we come to a judge, let neither of us presume sentence in his own favor. What judge shall we find? Having examined all men, I do not know whether we can find a better judge than a man through whom God speaks. Let us not, then, in this matter and this controversy, go to secular letters; let not a poet judge between us, but a prophet.

**5** The blessed apostle Peter, set on the mountain with two other disciples of Christ the Lord, James and John, with the Lord himself, heard a voice brought down from heaven: *This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Hear him*[^6]. Commending this, the said Apostle said in his epistle: *This voice we heard brought down from heaven when we were with him on the holy mountain*[^7]. And when he had said, *This voice we heard brought down from heaven*, he added and said: *And we have a more sure prophetic word*[^8]. That voice sounded from heaven, and the prophetic word is more sure. Attend, dearly beloved; may the Lord help both my will and your expectation, that I may say what I want, as I want. For who of us would not marvel that, the voice having been brought down from heaven, the Apostle said the prophetic word was more sure? He indeed said more sure, more sure, not better, not truer. For that word from heaven was as true as the prophetic word, as good, as useful. What is it, then, "more sure," except that in it the hearer is more confirmed? Why this? Because there are unbelieving men who so disparage Christ that they say he did what he did by magic arts. The unbelievers, then, could refer even that voice brought down from heaven, by human conjectures and unlawful curiosities, to magic arts. But the Prophets were before—I do not say before that voice, but before the flesh of Christ. Christ was not yet a man, when he sent the Prophets. Whoever says he was great—if, then, he did by magic arts that he should be worshipped even when dead, was he a magician before he was born? See why the apostle Peter said: *We have a more sure prophetic word*. The voice from heaven, by which the faithful are admonished; the prophetic word, by which the unbelievers are convicted. We have understood, as far as it seems to me, dearly beloved, why the apostle Peter said: *We have a more sure prophetic word*, after the voice brought down from heaven.

**6** And how great is the very condescension of Christ? This Peter who speaks thus was a fisherman, and now an orator has great praise, if he can be understood by the fisherman. Therefore, speaking to the first Christians, the apostle Paul says: *See your calling, brothers, that not many are wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many noble. But God chose the weak things of the world to confound the strong, and God chose the foolish things of the world to confound the wise, and the ignoble and contemptible things of the world God chose, and the things that are not, as though they were, that the things that are might be brought to nothing*[^9]. For if Christ first chose an orator, the orator would say: "I was chosen by the merit of my eloquence." If he chose a senator, the senator would say: "I was chosen by the merit of my dignity." Last of all, if he first chose an emperor, the emperor would say: "I was chosen by the merit of my power." Let these be quiet and deferred a little, let them be quiet—not omitted, not despised, but a little deferred, wherein they can glory of themselves in themselves. "Give me," he says, "that fisherman, give me the unschooled, give me the unskilled, give me him with whom a senator does not deign to speak, not even when he buys a fish. Give me him," he says. "If I fill him, it will be plain that I do it. Although I am going to make both a senator and an orator and an emperor: at some time I am going to make a senator too, but more surely a fisherman. A senator can glory of himself, an orator can, an emperor can. The fisherman can glory only of Christ. Let him come for the sake of teaching salutary humility. First let the fisherman come. Through him the emperor is better led."

**7** Remember, then, the holy fisherman, just, good, full of Christ, to whose nets cast through the world, to be caught with the rest, even this people of yours belonged. Therefore remember that he said: *We have a more sure prophetic word*[^10]. Give me, then, for that controversy a Prophet as judge. What was at issue between us? You said: "Let me understand that I may believe." I said: "Believe that you may understand." A controversy arose, let us come to a judge, let a Prophet judge—rather, let God judge through the Prophet. Let us both be silent. What we both said has been heard. "Let me understand," you say, "that I may believe." "Believe," I say, "that you may understand." Let the Prophet answer: *Unless you believe, you will not understand*[^11].

**8** But do you think, dearly beloved, that even the one who says, "Let me understand that I may believe," says nothing? For what do we now do, except that those may believe—not those who do not believe, but those who as yet believe too little? For if they had in no way believed, they would not be here. Faith brought them to hear. Faith made them present to the word of God, but that very faith which has sprouted must be watered, must be nourished, must be strengthened. This is what we do. *I*, he says, *planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. And so neither is he who plants anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^12]. By speaking, exhorting, teaching, persuading, we can plant and water, but not give the increase. But that man with whom he was speaking knew this—who, for his faith sprouting and still tender and still weak and in great part tottering, yet not no faith but some faith, prayed for a helper—the man to whom he said: *I believe, Lord*[^13].

**9** Just now, when the Gospel was read, you heard: *If you can believe*, the Lord Jesus said to the boy's father, *if you can believe, all things are possible to one who believes*[^14]. And he, looking at himself, and set before himself, not having rash confidence but first examining his conscience, saw that there was in him some faith, saw also a tottering. He saw both. He confessed that he had the one, and for the other he asked help. *I believe*, he said, *Lord*[^15]. What followed, except "help my faith"? He did not say this: I believe, *Lord*. I see here something whence I do not lie. I believe, I speak truly. But I see here also something or other that displeases me. I want to stand, but still I sway. Standing I speak, I have not fallen, because I believe. But still I sway: *help my unbelief*[^16]. Therefore, dearly beloved, even that man whom I set against myself, and on account of whose controversy arising between us I asked for a Prophet as judge, says something himself too, when he says, "Let me understand that I may believe." For surely now what I speak, I speak for this, that those who do not yet believe may believe. And yet, unless they understand what I speak, they cannot believe. Therefore in some part it is true what he says, "Let me understand that I may believe," and I who say, as the Prophet says, "Rather believe that you may understand"—we both speak truly; let us agree. Therefore understand that you may believe, believe that you may understand. Briefly I say how we may take both without controversy. Understand, that you may believe, my word; believe, that you may understand, the word of God.
