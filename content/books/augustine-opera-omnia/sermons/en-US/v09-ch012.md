# Sermon 311: On the Feast of Cyprian the Martyr

**1. 1.** The passion of the most blessed Cyprian the martyr has made this a festive day for us: the celebrity of whose victory has gathered us, most devoted, in this place. But the celebration of the martyrs' solemnity ought to be the imitation of their virtues. It is easy to celebrate the honor of a martyr: it is a great thing to imitate the faith and patience of a martyr. Let us so do this that we desire that: let us so celebrate this that we rather love that. What do we praise in the martyr's faith? That he contended even to death for the truth, and therefore conquered. He despised the world flattering, he did not yield to it raging: therefore he went a victor to God. Errors and terrors abound in this world: the most blessed Martyr overcame errors by wisdom, terrors by patience. Great is what he did: following the lamb, he conquered the lion. When the persecutor raged, the lion roared: but because the lamb was looked to above, the lion was trodden below: he who by death destroyed death, hung on the wood, shed his blood, redeemed the world.

**2. 2.** First the blessed Apostles, rams of the holy flock, saw the Lord Jesus himself hanging, grieved at him dying, were terrified at him rising, loved him in his power, and they themselves shed their blood for what they saw. Consider, brothers, what it was to be sent through the whole world, to preach that a dead man had risen, had ascended into heaven; and for that preaching to suffer all that the raging world would inflict—losses, exiles, chains, torments, flames, beasts, crosses, deaths. This for whom? For surely, my brothers, was Peter dying for his own glory, or preaching himself? One was dying, that another might be honored; one was being killed, that another might be worshiped. Would he do this, except from the ardor of love, out of awareness of the truth? They had seen what they were saying: for when would they die for a thing they had not seen? What they had seen, they ought to have denied. They did not deny it: they preached as dead the one they knew to be alive. They knew for what life they despised life: they knew for what happiness they bore passing unhappiness, for what rewards they despised these losses. Their faith would not be weighed against the whole world. They had heard: *What does it profit a man, if he gains the whole world, but suffers the loss of his soul?*[^1] The enticement of the world did not retard them as they hastened, as they departed from things soon to pass—happiness, however and in whatever way shining, to be left here, not to be carried over to another life, sometime to be left here even by the living.

**3. 3.** Despise the world, then, Christians; despise the world, despise it. The martyrs despised it, the apostles despised it, blessed Cyprian despised it, whose memory we celebrate today. You want to be rich, you want to be honored, you want to be healthy: he despised all this, at whose memorial you have gathered. What, I beg you, do you love so much, which the one you so honor despised? whom, if he had not despised these things, you certainly would not so honor. Why do I find you a lover of those things whose despiser you venerate? Surely, if he had loved these, you would not venerate him. So do not love them either: for he entered, and closed the door against you. Despise them too; and enter after him. The way you may enter lies open: Christ is the gate. And the door is open to you, when his side was pierced by the lance. Recall what flowed from there; and choose by what you may be able to enter. From the side of the Lord, hanging and dying on the wood, after it was pierced by the lance, water and blood flowed forth. In the one is your cleansing, in the other your redemption.

**4. 4.** Love, and do not love: love unto something, and unto something do not love. For there is what may be loved unto progress, and there is what may be loved unto hindrance. Do not love the hindrance, if you do not want to find torment. What you love on earth is a hindrance: it is birdlime to spiritual wings, that is, to the virtues by which one flies to God. You do not want to be caught, and you love the birdlime? Are you not caught just because you are sweetly caught? The more it delights, the more strongly it strangles. I say these things: and you praise, and you cry out, and you love. There answers you—not I, but wisdom: I want conduct, not voices. Praise wisdom by living; not by sounding, but by sounding in harmony.

**5. 5.** The Lord says in the Gospel: *We sang to you, and you did not dance*[^2]. When would I say this, if I did not read it? Vanity mocks me, but authority aids me. If I had not said first who said this, who of you could bear me saying: *We sang to you, and you did not dance?* Surely in this place, even if a psalm is to be sung, is anyone to dance? Once, not very many years ago, the wantonness of dancers had even invaded this place. This so holy a place, where lies the body of so holy a Martyr, as many remember who are of an age; this place, I say, so holy, the pestilence and wantonness of dancers had invaded. Through the whole night wicked things were sung here, and to those singing there was dancing. When the Lord willed, through our holy brother your bishop—from the time the holy vigils began to be celebrated here—that pestilence, struggling a little, afterward yielded to diligence, blushed at wisdom.

**6. 6.** Since, then, by God's favor these things are not done here now, because we do not celebrate games for demons, where such things are usually done for the delight of those who are worshiped, and by their uncleanness they usually deprave their worshipers, but here the sanctity and solemnity of the martyrs is celebrated; here there is no dancing, and where there is no dancing, yet from the Gospel it is read: *We sang to you, and you did not dance*[^3]. They are rebuked, scolded, accused, who did not dance. Far be it that that wantonness should return: hear rather what wisdom wants to be understood. He sings, who gives the command; he dances, who does it. What is it to dance, but to be in harmony with the song by the motion of the limbs? What is our song? I will not bring it forth myself, let it not be mine. I am better a minister than an author. I tell our song: *Do not love the world, nor the things that are in the world. Whoever loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him: for all that is in the world is the desire of the flesh, and the desire of the eyes, and the ambition of the age, which is not from the Father, but is from the world. And the world passes away, and its desire: but whoever does the will of God abides forever, just as God also abides forever*[^4].

**7. 7.** What a song, my brothers! You have heard the singer, let us hear the dancers: do you with the harmony of your conduct what the dancers do with the motion of their limbs. Do this within: let your conduct be in harmony. Let desire be uprooted, let love be planted. Whatever comes from that tree is good. Desire can beget nothing good: love nothing evil. And it is said, and it is praised; and no one is changed. Far be it, it is not true what I have said. The fishermen were changed, afterward very many senators too were changed: Cyprian was changed, whose memory we keep today. He himself writes, he himself testifies, of what kind his life once was, how wicked, how impious, how worthy of disapproval and detestation. He heard the singer: he showed himself dancing, not in body, but in mind. He fitted himself to the good song, he fitted himself to the new song: he fitted himself, he loved, he persevered, he contended, he overcame.

**8. 8.** And you say: The times are troublesome, the times are grievous, the times are wretched. Live well, and you change the times by living well: you change the times, and you have nothing to murmur about. For what are times, my brothers? The spaces and rollings-on of the ages. The sun has risen, and after twelve hours have passed it sets in another part of the world; another day in the morning it rises, again it sets; count how often: these are the times. Whom has the rising of the sun harmed? whom has the setting of the sun harmed? So time has harmed no one. Those who are harmed are men; those by whom they are harmed are men. O great grief! men are harmed, men are despoiled, men are oppressed. By whom? not by lions, not by serpents, not by scorpions; but by men. Those who are harmed grieve. If they could, would they not do the very thing they blame? Then we find the man who was murmuring, when he could do the thing he was murmuring about. I praise, I praise, if he did not do what he was accusing.

**9. 9.** But those, dearly beloved, who seem powerful in the world, how are they praised when they do less than they can? Scripture itself praised such a one: *Who could transgress, and did not transgress; who did not go after gold*[^5]. Gold ought to go after you, not you after gold. For gold is good. For God created nothing evil. Do not you be evil; and gold is good. Behold, I set gold between a good man and a bad one. Let the bad man take it; the needy are oppressed; judges are corrupted, laws are perverted, human affairs are thrown into confusion. Why this? Because a bad man took the gold. Let the good man take it; the poor are fed, the naked are clothed, the oppressed are freed, captives are redeemed. How many goods come from the gold which a good man has? how many evils from the gold which a bad man has? Why then do you sometimes say in irritation: Oh, if only there were no gold at all? Do not you love gold. If you are evil, you go after gold: if you are good, it goes after you. What is, it goes after you? You lead, you are not led: because you possess, you are not possessed.

**10. 10.** So let us return to the words of holy Scripture. *Who did not go after gold. Who could transgress, and did not transgress. Who is this, and we will praise him?*[^6] Who is this, or who is this? How many hear: and who is this? And yet far be it that I should despair of there being someone here—indeed not someone, but several. Far be it that I should despair of the threshing-floor of so great a householder. He who sees the threshing-floor from afar thinks it only chaff: but he who knows how to look closely finds the grain. Where the chaff offends you, there a heap of grain lies hidden. Where what is pounded in the threshing offends you, there is what the threshing purges: there it is, be certain; there it is. In short, he is certain who sowed, who reaped, who gathered to the floor: he knows there is something there to fill the granary, when it has been winnowed. There was some small winnowing in the time of persecution: what grain came forth from it? From it bloomed the Massa Candida of Utica: from it came so great and choice a grain, this most blessed Cyprian. How many rich men then despised what they had? How many poor men then failed in the trial? Behold, in that trial, as in a winnowing, it did not harm the rich to have gold; what did it profit the poor not to have gold? Those conquered, those failed.

**11. 11.** Nothing makes good conduct but good loves. Let gold be taken away from human affairs: rather, let gold be present, to test human affairs. Let the human tongue be cut off because of the blasphemers of God: and then from where will come the praisers of God? What has the tongue done to you? Let there be one who sings well, and the instrument is good. Give a good mind to the tongue: good things are said, the discordant are reconciled, the mourning are consoled, the dissolute are rebuked, the angry are restrained; God is praised, Christ is commended, the mind is inflamed to love; but divine, not human; spiritual, not carnal. These good things the tongue does. Why? Because the mind that uses the tongue is good. Give a bad man to the tongue: there will be blasphemers, quarrelers, slanderers, informers. All evils from the tongue, because he who uses the tongue is bad.

**12. 11.** Let not things be taken away from human affairs: let there be things, and let there be the use of good things. For there are some goods which exist only in good men, and there are other goods which are common to good and bad. Goods which exist only in good men: godliness, faith, justice, chastity, prudence, modesty, love, and the like. Goods which are common to good and bad: money, honor, the power of this world, office, the very health of the body. And these are goods, but they seek good men.

**13. 12.** Now here that murmurer, who always looks for something to criticize—and this in God; who, would that he would return to himself, see himself, criticize himself, correct himself—that critic and arguer, then, will soon object to me concerning God: And why does God, who governs all things, give these goods to the bad? He would give them only to the good. Do you expect to hear from me the counsel of God? Who, from whom, and what? Yet according to my measure, as much as I grasp, as much as he deigns to grant, I declare to you what perhaps will not suffice for you, but there is someone here for whom it will suffice. So let me sing: for truly in so great a multitude there cannot be lacking to me one who will dance. Behold, hear, you wise man, but in reverse: hear. That God gives these goods even to the bad, if you are willing to understand, is your instruction, not God's perversity. Still I know you have not understood what I said: hear, then, what I was saying—he to whom I was saying it, who criticize God and accuse God, because he gives these earthly and temporal goods even to bad men, which by your sense you think he ought to give only to the good. For from this it is that a deadly impiety has crept upon some, so that they altogether believe God does not regard human affairs. For they say and dispute: Surely, if God regarded human affairs, would that man have riches, would that man have honors, would that man have power? God does not care for human affairs: for if he cared, he would give these things only to the good.

**14. 13.** Return to your heart, and from there to God. For from what is near you return to God, if you return to your heart. For when these things offend you, you have gone out even from yourself: you have become an exile from your own breast. You are moved by things that are outside you, and you lose yourself. You are within, these things lie outside; outside they are good, but they are outside. Gold, silver, all money, clothing, retinues, households, herds, honors, are outside. If these lowest goods, earthly goods, temporal goods, passing goods, were not given to the bad too, they would be believed great by the good. Therefore God, who gives these goods to the bad, teaches you to desire better things. Behold I say, by this moderation of human affairs God your Father in a certain way addresses you: and like a foolish child he teaches you with these words, which, as I can, I bring forth to you, the more confidently the more he deigns to remain in me. Imagine that God, who renewed you and adopted you, says to you: O son, what is it that daily you rise, and pray, and bend the knee, and strike the earth with your forehead, and sometimes even weep, and say to me: My Father, my God, give me riches? If I gave them to you, you think you have obtained something good, and great.

**15. 13.** Because you asked, you received: behold, make good use of it. Before you had it, you were humble: you began to have riches, and you despised the poor. What kind of good is it, by which you have been made worse? You were made worse, because you were bad: and you did not know what could make you worse; therefore you asked these things of me. I gave, and I tested: you found out, and you were found out. You lay hidden when you did not have. Correct yourself: vomit out greed, drink in love. What great thing is it that you ask of me, your God says to you? Do you not see to whom I have given these things? do you not see to what sort of men I have given them? If it were a great good that you ask of me, would a robber have it? would a faithless man have it? would my blasphemer have it? would a disreputable actor have it? would a shameless harlot have it? Would all these have gold, if gold were a great good?

**16. 13.** But you say to me: Is gold then not good? Indeed gold is good. But bad men do bad things with good gold: good men do good things with good gold. Because, then, you see to whom I have given these things; ask better things of me, ask greater things of me; ask spiritual things of me, ask me myself of me.

**17. 14.** But evil things, you say, happen in the world, harsh, unclean, hateful. It is foul, let it not be loved. Behold, it is such, and so it is loved. The house is in ruins, and one is loath to move. Mothers or nurses, lest infants suck too long, when they have seen them growing up, and that it is no longer fitting to be nourished with milk—and yet the infants gape troublesomely for the breast—smear their nipples with some bitterness, by which the little one, offended, no longer seeks the milk. Why, then, is it still sucked so delightedly, if the world has been made bitter to you? God has filled the world with bitterness; and you gape, you lean upon it, you suck; you take pleasure only from this and that. How long? What if it were sweet? how it would be loved! Do these things offend you? choose another life. Love God, despise these things. Look down on human affairs, you who at some time will go from here: for you will not be here forever. And yet, bad as the world is, bitter as the world is, full of calamities as the world is, if it were said to you by God that you would be here forever, joy would not contain you, you would exult, you would give thanks. From what? Because you would not end your misery. That very thing is the greater unhappiness, which forces itself to be loved. It would be less, if it were not loved: it is the worse, the more it is loved.

**18. 15.** There is another life, my brothers: there is after this life another life, believe it. Prepare yourselves for it: despise all present things. If you have them, do good from them: if you do not have them, do not burn with desire. Move, transfer ahead of you: let what you have here go there where you are to follow. Hear the counsel of your Lord: *Do not store up for yourselves treasure on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves dig through and steal; but store up for yourselves treasure in heaven, where no thief comes near, where no moth corrupts. For where your treasure is, there is your heart also*[^7]. You hear daily, faithful man, *Lift up your heart*: and as if you heard the contrary, you sink your heart into the earth. Move. Do you have the means? do good. You do not have the means? do not murmur against God. Hear me, O poor: What do you not have, if you have God? Hear me, O rich: What do you have, if you do not have God?
