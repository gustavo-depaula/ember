# Sermon 208: In Lent

**1.** The solemn time has come, on which we should admonish and exhort your Charity in the Lord: although the time itself, even if we are silent, sufficiently admonishes and exhorts you to be fervent, more urgently and more eagerly than usual, in fasts and prayers and alms. But the ministry of our sermon is added, that, by the trumpet of this voice too, your spirit, about to do battle against the flesh, may receive its strength. Let your fasts, then, be without quarrels, shouts, blows: so that even those who are under your yoke may feel a careful and kindly remission; that harsh severity may be reined in, not that wholesome discipline may be loosened. But when, by some kind of foods even permitted and lawful, for the sake of chastising the body, you abstain, remember that *all things are clean to the clean*[^1]: lest you think anything unclean, except what unbelief has defiled. *For to the unclean and unbelieving*, says the Apostle, *nothing is clean*[^2]. But plainly, when the bodies of the faithful are subjected to servitude, whatever is lessened of bodily pleasure profits spiritual salvation. And therefore care must be taken lest you seek precious foods, either some in place of others, or even more precious ones without the flesh of animals. For when the body is chastised and subjected to servitude[^3], delights are to be restricted, not changed. For what does it matter in what kind of food immoderate desire is blamed? For not only of meats, but also of certain fruits and crops, was the desire of the Israelites condemned by the divine voice[^4]. And Esau lost his birthright not on account of pork, but on account of a lentil dish[^5]. To say nothing of what the Lord, hungering, answered the tempter even about bread itself[^6]: who surely was not taming his own flesh as though rebellious, but mercifully admonishing what we ought to answer in such temptations. Wherefore, dearest ones, from whatever foods it has pleased you to abstain, remember to preserve your purpose with pious temperance, not to condemn the creature of God by a sacrilegious error. Whoever of you too are bound to spouses, now especially do not despise the apostolic admonitions, to abstain from one another for a time, that you may give yourselves to prayers[^7]. For what is usefully done on other days too, it is exceedingly shameless if it is not done now. I judge it ought not to be burdensome for the married, by a yearly observance on the solemn days, to do what widows have professed for a part of their life, what holy virgins have undertaken for their whole life.

**2.** But now to increase alms on these days is in a certain manner a matter of debt. For where do you more justly bestow, by showing mercy, what you take from yourselves by abstaining? And what more unjust than that what abstinence bestows less, remaining covetousness should keep, or deferred luxury consume? Attend, then, to whom you should owe what you deny yourselves; so that what temperance withdraws from pleasure, mercy may add to love. What shall I now say of that work of mercy, where nothing is spent from the storehouses, nothing from the purse, but it is forgiven from the heart; which, if it remains rather than withdraws, begins to be ruinous? I mean anger kept in the heart against someone. But what is more foolish than to avoid an enemy outside, and to keep within the inmost breast one far worse? Whence the Apostle says: *Let not the sun go down upon your anger*; and soon subjoins: *Neither give place to the devil*[^8]. As though he does this, who does not quickly drive anger from the mind, that through it, as through a door, he gives the devil an approach. First, then, it must be brought about that this sun does not go down upon anger; lest the sun of righteousness desert the mind itself. But in whosoever's breast it has so far remained, let at least now the day, drawing near, of the Lord's passion drive it out — he who was not angry with his slayers, for whom, hanging on the wood, he both poured forth a prayer and his blood[^9]. In whosoever's breast among you, then, it has lasted with most shameless brow up to these holy days, now let anger at least depart from there, that prayer may proceed secure: lest it offend, or palpitate, or, under the goads of conscience, fall silent, when it comes to that place where it must say: *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*[^10]. You are about to ask something, that it may not be repaid you, and something, that it may be bestowed. *Forgive*, then, *and it will be forgiven you: give, and it will be given you*[^11]. These things, brothers, even if I do not admonish you, you ought to care for by perpetual meditation. But since our voice, the minister of so many divine testimonies, is aided also by the celebrity of this present day; I ought not to fear lest any of you perhaps despise me, or rather the Lord of all in me: but rather to hope that his flock, recognizing that what is said is his, may, effectively hearing, hear him.
