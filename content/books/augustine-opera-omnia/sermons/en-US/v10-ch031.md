# Sermon 356

**1.** A sermon must be rendered to your love about ourselves. For as the Apostle says, *we have been made a spectacle to the world and to angels and to men*[^1]; those who love us seek what to praise in us; but those who hate us detract from us. But we, placed in the middle of both, with the help of the Lord our God, ought so to guard both our life and our reputation that our praisers may not blush before our detractors. But how we wish to live, and how, by God's favor, we already live, although many of you know from the holy Scripture, yet to remind you, the reading itself from the book of the Acts of the Apostles will be recited to you, that you may see where the form is described which we desire to fulfill. While, then, it is recited, I wish you most attentive, so that after its recitation, what I have set out to say I may speak, the Lord granting, to your attention. And Lazarus the deacon read: *When they had prayed, the place was moved in which they were gathered, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and they spoke the word of God with confidence to all willing to believe. But of the multitude of believers there was one soul and one heart: and no one said that anything of what he possessed was his own, but all things were common to them. And with great power the Apostles gave testimony of the resurrection of the Lord Jesus: and great grace was upon them all. For neither was anyone needy among them. For as many as were possessors of estates or houses, selling them, brought the prices of what they sold, and laid them before the feet of the Apostles. And it was distributed to each one according as each had need.* And when Lazarus the deacon, reciting, had handed the book to the bishop, Augustine the bishop said: I too wish to read. For it delights me more to be a reader of this word, than a disputer of my own word. *When they had prayed, the place was moved in which they were gathered, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and they spoke the word of God with confidence to all willing to believe. But of the multitude of believers there was one soul and one heart: and no one said that anything of what he possessed was his own, but all things were common to them. And with great power the Apostles gave testimony of the resurrection of the Lord Jesus: and great grace was upon them all. For neither was anyone needy among them. For as many as were possessors of estates or houses, selling them, brought the prices of what they sold, and laid them before the feet of the Apostles. And it was distributed to each one according as each had need*[^2].

**2.** You have heard what we wish: pray that we may be able. But a certain necessity has arisen that I should do these things more diligently: since, as you already know, a presbyter established in our fellowship—to what fellowship the reading bears witness which just now, when we recited it, you heard—dying, made a will, because he had something from which to make it. He had something to call his own, when he lived in that fellowship where it was lawful for no one to call anything his own, but all things were common to them. If some lover and praiser of ours were proclaiming this fellowship to a detractor of ours, and said: With Bishop Augustine all his fellow-dwellers live as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles, at once that detractor, shaking his head, advancing his tooth, would say: Is it really so lived, as you say? Why do you lie? Why with false praise do you honor the unworthy? Did not a presbyter, set just now in their fellowship, make a will, and dispose of what he had as he wished, and leave it? Are all things really common there? Does no one really call anything his own? Under these words what would that praiser of mine do? Would not that detractor stop up his mouth as it were with lead? Would he not repent of having praised? Would he not, suffused with awe and confounded by his speech, curse either us or that testator? This was the necessity, that we should come to this diligence.

**3.** I announce to you, then, that of which you may rejoice. All my brothers and fellow-clerics who dwell with me, presbyters, deacons, subdeacons, and Patricius my nephew, I have found such as I desired. But those who out of their own poverty, whatever it was, have not yet done what they determined, are these two, Valens the subdeacon, and my nephew just mentioned. The subdeacon was hindered by his mother's life, because she lived from it, and in him too the attainment of the lawful age was awaited, that what he did, he might do most firmly. But he has not yet done it, because he has those little fields in common with his brother, and they are possessed undivided. He desires so to confer them on the Church, that from them may be nourished those who are in the purpose of holiness, as long as they live in this life. For it is written, and the Apostle says this: *Whoever does not provide for his own, and especially his household, denies the faith, and is worse than an unbeliever*[^3]. But further, the slaves too are his in common with his brother, not yet divided. He arranges to set his hand to them, and cannot before he divides them. For which belong to him, he does not yet know. To him indeed, who is the elder, belongs the division; and to his brother the choice. And that brother of his serves God, is a subdeacon with my holy brother and fellow-bishop Severus in the Church of Milevis. This is being done, this must be carried out without delay, that those little slaves be divided, set free, and so he may give to the Church, that it may take up their nourishment. But my nephew, from the time he was converted, and began to be with me, was himself too hindered from doing anything about his little fields by the life of his mother, who held the usufruct. This year she died. Between him and his sisters there are certain cases, with Christ's help to be quickly settled: that he too may do what befits a servant of God, what the profession itself, and this reading, demand.

**4.** The deacon Faustinus, as nearly all of you know, was here converted from the warfare of the world to the monastery: here baptized, then ordained deacon. But because what he seems to possess is small, as the jurists say, by right, not in body, he had left it, and it was held by his brothers. He never thought about it from the time he was converted, nor did he himself seek anything from his brothers, nor was anything sought from him. Now, because it has come to this point of time, with my counsel he divided that property: and gave half to his brothers, half to the poor Church established in that same place.

**5.** Under what discipline and scourge of God the deacon Severus is, you know: yet he has not lost the light of his mind. He had bought one house here on account of his mother and sister, whom he desired to bring here from his own country. But he had bought it not with his own money, which he did not have, but from the contribution of religious men, whom, when I asked, he even indicated by name. About it I cannot say what he has done, or what he disposes, except that he too put the whole into my will, that what I wish, this might be done with it. But he has certain cases with his mother, of which cases he set me as judge, that when those cases are ended, what I wish may be done with that house. But what shall I be able to wish, God governing, except what justice commands, and piety demands? He also has some little fields in his own country: he arranges so to distribute them that there too, to the poor Church established in that place, he may bestow.

**6.** The deacon Hipponiensis is a poor man: he has nothing to confer on anyone. Yet from his labors, before he was a cleric, he had bought some little slaves: today, in your sight, he is going to set them free by episcopal proceedings.

**7.** The deacon Heraclius is engaged before your eyes, his works shine before our eyes. From his work, his money spent, we have the memorial of the holy martyr. With his own money he also bought a property by my counsel, for he wished that very money to be given out through my hands, as it pleased me. If I were greedy for money, or in this case cared more for those very needs of mine which I have on behalf of the poor, I would take the money. Why, says someone? Because that property which, bought by him, was given to the Church, as yet yields nothing to the Church. For it was worth less than the price. What had been borrowed, this is still being repaid from its fruits. I am an old man, how much fruit can come to me from that property? Do I promise myself that I shall live so many years, until it pays its own price? What, then, it has scarcely repaid over a long time in installments, I would soon have whole, if I had wished to take it. I did not do so, I had something else in view. For I confess to you, his age too was as yet suspect to me, and I feared lest perhaps, as men are, it should displease his mother, and she should say that the young man had been led on by me to consume his father's goods, and to leave him needy. Therefore I wished his money to be kept in that property, so that if anything, which God avert and does avert, had turned out otherwise than we wish, the estate might be returned, lest the bishop's reputation be blamed. For I know how necessary my reputation is to you: for my conscience suffices me. He also bought a space behind this later church, known to you; and with his own money built a house: and this you know; a few days before I had this discourse with you about this matter, he gave it to the Church: it is known to you. For he was waiting to finish it, that he might give it finished. But he had no necessity of building a house, except that he thought his mother would come here. If she had come before, she would dwell in her son's property: now if she comes, she will dwell in her son's work. I bear witness to him, he remained poor: but in the possession of love he remained. Some little slaves had been left to him, already indeed living in the monastery, whom, however, by ecclesiastical proceedings he is going to set free today. Let no one, then, say: He is rich; let no one suppose it, let no one speak evil, let no one rend himself and his soul with his own teeth. He has not money, money kept back: would that he might restore what he owes!

**8.** The rest, that is, the deacons, are poor, by God's favor, they await the mercy of God. Whence they have nothing to do: having no resources, they have ended the desires of the world. They live with us in common fellowship: no one distinguishes them from those who brought something. The unity of love is to be preferred to the advantage of an earthly inheritance.

**9.** There remain the presbyters. For I wished thus to ascend to them step by step. I would say it quickly: they are poor men of God. They brought nothing to the house of our fellowship, except that very love than which nothing is more dear. Nevertheless, because I know that rumors have arisen about their riches, they are not to be compelled by me to anything, but to be cleared before you by my discourse.

**10.** I tell you, who perhaps do not know, for very many of you do know: the presbyter Leporius, although noble in worldly birth, and born among his own in a most honorable position, yet already serving God, with all that he had abandoned, I received as a poor man: not because he had nothing, but because he had already done what this reading urges. He did not do it here, but we know where he did it. The unity of Christ is one, the Church is one. Wherever there is a good work, it belongs to us too, if we rejoice with it. There is one garden where you know: there he established a monastery for his own people, because they too serve God. That garden does not belong to the Church, but neither to himself. And to whom, someone may say? To that which is the monastery there. But what is true, up to this time he so bore the care for them, that the small expenses by which they are sustained he had with himself, and he himself, as it seemed, paid them out. But lest on account of this place be given to men gnawing at their suspicions and not filling their belly, this pleased both me and him, that they should so manage among themselves, as though he had already departed from the world. For will he, when he dies, dispense anything to them himself? It is better that he see them living well, God governing, living thus in the discipline of Christ, that he may only rejoice over them, not be occupied with their needs. Therefore he too has no money which he can or dares call his own. The hostel which must be built you now see built. I enjoined it on him, I ordered it. He obeyed me most gladly, and as you see, has worked at it: just as by my command he also built the basilica to the eight martyrs, from those things which God gives through you. For he began from the money which had been given to the Church for the hostel, and when he had begun to build, as religious men are who desire their works to be written in heaven, they helped as each one wished, and he built it. We have the work before our eyes: let every man see what has been done. About the money, because he has none, let them believe me, let them restrain their tooth, lest they break it. He had bought, from that money of the hostel, a certain house in Carraria, which he thought would be useful to him for its stones, but the stones of that house were not necessary for the building's construction, since they were provided from elsewhere. The house, then, so remained, it yields a rent, but to the Church, not to the presbyter. Let no one any more say: Into the presbyter's house, to the presbyter's house, before the presbyter's house. Behold where the presbyter's house is: where my house is, there is his: elsewhere he has no house, but everywhere he has God.

**11.** What more do you seek? except that I remember I also promised to report to you what I had done between the two, namely a brother and sister, the children of the presbyter Januarius, because a money controversy had arisen between them, but yet, as between brothers, with love preserved, by God's favor. I had promised, then, to hear between them, and whatever it was, by judging to settle it. I had prepared myself as judge: but before I judged, they themselves settled that on which I was going to judge. I did not find anything on which to judge, but on which to rejoice. They acquiesced altogether harmoniously to my will and counsel, that they should be equal in the money which their father left, the Church renouncing it.

**12.** After my sermon men will speak, but whatever men speak, with whatever breeze blowing, something will be carried thence to my ears. And if it is such that it is necessary for us to clear ourselves again, I will answer the slanderers, I will answer the detractors, I will answer the unbelievers, who do not believe us, their prelates: I will answer as I can what the Lord shall grant: meanwhile now it is not necessary, because perhaps they will say nothing. Those who love us, let them freely rejoice; those who hate us will silently grieve. Yet if they put forth their tongues, they will hear, by God's favor, along with you my answer, not my quarrel. For I am not going to name men and say: That one said this, that one detracted thus, when perhaps false things, since this too can happen, are carried to me. Nevertheless, whatever things shall be reported, if it seem necessary, I will speak of it to your love. I wish our life to be before your eyes. I know that those seeking license to do evil seek for themselves examples of those living badly, and defame many, that they may seem to have found associates. Therefore what is ours, we have done: more than to do, we have not. We are before your eyes. We desire nothing of anyone, except your good works.

**13.** And I exhort you, my brothers, if you wish to give anything to the clerics, know that you ought not to foster as it were their vices against me. Offer to all what you wish to offer of your own will. What will be common, will be distributed to each one according as each shall have need. Attend to the treasury, and we shall all have it. It greatly delights me, if it be our own manger, that we may be the beasts of God, you the field of God. Let no one give a cloak, a linen tunic, except into the common store. From the common store he will receive it, because he will receive it. To myself, since I know that I wish to have in common whatever I have, I do not wish your holiness to offer such things, which I alone may as it were more becomingly use. There is offered to me, for instance, a precious cloak: perhaps it befits a bishop, although it does not befit Augustine, that is, a poor man, born of poor parents. Now men will say that I clothe myself in precious garments, such as I could not have had either in my father's house, or in that worldly profession of mine. It does not befit: I ought to have such as I can, if I have it not, give to my brother. Such as a presbyter can have, such as a deacon and subdeacon can becomingly have, such I wish to receive: because I receive into the common store. If anyone gives a better one, I sell it: because I am wont to do this, that when a garment cannot be common, the price of the garment may be common; I sell it, and give it out to the poor. If it delights him that I have it, let him give such a one of which I may not be ashamed. For I confess to you, I am ashamed of a precious garment: because it does not befit this profession, it does not befit this admonition, it does not befit these limbs, it does not befit these gray hairs. This too I say: if perhaps in our house, in our fellowship, anyone is sick, or after sickness, so that it is necessary for him to take refreshment before the hour of the meal: I do not forbid religious men or women to send what seems good to them to send: yet a midday meal and a supper outside no one shall have.

**14.** Behold I say it, you have heard it, they hear it. Whoever has wished to have anything of his own, and to live from his own, and to act against these our precepts, it is little for me to say, he will not remain with me, but he will not be a cleric. For I had said, and I know I said it, that if they are unwilling to undertake the social life with me, I would not take from them the clerical office; they might remain apart, live apart, live for God as they could. And yet I set before their eyes how great an evil it is to fall from one's purpose. For I preferred to have even the lame, than to mourn the dead. For whoever is a hypocrite, is dead. As, then, whoever had wished to remain outside and live from his own, I would not take from him the clerical office: so now, because this social life has pleased them, by God's favor, whoever lives with hypocrisy, whoever shall be found having property of his own, I do not permit him to make a will of it, but I will erase him from the roll of the clergy. Let him appeal against me to a thousand councils, let him sail against me wherever he wishes, let him be wherever he can: the Lord will help me, that where I am bishop, he cannot be a cleric. You have heard it, they have heard it. But I hope in our God and his mercy, that just as they received this arrangement of mine cheerfully, so they will keep it purely and faithfully.

**15.** I said that the presbyters dwelling with me have nothing of their own, among whom is also the presbyter Barnabas. But about him too I heard certain things had been bandied about, above all that he bought an estate from my beloved and honorable son Eleusinus. This is false: that man gave it to the monastery, did not sell it. I am a witness. What more you seek, I do not know. I am a witness: he gave it, he did not sell it. But while it is not believed that he could give, he is believed to have sold. Blessed the man who did so good a work that it was not believed! Yet at least now believe, and gladly cease to hear detractors. I have already said, I am a witness. It was said of him too that in the year of his being provost he industriously incurred debts, so that, while I wished the debts to be repaid, I should give him, at his request, the Victorianan estate: as though he had said to me: That I may repay my debts, give me for ten years the Victorianan estate. And this too is false. But there was something whence the rumor arose. He incurred debts to be repaid. They were repaid by us partly, as far as they could be. Something remained which was owed even to that monastery which God established through him. Since, then, it remained, we began to seek whence we might repay the debt. To the leasing of that estate no one came, except one offering forty solidi as rent. But we saw the estate could give more, that the debt might be more quickly repaid; and I committed to his good faith, that from that leasing the brothers should not seek gains, but whatever the estate gave, of its very fruits they should reckon toward the debt. It is done in good faith. The presbyter is ready that I appoint another, who from the fruits may repay the brothers. Let there be someone of your number, to whom I may commit this, of the number of those who reported such things to us. For there are among you religious men, who grieved that he was rebuked by a false rumor, and yet believed the deed done. Let someone, then, of these come to us, take up the possession, faithfully give out all the fruits at their prices, that what is owed may be more easily repaid, and today the care of the presbyter withdraws from it. The place itself too, in which the monastery was established by my aforesaid honorable son Eleusinus, was given to the presbyter Barnabas himself before he was ordained presbyter: in that place he established the monastery. But yet, because the place had been given in his name, he changed the documents, that it may be possessed in the name of the monastery. About the Victorianan estate, I ask, I exhort, I beg, that if anyone is religious, let him act in good faith, and render this service to the Church, that the debt may be quickly repaid. But if no one of the laity be found, I will set another in charge: now that one will not approach it. What more do you wish? Let no one tear the servants of God, because it is not expedient for those who tear them. The reward of the servants of God indeed grows by false detractions, but the punishment of the detractors grows too. For not without cause was it said: *Rejoice and exult, when they detract from you, saying false things: because your reward is great in heaven*[^4]. We do not wish to have a great reward at your loss. Let us have less there, and yet reign there with you.

[^2]: Acts 4:31-35
[^3]: 1 Tim 5:8
[^4]: See Matt 5:11-12
