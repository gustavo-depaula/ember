# Sermon 341: Augm. (Dolbeau 22)

**1.** This psalm, as is known to all Christians, since it is written there: *They have dug my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones; but they themselves considered and gazed at me, they divided my garments among themselves and cast lots over my garment*[^1]. For it was figured in the person of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. But how the person of our Lord Jesus Christ is commended and intimated to the hearts of the faithful, although I do not think it hidden from you, you must yet be reminded, because some do not know, and many have forgotten what they heard, and some wish to be confirmed in what they hold, nor are there lacking those who wish to hear even that in which they are firm from us, for our own sake. But this understanding, which, according to the strength the Lord deigns to supply, I try to intimate to your love, avails for understanding many obscure things in the most sacred books, that is, how Christ is spoken of.

**2** [1]. For, as far as we have been able to observe the holy pages, he is named in three ways, when he is preached whether through the law and prophets, or through the apostolic epistles, or through faith in the deeds which we know in the Gospel. One way is according to God and that divinity coequal and coeternal with the Father before the assumption of the flesh. Another way is that by which, the flesh assumed, he is now the same God who is man and the same man who is God, according to a certain property of his excellence by which he is not made coequal to the rest of men, but is the mediator and head of the Church, and is read and understood. The third way is when, in a manner, the whole Christ in the fullness of the Church, that is, head and body, according to the fullness of a certain perfect man, in which perfect man we are members, is both preached to believers and offered, recognizable, to the prudent. Yet we cannot, in so small and narrow a time, either recall or explain all the testimonies of the Scriptures by which all these three kinds are proved, but yet we will not leave them unproved, so that, certain testimonies recalled, the rest which we are not permitted to recall on account of the shortness of time you may now be able by yourselves to observe in the Scriptures and find.

**3** [2]. To the first way, then, of intimating our Lord Jesus Christ our Savior, the only Son of God, through whom all things were made, pertains that which is most noble and most illustrious in the Gospel according to John: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. This was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was nothing made. What was made, in him is life; and the life was the light of men, and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it*[^2]. Wonderful and astonishing are these words, before they are understood; understood, they are to be embraced. But that they be understood, it is not furnished by human resources, but by him himself inspiring to know them, who deigned to inspire fishermen to say them. For that fisherman said this, the son of Zebedee, who left his father and his boat and his nets and followed God, not leaving a human father, but choosing God as Father. To him, who left a little boat and nets, surely the whole world was reckoned as left. For our Lord Jesus Christ did not attend to what the poor who followed him left, that is, what they left of their own substance, but what they left of their own desire. For everyone who has little wishes to have more, and he who leaves the little he had left more which he desired to have. And therefore, when that rich man had withdrawn saddened from the Lord, whom, to receive counsel, he called good master, and the one giving counsel he left as if a bad master, the disciples, after that man was saddened and withdrew, after they heard that through the mercy of God even the rich can enter the kingdom of heaven—of whose salvation they had despaired, when they heard that a camel more easily enters through the eye of a needle than a rich man into the kingdom of heaven—subjoined, saying: *Behold, we have left all things and followed you: what then will there be for us?*; and the Lord: *You will sit,* he says, *on twelve seats, judging the twelve tribes of Israel*[^3]. He also promised to others leaving all their things and following him a great reward, and a great solace in the very promise and joy in the display, since whoever for his sake leaves all that he had in this world, *shall receive,* he says, *in this world a hundredfold, but in the world to come eternal life*[^4]. All these things, if expounded more diligently, hold us and turn us from the matter proposed. Yet let your love now attend to what pertains to the matter undertaken, that the first poor men left all their things and followed God and were made apostles, and as much is reckoned to them leaving few things as is reckoned to those leaving great things. And what is truly to be wondered at, then, hearing from the mouth of the Lord that all things must be left and God must be followed, the rich man withdrew sad, hearing this from the mouth of the Lord; now, the Lord not seen in the flesh, men hear this from his Gospel and do what that man did not do, and so it is fulfilled in them: *Blessed are those who do not see and believe*[^5].

**4.** {Why, then, did the Lord first choose the lowborn, the poor, the unskilled, the uncultivated, when he had before his eyes a great crowd, fewer indeed in comparison with the many poor, but in their own kind many rich, noble, learned, wise, for whom he also afterward provided—for he did not abandon them, all such kinds came to the faith—the Apostle explains the mystery: *He chose the weak things of this world, that he might confound the strong; and he chose the foolish things, that he might confound the wise; and the lowborn things of the world God chose, and the things that are not,* that is, are not reckoned, *as though they were, that the things that are might be emptied out*[^6]. For he had come to teach humility, to storm pride; God had come humble. In no way here would he seek the lofty, who had come so humble. First, because he chose to be born of that woman, who was betrothed to a carpenter. He did not, then, choose ample birth, lest in this earth nobility be proud. He did not even choose, for being born, the amplest city, but he was born in Bethlehem of Judea, which is not even judged worthy of the name of city. For even today the inhabitants of that place call it a village: so small and slight and almost nothing it is, unless it were ennobled by the nativity of Christ}. He came, then, not one who would be noble from the place, but one who would himself make the place noble, and so the rest of the things of our Lord Jesus Christ which it is long to recall. {He chose, then, the weak, the poor, the unlearned, the lowborn: not because he abandoned the firm, the rich, the wise, the noble, but if he chose those first, they would seem to be chosen by the merit of their dignities, by the merit of their riches, by the merit of their birth, and so, inflated about these things, they would not receive the salvation of humility, without which no one can return to that life, from which he would not have fallen except through pride}. On the contrary, then, the physician heals the disease: the cold by hot remedies, the hot by cold, the dry by moist, the moist by dry. If we see, then, the art of medicine heal the languishing by contraries, it is not to be wondered at if we are healed by the humility of God, who were sick by human pride. More healthfully, then, did the Lord gain an orator from a fisherman, than a fisherman from an orator. For the orator Cyprian was a martyr, but earlier the apostle was a fisherman. Christian emperors were made afterward, but earlier fishermen preached Christ himself. And so far did God, truly choosing the weak things of the world, confound the strong—but he confounded that he might heal, he cast down that he might raise up—that what we have known in our own times and must not be kept silent might be made manifest to us, the faith being known by the very deeds, that *God chose the weak things of the world, that he might confound the strong.* For in our times an emperor came to the city of Rome: there is the temple of an emperor, there is the tomb of a fisherman. And so, to beg salvation from the Lord, that pious and Christian emperor did not proceed to the proud temple of an emperor, but to the tomb of a fisherman, where, humble, he might imitate that fisherman, that he might then by some regard obtain from the Lord what a proud emperor could not merit.

**5.** Why, then, did I say this? Because we recalled that way of intimating Christ according to the divinity before the assumption of the flesh, a certain way wonderful and astonishing to all hearers, but known to few who understand, when they so knock that they are as it were dazzled by a kind of flashing of that eternal and ineffable light, saying and recalling: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God* and the rest, the understanding of which words, lest you seek superfluously from us, we said that you can understand these things by the inspiration of him by whose inspiration it was brought about that an unlearned fisherman preached them. For that fisherman did not know these things by himself, nor was he of such great talent or learning as to penetrate with the sharpness of his mind and transcend all this air and all the aethereal powers, thence arrive at the natures of the luminaries, virtues, powers, and angels, and all the most supereminent spiritual creation, fallen by no sin, but always clinging to the contemplation of unchangeable truth, and transcend that too and arrive at that *which neither eye has seen nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^7]. What kind of thing is the Word of the Father, how is it the Word? Was it thought? did it sound? Surely not. For if it was thought, it spent time; if it sounded, the air being struck, it passed through. Not so the Word of God, but a remaining Word, and always the Word, uttered and not taken away, or rather not even uttered, lest a certain corporeal extension be understood. And how it is to be said, no one expresses with a human mouth: it is piously believed that the Word is begotten; he himself can speak and utter himself, the Son of God alone can himself; but the one to whom he utters himself can understand him, but cannot utter him. Whence did the fisherman see this, but because he himself willed to show himself? Whence did the fisherman see this, from where he drank; but whence did he drink this? Let us attend to the banquet of the Lord, if perhaps we shall find whence this fisherman drank. All the disciples were reclining with the Lord; John is written in the Gospel to have been wont to recline upon the breast of the Lord. What wonder, then, if from his breast he drank what he said about his divinity? For the Lord of the banquet and the Lord of those banqueting would not allow his disciple to fill his belly from that table and not fill his mind from his breast. So indeed he did: he fed and satisfied the disciple from his breast. But that one, satisfied, belched, and that very belching is the Gospel. You have seen, then, with the eyes of faith in the Gospel the fisherman feasting; hear him belching: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. This was in the beginning with God.* You despised it, when you heard "word"—for daily words are heard—now do not despise, because *the Word was God.* "And how shall I understand God and Word?" Let him make you drink, who satisfied the fisherman. Meanwhile now hear him belching, believe him belching, that you too, ascending by the step of faith, may be satisfied with the liveliness of understanding.

**6.** "What then?", you will say. "Now I am to believe that the Word of God is the Son of God." The only Son of God, not two words, but one Word, although there are two words of Scripture, for example those two precepts pertaining to the twofold love, or those pertaining to recompense. For the Lord says certain things to the pious set at the right hand, and says other things to the impious set at the left. He does not say to the pious what he says to the impious. As it were two words are distributed by the narrator according to our capacity, according to our merits: here they are distributed, but there a certain thing &lt;...&gt;, as, if I can say a certain likeness from corporeal things—yet fitting to the senses of the weak—if I can say it, how the brightness either of fire or of a star or of the moon or of the sun is one and is in one manner. But if it is attended to by different eyes, by one sincere and sound, by another wounded and bleary, to the one it seems a placid light, to the other as it were raging and angry. For it strikes back the wounded gaze and causes pain, whence to the sound one it caused joy. See, it is placid, see, it is savage. Is the light itself divided, the light itself unlike? No, but according to the merits of those diversely gazing. Attend, my brethren, and from small things know greater. So the one Word of God, but it distributes worthy things according to merits, saying to those: *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which has been prepared for you from the origin of the world,* but saying to those: *Go into the eternal fire which has been prepared for the devil and his angels*[^8]. What is so diverse as *come into the kingdom* and *go into the eternal fire?* Is the word, then, diverse? No. It is one, but the merits of the hearers are diverse. Hear the prophet in the psalms saying this same thing: *God has spoken once: these two things I have heard*[^9]. A great question: now, if you have understood the things I said, let it not trouble you. *He has spoken once,* and you have heard *two?* *He has spoken once* according to the one Word, how then have you heard *two?* *Because power is yours,* he says, *and to you, Lord, mercy*[^10]—power, then, for punishing, mercy for freeing. Hear what follows: *For you will render,* he says, *to each according to his works*[^11].

**7.** O the only Word, sweet Word, may it inspire in us its love; but it inspires through the Holy Spirit. For so is the Trinity: the Father who begot, the Word which he begot, the Spirit by whom love is inspired; he who begot, and that which he begot, and that by whom it is inspired. That the most sweet and most excellent and ineffable Trinity be loved, supereminent over all the creation which it began, perfected, ordered, supereminent altogether, it desires the hearts of those who love it. But "desires" is rightly said, because it makes us desire. For the Spirit too is said to rejoice, because it makes us rejoice. And of God: *The Lord your God tempts you, that he may know whether you love him*[^12]. What is *that he may know,* but that he may make you know? Therefore it feeds the hearts of those who desire, who love purely, who love freely, because they find nothing better to love—or let them find something better, and let them receive this as a reward; let them ask this, if they find nothing better. If, then, your Creator did not give you himself, than whom you find nothing better, you would always have had to groan rather than seek something else; if your Creator did not give you himself, than whom nothing is better—if indeed he admits these words to be said of himself; but he admits them because we are little ones, he admits them because, if we wished to say something worthy, we would say nothing at all—if, then, your Creator, as I had begun to say, were unwilling to give you himself, you would always have had to groan rather than for something else. But now he himself offers himself, and you seek something else; he asks in a manner to be loved, by whom he is not loved. O you are wretched, not he! He seeks, then, hearts that sincerely love him, which by pious intention surpass every changeable creature—but they surpass it, if they have been humble: high places are not surpassed by the high. If you wish to surpass every creature and arrive at that other thing which you heard from the mouth of the belching fisherman, be humble, seek piously. For when you have transcended all changeable things, both corporeal and spiritual, you will come to behold that Trinity, and you will drink whence he drank; to which when you have come, you will laugh at all the slanderers—and when you have first laughed, afterward perhaps you will weep for those who, by vain contentions, stir up smoke for themselves, lest they see it.

**8.** Let this, then, suffice, brethren, to have intimated to you. But that you may understand, knock at him. Let no one say to himself with carnal thought: "How was the Word with God and how in the womb of the virgin, that it might be born? Did the Word itself, then, descend, so that, when it was in the womb of the Virgin Mary, it withdrew from the Father? If it did not withdraw from the Father, how could it be here? Or did perhaps half remain with the Father, and half be in the womb? Or did perhaps a great part remain with the Father, and a little something or small fragment descend to the womb of the virgin?" Do not cut God up into fragments; let the dissensions of your thoughts be in a manner unified in him, let him not be torn apart in them; let him gather you, do not you scatter him. "And how," you say, "shall I understand it? I do not know, I cannot. He is both with the Father, and is in the womb of the virgin. Who is there who can understand this?" But you hear of God; you make yourself a certain body by the custom of carnal thought. When you have done this, you must now divide. For you do not find the whole everywhere. One part of the earth here, another outside in the street, not the same, because it is earth, because it is a body: one part is large, another small. So too water is divided: one part is near this shore, another near that, and not the same part there which is here; although the whole seems as it were everywhere to be stretched out in its places, yet it does not have the same part everywhere, but one here, another elsewhere. So this diffusion of the air: one air is in this basilica, &lt;another in that&gt;, since one air is throughout the whole, but one part in this, another part in that; not that part there which is here, nor that here which is there. So too the parts of the sky: those are different which we behold when we look at the east, different the parts which we behold when we turn ourselves to the west. It cannot be that one and the same part is everywhere: even if the whole seems through its parts to be everywhere, yet not the whole of it is everywhere, but one part elsewhere, another elsewhere. Do not, then, think corporeally of God in this way. Or can you not think of anything but such things? Perhaps I will give an example, whence this may come into the notice of the intent of your love.

**9.** You wish to divide the Word of God, and it does not seem credible to you that the whole is with the Father, the whole is in the womb of the Virgin Mary. I say more: the whole is wherever you wish, but it did not everywhere take on the person of a man, with whom God and man might become one. I do not wish you to divide: grasp as much as you can. Do you wish to divide the Word of God? Hear the word of man. Surely it seemed to you that the Word of God could not be both in Mary and with the Father, except as it were distributed in particles, so that one part of it were here, another there. When you hear a word from us, or when you hear words from us—attend to what we say: for from this word you better get the proof, which is said to you who are many, than from those words which you say to one another. For you deal with fewer, we deal with so many: all hear what we say, and all hear the whole. If I set before you bodily food on which to feed, you would divide the food among yourselves, and one would take away this part, another that, to eat it. Even if you all fed on the same and one food, yet not all on the same parts, but you would divide among yourselves, according to your small measures, in particles what was set before you, one taking that part, another this; one food would reach all, but not the whole food all. Surely it would be so. But as that food would be set before your mouth, so now a certain food of voices and words is set before your ears, and yet the whole reaches all. Or does perhaps, when I speak, one take for himself one syllable, another another? or one one word, another another word? If it were so, I would have to say as many words as I see men, that they might reach all, at least single words. And it is indeed easily done: I say more than there are men here, and all reach all. The word of man, then, is not divided through syllables, that all may hear it, and is the Word of God cut into fragments, that it may be everywhere? Do we think, brethren, that these sounding and passing things are in any part to be compared to that Word remaining unchangeably? or, because I said these things, have I compared them? But in whatever way I wished to intimate to you, that what God displays in corporeal things may avail you to believe the things you do not yet see about spiritual things. But now let us pass to better things. For words sound and go away. Think of spiritual things, think of justice. One thinking of justice set in these western parts, another thinking of justice set in the east, whence comes it that the one thinks the whole of it and the other the whole? and the one sees the whole and the other the whole? For he who sees justice, according to which he does something, himself acts justly. He sees within, he acts without. Whence does he see within, if nothing is present to the seer? But if it is present, since he himself is set in a part, will the thought of the other not reach the same part? But since you, set here, see in your mind the same that he sees, set so far away, and the whole shines for you, the whole also for him, see that those things which are divine and incorporeal are everywhere whole, and believe the Word whole in the Father, whole in the womb. For you believe this of the Word of God, who is God with God.

**10.** [3]. And hear another intimation, another way of intimating Christ, which the Scripture preaches. For it says these things before the assumed flesh. But how does the Scripture preach this one? *The Word,* it says, *was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^13]. For he who had said: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. This was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was nothing made,* would preach to us the divinity of the Word in vain, if he kept silent the humanity of the Word. For that I may see that, this is done with me; that I may be purified for contemplating that, he himself comes to the aid of my weakness. By taking on from human nature that very human nature, he was made man. He came with the beast of the flesh to the one who lay wounded on the road, that he might fashion and nourish our little faith by the sacrament of his incarnation, and make the understanding serene for seeing that which he never lost through that which he took on. For he began to be man, he did not cease to be God. Therefore this is the preaching of our Lord Jesus Christ according to that he is mediator, according to that he is head of the Church, that he is God-man and man-God, when John says: *And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.*

**11** [4]. Hear now both in that most well-known passage of the apostle Paul: *Who, though he was in the form of God,* he says, *did not count it robbery to be equal to God*[^14]. This is: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.* How did the Apostle say: *He did not count it robbery to be equal to God,* if he is not equal to God? But if the Father is God, and he is not God, how equal? Where, then, John has: *The Word was God,* there Paul has: *He did not count it robbery to be equal to God.* And where John has: *The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us,* there Paul has: *But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^15]. Attend. Through this, then, that he was made man, through this, that *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us,* through this *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant.* For what did he *empty?* Not that he might lose the divinity, but that he might put on the humanity, appearing to men as what he was not, before he was man. So by appearing he emptied himself, that is, keeping the divinity of his majesty and offering the garment of the flesh of humanity. Through that, then, by which *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant,* not taking the form of God—for when he spoke of the form of God, he did not say: he took, but: *though he was in the form of God;* but when it came to the form of a servant: *Taking,* he says, *the form of a servant*—through this, then, he is mediator and head of the Church, through whom we are reconciled to God, through the sacrament of his humility and passion and resurrection and ascension and future judgment, so that those two things may be heard, since God has spoken once. Where may two things be heard? Where he renders *to each according to his works.*

**12** [5]. Holding this, then, do not wonder at the questions of men, which creep like a cancer, as the apostle said; but guard your ears and the virginity of your mind, as those betrothed by the friend of the bridegroom to be presented as a chaste virgin to one husband, Christ. The virginity of the body is in a few of the Church, the virginity of the mind ought to be in all. This virginity the serpent wishes to corrupt, of which the same Apostle says: *I betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. And I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so your senses too be corrupted from the chastity which is in Christ*[^16]. *Your senses,* he says, that is, your minds. For this is more proper. For the senses are understood also of this body: of seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching. The Apostle feared lest our minds be corrupted, where is the virginity of faith. Now then, O soul, keep your virginity, to be made fruitful afterward in the embrace of your bridegroom. *Hedge in, then,* as it is written, *your ears with thorns*[^17]. The question of the Arians troubled the weak brethren of the Church, but in the mercy of the Lord the catholic faith prevailed. For he did not abandon his Church, even though for a time troubled, that it might always entreat him, by whom it might be confirmed on the solid rock. And still the serpent mutters and is not silent: it seeks, by a certain promise of knowledge, to cast out of the paradise of the Church the one whom it would not permit to return to that paradise, from which it was first cast out.

**13** [6]. Attend, my brethren: what was done in that paradise, this is done in the Church. Let no one seduce you from this paradise: let it suffice that we fell thence, or, having had the experience, let us be corrected. He is the serpent, who always suggests impiety; he sometimes promises impunity, just as he promised there too: *You shall not die the death,* he says, because God said: *You shall die the death*[^18]. He suggests such things now too, that Christians may live badly: "Will he destroy them all?" He says: "I will damn, I will pardon those who change themselves: let them change their deeds, I change my threats." He is, then, the one who murmurs and mutters and says: "See, it is written: *The Father is greater than I*[^19], and you call him equal to the Father?" I accept what you say, and I accept both, because I read both. Why do you accept one and refuse the other? For you read both with me. See, *The Father is greater than I* I accept, not from you, but from the Gospel; and you, accept from the Apostle that he is equal to God. Join both: let both agree, because he who spoke through John in the Gospel, he himself spoke through Paul in the epistle. He cannot disagree with himself, but you do not wish to understand the agreement of the Scriptures, since you yourself love to quarrel. "But from the Gospel," you say, "I prove: *The Father is greater than I.*" And I from the Gospel: *I and the Father are one*[^20]. "How are both true?" How does the Apostle teach us? Hear: *I and the Father are one. Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal to God.* Hear: *The Father is greater than I. But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant.* See, I have shown why greater, you show in what he is equal. For we read both. He is less than the Father, insofar as he is the Son of man; equal to the Father, insofar as he is the Son of God, because *the Word was God.* Mediator and God and man: God equal to the Father, man less than the Father. He is, then, both equal and less: equal in the form of God, less in the form of a servant. You, then, say whence equal. Is he equal in one part, and less in another? See, the assumption of flesh excepted, show me equal and less. Whence will you demonstrate it, I wish to see.

**14** [7]. Attend to the stolid impiety being wise according to the flesh, as it is written: *To be wise according to the flesh is death*[^21]. I set aside for now, and I do not yet speak of the incarnation of our Lord the only Son of God, but as if what has already been done were not yet done, I attend with you: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.* I attend with you: *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal to God.* There show me the less. What will you say? Will you divide God by qualities, that is, by certain corporeal or animal affections, in which we perceive being to be one thing and another? Naturally I can indeed say it, but whether it be so that you too may understand, God shall see. Therefore, as I had begun to say, before the assumption of the flesh, before the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, show me the less, show me the equal. Is God one thing and another, so that on one side the Son is less than him, on another side equal? As if we should say: "There are certain bodies," you could say to me: "Equal in length, but less in strength." For very often two such bodies occur, that in the stature of length they are equal, but in strength one is less, the other greater. Are we, then, going to think of such things as bodies: are we going to think of God and his Son so? May God avert these thoughts from the hearts of Christians. He was whole with the Father, whole in the flesh, whole above the angels. But perhaps you will think so as to say: "Equal both in strength and in length, but they are unequal in color"? Where is color, but in bodies? But there is the light of wisdom. Show me the color of wisdom. Show me the color of justice. If these have no color, would you say those things of God, if you had the color of shame?

**15** [8]. What, then, will you say? Are they equal in power, but is the Son less in prudence? God is unjust, if he gave to a lesser prudence equal power. If they are equal in prudence, but the Son is less in power, God is envious, who gave to an equal prudence less power. But in God everything that is said is the selfsame thing. For in God power is not one thing and prudence another and fortitude another and justice another or chastity another. Whatever of these you say of God, it is neither understood as one thing and another, and nothing is worthily said, because these are things of souls, which that light in a manner suffuses and affects according to their qualities. As when this visible light rises for bodies, if it be taken away, there is one color for all bodies, or rather it must be said there is no color; but when, brought in, it has illumined the bodies, although it is itself of one manner, yet according to the diverse qualities of bodies it sprinkles diverse splendors on them. Therefore these are affections of souls, which are well affected by that light which is not affected, and formed by that which is not formed.

**16** [9]. Yet we say these things of God, brethren, because we find nothing better to say. I call God just, because in human words I find nothing better: for he is beyond justice. It is said in the Scriptures: *The Lord is just and has loved justice*[^22]. But there it is said both that God repents, there it is said also that God does not know. Who would not shudder? Does God not know, does God repent? Therefore yet to these words too Scripture healthfully descended, which you too shudder at, lest you think those things which you think great to be worthily said. As if you wished to believe that, according to a human affection, something causes God to repent, and another who already understands better corrected you, expounding to you that, if you find anything of the kind in the Scriptures, it is not said because God suffers something of the kind, such as you, in the condemnation of your plan or deed, with grief of heart, but on account of this, that he changes otherwise than men suppose, because, if men do anything of the kind, they do it by repenting, if they turn from a purpose, while his plan remains certain and eternal, but because it was hoped he would do one thing and he does another, it is said figuratively that he repents. And so, if you ask: "What, then, is worthily said of God?" Perhaps he answers you and says that he is just. But another, understanding better than this one, says that even this word is surpassed by his excellence, and that even this is unworthily said of him, although it is said congruently according to the capacity of men. But when he wishes to prove from the Scriptures, because it is written: *The Lord is just,* he is rightly answered that it is set down in the same Scriptures that God repents. As you do not take that according to the custom of those speaking, as men are wont to repent, so understand that what is said, "just," does not befit his supereminence, although Scripture set this down well, that through whatever words the mind may step by step be led to that which cannot be said. You call God just indeed, but understand something beyond the justice which you are wont to think even of a man. But because the Scriptures called him just, therefore they also called him repenting and not knowing, which you now do not wish to say. As, then, you understand those things you shudder at to be said on account of your weakness, so these things which you make much of are said on account of a certain weakness of the stronger. But he who has transcended these things and has begun to think worthily of God, as far as is granted to man, will find a silence to be praised by the ineffable voice of the heart.

**17** [10]. Therefore, brethren, because in God power is the same thing as justice—and whatever you say of him, you say the same thing, although you say nothing worthily—you cannot say that the Son is equal to the Father in justice and not equal in power, or equal in power and not equal in knowledge, because from any one thing equal he is equal in everything, because all the things you say there are the same and are worth the same. It suffices, then, that you cannot say how the Son is equal to the Father, unless you grant certain diversities in the substance of God. When you have granted them, truth will cast you outside, nor will you approach that sanctuary, where it is seen most sincerely. But since you cannot say equal on one side and unequal on another, because there are no parts in God, you cannot say equal in one quality, less in another, because there are no qualities in God; according to God you cannot say equal, except in every way equal: whence, then, can you say less, except because he took on the form of a servant? And so, brethren, always attend to these things. If you have received a certain rule in the Scriptures, the light itself will show you everything. Wherever you find the Son named equal to the Father, understand it according to a certain essence of the divinity; wherever you find him less, understand it according to the form of a servant assumed: according to what is said, *I am who I am,* and according to what is said, *I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob*[^23]; so you will hold both what is in his nature, and what is in his mercy.

**18.** But let it not move you that this too was said according to this difference, what the Lord spoke in the Gospel and in the psalm which we just now sang. But that we may expound and intimate it by a clearer testimony, it is said in the Gospel: *To my Father and your Father, to my God and your God*[^24]. Let *my Father and your Father* not trouble you. For he is always Father to the Son: never was the Son not begotten, never was he not Father. But he is our Father in another way, through the mercy of adoption. Him he begot, us he adopted. Him he begot *before the daystar,* which do not take as one star, but mystically the daystar (lucifer) is that which bears light (lucem ferat), not that it is light, but that it is illumined by the light, so that it may bear light. For of that archangel too who did not stand in the truth it was said that he *rose like the daystar,* but he did not stand in the light itself. Therefore every soul that is illumined, so that it may shine, is called a daystar; if it turns itself away from the illuminating light, it is darkened. Therefore John the evangelist says of the Lord himself: *He was the true light.* And as if it were asked: "What is the *true light?*", *which illumines every man,* he says—not, then, that which is illumined, but *which illumines.* But of John the Baptist he says: *He was not the light*[^25]. But what *light was he not?* Such as is that *which illumines* and is not illumined. For the light which is illumined was John, because he had received of his fullness. Whence the Lord himself says: *And you wished to exult for an hour in his light*[^26]. And to his disciples he says: *You are the light of the world*[^27]. For they were illumined that they might be light, but he was *the true light which illumines every man,* another thing is the made light which is illumined. *The true light*, then, *which illumines,* this is our Lord Jesus Christ; but the made light which is illumined, this is John, this the apostles, this all the holy souls and most blessed intellectual spirits, which by receiving light become daystars. Therefore *before the daystar I begot you,* that is, before every creature. It is to be understood as before every creature, in which the spiritual and rational creature holds the primacy, which, illumined, bears light. Both, then, brethren, let us hold in our Lord Jesus Christ: both according to that he is God, he is equal to the Father, and according to that he is man, he is less than the Father. But what I had begun to say, let no one stumble at, that he says: *My Father and your Father.* For he is always the Father of the only-begotten Son, always he was born and born before the daystar, that is, before every creature which by being illumined becomes light. Rightly, then, *my Father and your Father,* because we received from him to be sons of God: *who gave us power to become sons of God*[^28]. And our adoption, of which the Apostle speaks, is well known to your love, of which he says: *awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^29], and again: *God sent his Son made of a woman, made under the law, that he might redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^30]. Rightly, then, first singularly he says *my Father,* then *your Father.* But how *my God and your God?* Now if the rule is known, what do you await from me? *My Father* always, *my God* from the time he is man. Hear the psalm which was read: *Upon you I was cast from the womb, from my mother's belly you are my God*[^31]. I think enough has been said about that way too, by which our Lord Jesus Christ our Savior, head of the Church, made mediator, through whom we are reconciled to God, is intimated in the Scriptures as God and man.

**19** [11]. The third way is how the whole Christ according to the Church, that is, head and body, is preached. For both head and body are one Christ, not because without the body he is not complete, but because he deigned to be complete with us too, who without us too is always complete, not only in that he is the Word, the only-begotten Son equal to the Father, but also in that very man whom he took on and with whom together he is God and man. But yet, brethren, how are we his body, if not also with us he is one Christ? Where shall we find this, that one Christ is head and body, that is, the body with its head, the bride with her bridegroom? As it were singularly he speaks in Isaiah, and one and the same speaks, and see what he says: *As a bridegroom he bound a miter on me and as a bride he clothed me with an ornament*[^32]. He calls the same one bridegroom and bride: bridegroom according to the head, bride according to the body. They seem two, and it is one. Otherwise, how are we members of Christ, the Apostle saying most openly: *But you are the body of Christ and members?*[^33] If we are members of Christ, we are also the body, all together: not we who are only in this place, but also throughout the whole earth; nor we who are only at this time, but—what shall I say?—from just Abel to the end of the world, where now men neither beget nor are begotten, whatever of the just has made its passage through this life, whatever is here now—that is, not in place, but in this life—whatever of those to be born hereafter is to come, all this is one body of Christ, but the individuals are members. If, then, all are the body, but the individuals are members, there is surely a head whose body this is. *And he is,* he says, *the head of the body, the Church, the firstborn, himself holding the primacy*[^34]. And because he says of him also that he is *the head of all principality and power*[^35], this Church, which is now a pilgrim, is joined to that heavenly Church, where we have the angels as fellow citizens, because we would shamelessly claim that we are to be equal after the resurrection of the bodies, unless the truth had promised, saying: *They will be equal to the angels of God*[^36]; and one Church is made, *the city of the great king*[^37], of which he also willed to be the Son, taking flesh from its pilgrim part, its king and its fructifier, that he might recall what had strayed thence. For it is Sion in the figure of a mystery, of which it is written: *Mother Sion, a man shall say, and a man was made in her, and the Most High himself founded her*[^38], that is, he himself who *was made a man in her,* though he was most lowly, *the Most High himself founded her,* because *all things were made through him, and without him was nothing made.* A truncated body cannot be called whole without the head, but the head wishes to be united with its body. And Christ is one with his body by graciousness, not by necessity. For we are in need of the good of God, God is not in need of our good. Hear the prophet: *I said to the Lord: You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^39].

**20** [12]. So, then, sometimes in the Scriptures Christ is intimated so that you may understand the Word equal to the Father. So sometimes, that you may understand the mediator man, when *the Word was made flesh,* that it might dwell *among us,* when that only-begotten, through whom all things were made, *did not count it robbery to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, made obedient unto the death of the cross*[^40]. But so sometimes, that you may understand head and body, the Apostle himself most openly expounding what was said of man and wife in Genesis: *And they shall be,* he says, *two in one flesh*[^41]. Attend to him expounding, lest we seem to dare to say something by our own conjectures. *And they shall be,* he says, *two in one flesh,* and he added: *This is a great sacrament*[^42]. And lest anyone still think it to be in man and wife according to the natural coupling of the two sexes and the corporeal mingling: *But I,* he says, *speak in Christ and in the Church*[^43]. According to this, then, that is taken *in Christ and in the Church* which was said: *They shall be two in one flesh: now not two, but it is one flesh.* As bridegroom and bride, so head and body, because *the head of the woman is the man*[^44]. {Whether, then, I say head and body, or whether I say bridegroom and bride, understand it as one. And therefore the same Apostle, even when he was still Saul, heard: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^45], since the body is joined to the head, and when, now a preacher of Christ, he suffered from others what he himself as persecutor had done: *That I may fill up,* he says, *what is lacking of the pressures of Christ in my flesh*[^46], showing that what he suffered pertained to the pressures of Christ. This cannot be understood according to the head, which now in heaven suffers nothing of the kind, but according to the body, that is, the Church, because that body with its head is one Christ.}

**21** [13]. Therefore present yourselves a body worthy of such a head, a bride worthy of such a bridegroom. That head cannot have any but a worthy body; nor does that man take any but a wife worthy of himself. *That he might present to himself,* he says, *a glorious Church, not having spot or wrinkle or anything of the kind*[^47]. {This is the bride of Christ, not having *spot or wrinkle.* Do you not wish to have a spot? Do what is written: *Wash yourselves, be clean, remove the evils from your hearts*[^48]. Do you not wish to have a wrinkle? Be stretched out on the cross. For it is not only needful that you be washed, but also that you be stretched out, that you be without spot or wrinkle. For through the washing sins are taken away, but through the stretching there comes about the desire of the world to come, on account of which Christ was crucified. Hear Paul himself washed: *Not,* he says, *by the works of justice which we did, but according to his mercy he saved us, through the washing of regeneration*[^49]. Hear the same one stretched out: *The things,* he says, *that are behind forgotten, stretched out to the things that are before, according to intention I follow toward the palm of the supernal vocation of God in Christ Jesus*[^50]. Rightly, then, he himself, without the spot of iniquity and without the wrinkle of a double heart, as a good and faithful friend of the bridegroom, betrothes to him a chaste virgin to be presented to one husband, without spot and wrinkle}. For not without cause is the prophecy of Isaiah recalled beside the way of the fuller.

**22.** All such things, brethren, are in a sacrament: whatever in the Scriptures sounds as it were absurd, as it were not necessary, is closed. But not closed and empty: God closes over something full, but seeks those who knock, that he may open. Surely, weigh it. For, to say something cheerfully, when boys buy themselves nuts, lest they be deceived, they weigh them in the hand and, when they have found one heavy, they more securely keep what is still closed. Weigh, then, when you hear certain sentences closed in such holy Scriptures, so illustrious, known to the whole world, spread throughout the whole earth; from the time they are preached up to this time, nothing else has been done in human affairs than what was foretold there. A great weight, then, of authority. According to this weight weigh the sentences, and let your soul—which perhaps already wished to despise it—say to you what was said: *And they shall be two in one flesh.* Now it was saying to itself: "What is this? Was God really going to take care how man and woman are mingled together, that he should say: *They shall be two in one flesh?*" Do not throw it away; you are a boy: weigh it. "And how," it says, "shall I weigh?" Say to yourself: "Truly any fool would say this, much more anyone whatever who is called a man of God. Moses, who wrote these things, had at least a middling heart." Add, that not without cause these writings have become known to the whole world and are honored throughout the whole world by the religion of believers: "Not without cause would he say: *They shall be two in one flesh,* unless there were there something, I know not what, which human thought shudders at, but it is in some way closed. It is not empty." When you have said these things to yourself, you weigh it; if you have weighed it, you have found that it is heavy; you hold it securely. But perhaps you are so weak a boy that you cannot break it: only hold it and rejoice, know that you hold something full; there will not be lacking one to break it and feed you. "And who," it says, "will break it?" There is indeed one to break it—as if we are dealing with a little child—give it to a certain kindly father who said: *Not to confound you do I say these things, but I admonish you as my dearest sons*[^51]. See, there is the apostle—in a certain sure way he is a father—he breaks for you this which you carry closed, which you have already weighed and felt to be heavy. He will break it—do not fear—who has toward you both paternal love, when he says: *Though you have many tutors in Christ, yet not many fathers. For in Christ Jesus through the Gospel I begot you*[^52], and maternal, when he says: *I became a little one in your midst, like a nurse cherishing her sons*[^53]. {Therefore he did not say "mother," because sometimes mothers either too delicate or not loving their sons, when they have given birth, hand them to others to be nursed. Again, if he had only said: *like a nurse cherishing,* and had not added: *her sons,* he would seem to have received them, born of another, to be nursed. Therefore he both called himself a nurse, because he was nourishing, and his sons, because he himself had given birth, saying: *My little sons, whom I again bring to birth, until Christ be formed in you*[^54]. But he brings to birth, as the Church gives birth, by her womb, not by her seed.} Give, then, to this father or mother—call it what you will: he is not angry, since he willed to be both in affection, though he is neither in sex—give to be broken what you carry shut up, so heavy, of such great authority. In the book of Genesis it is written: it is no light thing, something there lies hidden shut up. Does he not seem to have said something who said *sacrament?* "For I perceive: it is heavy, but it is still closed." *But I,* he says, *speak in Christ and in the Church.* See the foods: feed, you who did not despise the closed thing. But he who despises the closed thing and throws it away will not arrive at the food.

**23.** One thing comes to my mind from another, since I have mentioned a nut, which indeed we weave most fittingly into this sermon, because it came about that we should treat of closed sacraments. For not in vain did Jacob set three variegated rods in the water, whence the flocks, gathered at their time, might drink, and he willed that they be not of one wood, but of diverse. For the effect of what he was contriving it would have sufficed that they be of one wood, nor was it needful that there be three, but either fewer or more, provided only he set variegated rods in the waters. What, then, does it mean that he set three rods and of three woods, unless because it intimates a mystery, but this is closed? According to the strength which the Lord deigns to grant us, I will break and open it to you. Jacob had made a pact with his father-in-law whose sheep he pastured, that, if anything variegated should be born either among the sheep or among the goats, it should be his, that is, should pertain to the wage of the shepherd. This he procured by the variegated rods, which, when the flocks saw them at conception, by such a desire impressed through the eyes they would bear variegated offspring. In the variegated flocks was signified the variety of the nations. And those flocks were of one manner, and conceived variegated and bore variegated. For from the one Jewish nation arose the first preachers of the Gospel, but, that many nations might be begotten, it was needful that they conceive variegated and bear variegated. And this is the *portion of Jacob.* For in Jacob Christ was prefigured. For the lesser people was in Jacob, whence it was said: *And the greater shall serve the lesser*[^55]. You will recall that I have already expounded to your holiness about Esau and Jacob, to whom too it was said in that blessing which he received from his father: *All nations shall serve you*[^56]. The variety of the nations, then, pertained to Jacob, but unless from the people of the Jews there were announcers, so that the flocks of one manner might conceive from that drink of the rods, they would not bear the faithful through the variety of the nations.

**24.** But whence would the flocks conceive the variety of the nations? From the three rods, namely, this could indeed be done. For the animals were at conception, when he variegated the rods, that is, peeled them in places at intervals, and set them in the waters, whence they might draw in drinking the desire of variety which would appear in the colors of those being born. But from any number and from any rods this could be done! But the sacrament of the future Christian people was not known to the people of the Jews, except to a few holy prophets and a few envious doctors of the law, to whom the Lord says: *Woe to you who have the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and neither do you enter nor do you permit others to enter*[^57]. Who are signified also in that parable, where the tenants of the vineyard who did not render the wage said: *This is the heir. Come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours*[^58]—which they would not say, unless they had in some part known Christ, whose divinity, however, by which he is equal to the Father, was hidden from them: *For if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*[^59]. But he sent preachers not from their number, who *chose the weak things of the world, that he might confound the strong,* so that it might be said: *Where is the wise man? where is the scribe? where is the disputer of this world? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?*[^60] Wherefore the sacrament which was hidden from them, revealed to the unlearned and uneducated, was, through the baptism of Christ, intimated also to the diverse nations: therefore from the three rods set in the water the flocks conceived variegated offspring. For as long as God the Father was preached, but the incarnation of the Son was hidden, which was still being foretold prophetically and understood by very few, the variety of the nations was not brought forth. But it came about that those sheep drank from the three rods, that is, the first Israelites, through whom there would be born, pertaining to the lot of Jacob—that is, to the inheritance of Christ—the variety of the nations; of which Israelites the Apostle says: *For I too am an Israelite of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin*[^61]. For Israelites too were Peter and Andrew and John and James and the other apostles and the rest of the first preachers of Christ, of whom the Apostle says that the nations are *their debtors:* *For if,* he says, *the nations have shared in their spiritual things, they ought also to minister to them in carnal things*[^62]. Those Israelites, then, were as it were flocks of one manner, because they were of one nation: they drank in a manner the sacrament of the Lord's incarnation, that through the sacrament of the Lord's incarnation they might bear in the Gospel diverse nations, that is, a variety of flocks.

**25.** How, then, is the sacrament of the Lord's incarnation shown in the three rods? Of what were those rods? One was *of nut-wood,* another was *of plane,* another *of storax.* So it is written. Let us, then, question our own faith how the faith of the Lord's incarnation stands. For we believe him born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary. His very birth pertains to the nut-rod. For as in a nut through the wood one arrives at the food, so our Lord Jesus Christ would not carry us, unless we arrived at his body through the wood of the cross. Which is so clear that you anticipated my voice by understanding; which, before I explained what I had begun to say, you declared by your own voice. Who led you to this food, that you so quickly understood, but he who hung on the wood? For unless through the cross of the Lord, you would not be Christians; if you were not Christians, you would not so quickly and so sweetly receive this. The rod of plane, to what does it pertain? We say that Christ was born of the Holy Spirit. I think the rod of plane congruently pertains to the Holy Spirit. For the rod of storax which remains is not doubted to be attributed, on account of its most sweet odor, to the inviolate integrity of the Virgin Mary. For from this the report of the most pleasant fragrance and sweet smell of the Lord's nativity arose, that he was born of a virgin. About the rod of plane the understanding is more laborious, how it is shown to pertain to the Holy Spirit. The Lord will be present, your prayers aiding, and will open to you through our ministry and through our humble and devout service to your progress, how we ought to take the rod of plane as the Holy Spirit. In the plane I seek what I may choose, and I find no other reason for the plane tree to be praised, except that it provides a very wide shade to those resting from the heat. Those who know such a tree to be so understand that I speak the truth. For this the plane tree is chosen and sought, on account of the breadth of its shade and the delight of its shadiness, where we rest from the heat. But the Virgin Mary, who was going to conceive a son not by the heat of lust, but in a certain coolness of most faithful chastity and uncorrupted virginity, not gaping after virile embraces, but conceiving by faith, a virgin pregnant, a virgin about to give birth, a virgin to remain—she, then, received of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit himself provided her a coolness from the heat of all carnal concupiscence, and therefore was figured by the rod of plane. I lie, if in the very Gospel the angel does not speak and say to her: *The Spirit of God shall come upon you, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow you*[^63].

**26.** Now, then, dearly beloved, as the Lord willed, he provides something not from our merit, but on account of our faith which he himself deigns. As we always say and must never keep silent: let every advance of the word be found in your works. Very wretched is the earth which, when it is well rained on, either bears no fruit or even brings forth thorns. Grieve with us over those whom we lament. We are wont to say that fasting in these festal days of the pagans is to be practiced for this, that we may pray to God for those very pagans. But we so shudder at the unhappy luxuries of many that we exhort you, brethren, to pray with us for certain Christian brethren, that they may at length suffer themselves to be amended and corrected from that wickedness. For what is this, how great and how much to be lamented an evil! He does not despise such trifling, such empty amusements: what is such a Christian going to despise, what is he going to suffer for Christ, when some temptation of tribulation comes? Whom spittle overwhelms, how does a river sweep him away? Therefore our grief, brethren, I shall not have expressed in vain to your holiness. Let those who are here today who did not fast yesterday grieve that they so lived on the other festal days of the pagans, with us sad for them, and let them at length deign both to take away sadness from us and wickedness from themselves.
