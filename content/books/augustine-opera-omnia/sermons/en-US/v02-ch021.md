# Sermon 33/A

**1** Of the words of this psalm which we just sang, may the Lord grant that we be able to speak to you. For we said: *I will praise the Lord in my life; I will sing to my God as long as I live*[^1]. By these words let us first warn your Charity that, when you hear or say *as long as I live, I will sing to my God*, you not suppose that when this life is finished the praise of God will be finished for us. For we shall praise the more then, when we live without end. For if we praise in the pilgrimage from which we pass over, how shall we praise in the home from which we shall never migrate? As it is said and read and sung in another psalm: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house; forever and ever they will praise you*[^2]. Where you hear *forever and ever*, there is no end. And to live the blessed life, where God is without doubt beheld, loved without offense, praised without end—this very thing will be our living: to see, to love, to praise God. If, then, we praise when we believe, how shall we praise when we shall see? How shall sight praise, if faith praises thus? For the Apostle says: *For as long as we are in the body, we are away from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^3]. Now, then, by faith, then by sight. Now we believe what we do not see, then we shall see what we believed. He who believes is not confounded, because what he will see is true. But our Lord first built faith in us, where, if the reward of faith is rendered, it is not sought beforehand out of order.

**2** Someone says: "Why, then, did the psalm say, *I will sing to my God as long as I live*, and not say, I will sing to my God forever?" For where it is said *as long as I live*, an end is, as it were, signified—but only if it is not understood. If you think it was said of this life, *as long as I live*, examine this life, whether here it is "long." However long you live here, it is not long. How is that long which does not satisfy you? A boy says a man has lived long whom he sees old. But when he has come to where that man has come, then he sees how it was not long. So utterly does age fly, so do the moments run by within it, that we see that the day before yesterday we were boys, yesterday young men, today old men. Where, then, you think it was said of this life, *as long as I live, I will sing to my God*, there is a truthful understanding that it was not said of this life, because he said *as long as*. For truth would never say *as long as* of this life, where nothing is long. The wise of this world were able to see this, and cannot Christians see it? A certain one of the wise of the world, a most eloquent man, said: *For what is this very "long," in which there is something final?*[^4] He altogether denied that "long" which could at some time come to an end. Utterly at some time, not however long you have lived, if perhaps you have come to extreme old age. For the life of one man, especially in this age, *is a vapor appearing for a little while*[^5]. What I said, Scripture said. To men exulting, and prevailing in much through pride, and not knowing whether they will die at once, the divine Scripture spoke, and warned them, so proud and trusting in vain things, of their transitory frailty. *For what is your life?* it says. *It is a vapor appearing for a little while, and then it will be done away*[^6]. He, then, who raises himself in elation trusts in a vapor; he is lifted up in honor, and perishes with the vapor. Pride is therefore to be repressed, and trodden underfoot with all the effort we can. And we must understand that we live mortally on this earth, and we must think of the end where there will be no end. For not even if you, as I had begun to say, whoever you are who have grown old, are greatly lifted up, if you think you have lived long, you who will at some time come to an end—but even Adam himself, if he were still living, and were to die not now but at the end of the world, would have nothing long, in which there was something final. And it is most truly said, and prudently understood, and not only is it preached to be true, but it is even acknowledged by the hearers.

**3** Let us bring our mind back to the psalm we sang, and find that he would not say, *I will sing to my God as long as I live*, except of that life where there is "long." For if in this life there is for this reason nothing long, because there is something final, we are not called, when we are made Christians, to long for this life. For we are not made Christians in order that it may be well with us in this life. For if we think we are therefore made Christians, that it should be well with us here according to this temporal life, according to flitting and vaporous happiness, we err greatly, and our feet will totter, seeing someone prevail in such dignity that he stands out above the rest with whom he lives, be sound in body, come to decrepit old age. The poor Christian sees this, the lowly one, sighing daily in groans at his labor, and perhaps says within himself: "What does it profit me that I have been made a Christian? Am I therefore better than the one who is not, than the one who does not believe in Christ, than the one who blasphemes my God?" The psalm warns him: *Do not trust in princes*[^7]. Why does the flower of the grass delight you? *All flesh is grass*[^8]—the prophet speaks; not only speaks, but cries out. The Lord cried out to him: *Cry out*, he says. And he answered: *What shall I cry out? All flesh is grass, and all the honor of the flesh is like the flower of grass. The grass withered, the flower fell*[^9]. Then did the whole perish? Far be it. *But the word of the Lord abides forever*[^10]. Why does the grass delight you? See, the grass perished. Do you want not to perish? Hold to the Word. So too in this psalm. For the poor, lowly Christian was perhaps attending to some rich and powerful pagan, was attending to the flower of the grass, and was perhaps choosing rather to have him for a patron than God. To him the psalm speaks: *Do not trust in princes, and in the sons of men, in whom there is no salvation*[^11]. He at once replies: "Does it speak of this one, in whom there is salvation? See, he is sound. Today I see him vigorous. I, rather, am constantly a wretched invalid." Why do you attend to these things that please you and delight you? There is no salvation. *His spirit will go forth, and he will return to his earth*[^12]. See, all his salvation, *a vapor appearing for a little while*. *His spirit will go forth, and he will return to his earth*. Let some years pass. Let a river be stirred, as usually happens, running here and there through some tombs of the dead. Distinguish the bones of the rich from the bones of the poor. When his spirit goes forth, he returns to his earth. Most excellently he said nothing of his spirit, because that man too, when he lived, thought nothing spiritually. *He will return to his earth*, according to the flesh, surely, according to the body, where he prevailed, where he was puffed up, where he deceived you, in the happiness of whose flesh he beguiled you. *His spirit will go forth, and he will return to his earth; on that day all their thoughts will perish*[^13]. Those thoughts which were earthly: "See, I do this, see, I accomplish this, see, I arrive here, see, I buy that, see, I acquire that, see, I come to such and such an honor"—*on that day all his thoughts will perish*. But if, since *the word of the Lord abides forever*, you hold to the Word, that he may give you eternal life, not only will your thought then not perish, but then it comes. When his perishes, then yours comes. For he was thinking of temporal and earthly things, to add wealth to wealth, to heap up monetary means, to shine in honors, to swell in power. Therefore, because he was thinking such things, *on that day all his thoughts will perish*. But you, if you were thinking, because you have been made a Christian, not of temporal happiness, but of everlasting rest—when your body has returned to its earth, then your soul finds its rest.

**4** Attend to the Gospel, and see and inspect the thoughts of two men. *There was a certain rich man, who was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day*[^14]. Every day grass, and the flower of grass. Let not his happiness seduce you, *who was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day*. He was proud, he was impious, he thought vain things, he desired vain things. When he died, on that day his thoughts perished. *But there was at his gate a certain poor man named* Lazarus[^15]. He was silent about the name of the rich man and spoke the name of the poor man. The one whose name was talked about, his God was silent about; the one about whose name there was silence, him God named. Do not marvel. God recited what he found written in his book. For of the impious it was said: *And in your book they will not be written*[^16]. Likewise to the apostles, when they gloried because the demons had been made subject to them in the name of the Lord, lest about this matter, like men, they should be lifted up and boast of themselves—though about so great a matter, though about a glorious power: *Do not*, he said, *rejoice in this, that the demons are subject to you; but rejoice, that your names are written in heaven*[^17]. For God, the inhabitant of heaven, was silent about the name of the rich man, because he did not find it written in heaven. He spoke the name of the poor man, because he found it written there—or rather, he commanded it to be written there. But see that poor man. For we have spoken of the thoughts of the impious rich man, splendid, clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasting splendidly every day, because, when he had died, all his thoughts perished. But the poor man Lazarus was at the gate of the rich man, *full of sores, and longed to be filled with the crumbs that fell from the rich man's table, and no one gave them to him; but even the dogs came and licked his sores*[^18]. There I want to see you, Christian: for the deaths of these two are told. God is indeed able both to give salvation in this life, and to take away want, and to grant sufficiency to a Christian. And yet, even if this were not so, what would you choose? To be poor like that man, or to be rich like the other? Let it not deceive you. Hear the end, and observe the evil choice. For without doubt that poor man, since he was devout, set in temporal hardships, was thinking that that life would at some time end and that eternal rest would be obtained. Both died, but on that day the thoughts of that poor man did not perish. For it happened that the needy man died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom. On that day all his thoughts were healed. And since Lazarus in the Latin tongue is rendered "helped"—in the Latin tongue he is called "helped" who in Hebrew is Lazarus—the psalm warned well: *Blessed is he whose helper is the God of Jacob*[^19]. When his spirit goes forth, and his flesh returns to its earth, his thoughts will not perish, because *his hope is in the Lord his God*[^20]. This is learned in the school of Christ the teacher, this is hoped for by the spirit of the faithful hearer, this is the most true reward of the Savior.
