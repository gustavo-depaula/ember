# Sermon 105

**1.** We have heard our Lord, the heavenly master and most faithful counselor, exhorting us—the same who urges us to ask, and is the giver when we ask. We have heard him in the Gospel exhorting us to ask him insistently, and to knock even to the likeness of importunity. For he set before us, by way of example: If any of you had a friend, from whom he asked three loaves at night, when a friend had come to him from a journey, and he had nothing to set before him: but if that friend should answer that he was already resting, and his servants with him, and that he ought not to be troubled by his entreaties—but if the other should keep insisting by knocking and persevere, and not depart frightened off by shame, but, compelled by necessity, press on: he would rise, even if not for friendship's sake, certainly because of the other's importunity, and would give him as many as he wished [^1]. But how many did he want? That man wanted nothing more than three. In this likeness, then, the Lord added an exhortation, and altogether spurred us to ask, to seek, to knock, until we receive what we ask, what we seek, for what we knock, using a contrasting example: as about that judge who neither feared God nor reverenced men, and yet, when a certain widow appealed to him daily, overcome by weariness, gave what he could not, unwilling, of kindness [^2]. But our Lord Jesus Christ, a petitioner among us, a giver with the Father, would surely not exhort us only to ask, unless he wished to give. Let human sloth blush: he wants to give more than we to receive: he wants to have mercy more than we to be freed from misery: and surely, if we are not freed, we shall remain wretched. For he, in that he exhorts us, exhorts us for our own sake.

**2.** Let us awake, and believe the one who exhorts, obey the one who promises, and rejoice toward the one who gives. For perhaps a friend has sometime come to us too from a journey, and we did not find what to set before him; and we suffered need, and received both for ourselves and for him. For it cannot but be that someone has suffered a friend asking something which he could not answer: and then he finds himself not to have, when he is compelled to give. A friend comes to you from a journey, that is, from the life of this world, in which all pass like pilgrims, nor does any remain as possessor; but to every man it is said: *You have been refreshed, pass on; make your journey, give place to the one coming*. Or perhaps some friend of yours, wearied from an evil way, that is, an evil life, not finding the truth, by hearing and grasping which he might become blessed, but exhausted in all the desire and want of the world, comes to you, as to a Christian, and says: Give me an account, make me a Christian. And he asks what perhaps you, through the simplicity of faith, did not know: and there is nothing from which you may refresh the hungry, and, admonished, you find yourself in want; and when you want to teach, you are compelled to learn: and while you blush before the one who questioned, not finding in yourself what he sought, you are compelled to seek, that you may deserve to find.  
And where will you seek? Where, except in the Lord's Books? Perhaps what he asked is set down in a book, but it is obscure. Perhaps the Apostle said this in his Letter. He said it in such a way that you can read it, you cannot understand it: you are not permitted to pass over. For the questioner urges; you are not allowed to question Paul himself, or Peter, or some Prophet. For now that household rests with its Lord, and the ignorance of this world is strong, that is, it is midnight, and the hungry friend urges. Perhaps simple faith sufficed for you, it does not suffice for him. Is he to be deserted? is he to be cast out of the house?

**3.** Therefore to the Lord himself, to him with whom the household rests, knock by praying, ask, insist. Not as that friend set in the likeness, overcome by weariness, will he rise and give. He wants to give: you, knocking, have not yet received; knock, he wants to give. And what he wants to give, he defers, that you may desire the deferred thing more, lest what is quickly given grow cheap.  
But when you have come to the three loaves, that is, to the food and understanding of the Trinity, you have both that from which you live, and that from which you may feed. And do not dread the pilgrim coming from the way, but by receiving him make him a domestic citizen: and do not fear lest you run out. That bread will not run out, but will end your need. It is bread, and bread, and bread: God the Father, God the Son, God the Holy Spirit. Eternal Father, coeternal Son, coeternal Holy Spirit. Unchangeable Father, unchangeable Son, unchangeable Holy Spirit. Creator both Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. Shepherd and giver of life, both Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. Food and eternal bread, both Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. Learn, and teach; live, and feed. God, who gives to you, gives you nothing better than himself. Greedy man, what else were you seeking? Or if you seek something else, what suffices you, for whom God does not suffice?

**4.** But there is need that you have love, have faith, have hope: that what is given may be able to be sweet to you. And these very things are three, faith, hope, love. And these very things are gifts of God. For faith we received from him: *As God*, he says, *has apportioned to each a measure of faith* [^3]. And hope we received from him, to whom it is said: *In which you have given me hope* [^4]. And love we received from him, of which it is said: *The love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us* [^5]. But these three likewise are somewhat diverse; but all gifts of God. *For these three remain, faith, hope, love: but the greater of these is love* [^6]. Among those loaves no loaf was said to be greater than the others: but simply three loaves were asked and given.  
Behold, another three: *Who of you, if his son asks him for bread, will hold out a stone to him? Or who of you, if he asks for a fish, will hold out a serpent to him? Or if he asks for an egg, will hold out a scorpion to him? If, then, you, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your sons; how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him* [^7]? Let us, then, consider these three again, in case those three are there, faith, hope, love: but the greater of these is love. Set, then, three things, bread, fish, egg: the greater of these is bread. Therefore we rightly understand by bread, among these three, love. For this reason he set a stone opposite to bread, because hardness is contrary to love. By the fish we understand faith. A certain holy man said, and it delights us to say it: The good fish is devout faith. It lives among the waves, nor is it broken or dissolved by the waves. Devout faith lives among the temptations and storms of this world: the world rages, and it is whole. Only watch against that serpent contrary to faith. For in faith she is betrothed, she to whom it is said in the Song of Songs: *Come from Lebanon, my bride, coming and passing through from the beginning of faith* [^8]. And for this reason betrothed, because the beginning of betrothal is faith. For something is promised by the bridegroom, and she is held by the promise believed. But the Lord set the serpent opposite to the fish, the devil opposite to faith. Therefore to this betrothed one the Apostle says: *I have betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ* [^9]: and: *I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so also your minds be corrupted from the chastity which is in Christ* [^10]; that is, which is in the faith of Christ. *For Christ to dwell*, he says, *by faith in your hearts* [^11]. Let the devil, then, not corrupt faith, let him not devour the fish.

**5.** There remains hope, which, as it seems to me, is compared to the egg. For hope has not yet come to the thing: and an egg is something, but it is not yet a chick. Four-footed animals, then, bring forth their young, but birds the hope of their young. Hope, then, exhorts us to this, that we despise present things, await things to come; forgetting what is behind, that we stretch out with the Apostle to what is before. For he says thus: *But one thing, forgetting what is behind, stretching out to what is before, according to my intent I follow on to the prize of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus* [^12]. Nothing, then, is so hostile to hope as to look back, that is, to set hope in those things which slip past and pass away: but in those which have not yet been given, but are one day to be given, which will never pass away. But when the world swarms with temptations, like the sulfurous rain of Sodom, the example of Lot's wife must be feared. For she looked back; and where she looked, there she remained. She was turned into salt [^13], to season the prudent by her example. The apostle Paul speaks thus of this hope: *For in hope we were saved. But hope that is seen is not hope: for what a man sees, why does he hope for? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience* [^14]. *For what a man sees, why does he hope for?* It is an egg. It is an egg, and is not yet a chick. And it is covered with a shell: it is not seen, because it is covered; let it be awaited with patience; let it grow warm, that it may come to life. Be intent, stretch out to what is before, forget the past. For the things that are seen are temporal. *Not looking*, he says, *at the things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen. For the things that are seen are temporal; but the things that are not seen are eternal* [^15]. On those things, then, which are not seen, stretch out your hope: await, endure. Do not look back. Fear the scorpion for your egg. See that it strikes with the tail, which it has behind. Let not the scorpion, then, destroy your egg, this world your hope, so to speak, with that poison which is contrary, which is in the tail behind. How much the world says to you, how much it clamors behind your back, that you may look back: that is, that you may set your hope in present things (and not even present; for things that never stand still are not to be called present); and that you may turn your mind away from what Christ promised and has not yet given, but, because he is faithful, will give, and may want to rest in a perishing world.

**6.** For God mingles bitternesses with earthly happinesses, that another happiness may be sought, whose sweetness is not deceptive: and from these very bitternesses the world tries to turn you away from that toward which you stretch out before, and to turn you back. From these very bitternesses, from these very tribulations you murmur, and say: Behold, everything perishes in Christian times. Why do you clamor? God did not promise me this, that these things would not perish: Christ did not promise me this. The Eternal promised eternal things: if I believe, from mortal I shall become eternal. Why do you clamor, O unclean world? why do you clamor? Why do you try to turn me away? You want to hold me, perishing yourself: what would you do, if you remained? Whom would you not deceive, sweet, if, bitter, you feign nourishment? If I have hope, if I hold hope, my egg has not been struck by the scorpion. *I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall be always in my mouth* [^16]. Let the world be happy, let the world be overthrown: *I will bless the Lord*, who made the world. *I will bless* him utterly. According to the flesh let it go well, according to the flesh let it go badly: *I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall be always in my mouth*. For if I bless when it goes well, and blaspheme when it goes badly, I have received the scorpion's sting, pricked I have looked back; which be far from us. *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it was done: blessed be the name of the Lord* [^17].

**7.** The city that bore us carnally remains. Thanks be to God. Would that it might be generated spiritually too, and pass with us to eternity. If the city that bore us carnally does not remain, the one that bore us spiritually remains. *The Lord building Jerusalem* [^18]. Did he, by sleeping, lose his building, or, by not guarding it, let the enemy in? *Unless the Lord guards the city, in vain does he keep watch who guards it* [^19]. And what city? *He who guards Israel does not sleep, nor will he slumber* [^20]. What is Israel, except the seed of Abraham? What is the seed of Abraham? except Christ? *And to your seed*, he says, *which is Christ* [^21]. And what of us? *But you are Christ's: therefore you are the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise* [^22]. *In your seed*, he says, *all the nations will be blessed* [^23]. A holy city, a faithful city, a city on earth a pilgrim, founded in heaven. O faithful one, do not corrupt hope, do not lose love, gird your loins, ascend, hold out your lamps, *await the Lord, when he comes from the wedding* [^24]. Why are you terrified, that earthly kingdoms perish? For this reason the heavenly was promised you, lest you perish with the earthly. For these things were foretold to perish, foretold altogether. For we cannot deny what was foretold. Your Lord whom you await said to you: *Nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom* [^25]. Earthly kingdoms have changes: he will come of whom it was said: *And of his kingdom there will be no end* [^26].  
Those who promised this to earthly kingdoms were not led by truth, but lied out of flattery. A certain poet of theirs brought in Jupiter speaking, and said of the Romans:  
*For these I set no bounds of power or time;  
I have given empire without end.*  
The truth plainly does not answer thus. This kingdom, which you gave without end, O you who gave nothing, is it on earth, or in heaven? Surely on earth. And even if it were in heaven: *Heaven and earth will pass away* [^27]. The things that God himself made will pass away; how much sooner what Romulus founded? Perhaps, if we wished to harry Virgil from this and to taunt him, why he said this; in part he would take us up, and would say to us: I too know it; but what was I to do, who was selling words to the Romans, unless by this flattery I promised something that was false? And yet even in this I was cautious, when I said, *I have given empire without end*, I brought in their own Jupiter, who said this. I did not speak a false thing in my own person, but I imposed on Jupiter the person of falsity: as God was false, so the seer was a liar. For do you want to know that I knew these things? In another place, when I did not bring in Jupiter the stone speaking, but spoke in my own person, I said:  
*Not the Roman state and kingdoms destined to perish.*  
See that I said the kingdoms would perish. I said the kingdoms would perish, I did not keep silent. That they would perish, by the truth he did not keep silent: that they would remain forever, by flattery he promised.

**8.** Therefore let us not lose heart, brothers: there will be an end to all earthly kingdoms. Whether the end is now, God sees. For perhaps it is not yet, and out of a certain weakness, or mercy, or misery, we wish that it not yet be: yet shall it for that reason not be? Fix your hope in God, desire eternal things, await eternal things. You are Christians, brothers, we are Christians. Christ did not come down into the flesh for pleasures: let us rather endure present things than love them: of adverse things the ruin is manifest, of prosperous things the flattery is false. Fear the sea even when it is calm. Not in vain at all do we hear: *Lift up your heart*. Why do we set our heart on earth, when we see that the earth is overthrown? We can only exhort you, that you may have what to say, and what to answer for your hope to the mockers and blasphemers of the Christian name. Let no one by murmuring turn you away from the expectation of things to come. All who, on account of these adverse things, blaspheme our Christ are the scorpion's tail. Let us set our egg under the wings of that gospel hen, who cries: *Jerusalem, Jerusalem*—to that false and lost city—*how often have I wished to gather your children, as a hen her chicks, and you would not* [^28]? Let it not be said to us: *How often have I wished, and you would not?* For that hen is the divine Wisdom: but she took on flesh, that she might suit her chicks. See the hen, bristled in feathers, with drooping wings, with broken voice, and shaken, and weary, and faint, suiting herself to her little ones. Let us set our egg, then, that is, our hope, under the wings of that hen.

**9.** You have perhaps noticed how a hen cuts a scorpion to pieces. Would, then, that that hen might also cut to pieces and devour these blasphemers, creeping on the earth, coming out of their caverns, and stinging wickedly; that she might pass them into her body, and turn them into an egg. Let them not be angry: we seem stirred; but we do not return curses for curses. We are cursed and we bless, blasphemed we entreat [^29]. But let no one say: He spoke of Rome, it was said of me: O if only he would keep silent about Rome: as though I were a mocker, and not rather an entreater of the Lord, and your exhorter, such as I am. Far be it from me to mock. May God turn it away from my heart, and from the grief of my conscience. Did we not have many brothers there? do we not still have them? Did not a portion of the pilgrim city of Jerusalem dwell there in great numbers? did it not there endure temporal things? but it did not lose eternal things. What, then, do I say, when I do not keep silent about her, except that what they say of our Christ is false—that he himself destroyed Rome, that gods of stone and of wood guarded Rome? Add cost: of bronze. Add more: of silver and gold: *The idols of the nations are silver and gold* [^30]. He did not say: Stone; he did not say: Wood; he did not say: Earthenware: but what they hold for great, *silver and gold*. Yet that very silver and gold *have eyes, and do not see* [^31]. Gods of gold and gods of wood are unequal in cost: in having eyes and not seeing, they are equal. Behold to what guardians learned men entrusted Rome—guardians having eyes, and not seeing. Or if they could preserve Rome, why did they themselves perish first? They say: Then Rome perished. Yet they perished. Not, they say, did they themselves perish, but their images. How then could they guard your roofs, who could not guard their own images? Alexandria long ago lost such gods. Constantinople, from the time it was founded as a great city—since it was founded by a Christian emperor—long ago lost those very false gods: and yet it both grew, and grows, and remains. As long as God wills, it remains. For we do not, because we say this, promise eternity to that city either. Carthage remains in the name of Christ, and Caelestis was long ago overthrown, because she was not heavenly, but earthly.

**10.** And what they say is not true, that Rome was at once captured and afflicted when the gods perished. It is utterly untrue: those very images were overthrown before; and so the Goths were conquered along with Radagaisus. Remember, brothers, remember: it is not long ago, it is a few years, recall it. When all the images in the city of Rome had been overthrown, Radagaisus, king of the Goths, came with a huge army, much more numerous than Alaric's was. Radagaisus was a pagan man: he sacrificed to Jupiter daily. It was reported everywhere that Radagaisus did not cease from sacrifices. Then all those said: Behold, we do not sacrifice, he sacrifices; we are bound to be conquered by the one sacrificing, we to whom it is not lawful to sacrifice. God, showing that temporal welfare itself, earthly kingdoms themselves, do not lie in those sacrifices, Radagaisus was conquered, with the Lord's help, in a wondrous way. Afterward came the Goths, who did not sacrifice—though by Christian faith they were not Catholics, yet enemies of idols; they came as opponents of idols, and they themselves captured the city: they conquered who presumed upon idols, and who still sought the lost idols, and who still wished to sacrifice to the lost ones. But there too were our people, and they were afflicted: but they knew how to say: *I will bless the Lord at all times* [^32]. They were afflicted in the earthly kingdom: but they did not lose the kingdom of heaven: rather, by the exercise of tribulations, they were made better for laying hold of it. And if in tribulations they did not blaspheme, they came out, as it were, whole vessels from the furnace, and were filled with the Lord's blessing. But these blasphemers, pursuing earthly things, desiring earthly things, setting their hope in earthly things—when, willy-nilly, they have lost them, what will they hold? where will they remain? Outside nothing, within nothing: an empty chest, an emptier conscience. Where is rest? where is salvation? where is hope? Let them come, then, let them cease to blaspheme, let them learn to adore: let the stinging scorpions be eaten by the hen, let them be turned into the body of the one passing them on; let them be exercised on earth, let them be crowned in heaven.
