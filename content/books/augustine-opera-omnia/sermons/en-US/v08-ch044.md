# Sermon 302: On the Birthday of Saint Lawrence

**1.** Today is the solemn day of the blessed martyr Lawrence. To this holy feast the fitting readings have sounded. We have heard and sung, and most attentively received the Gospel reading. Let us then follow the footsteps of the martyrs by imitating them, lest we celebrate their feasts in vain. But who does not know how great is the merit of the martyr mentioned? Who has prayed there, and not obtained? To how many sick has his merit furnished even temporal benefits, which he himself despised. For they were granted, not that the weakness of those praying might remain, but that, from the earthly things granted, love might arise for seeking the better. For often a father grants his little children certain small and playful things, over which they cry most of all if they do not receive them. Kindly and fatherly indulgence imparts these, gives these, which he does not wish to remain in his sons when they are already somewhat grown, already advancing. So he gives nuts to boys, for whom he keeps an inheritance. To those who play and amuse themselves with certain trifles fatherly love yields, lest the weakness of their age fail. This belongs to one who soothes, not to one who builds up. What the martyrs built up, what they were able to grasp, what they grasped with a great heart, for which they poured out their blood, you have heard in the Gospel: *Your reward is great in heaven*[^1].

**2.** Yet, dearly beloved, since there are two lives, one before death, the other after death, both of these lives have had and have their lovers. What this brief life is like, what need is there to describe? We have experienced how full of hardship, how full of complaint; surrounded by temptations, full of fears; burning with covetous desires, subject to mishaps; in adversity grieving, in prosperity swelling; exulting in gains, tortured in losses. And in the very gains it trembles amid exultation, lest it lose what it has acquired; lest he be sought after for this who, before he had it, was not sought after. True unhappiness, deceptive happiness. The lowly longs to rise, the exalted fears to descend. He who has not envies him who has; he who has despises him who has not. And who can unfold in words the great and so conspicuous foulness of this life? And yet this foulness has lovers such that we may wish to find very few who love the eternal life, which they cannot end, in the way this is loved, which both ends quickly, and, if it be prolonged, is feared every day lest it end hour by hour. What shall we do? What shall we do? What shall we say? What goads of warning, what fires of exhortation, shall we apply to hard and sluggish hearts, frozen with the ice of earthly numbness, that they may at some time shake off the torpor of the world, and burn for things eternal? What, I say, shall we do? What shall we say? It lies at hand, and meanwhile occurs to me, that the daily things themselves admonish us, and suggest what we should say. From the love of this temporal life, come, if it can be done, to loving the eternal life, which the martyrs loved, who despised these temporal things. I ask, I beseech, I exhort, not only you, but with you us also: let us love the eternal life. I do not wish more—though it is more abundant—that we love it as the temporal is loved by its lovers, not as the temporal life was loved by the holy martyrs. For they either did not love it at all, or loved it least, and easily preferred the everlasting to it. So I did not look to the martyrs when I said: Let us love the eternal as the temporal is loved: but as the temporal is loved by its lovers, so let us love the eternal, whose love a Christian professes.

**3.** For we have been made Christians for this, not for this temporal life. For how many Christians are snatched away untimely, and sacrilegious men endure in this life to decrepit old age? But again, among them too many die untimely. Many losses of Christians, and gains of the impious: and again many losses of the impious, and gains of Christians. And many honors of the impious and humiliations of Christians: and again many honors of Christians, and humiliations of the impious. So since these goods and evils are common to both, did we, brethren, when we were made Christians, give our name to Christ and submit our forehead to so great a sign in order to avoid these evils or obtain these goods? You are a Christian, you carry on your forehead the cross of Christ. Your mark teaches what you profess. When he hung on the cross—the cross you carry on your forehead; it is not the sign of the wood that delights you, but the sign of him who hung on it—when, then, he hung on the cross, he looked around at those raging, bore those insulting, prayed for his enemies. The physician, even while he was being killed, healed the sick by his blood. For he said: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^2]. Nor was that voice empty or vain. And of those very ones afterward thousands believed in him whom they had killed, that they might learn to suffer for him who suffered for them and from them. From this, then, brethren, it is understood, from this sign, from this mark which a Christian receives even when he becomes a catechumen—from this it is understood why we are Christians: because it is not for temporal and passing things, whether goods or evils, but to avoid evils that will not pass, and to obtain goods that will have no end.

**4.** Yet, as I had begun to say, brethren, what I had warned, what I had set forth, I beseech you, let us attend to how this temporal life is loved by its lovers. In how great fear are men lest they die, who are going to die! You may see a man tremble, flee, seek hiding places, hunt for defenses, plead, throw himself down; if it can be done, give whatever he has, that life may be granted, that he may live one day more, that his uncertain span may always be stretched out a little longer. Men do such great things: who does anything like this for eternal life? Let us address the lover of the present life: What are you doing, why do you hurry, why do you tremble, why do you flee, why do you seek hiding places? That I may live, he says. Truly that you may live? That you may live always going to live? No. So you are not trying to take away death, but to defer it. You who do such great things to die a little later, do something to never die.

**5.** How many do we find who say: Let the treasury take my goods, that I may die later: how rarely do we find one who says: Let Christ take my goods, that I may never die. And yet, O lover of temporal life, if the treasury takes them, it despoils you in this age; if Christ takes them, he keeps them for you in heaven. For this life men wish to have wherewith to live, and for this they wish to give what they live by. What you keep for yourself to live by, this you give that you may live, perhaps about to perish of hunger. And yet you say: Let him take it, what is it to me? I am willing to beg. You give what you live by, ready to beg that you may live. You are ready, even your necessities given away, to beg in this world; and you are not ready, your superfluous things distributed, to reign with Christ? I ask, weigh it. If any balance of fairness is found in the strongbox of your heart, bring it out, and put these two in it, and weigh: to beg in this world, and to reign with Christ. There is nothing to weigh. For in comparison with that thing this has no weight. If I said: to reign in this world, and to reign with Christ; there would be nothing to weigh. I am sorry I said: Weigh; there is simply nothing to weigh. *What does it profit a man, if he gain the whole world, but suffer the loss of his own soul?*[^3] But he who has not suffered the loss of his soul reigns with Christ. And who reigns secure in this world? Suppose he reigns secure: does he reign eternal?

**6.** Note this, what I was setting forth, what lovers the present life has, the temporal life, the brief life, the foul life, what lovers it has. Often a man becomes naked, a beggar, for this life. You ask him why? He answers thus: that I might live. What did you love, and what do you love? Where have you arrived? What will you say, evil lover, perverse lover? What will you say to this beloved of yours? Speak, address her, flatter her, if you can. What will you say? Your beauty has brought me to this nakedness. She cries out to you: I am foul, and do you love me? She cries out: I am hard, and do you embrace me? She cries out: I am flighty, and do you try to follow me? Behold, your beloved answers you: I will not stand with you; and if I am with you a little while, I will not remain with you; I could strip you, I could not make you blessed.

**7.** So, since we are Christians, having implored to our help the Lord our God against the blandishments of the evilly beloved, let us love the beauty of that life which *eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*. For this *God has prepared for those who love him*[^4]; and that life is God himself. You acclaimed, you sighed. Let us love this strongly. May the Lord grant that we love it. For it let us shed tears, not only that it may be obtained, but also that it may be loved. What shall we warn, what shall we show? Shall we recite books, to show how uncertain these things are, how transitory, how nearly nothing, how true is what is written: *For what is your life? It is a vapor appearing for a little while, then it will be done away*[^5]. He was living yesterday, he is not today; a little before he was seen, now he is not who was seen. A man is led to the tomb: they return sad, soon forgetting. It is said: How nothing is man, and man himself says this; and man does not correct himself, that man may be not nothing, but something. So the martyrs were the lovers of this life, and the martyrs are the acquirers of this life. They have what they loved; they will have it more abundantly in the resurrection of the dead. This road, then, they paved for us by their great sufferings.

**8.** Saint Lawrence was an archdeacon. The wealth of the Church was sought from him by the persecutor, as is handed down; whence he suffered so many things, which are dreadful to hear. Placed on a gridiron, he was scorched in all his members, tortured with the most atrocious punishments of the flames; yet conquering all the troubles of the body by the great strength of love, with the help of him who had made him such. *For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared, that we should walk in them*[^6]. But, to kindle the persecutor to anger, he did this, not wishing him to be angry, but desiring to commend his faith to posterity, and to show how secure he was as he died: Let the wagons go with me, he said, in which I may bring the wealth of the Church. The wagons were sent; he loaded them with the poor, and ordered them to return, saying: This is the wealth of the Church. And it is true, brethren, the needs of the poor are great wealth for Christians, if we understand where we should keep what we have. The needy are before us; if we keep it there, we shall not lose it. We do not fear lest anyone take it away: for he who gave it keeps it, nor can we find a better guardian, nor a more faithful one to promise.

**9.** Thinking of these things, then, let us imitate the martyrs untiringly, if we wish the feasts we celebrate to profit us. We have always admonished these things, brethren, we have never ceased, we have never kept silent. The eternal life is to be loved, the present despised. We must live well, hope for the good. He who is evil must be changed; the changed must be instructed; the instructed must persevere. *For he who perseveres to the end, this one will be saved*[^7].

**10.** But they say: Many evil men do many evils. And what would you wish? Good things from evil men? Do not look for the grape on thorns: you are forbidden. *Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks*[^8]. If you can do anything, if you yourself are no longer evil, wish the evil man to be good. Why do you rage against the evil? Because they are evil, you say. You add yourself to them by raging against them. I give counsel: does the evil man displease you? let there not be two. You reprehend, and you are joined to him: you increase the number of him you condemn. Do you wish to conquer evil by evil? to conquer malice by malice? There will be two malices, both to be conquered. Do you not hear the counsel of your Lord through the apostle: *Do not be conquered by evil, but conquer evil in good?*[^9] Perhaps he is worse: since you too are evil, yet there are two evil men. I would wish that at least one were good. Finally he rages even to death. What then after death, where punishment no longer reaches that evil man, and only the malice of the other evil man is exercised? This is to be mad, not to take vengeance.

**11.** What else shall I say to you, my brethren, what shall I say to you? Let such men not please you. Shall I really think this of you, that such men please you? Far be it from us to think this of you. But it is a small thing that such men displease you, a small thing; there is something to be demanded of you. Lest anyone say: God knows that I did not do it, God knows that I did not do it, and God knows that I did not wish it to be done. Behold, you have said two things: I did not do it, and I did not wish it to be done. Still it is a small thing. It is a very small thing if you did not wish it, if you did not also forbid it. Evil men have their judges, they have their powers, of whom the apostle says: *For not without cause does he bear the sword. For he is an avenger in wrath, but against him who does evil. In wrath an avenger against him who does evil. But if*, he says, *you do evil, fear*. *For not without cause does he bear the sword. But do you wish not to fear the power? Do good, and you will have praise from it*[^10].

**12.** What then, someone says, had Saint Lawrence done evil, that he should be killed by the power? How was that fulfilled in him: *Do good, and you will have praise from it*, when by doing good he earned such great torments from it? The holy martyr Lawrence, if he did not have praise from it, would not be honored today, would not be proclaimed by us, would not be praised with so great renown. So he has praise from it, even though it is unwilling. For the apostle did not say: Do good, and the power itself will praise you. For all the apostles and martyrs did good; and the powers did not praise them, but rather killed them. So if he said: Do good, and it will praise you, he would deceive you. But now he tempered his words, looked around, weighed, moderated, pruned. Examine what you heard: *Do good, and you will have praise from it*. For if the power is just, you will have praise from it, with the power itself praising. But if it is unjust, then, dead for the faith, for justice, for the truth, you will have praise from it, even with it raging. For you will have it from it, not with it praising, but with it furnishing you the occasion of praise. So do good, and you will have it, and you will be secure.

**13.** But that evil man did such great things, oppressed so many, brought so many to beggary and want. He has his judges, he has his powers. The commonwealth is ordered. *For those that are, are ordained by God*[^11]. Why do you rage? What power have you received, except that these are not public punishments, but open robberies? For what? Consider in the very ranks of the powers: one destined for punishment and condemned, over whom the sword hangs, may not be struck except by the one whose office it is. The executioner who handles the inquiry serves: by him the condemned is struck. If a clerk should strike the condemned, already destined for punishment, does he not also kill the condemned? and he is condemned as a murderer. Surely the one he killed was already condemned, already destined for punishment; but to strike out of order is murder. If it is murder to strike the condemned out of order, what is it, I ask you, to wish to strike one unheard, to wish to strike one unjudged, to wish to strike an evil man with no power received? For we do not defend the evil, or say that the evil are not evil. Those who judge will render an account for it. Why do you wish to render the difficult account of another's death, who do not carry the burden of power? God freed you, that you should not be a judge: why do you usurp what is another's? Render an account of yourself.

**14.** O Lord, how you pricked the hearts of the raging when you said: *Let him who is without sin be the first to cast a stone at her*[^12]. Pricked in their hearts by a grave and sharp word, they acknowledged their consciences, and blushed before present justice; and departing one after another, they left the wretched woman alone[^13]. But she was not the only guilty one: for with her there was the judge, not yet judging, but granting mercy beforehand. For, with the raging departing, the wretched woman and Mercy were left. And the Lord said to her: *Has no one condemned you?* She answered: *No one, Lord. Neither will I*, he said, *condemn you: go, and now sin no more*[^14].

**15.** But that soldier did such great things to me. I would like to know, if you were a soldier, whether you would not do the like. We do not wish such things to be done by soldiers, that the poor be afflicted, we do not; we wish them too to hear the Gospel. For it does not forbid doing good in soldiering, but in wickedness. For when soldiers came to John's baptism, they said: *And what shall we do?* John said to them: *Do violence to no one, accuse no one falsely; let your wages be enough for you*[^15]. And truly, brethren, if soldiers were such, the commonwealth itself would be happy; but if not only the soldier were such, but also the tax collector were such as is there described. For the tax gatherers, that is, the publicans, said to him: And we, *what shall we do?* The answer was: *Exact nothing more than what is appointed for you*[^16]. The soldier is corrected, the tax collector is corrected: let the provincial be corrected too. You have here a correction directed at all. *What shall we* all *do? Whoever has two tunics, let him share with one who has none; and whoever has provisions, let him do likewise*[^17]. We wish soldiers to hear what Christ commanded: let us hear it too. For Christ is not theirs, and not ours: nor is God theirs, and not ours. Let us all hear, and live in peace and concord.

**16.** He oppressed me, when I was a merchant. Did you yourself conduct your business well? In the very business did you commit no fraud? In the very business did you not swear falsely? Did you not say: By him who carried me across, the sea itself, I bought it for so much—when you did not buy it for so much? Brethren, I say to you more expressly, and, as far as the Lord grants, freely: None rage against the evil except the evil. The constraint of power is another matter. For a judge is often compelled to draw the sword, and would not wish to strike. As far as concerns him, he wished to keep the sentence bloodless: but he did not wish public discipline perhaps to perish. It belonged to his profession, to his power, to his necessity. What belongs to you, except to ask God: *Deliver us from evil?*[^18] O you who said: *Deliver us from evil*, may God deliver you from yourself.

**17.** In sum, brethren, why do we hold you long? We are all Christians: we ourselves bear an even greater burden of peril. Often it is said of us: Why does he go to that power? and what does a bishop seek with that power? And yet you all know that your necessities compel us to come where we do not wish: to wait, to stand before the door, to wait for the worthy and the unworthy who enter, to be announced, hardly at some time to be admitted, to bear humiliations, to ask, sometimes to obtain, sometimes to go away sad. Who would wish to suffer these things, unless we were compelled? Let us be dismissed, let us not suffer those things, let no one compel us: behold, let it be granted us, give us a holiday from this affair. We ask you, we beseech you, let no one compel us; we do not wish to have an account with the powers; he knows that we are compelled. And we hold the powers themselves as we ought to hold Christians, if we find Christians in that same power; and pagans, as we ought to hold pagans; wishing good to all. But, someone says, let him admonish the powers to do good. Are we to admonish in your presence? Do you know whether we admonished? You do not know whether we did it or not. This I know, that you do not know, and you judge rashly. Yet, my brethren, I beseech you, it can be said to me about the powers: He should admonish him and he would do good. And I answer: I admonished, but he did not hear me; and I admonished there, where you did not hear. Who admonishes a people aside? We could indeed admonish one man aside, and say: Do thus, or do thus, where no one else was. Who leads a people aside and admonishes a people with no one knowing?

**18.** This necessity compels us to speak such things to you, lest we render an evil account to God concerning you; lest he say to us: You should have admonished, you should have given, I would have exacted. So turn away, then, altogether turn away from these bloody deeds. Let it not pertain to you, when you see and hear such things, except to have mercy. But the evil man has died! He is to be grieved over twice, because he is twice dead: both in time, and forever. For if a good man had died, we would grieve with human affection, because he has left us, because we wished him to live with us. Evil men are more to be grieved over, because after this life they are received into eternal punishments. So let it pertain to you to grieve, my brethren, let it pertain to you to grieve, not to rage.

**19.** But it is a small thing, as I said, it is a small thing that you do not do it, a small thing that you grieve, unless you also, as far as your strength allows, forbid those things that do not belong to the people's power. I do not say, brethren, that any of you can go out and forbid the people: this not even we can do; but each one in his own house—his son, his servant, his friend, his neighbor, his client, his lesser—deal with them, that they not do these things. Persuade those whom you can; and to others apply severity, over whom you have power. One thing I know, which all know with me, that in this city many houses are to be found in which there is not even one pagan; no house is found where there are not Christians. And if it be carefully sifted, no house is found where there are not more Christians than pagans. It is true, you agree. So you see that the evils would not be done, if Christians had not willed them. There is nothing to be answered. Hidden evils can be done, public ones cannot, with Christians forbidding and unwilling; because each one would restrain his servant, each one his son: the young man would be tamed by the severity of his father, the severity of his uncle, the severity of his master, the severity of a good neighbor, the severity of correction from his elder, of his very body. If these things were so done, the evils would not greatly sadden us.

**20.** My brethren, I fear the wrath of God: God does not fear crowds. How quickly it is said: What the people did, it did: who is there to take vengeance on a people? Indeed, who is there? Not even God? Did God fear the whole world, when he made the flood? Did he fear so many cities of Sodom and Gomorrah, when he destroyed them with fire from heaven? I will not now speak of present evils, how great they were and where they were done, and what followed I will not recall, lest I seem to insult. Did God in his wrath separate those who did these things from those who did not? Rather he joined those who did them with those who did not forbid them.

**21.** Let us then at last unfold the sermon. My brethren, we exhort you, we beseech you by the Lord and his gentleness, that you live gently, that you live peaceably; that you peaceably permit the powers to do what pertains to them, for which they will render an account to God and to their superiors; that whenever something must be asked, you ask honorably and peaceably; that with those who do evil, and unhappily and disorderly rage, you do not mingle; that you not desire to be present at such deeds or at the watching of them; but as far as you can, each one in his own house and in his neighborhood, with the one with whom he has some bond of kinship and love, admonish, persuade, teach, correct; even by whatever threats restrain from such great evils: that at some time God may have mercy, and give an end to human evils, and not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities*,* but, as far as the East is from the West, may put our sins far from us; and for the honor of his name may deliver us*,* and be merciful to our sins, *lest perhaps the gentiles say: Where is their God?*[^19]  
AFTER THE TREATISE

**22.** Brethren, on account of those who flee to the protection of mother Church, on account of that common refuge of all, do not be slothful and sluggish in frequenting your mother and not withdrawing from the Church; for she is anxious lest an undisciplined multitude dare anything. As for the rest, so far as concerns those powers, since there are also laws promulgated in the name of God by Christian emperors, which sufficiently and abundantly protect the Church, and they themselves seem such that they would not dare to act against their mother, whence both among men they would be blamed and before God would have judgment—far be this from them; we neither believe it of them, nor do we see it so. But, lest an undisciplined multitude dare anything, you ought to frequent your mother, because, as I said, this is not the affair of one or two men, but the common refuge. And let one who has no case fear lest he have one. I say to your love: To the Church both the unjust flee from the face of those who live justly, and those who live justly flee from the face of the unjust, and sometimes the unjust themselves flee from the face of the unjust. There are three kinds of those who flee. The good do not flee from the good; the just alone do not flee the just; but either the unjust flee the just, or the just flee the unjust, or the unjust flee the unjust. But if we should wish to discern, so that those who do evil be taken out of the Church, there will be no place where those who do good may hide themselves; if we should wish to permit the guilty to be taken from here, there will be nowhere for the innocent to flee. It is better, then, that even the guilty be protected in the Church, than that the innocent be snatched out of the Church. Hold to this: that, as I said, your frequenting, not your savagery, be feared.
