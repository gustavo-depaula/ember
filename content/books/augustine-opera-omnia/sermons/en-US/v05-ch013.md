# Sermon 159

**1. 1.** Yesterday a sermon was prolonged about our justification, which is ours from the Lord our God, we ministering, he granting, you hearing. And although in this life we are laden with the burden of corruptible flesh, surely not without sin—for if we say that we have no sin, we lead ourselves astray, and the truth is not in us[^1]—yet that we have been justified according to the manner of our pilgrimage, living from faith until we enjoy sight, was made clear, as I reckon, to your Charity. We begin, then, from faith, that we may come to sight; we run the way, we seek the homeland. On pilgrimage our soul says, *For before you is all my desire, and my groaning is not hidden from you*[^2]. But in the homeland there will be no place for praying, but only for praising. Why will there be no place for praying? Because nothing is lacking. What is here believed, there is seen; what is here hoped for, there is held; what is here asked for, there is received. Yet there is some perfection in this life, to which the holy martyrs attained. And therefore ecclesiastical discipline holds, as the faithful know, that when the martyrs are recited at the altar of God in that place, prayer is not made for them; but for the rest of the commemorated dead prayer is made. For it is an injury to pray for a martyr, to whose prayers we ourselves ought to be commended. For he contended against sin even to blood. But to certain ones, still imperfect, and yet justified in part, the Apostle says to the Hebrews, *For you have not yet fought to blood, contending against sin*[^3]. If, then, they had not yet fought to blood, without doubt some did fight to blood. Who fought to blood? The holy martyrs, surely; of whom the reading from the holy apostle James was just now heard. *Consider it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations*[^4]. It is said to those now perfect, who can even say, *Test me, O Lord, and try me*[^5]. *Knowing*, he says, *that tribulation works patience; but patience has a perfect work*[^6].

**2. 2.** For righteousness is to be loved; and in this loving of righteousness there are degrees of those making progress. The first is that all the things that delight not be preferred to the love of righteousness. This is the first degree. What do I mean by what I said? That among all the things that delight you, righteousness itself should delight you more; not that other things do not delight, but that it should delight more. For some things naturally delight our infirmity, as food and drink delight the hungry and thirsty; as this light delights us, which is poured from heaven when the sun has risen, or which shines from the stars and the moon, or which on earth is kindled by lamps consoling the darkness of the eyes; a tuneful voice and a most sweet song delights, a good odor delights; whatever pertains to some pleasure of the flesh delights our touch too. And all these things, which delight us in the senses of the body, some are lawful. For these great spectacles of nature delight the eyes, as I said; but the spectacles of the theaters also delight the eyes. These are lawful, those unlawful. A sacred psalm sweetly sung delights the hearing: but the songs of actors also delight the hearing. This lawfully, that unlawfully. Flowers and spices delight the sense of smell, and these are a creation of God; but the incense on the altars of demons also delights the sense of smell. This lawfully, that unlawfully. Food not forbidden delights the taste; but the banquets of sacrilegious sacrifices also delight the taste. This lawfully, that unlawfully. Conjugal embraces delight; the embraces of harlots also delight. This lawfully, that unlawfully. You see, then, dearly beloved, that in these senses of the body there are lawful and unlawful delights. Let righteousness so delight that it conquers even the lawful delights; and to that delight by which you are lawfully delighted, prefer righteousness.

**3. 3.** Let us set before our eyes, for the sake of what I said, an example of a contest. I ask whether you love righteousness; you will answer: I love it. Which you would not answer truly, unless it delighted you in some measure. For nothing is loved but what delights. *Delight in the Lord*, Scripture says[^7]. But the Lord is righteousness. For you ought not to fashion God for yourself as it were an idol. God is like things invisible; and those things in us are better which are invisible. Faith is better than the flesh, faith is better than gold, and faith is better than silver, than money, than estates, than household, than riches; and all these are seen, faith is not seen. To which, then, shall we suppose God more like, the visible, or the invisible? The precious, or the cheap? Let me speak of cheaper things. You have two servants, one deformed in body, another most beautiful; but the deformed one faithful, the other unfaithful. Tell me which you would love more: and I see that you love the invisible. What then, when you love more the faithful servant, though deformed in body, than the beautiful unfaithful one—have you erred, and preferred ugly things to beautiful? Surely not: but you have preferred more beautiful things to ugly. For you despised the eyes of the flesh, and raised up the eyes of the heart. You questioned the eyes of the flesh, and what did they report to you? This one is beautiful, that one ugly. You rejected them, you disapproved their testimony; you raised the eyes of the heart upon the faithful servant and the unfaithful servant; you found this one ugly in flesh, that one beautiful; but you pronounced and said: What is more beautiful than faith? What more deformed than unfaithfulness?

**4. 4.** Therefore, above all pleasures—that is, above even lawful delights—righteousness is to be loved. For if you have inner senses, all those inner senses are delighted with the delight of righteousness. If you have inner eyes, see the light of righteousness: *For with you is the fountain of life, and in your light we shall see light*[^8]. Of that light the Psalm says, *Enlighten my eyes, lest I ever sleep in death*[^9]. Likewise, if you have inner ears, hear righteousness. Such ears he sought who said, *He who has ears to hear, let him hear*[^10]. If you have an inner sense of smell, hear the Apostle: *We are the good odor of Christ to God in every place*[^11]. If you have an inner taste, hear: *Taste and see that the Lord is sweet*[^12]. If you have an inner touch, hear what the bride sings of the bridegroom: *His left hand is under my head, and his right hand will embrace me*[^13].

**4. 5.** Let us, then, as I had begun to say, set forth an example of this contest. Let us see, my brothers, who he is; I ask, and let him answer what I am about to say, whether righteousness so delights him that he prefers it to the other delights pertaining to these senses of the body. Behold, your gold delights you, it delights your eyes: it is a beautiful metal, most gleaming, it delights. It is beautiful, I do not deny; for if I denied it beautiful, I would do injury to the Creator. The tempter comes, then, and says to you: I take your gold away, unless you give false testimony for me; but if you give it, I add to your gold. Two delights contend in you: now I ask you which you prefer, which delights you more, gold or truth, gold or true testimony. Or does this one gleam, and that one not gleam? Faith is sought in true testimony. Gold gleams, faith does not gleam? Blush, have eyes; what you loved in your servant, render to your Lord. For a while ago, when I asked about your two servants—one deformed and faithful, the other beautiful and unfaithful—which you would love more, you answered me justly, and preferred what was to be preferred. Return into yourself, because now the matter concerns you yourself. Surely you loved the faithful servant. Is your Lord unworthy to have you as a faithful servant? And what great thing did you promise to your faithful servant? That you might love him much, the highest reward of liberty. What great thing did you promise to your faithful servant? Temporal liberty. Do we not see many in service not in want, and free men begging? And you demanded faith from the one to whom you promised liberty: and you do not keep faith with the One who promises you eternity!

**5. 6.** It would be long to run through each of the bodily senses; but what I have said of the eyes, understand of the rest; and prefer to the delight of the flesh the delight of the mind. For unlawful pleasures delight your flesh; let the invisible, beautiful, chaste, holy, tuneful, sweet righteousness delight your mind, so that you are not driven to it by fear. For if you are driven to it by fear, it does not yet delight you. You ought not to sin, not from fear of punishment, but from love of righteousness. Hence the Apostle says, *I speak in human terms because of the infirmity of your flesh. For just as you presented your members to serve uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present your members to serve righteousness unto sanctification*[^14]. What did I say? *I speak in human terms*: I say what you can bear. When you presented your members to iniquity for perpetrating shameful deeds, were you led by fear, or invited by delight? What do you say? Answer us, for even you who now live well perhaps once lived badly. When you were sinning, you delighted in your sins; did fear lead you to sinning, or the sweetness of sin? You will answer: Sweetness. Does sweetness lead to sin, and fear thrust toward righteousness? Test yourselves, examine yourselves. Let him who threatens take the gold: righteousness is sweeter, righteousness is brighter. Let him who promises not give the gold: righteousness is to be preferred to gold, to be preferred by delighting; it is more gleaming, brighter, sweeter, more pleasant. Now then, if anyone tests himself, and has overcome in this contest, he has heard the Apostle saying, *I speak in human terms because of the infirmity of your flesh*. Without doubt he spared their infirmity; and he tried to say something more agreeable to the less fit.

**6. 7.** Behold, he says, I say this which you can grasp; you presented your members to unlawful delights, you were led by the sweetness of sins to do them; let the sweetness and the pleasantness of righteousness lead you to right deeds; love righteousness as you loved iniquity. Righteousness is worthy that it obtain from you that you present to it what you presented to iniquity; this is, *I speak in human terms*—that is, what your infirmity can still bear. What, then, did the Apostle hold back? What did he defer to say? What he deferred, I will say, if I can. Weigh righteousness and iniquity; is righteousness only as worthy as iniquity was worthy? Is this to be loved as that was loved? Far be it that it be so—and yet would that it were even so. Therefore more? More, entirely. In iniquity you followed pleasure; for righteousness bear pain. In injustice, I say, you followed delight; for righteousness bear pain; this is "more." Behold some shameless young man of slippery age, by enticing sweetness cast his eyes on another's wife, loved her, desires to attain her; yet he seeks to lie hidden: for he so loves the pleasure that he more fears the pain. Why does he seek to lie hidden? He fears to be caught, bound, brought in, shut up, led out, tortured, killed. Fearing all these things, in that craving of his sweetness he seeks a hiding place; he watches for the husband's absence, he fears to find the very helper of his shameful deed, because he dreads to involve himself with a witness. And we see that he is led by sweetness: but that sweetness is not so great that it conquers even the fear and pain and dread of punishments. Give me beautiful righteousness, give me the beauty of faith; let it come forth into the midst, let it show itself to the eyes of the heart, let it breathe fervor into its lovers. Now it is said to you: Do you wish to enjoy me? Despise whatever else delights you, despise it for me. Behold, you have despised it; that is too little for it: This is human, on account of the infirmity of your flesh. It is too little that you despise whatever delighted you: despise whatever terrified you; despise prisons, despise chains, despise the rack, despise torments, despise death. These you have conquered, me you have found. In both degrees prove yourselves lovers of righteousness.

**7. 8.** We find, perhaps, some who prefer the delight of righteousness to the pleasures and delight of their own body: but one who, for its sake, would despise punishments, pains, and death—do you suppose there is anyone among you? Let us at least think on what we do not dare to profess. What do we think on? Where do we think on it? Thousands of martyrs lie before our eyes, themselves the true and perfect lovers of righteousness. Of them it was said, *Consider it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations; knowing that the testing of your faith works patience; but patience has a perfect work*[^15]. What can be added, that it may have a perfect work? He loves, he burns, he is fervent; he tramples all the things that delight, and passes on; he comes to the harsh, the dreadful, the savage, the threatening; he tramples, breaks through, and passes on. O to love, O to go, O to perish to oneself, O to attain to God! *He who loves his soul will lose it; and he who loses his soul for my sake will find it unto eternal life*[^16]. So the lover of righteousness must be armed, so the lover of invisible beauty must be armed. *What I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light; and what you hear in the ear, preach upon the housetops*[^17]. What is, *What I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light*? What I say and you hear in the mind, speak with boldness. *And what you hear in the ear, preach upon the housetops*. What is, *you hear in the ear*? You hear in secret; because you still fear to profess and confess. What, then, is, *preach upon the housetops*? Your houses are your bodies; your houses are your flesh. Climb upon the roof, trample the flesh, and preach the word.

**8. 9.** But first, my brothers, lament what you were, that you may be able to be what you are not yet. This of which I speak is great. And whence is it great to us? It is highest, perfect, best; whence is it to us? Hear whence it is to us: *Every best gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no change, nor shadow of alteration*[^18]. From there comes what good we have, from there what we do not yet have. You do not have it? *Ask, and you will receive*[^19]. *If you*, says the Savior, *if you, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children; how much more will your heavenly Father give good things to those who ask him*[^20]? Let every person, then, examine himself, and whatever good he finds in himself that pertains to our justification, let him give thanks to the one who gave it; and by giving thanks to the one who gave, let him ask of him also what he has not yet given. For you do not advance in receiving while he fails in giving. However capacious the throat, however capacious the belly you bring, the fountain overcomes the thirsty.
