# Sermon 72: On the Words of the Gospel of Matthew 12:33: "Either Make the Tree Good and Its Fruit Good"

**1. 1.** Our Lord Jesus Christ admonished us that we be good trees and be able to have good fruits. For he says: *Either make the tree good, and its fruit good; or make the tree bad, and its fruit bad. For the tree is known by its fruit*[^1]. Where he says: *Make the tree good, and its fruit good*, this surely is not an admonition, but a wholesome precept, obedience to which is necessary. But what he says: *Make the tree bad, and its fruit bad*, is not a precept, that you should do it, but a warning, that you should beware. For he said it against those who thought that they, when they were bad, could speak good things or have good works. This the Lord Jesus says cannot be. For first the man must be changed, that the works may be changed. For if the man remains in that which is bad, he cannot have good works; if he remains in that which is good, he cannot have bad works.

**1. 2.** But who was found good by the Lord, since Christ died for the impious? So he found all bad trees, but gave to those believing in his name the power to become sons of God[^2]. Whoever, therefore, is a good man today, that is, a good tree, was found bad, and made good. And if, when he found them, he had wished to root out the bad trees, what would have remained that was not worthy to be rooted out? But he came first to dispense mercy, that he might afterward exercise judgment, to whom it is said: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^3]. So he gave to believers the remission of sins, he was unwilling to keep account with them even of the past records. He gave the remission of sins: he made the trees good. He deferred the axe: he gave security.

**2. 3.** Of this axe John speaks, saying: *Now the axe is laid to the root of the trees. Every tree that does not make good fruit will be cut down, and cast into the fire*[^4]. With this axe the householder threatens in the Gospel, saying: *Behold, it is three years that I come to this tree, and I find no fruit on it. Now I ought to clear the place: therefore let it be cut down*[^5]. And the vinedresser intercedes: *Lord, leave it this year too; I will dig around it, and apply a basket of dung; if it makes fruit, well; but if not, you will come and cut it down*[^6]. As through three years the Lord visited the human race, that is, in three certain times. The first time, before the Law; the second, in the Law; the third is now, which is the time of grace. For if he did not visit the human race before the Law, whence Abel? whence Enoch? whence Noah? whence Abraham? whence Isaac? whence Jacob? whose Lord he wished to be called, and whose all nations were, as though he were the God of three men, and: *I am*, he says, *the God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob*[^7]. But in the Law, if he did not visit, he would not give the Law itself. After the Law the householder himself too came: he suffered, died, and rose again, gave the Holy Spirit, made the Gospel to be preached through the whole world. And still a certain tree remained unfruitful. There is still a certain part of the human race: still it does not correct itself. The vinedresser intercedes: the Apostle prayed for the people: *I bend*, he says, *my knees for you to the Father, that, rooted and grounded in love, you may be able to grasp with all the saints what is the breadth and length, height and depth; to know also the love of Christ surpassing knowledge, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God*[^8]. By bending his knees, he intercedes for us before the householder, that we may not be rooted out. So, because it is necessary that he come, let us act so that he find us fruitful. The digging-around of the tree is the humility of the penitent: for every ditch is humble. The basket of dung, the filth of penance; for what is filthier than dung? and yet, if you use it well, what is more fruitful?

**3. 4.** Let each one, then, be a good tree. Let him not think he has good fruits, if he remains a bad tree. There will be no good fruit, except of a good tree. Change the heart, and the work will be changed. Root out craving, plant love. For just as craving is the root of all evils[^9]; so love is the root of all good things. Why, then, do men murmur or contend among themselves, saying: "What is good?" Oh, if you knew what is good! What you wish to have is not greatly good. What you do not wish to be, this is good! For you wish to have health of body: it is good; yet do not think it a great good, which even the bad man has. You wish to have gold and silver; behold, this too I say: it is good; but if you have used it well; but you do not use it well, if you are bad. And through this gold and silver is bad to the bad, good to the good; not because gold and silver make them good, but because, since it finds them good, it is turned to a good use. You wish to have honor: it is good; but this too if you use it well. To how many has honor been an occasion of ruin! To how many has honor been the ministry of a good work!

**4. 5.** Let us distinguish, then, these goods, if we can: because we are speaking of good trees. And here there is nothing that anyone ought so to think of, except to turn his eyes upon himself, to learn in himself, to examine himself, to inspect himself, to seek himself, and to find himself; and what displeases, let him kill; what pleases, let him fit and plant. For when a man has found himself empty of better goods, why is he greedy for outward goods? Behold, what does a chest full of goods profit, if the conscience is empty? You wish to have good things, and do not wish to be good! Do you not see that you ought to blush at your goods, if your house is full of goods and has you bad? For what is there that you wish to have bad? Tell me. Nothing at all: not a wife, not a son, not a daughter, not a manservant, not a maidservant, not an estate, not a tunic, finally not a boot; and yet you wish to have a bad life! I beg you, prefer your life to your boot. All the things that lie around before your eyes, elegant and beautiful, are dear to you; and to yourself you are cheap and foul? If the goods, of which your house is full, which you longed to have, which you feared to lose, could answer you, would they not cry out to you: "As you wish to have us good, so we too wish to have a good Lord"? With a silent voice they appeal against you to your Lord: "Behold, you gave so great goods to this man, and he himself is bad! What does it profit him what he has, when he does not have the one who gave all things?"

**5. 6.** So someone, admonished by these my words, and perhaps pricked, asks what is good, what kind of good, whence good. You have understood well that you ought to ask this. I will answer the one asking, and say: "This is the good which you cannot lose unwillingly. For you can lose gold, even unwilling; you can lose a house, can lose honors, can lose the very health of the flesh: but the good by which you are truly good, you neither receive unwilling, nor lose unwilling." I ask, then, what kind of good this is. The Psalm indeed admonishes us of a great thing, perhaps the one we seek. For it says: *Sons of men, how long heavy of heart?*[^10] How long that tree in the three years? *Sons of men, how long heavy of heart?* What is "heavy of heart"? *Why do you love vanity and seek a lie?*[^11] And afterward it says what is to be sought: *Know that the Lord has magnified his Holy One*[^12]. Now Christ too has come, now he has been magnified, now he has risen and ascended into heaven, now through the whole world his name is preached: *how long heavy of heart?* Let the past times suffice; now that that Holy One has been magnified, *how long heavy of heart?* After the three years what remains, except the axe? *How long heavy of heart? Why do you love vanity and seek a lie?* Still vain things, still useless things, still pompous and fleeting things, so, when Christ the Holy One has been magnified, still these things are sought! Now the truth cries out, and still vanity is sought! *How long heavy of heart?*

**6. 7.** Deservedly is this world strongly scourged: for the world has now come to know the words of the Lord. *And the servant*, he says, *not knowing the will of his lord, and doing things worthy of stripes, will be beaten with few*[^13]. Why? That he may seek the will of his lord. So the servant not knowing the will: this was the world, before the Lord magnified his Holy One; it was a servant not knowing the will of his lord; and therefore it was beaten with few. But the servant now knowing the will of his lord[^14], this is now, from when the Deity magnified his Holy One; and not doing his will, will be beaten with many. What wonder, then, if the world is much beaten? It is a servant knowing the will of the lord, and doing things worthy of stripes. So let it not refuse to be beaten with many: since, if it is unwilling to hear the instructor unjustly, it will justly suffer the avenger; or let it not murmur against the chastiser, when it sees itself worthy of stripes, that it may merit mercy.
