# Sermon 114/B: (Dolbeau 5)

**1.** The Gospel reading, most beloved, which we have just heard—not, as is usually done, procured by us, but yet by the ordering of the Lord, who governs our acts—strongly accords with this psalm of which we have resolved to speak to your love. For the Lord was speaking of his last coming and the end of the world, and he had already recalled many terrible things that human affairs must suffer, the more the end draws near. Then, reproving those who wish to live secure, not in the region of security, he terrified them dreadfully; saying that the coming of the Son of man to that judgment—dreadful to all, but yet even to be desired by the pious faithful—would be such, saying, then, that his coming would be such as it was in the days of Noah, he gravely terrified every heart, if there is faith there: *For just as,* he says, *in the days of Noah, they were eating and drinking, marrying, taking wives, buying, selling, while the ark was being built by Noah, and the flood came and destroyed them all*[^1]. For they were spending their time in ruinous security, in all worldly joys, and in these they took delight, until Noah entered the ark, and the flood found them naked and destitute. Saying this, then, he has now terrified every soul, but we have time to wake up. It is not yet the day of judgment, it is not yet the flood; still the incorruptible timbers are being cut from the forest, still the ark is being built.

**2.** For if those men were foolish, and deservedly perished by their own madness and contempt, who did not say in their heart: "Not without cause does this servant of God, righteous, acceptable to God, and wise, with such effort, with such works, build so great an ark, except that he has learned that something hangs over the world. In a manner, the building of this ark is a herald crying out: Turn to God"—for if they had thought this and changed their life and, turning themselves from impiety to God, made satisfaction to him for their offenses, groaning to his mercy, they would not have perished. For God was not merciful in Nineveh and would prove cruel toward the whole human race, if it turned to him. *In three days Nineveh shall be overthrown,* said Jonah, on account of their excessive sins[^2]. What is so brief as three days? Yet they did not, in so narrow a span of time, despair of God's mercy: they believed that even three days' lamentation and tears sufficed to bend his clemency. If, then, so great a city had three days' space to bend the mercy of God, through the hundred years' span in which the ark was being built, if those men changed their ways and conduct, and offered, to appease God, the sacrifice of a contrite heart, would they not, without any doubt, escape that destruction unharmed by the mercy of him whom they had bent? The three days of the Ninevites, then, reprove the hundred years of the ark's building. But here is one greater than Noah: see how long a time it is, see, since he has been building this ark. I think, brethren, that if the years be reckoned from when Christ began, by cutting incorruptible timbers from the forest of the gentiles, to set up and build the frame of this ark—that is, the Church—more are found than a hundred, and than two hundred, and than three hundred. And see, so many years have passed, and still it is being built, still Noah cries out, still the very building cries out. Nothing will destroy men but unbelief. Let them change their ways; let them believe God who promises so much, threatens so much, deceives in nothing.

**3.** It is long to say many things on this, and on account of the shortness of time and our common frailty, we hasten to the treatment of this psalm. But I would that someone briefly tell me why he does not change his evil ways and make them good. What does he lose thereby? If he believes, let him do it, because it will be true; if he doubts, let him do it, lest it be true. To the believing it is sure, to the doubting it is doubtful. I enumerate so many things from the beginning of the world to this day, which have happened according to the Scriptures, so that we read absolutely nothing in the Scriptures of God that we do not now see fulfilled for the greater part. Very few remain: are these alone false? Will the few that remain be false? Brethren, what great thing is it? We desire the work of an ordinary heart. Count up so many past things that have happened, and believe the few that remain. Let the faithful man consider that it is most firmly true; or at least he ought to consider this, lest perchance it be true.

**4.** For example, you had a journey by a shortcut, and you had resolved to go by the better road. Someone—anyone at all—announced to you that that road was beset by robbers. This road, which has been announced to you as beset by robbers, is level, easy, pleasant, hospitable, delightful; but someone or other announced to you that it is infested by robbers and so beset that either it cannot be crossed or can be crossed only with the greatest difficulty and danger. There is another road: it has much toil, difficulty, harshness, narrowness; not only are delights not found there, but scarcely is fitting hospitality provided. Does your heart not at once say to you, wishing to gain a few days of this life and looking to a life that must someday end: "By this road we go better; though with toil and difficulty and want, though with the wearing down of ourselves or our beasts, yet by this we go better"? Why better? Because safer. But now, if someone wishing to lead you by that delightful road says to you: "And did you so quickly believe him who said this road was beset by robbers?"—if you know the man to be trustworthy, and perhaps so known to you that he has never deceived you, what will you answer? "It cannot be that that man deceives me; I know the man, I have found him a serious man, I hold many proofs of him: he has always told me the truth, he has never lied." But you knew the man to be such: let us set down another, who did not know him. Would not this one too say: "I do not indeed know the man and am ignorant how truthful he is: it may be that he tells the truth, it may be that he lies; yet on account of the very doubt, lest perchance what he says be true, why do I not rather endure a laborious loss than undertake the delightful road with danger?"

**5.** Come now, my brethren, we are Christians; we all wish to make a journey; even if we are unwilling, we make a journey: no one is permitted to remain here, all who come into this life are compelled by the turning of times to pass on. There is no place for sloth: walk, lest you be dragged. As we make our journey and stand, as it were, at a kind of fork in the road, a certain man meets us—not a man, but God for the sake of men a man; he said to us: "Do not go this way; this passage seems indeed easy and light and delightful, worn by many and broad, but the end of this road has destruction. Since you are neither permitted nor is it expedient for you to stand and dwell here, you must go, but go this way: you will walk through certain difficulties, but, the difficulty quickly ended, you will come to a great breadth of joys, and you will escape those snares that no one escapes who chooses to walk that other way." This he said—I think he is known to us, if there is faith in us. Or does it still please you to test his trustworthiness? Let us recall past times and the old Scriptures. Is not that man the Word of God? Was not the *Word* afterward *made flesh and dwelt among us*[^3]? But before it became flesh and dwelt among us, did it not speak through the patriarchs and prophets? See what they announced to the human race.

**6.** God spoke, surely by his word, to Abraham, that his offspring would first be—when he himself, to whom it was said, by reason of old age now could not—that first there would be offspring from old Abraham and from Sarah, an aged and barren woman: it was believed, it came to pass; that this very offspring, that is, the people born of it according to the flesh, would be in servitude in Egypt, and for how many years: it came to pass; that it would also be freed from that captivity: it was freed; that it would receive the land of promise: it received it. Many things were said through the prophets; it was said to Abraham himself that he should look not only to that people, but *in your seed,* he says, *all the nations shall be blessed*[^4]. Near things were said, far-off future things were said: the near things came to pass; the things that were far in the future are now being done. The word of God spoke through the prophets that that nation would sin, that it would be handed over into the hands of its enemies, because it offended the Lord: all came to pass; that it would come into the captivity of Babylon: this too came to pass; that from there would come a king, Christ: Christ also came, Christ was born; because he himself foretold that he would come, Christ also came. It was said that the Jews would crucify him: they crucified him. It was foretold that he would rise and be glorified: it came to pass, he rose, he ascended into heaven. It was foretold that in his name all the earth would believe, it was foretold that kings would persecute his Church: these things came to pass. It was foretold that kings would believe in him: we now hold the faith of kings, and do we doubt the faith of Christ? The fragmentings of heresies too were foretold: do we not see these too, and groan amid them as they clamor on every side? It was foretold that the gentiles would oppress the Church for the sake of their idols: and it came to pass; it was foretold that those very idols would be destroyed through the Church and the name of Christ: and this too we see fulfilled. Scandals in the Church itself were foretold, the weeds were foretold, the chaff was foretold: all these things we both behold with our eyes and bear with whatever strength the Lord has imparted. In what did he deceive you who said to you: "Go this way"? Say securely, if you are faithful, taking such proofs of him who speaks to you—prove him by deeds, for he has deigned to be so proved. Say: "He tells me the truth in all things, I hold all his words true, in nothing has he lied. Such I have known him, he is the word of God. Through the mouths of his servants he spoke, and did not deceive: can what he says by his own mouth deceive?" But let him to whom he is not yet known, who still doubts about Christ, say even he: "I will go this way, lest perchance he tell the truth, whom the whole world has now believed"; and yet it will come to pass just as he foretold.

**7.** Brethren, many unbelievers will be found on the last day just as that multitude was found in the days of Noah. None escaped but those who were in the ark. Make yourselves, then, such: the builder's hands are present, Christ is building the ark. Fit yourselves to it, give yourselves into his hands, be squared, be joined together; let no one refuse the fingers of this craftsman. His grace knows how to fit you together; only do not you be, by perverse pride, rotten wood. So it will be; yet, my brethren, many laugh at these things.  
I see that the shortness of time is withdrawing from me the exposition of the psalm. For I do not wish to leave the passage of the Gospel I have undertaken unfulfilled, as far as the Lord prompts. For now, then, if it please your love, let us defer the psalm. It is not put off for long: there is soon to be the show that they call a public spectacle. We too have a producer, to whom let us come together. If the people from that broad road, laughing and jeering at that faithful guide of the journey, run to watch a spectacle they are not going to receive—and whoever should receive it would deceive himself; yet to what they are not going to receive, they so run, so flock together—how much more eagerly ought we to come together, we who are going to receive what we watch! If you come together for my sake, nothing is poorer than I; but if for his from whom I too receive, nothing is richer than he, nothing richer than he who is poor for our sake. Let us all receive from him, let us all rejoice in him. And if perhaps he shows you through me what he deigns to give, love also the producer's servant, but for the producer's sake, because I too, brethren, love you in him and for his sake. For apart from him we all are nothing.

**8.** Therefore, briefly, what in the Gospel reading is perhaps obscure to someone I will not pass over in silence, with the Lord's help. Let men fear lest they be found so on that last day. Let us fear, my brethren. We now exult, we rejoice, we acclaim. I beg you: let that day find us ready. He who says it does not lie, he has never lied; if you still doubt, beware lest perchance it be true. But one of you says to me: "It is necessary, then, that it sink into the heart of the faithful"; for I am not, merely by speaking these things now, going to make all such as the Lord said: *If anyone does not take up his cross and follow me*[^5], nor am I going to make all such as the Lord said: *If you wish to be perfect, go, sell all you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven, and come, follow me*[^6]. Brethren, is that road, then, so to be feared, where the guide says: *Follow me?* I know that I will not suddenly make all to whom I speak, or even very many, such. Now then, because it has been heard from the thunder of the Gospel—if indeed there are faithful hearts to be alarmed—because it was said: *In the days of Noah it was thus: they were eating, drinking, marrying, taking wives, buying, selling, until Noah entered the ark, and the flood came and destroyed them all*—many say to themselves: "We are bidden to await that day, and not to be found such as those were found who perished in that flood outside the ark. Surely the word of God frightens us, the Gospel trumpet frightens us. What do we do, if no wife is to be taken?"—some young man, some youth, says this—"is there to be no eating, no drinking, must we always fast?" Many say these things. And he who perhaps wished to buy something says to himself: "Is nothing now to be bought, lest I be found in the number of those who perished?"

**9.** What, then, do we do, if it is so? We must weep, as the apostles were saddened over the human race when they heard the Lord saying what perfection was: *Sell all you have and come, follow me*[^7], because he to whom it was said went away saddened. And when he called him good master, of whom he sought counsel for eternal life, he seemed a good master so long as he should say what he was asked. He said it, and saddened him. But as the man departed sad, the Lord followed up and said: *How hard it is for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven!*[^8] Hard, but yet at least hard. But a further example was added by the Lord, and what he had called hard he pronounced impossible. *It is easier,* he says, *for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven*[^9]. As though he closed it against the rich. What will be done, what will be done? It is closed: *Knock and it will be opened to you*[^10]. "With what," he says, "shall we knock?" With what but the hands? "What is: with what but the hands?" With what but works? Let us see, brethren, whether the Lord gave the rich too a place for such works. Let us say this from the Scriptures, lest we be found not so much announcers as flatterers. First the same Lord in that very place: *Sell all you have and come, follow me.* For the disciples were saddened, because the Lord said this. And saddened surely not for themselves, for they had left all and were following the Lord. And being saddened, what did they say? *Who then can be saved?*[^11] Now here I ask the apostles: O chief members of Christ, O pillars of his resurrection to be made firm, why so, why *Who then can be saved?*; has despair been formed about the rich? The rich are few, thousands of the poor will be able to be saved. What did the Lord say? *It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven.* Did he say: "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a man to enter the kingdom of heaven"? He said: *a rich man.* Let me say it quickly, brethren—we all indeed wish well, because this is commanded and this is hoped for, but yet I attend to the heap of chaff in the Scriptures that is to be consumed by fire, and I can say: Would that as few went into the fire as there are rich men! See, brethren, in this whole people that hears these things, how many are rich? I did not say this, that they themselves should go into the fire; but as many as are going to go, and those who are going to go, would that they could be as few as the rich are few in the human race! But as it is, both from the number of the rich many are going into the kingdom of heaven, and from the number of the poor many are going into the eternal fire. Until I make this clear in a few words, attend.

**10.** The Lord said it was easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man into the kingdom of heaven. And the disciples, saddened, said: *Who then can be saved?*, since they attended to so many thousands of the poor and so few rich in the human race. But what? They attended prudently, not who was rich in means, but who burned with greed. Someone or other, with an abundance of all things, is called rich, and so appears. But this man holds all those things as nothing, despises them, and truly holds them as a possessor, is not held by them, as it is written: *His hope is in the Lord his God*[^12]; not arrogant, not boastful, not overbearing in oppressing the poor, not greedy, not gaping after others' goods, not badly guarding and hoarding his own, but truly rich toward God and reckoning the riches themselves as nothing but the giver of riches: such a man is both rich and enters the kingdom of heaven. For to the saddened disciples the Lord said: *What is hard for men is easy for God*[^13]. You were stirred by the difficulty, because I named a camel through the eye of a needle, and what is truly hard and impossible for men is most easy for God. If he wills, he passes even that most enormous beast called a camel through the eye of a needle. And of himself he deigned to do this, and therefore the rich man too can enter the kingdom of heaven, because for his sake the camel went through the eye of a needle. What is this? Let us see if it appears. For not without cause did John the Baptist, the herald of the Lord himself, have a garment of camel's hair, because, for the judge who was to come after him, before whom he went, he had, as it were, a cloak from him. I recognize clearly, the camel being named, a certain figure of my Lord; I recognize the great one, and yet of humble neck; I recognize the great one whom no one would load with sufferings unless he had set himself down upon the earth. I see also the eye of the needle, through which one so great entered. In the needle I understand the prick of compunction, in the pricking the passion, in the eye the narrow straits. Now then the camel has gone through the eye of the needle: let not the rich despair, who are to follow into the kingdom of heaven.

**11.** But what kind of rich? See, someone or other off to the side, clad in rags, exulted and smiled when it was said that the rich man does not enter the kingdom of heaven. "I," he says, "will enter. These rags will obtain this for me; those will not enter who do us injuries, who oppress us." Clearly such will not enter, but you too, see whether you will enter. What if you too are poor and greedy, what if you are pressed by want and burn with avarice? If, then, you are such, whoever you are who are poor, you did not refuse to be rich, but you were unable. *Peace,* he says, *on earth to men of good will*[^14]. God, then, does not look at your means, but at your will. Attend to what your heart is full of, not to what your chest is empty of. If, then, you are such, of evil life, of evil desire, withdraw from the number of God's poor; you will not be among those of whom it was said: *Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven*[^15]. See, I find such a rich man—from comparison with whom you boasted and dared to aspire to the kingdom of heaven—I find him poor in spirit, that is, humble, pious, following the will of God and, if perhaps he loses something of that abundance of things, saying at once: *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away: blessed be the name of the Lord*[^16]. O rich man who is meek, not resisting the will of God, truly rejoicing in that land of the living! *For blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land as their inheritance*[^17]. But you are perhaps insolent, you have nothing in your storeroom, and out of the fantasy of your greed you dream of utterly empty treasures. This man rather, this rich man will enter; against you the kingdom of heaven will be closed, because it is closed against the greedy, closed against the proud and covetous. "But that widow who put two small coins into the treasury was poor!" Just so: she poor, but Zacchaeus rich. Did the widow enter, and Zacchaeus get shut out? Rather, it is truly the kingdom of the free for this reason, that it is given equally to the poor and the rich. In that kingdom Zacchaeus will not be richer than that widow, even though here he gave more than she. For he gave half his goods to the poor, she gave two small coins. They had unequal means, but equal love.

**12.** The rich man, then, enters the kingdom of heaven. Hear what kind he is described as; how the way is made for him, how he is called to enter, hear, you who have the substance of the world, hear and do something before the flood, hear the apostle writing to Timothy: *Charge the rich of this world*[^18]. And as if he asked what the Lord charged—"Sell all you have, give to the poor and you will have treasure in heaven, and come with me, follow the Lord"—the Lord had already charged this: let him who holds the promiser hold Christ the one who charges. Let him who wishes do it; let him undertake what he has heard from the Lord. To certain others the apostle says—and through the apostle the Lord: *Do you wish proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^19] What, then? *Charge,* he says, *the rich of this world,* what is the head of the evil, what is feared in riches, *not to be high-minded*[^20]. Let us describe this briefly. The rich man easily says: "Bad servant"; he is, as it were, high-minded and, unless he says it, perhaps does not rule his house. For very often he rules more by a terrifying word than by a savage lash. He says this—perhaps the necessity of ruling the house compels him to say it: let him not say it within, let him not say it in his heart, let him not say it in the eyes and ears of God; let him not therefore think himself better because he is rich; let him consider the frailty of his flesh, his garments laid aside. What shall I say, brethren, what shall I say? Let him consider, if all those outward ornaments be taken away, what kind of thing a rich man can be, since he is flesh and blood, since he came likewise from that mass from Adam and Eve; but the rich man cannot consider this: with difficulty do you take from him all his ornaments. Nor is it to be wished that they be taken away; it was to be wished that they be cast away. With difficulty, then, is he persuaded what kind of thing he is, whom all these things surround. Let him consider himself in his mother's womb, once naked and destitute, like that poor man too; he came into being possessing nothing; what he received will so remain here as it was not brought hither. Considering these things, the rich man is inwardly poor in spirit, that is, he deflates pride, he lowers himself. And if he has a terrifying face toward those who must be ruled by him, yet within is a humble heart under the eyes of God, and he knows with what conscience he beats his breast. But see whether what follows appears in such men. For he did not say: *Not to be high-minded,* and stop; for every rich man would answer you: "God knows I am not high-minded; and if perhaps I shout, and if I say something harsh, God knows my conscience, that I say these things from the necessity of ruling, not that I exalt myself over others"—as though more powerful because richer. Within, God sees these works. Let us see what follows. *Not to be high-minded,* he says, *nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*[^21]. Here too he may yet say it; and whether he speaks the truth, God alone can see, whether he does not presume on what he has, whether he does not place his hope there; for it follows: *But in the living God, who furnishes us all things to enjoy*[^22].

**13.** "And then what?" he says. *Let them be rich in good works.* This now comes out even to the eyes of men, there is no hiding it. Either it is done and appears, or it is not done and there is no way to lie. *Let them be rich in good works, let them give readily, share*[^23]. There humility appears. You have: let it be shared with him who has not. *Let them share.* And to what good is this? *Let them treasure up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may obtain true life*[^24]. If the rich be such, let them be secure: when the last day comes, they will be found in the ark, they will be in the building, they will not belong to the destruction of the flood; let them not be terrified that they are rich. And if a man is young and cannot contain himself, he is permitted to take a wife. Will the last day, then, find him among those of whom he said: *They were taking wives?* It will not so find him, if it finds him such as the apostle says. See how he joins together in the ark those who feared. For the apostle says: *For the rest,* brethren, *the time is short.* And what follows? *It remains that those who have wives be as though not having them, and those who buy as though not buying, and those who weep as though not weeping, and those who rejoice as though not rejoicing, and those who use this world as though not using it: for the figure of this world is passing away. I wish you to be without anxiety*[^25]. Brethren, if you wish to be secure, do not set your happiness in these things. And if some duty or the necessity of weakness compels you to use these things, do not trust in them, do not cling to them, count them among transitory and temporal things. For all these things slip away in a kind of river of things. You see that all those adversities that press upon human affairs cut down all these superfluous things.

**14.** Brethren, daily there is murmuring against God: "Bad times and hard times." Trifles get a beating—the very thing we were speaking of: bad times, hard times, troublesome times; and yet the public shows are put on. They are bad, they are hard: let them be corrected. You call the time hard? How much harder are you, who are not corrected by the hard time! So great still flourishes the madness of pomps, after such great superfluities men gape! Greed has no end, even when cut down. By what things, I ask, by what doings do they wish prosperity, what do they wish security for the doing of? Let some security be given: we shall see how many diseases come forth into the open, how great a luxury will overflow, greater than now. Security and quiet, for the sake of theaters and organs and pipes and pantomimes! You wish to use what you long for badly, and therefore you do not receive it. Hear, hear the apostolic voice, much freer than mine—for I know how many I offend; suppose me more timid: I do not dare to wound your weak spot—hear from the apostle what you do not wish: *You covet, and do not have*—these are the apostle's words—*you kill and are jealous, and cannot obtain; you quarrel, you wage war, and do not have; you ask, and do not receive, because you ask badly, that you may spend it on your lusts*[^26]. Surely he flattered no one; in many rotten places the iron reached the living flesh. Let us be healed, brethren, let us be corrected, let us be corrected: he is coming who came and was mocked and, because he came, is still mocked; he is coming, and there will be no leisure for laughing. My brethren, let us correct ourselves: see, better times will be, and see, they will be soon. What do you hope for here? Change your place, change your dwelling: "Lift up your heart." What do you hope for here? The human race arose, came to a kind of youth—these affairs flourished in the world—it sinks and declines into old age, it is now nearly decrepit. What do you hope for here? Seek something else. Do you seek rest? You seek a good thing: seek it in its own region. There is one place from which he came down to you, another to which he bids you ascend. Do not hope for times other than such as are read of in the Gospel; I do not say this kind or that kind: daily the books of the Lord are for sale, the reader reads; buy for yourself and read when you have leisure, indeed make sure you have leisure: for it is better to have leisure for this than for trifles. Read what kind of things are foretold up to the end of the world, and believe yourself, do not flatter yourself. The times are not bad because Christ came, but because they were hard and bad, he came who would console.

**15.** Attend, my brethren: it was necessary that the times be such, troublesome and hard. What would we do, if so great a consoler were not present? The human race was going to be gravely sick. As a physician taking on one great sick man, from Adam to the end—that is, that physician taking on the whole wounded human race—for from the time we were born here, from when we were sent out of paradise, there is surely sickness, but at the end it was going to be greater, and perhaps near to health for some, near to death for some. Since, then, the human race was sick, that great physician, taking on the sick man lying on a kind of great bed, the whole world, as a most skilled physician attends to the times of the sick man, both saw and foresaw what would come to pass, since indeed he himself, for the sake of his justice, procured this same sickness for the punishment of our sin. Therefore in the milder times of our sickness, that physician first sent his servants to examine us, he sent the prophets. They spoke, they preached; he cured some through them and healed them. They foretold a great access of fever in the final sickness, and a great tossing of this sick man, for which it was needful that the physician himself be presented and himself come. This, then, our physician said: "In the last time the sick man will be tossed more strongly and violently, to take up whose cure it is fitting that I come at that time; I will restore, I will console, I will exhort, I will promise, I will heal the believer." So it was done. He came, he became man, a sharer in our mortality, that we might be made sharers in his immortality. And still the sick man is tossed. And when he gasps in fever and is much in a heat, he says to himself: "Since this physician came, I suffer sharper fevers, I am tossed more grievously. O monstrous heats, whence did they come upon me? I think he did not enter the house with a good foot." So say all who are still sick with vanity. Why are they still sick with vanity? Because they are unwilling to take from him the draught of sobriety. You see those wretches tossed by their cares and by the various tribulations and terrors of the world, and saying: "Since Christ came, we suffer these times; since there have been Christians, the world fails in all things." O foolish sick man, not because the physician came has your sickness grown worse, but the physician came because your sickness was going to be worse: he foresaw it, he did not cause it; but he came to console you, and that you may be truly healthy.

**16.** For what is taken from you, what is removed, but the superfluous? For you were gaping after harmful things; the things you gaped after were not good for your fever. Is the physician harsh because he snatches harmful fruits from the hand of the sick man? What does he take from you, but the bad security you longed to swallow to the ruin of your bowels? And this very thing, that you groan and murmur, pertains to his medicine. Wishing to cure, he does not want you to be tortured unwillingly. It is necessary that the times be harsh. Why? Lest earthly happiness be loved. It is altogether necessary—it is medicinal—that this life be troubled, that the other life be loved. See, if, with such great sloth, men still cling to earthly things, still go mad in the theaters, what if all things smiled on vanity, what if your trifles were nowhere struck down? See, so many bitternesses are mingled in, and still the world is sweet. Come now, my most beloved brethren, I beseech you by the Lord, by his cross, by his blood, by his love, by his lowliness, by his loftiness I beseech and adjure you, do not hear these things in vain, do not think that we stand in this place truly as if for a spectacle. His mercy knows, under whose eyes we so tremble, that we are led by the duty of love to say these things to you, and compelled by that fear with which we know that we ourselves will render to the Lord an account of all things.
