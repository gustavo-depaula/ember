# Sermon 65: On the Words of the Gospel of Matthew 10:28: "Do Not Fear Those Who Kill the Body," and What Follows

**1. 1.** The divine words which were read admonish us, by fearing, not to fear, and, by not fearing, to fear. You noticed, when the holy Gospel was read, that our Lord God willed us to be firm before he died for us: but by admonishing us not to fear, and by admonishing us to fear. For he says: *Do not fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul*[^1]. Behold where he admonished us not to fear. See where he admonished us to fear. *But fear him*, he says, *who has power to kill both body and soul in hell*[^2]. So let us fear, that we may not fear. Fear seems to pertain to cowardice: fear seems to be of the weak, not of the strong. But see what Scripture says: *The fear of the Lord is the hope of strength*[^3]. Let us fear, that we may not fear: that is, let us fear prudently, lest we fear vainly. The holy martyrs, for whose solemnity this was read from the Gospel, by fearing did not fear: because by fearing God, they despised men.

**1. 2.** For what is to be feared by a man from men? And what is there whereby one man may terrify another, a man a man? He terrifies, and says: I kill you: and does not fear lest, while he threatens, he himself die first. I kill, he says. Who says it? to whom does he say it? I hear two: one terrifying, the other fearing; of whom one is powerful, the other weak, but both mortal. Why, then, does power, more inflated in honor, equal in weakness in the flesh, extend itself? Let him securely threaten death who does not fear death. But if he fears that whereby he terrifies, let him attend to himself and compare himself with the one he threatens. Let him find in the one he threatens an equal condition; and let him, together with that one, ask mercy from the Lord. For he is a man, and threatens a man, a creature a creature: but the one inflated under the Creator, the other fleeing to the Creator.

**2. 3.** So let the most valiant martyr say, a man standing before a man: I do not fear, because I fear. What you threaten, if he is unwilling, you do not do: but what he threatens to do, he is hindered by no one. Then, what you threaten, even if you are permitted, what do you do? You rage as far as the flesh, the soul is safe. You will not kill what you do not see: visible, you terrify the visible. We both have an invisible Creator, whom we ought together to fear; who created man himself from the visible and the invisible: he made the visible from earth, he gave life to the invisible by his breath. The invisible substance, then, that is, the soul, which raised the earth lying down, out of earth, does not fear when you strike the earth. You can strike the dwelling, but the dweller? He flees, bound by a struck chain, and will be crowned in the hidden place. Why, then, do you threaten, who can do nothing to the soul? Through the merit of him to whom you can do nothing, he will rise to whom you can do something. For through the merit of the soul, the flesh too will rise: and will be restored to the dweller, no longer going to fall, but to abide. Behold, I speak the words of a martyr, behold, not even on account of my flesh itself do I fear the one threatening. My flesh lies subject to power: but even the hairs of my head are numbered to the Creator[^4]. Why do I fear lest I lose my flesh, who do not lose a hair? How does he not attend to my flesh, to whom my cheap things are so known? The very body that can be struck and killed will for a time be ash, forever it will be immortal. But for whom this? To whom will the body be restored to eternal life, even though killed, destroyed, scattered? to whom will it be restored? To him who did not fear to lay down his soul, when he does not fear lest his flesh be killed.

**3. 4.** For indeed, brothers, the soul is held to be immortal, and is immortal according to a certain mode of its own: because it is a certain life, which by its presence can vivify the flesh. For through the soul the flesh lives. This life cannot die: and therefore the soul is immortal. Why, then, did I say: According to its own mode? Hear why. Because there is a certain true immortality, an immortality which is utter unchangeableness: of which the Apostle says, speaking of God: *Who alone has immortality, and dwells in inaccessible light; whom no man has seen, nor even can see: to whom is honor and glory forever and ever. Amen*[^5]. If, then, God alone has immortality, surely the soul is mortal. Behold why I said the soul is immortal according to its own mode. For it can also die. Let your love understand, and no question will remain. I dare to say, the soul can die, can be killed. Surely it is immortal. Behold, I dare to say, both it is immortal, and it can be killed: and therefore I said that there is a certain immortality, that is, utter unchangeableness, which God alone has, of whom it was said: *Who alone has immortality.* For if the soul cannot be killed, how did the Lord himself say, when he terrified us: *Fear him who has power to kill both body and soul in hell?*[^6]

**4. 5.** So far I have confirmed, not resolved the question. I have proved that the soul can be killed. The Gospel cannot be contradicted, except by an impious soul. Behold, here too it occurs to me, and there comes to mind what I shall say. Life cannot be contradicted, except by a dead soul. The Gospel is life, impiety and unbelief are the death of the soul. Behold, it can die, and is immortal. How, then, is it immortal? Because there is always some life which in it is never extinguished. How does it die? Not so as not to be life, but by losing life. For the soul is both the life of some thing, and itself too has its own life. Attend to the order of creatures. The life of the body is the soul: the life of the soul is God. As life is present to the body, that is, the soul, that the body may not die: so life ought to be present to the soul, that is, God, that the soul may not die. How does the body die? When the soul deserts it. The soul deserting it, I say, the body dies: and there lies a corpse, a little before desirable, now to be spurned. The members are there, eyes, ears: but these are the windows of the house, the dweller has departed. He who bewails the dead cries in vain at the windows of the dwelling: there is no one within who hears. How much does the affection of the mourner say, how much does it enumerate, how much does it recall; and through how great a madness of grief, so to speak, does it speak as if with one who feels, when it speaks with one who is absent? It enumerates his ways, enumerates the tokens of goodwill toward itself. You are the one who gave me this; you furnished this and that: you are the one who so loved me. If you attend, if you understand, if you suppress the madness of grief, the one who loved you has departed: in vain does the house suffer you as a knocker, in which you cannot find a dweller.

**5. 6.** Let us return to the matter I was speaking of a little before. The body is dead. Why? Because its life departed, that is, the soul. The body lives, and it is impious, is unbelieving, hard to believe, iron toward the correcting of its ways: while the body lives, the soul is dead, through which the body lives. For so great a thing is the soul, that it is fit to grant life to the body even when dead. So great a thing, I say, is the soul, so excellent a creature, that it is fit even when dead to vivify the flesh. For the very soul of the impious man, the soul of the unbeliever, the soul of the perverse, the hard man, is dead: and yet through that dead soul the body lives. Therefore it is there: it moves the hands to work, the feet to walk, directs the gaze to see, inclines the ears to hear; it judges flavors, flees pains, seeks pleasures. All these are the tokens of a living body, but from the presence of the soul. I ask the body whether it lives. It answers me: You see one walking, you see one working, you hear one speaking, you discern one seeking and fleeing, and do you not understand that the body lives? Through these works of the soul established within, I understand that the body lives. And I ask the soul itself whether it lives. It too has its own works, by which it may show its life. The feet walk, I understand the body lives, but by the presence of the soul. I ask whether the soul lives. These feet walk. Behold, from one motion. About life I ask the body and the soul. The feet walk, I understand the body lives. But whither do they walk? To adultery, it says. Then the soul is dead. For so most truthful Scripture said: *Dead is the widow who lives in delights*[^7]. Although there is much difference between delights and adultery, how can the soul, which is said to be dead in delights, live in adultery? It is dead. But not even acting so is it dead. I hear it speaking, the body lives. For the tongue would not be moved in the mouth, and strike out articulate sounds in various places, unless the dweller were within; and, as it were, a musician for this instrument, who would use his tongue. I quite understand. In this way the body speaks, the body lives. But I ask whether the soul too lives. Behold, the body speaks, it lives. What does it speak? As I was saying of the feet, They walk, behold the body lives; and I asked, Whither do they walk? that I might understand whether the soul too lived: so too when I hear one speaking, I understand that the body lives; I ask what it speaks, whether the soul too lives. It speaks a lie. If it speaks a lie, then it is dead. Whence do we prove this? Let us ask the Truth itself, which says: *The mouth that lies kills the soul*[^8]. I ask, Why is the soul dead? What I was saying a little before, I ask, Why is the body dead? Because its life, the soul, departed. Why is the soul dead? Because its life, God, deserted it.

**6. 7.** Briefly, then, these things known, know and hold for certain, that the body is dead without the soul: that the soul is dead without God. Every man without God has a dead soul. You bewail the dead: bewail rather the sinner, bewail the impious, bewail the unbeliever. It is written: *The mourning of the dead, seven days; but of the foolish and impious, all the days of their life*[^9]. Or indeed are the bowels of Christian compassion not in you, so that you bewail a body from which the soul has departed, and do not bewail a soul from which God has departed? Holding this, let the martyr answer the one threatening: Why do you compel me to deny Christ? You compel me, then, to deny the truth? If I am unwilling, what do you do? You strike my body, that my soul may depart from it: but my very soul has its body unto itself. It is not imprudent, it is not unwise. You wish to strike my body: do you wish that, while I fear lest you strike my body, and my soul depart from it, I strike my own soul, and my God depart from it? Do not fear, then, O martyr, the sword of the striker: fear your own tongue, lest you strike yourself, and kill not the flesh, but the soul. Fear for the soul, lest it die in the fire of hell.

**7. 8.** Hence, then, the Lord: *Who has power to kill both body and soul in the fire of hell*[^10]. How? When the impious man is sent into hell, will the body burn there, and the soul there? The death of the body is everlasting punishment: the death of the soul is the absence of God. Do you wish to know what is the death of the soul? Understand the prophet saying: *Let the impious be taken away, that he may not see the brightness of the Lord*[^11]. Let the soul, then, fear its own death, and not fear the death of its body. Because if it has feared its own death, and lived in its God, by not offending him and driving him from itself, it will merit at the end to receive its body; not unto eternal punishment, as the impious; but unto eternal life, as the just. Fearing that death, and loving that life, awaiting the promises of God, despising the threats of the persecutors, the martyrs both merited to be crowned with God, and left to us those solemnities to be celebrated.
