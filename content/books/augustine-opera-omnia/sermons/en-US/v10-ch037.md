# Sermon 359/B

**1.** The disturbance of yesterday, both ours and yours—and on your account more ours than yours—commanded silence, I confess; but, because love commands concerning my lord and brother, whom it is necessary to serve—and there is in you too an eagerness to hear, to which we desire that God deign to grant fruit in your morals and obedience—we serve you in the name of Christ, since we serve Christ, whose members you are. Yet this I confess in the sight of God, before him and before you, to whose ears my thought is a voice; him I invoke as witness upon my soul[^1], that nothing chiefly brings us to this city, to speak to you something which the Lord grants, except the love of your bishop—I do not mean the love by which we love him, but rather that by which we feel ourselves loved by him without any pretense. For although you have known us, in no way could you so know us as we ourselves know one another, who serve you in the love of Christ. For we do indeed love you too, and you see it, God knows. Nevertheless, dearest ones, know—this I speak before Christ—that, if you desired us more than all the things you desire, and felt in the mind of your bishop even a little adverse will against us, you would not see us here.

**2.** For just now he deigned to send us such a letter, that he said that, if we thought our coming should be either deferred or deliberated about, whether to come or not to come, we would offend that very love which was called God[^2]. Consider our weakness, committed to such long journeys in the middle of winter. Let your love receive something else. The blessed and venerable old man Xanthippus, primate of Numidia, summoned a council at Constantina on the fifth before the Kalends of February. A council was summoned by the primate of Numidia for the bishops of Numidia, where we too are. But in that city of Constantina, as I think, your love knows that there is a bishop from our own side. For he was nurtured in the word of God and held the presbyterate in the municipality of Thagaste, from there he was given as bishop to Constantina. We cannot tell you with what letters he wished to draw me, that I might come to Constantina: with the council also summoned, he took away all my delays and doubts. And yet, dearest ones, such a letter came from my lord and brother Aurelius your bishop to my humility, that by the gravity of its sentences and weight it overcame whatever I had previously arranged, and, with all else passed over, not so much drew me as snatched me. For I thought this, since he deigned to command with so great confidence, but also with so great a threat, not about himself, but about God, that I should say to myself: if I came here and yielded to his will—rather, to God's through him—I could satisfy by his letters, concerning my departure thence, the old lord Xanthippus, lest perhaps he be angry with me, because, leaving the council of the Numidians, where the reasoning of his own order required me to be present, I chose rather to come here.

**3.** But to what does all this that I have said pertain? Yesterday my eyes saw that there was a space which was not filled by the brothers, near both to our ears and our tongue; we were not the authors that the disputation should take place there rather, because we saw that they should rather be made to obey, so as to come to the bishops, than the bishops, so as to come to those places with a great disturbance of the greater multitude which had already, in a certain way, settled itself around the apse in the security of hearing: a much greater multitude would have had to be moved from these places to those places. But perhaps the men were to be despised; what of the weaker sex? Without doubt the women, when they had begun to press themselves in their eagerness to draw near, would they not make a greater noise too, and utter voices which it would be most unfitting for me to hear in church? This alone, then, it was: that those few who were leaning on the railings would deign to come to the spaces near to that place where we were speaking. What great thing was this that we asked? But this they would not even hear, and a disturbance was made, and great sorrow for us, which God just now, through the discourse of your truly venerable bishop, deigned to blot out. But for the rest, brothers, we admonish you not to think that a place for making a sermon there was not granted me by him, where it was so obstinately asked by certain ones and tumultuously demanded. Behold why I said all those things, because, if we felt even a little tightening in the heart of our most blessed brother, we would not come to this city—especially with other necessities pressing.

**4.** For what? Does not your love remember that against the party of Donatus from that place, from the middle of the railing, I disputed for four continuous days? Was it awaited that you should ask? Was it even suggested by us? He himself of his own accord saw it must be done and did it; he did not see it must be done, he did not do it. But perhaps you say: "What great or difficult thing did we ask?" Even if it is a small thing that you asked, it is no small thing, the obedience which we demand of you. From this I will rather speak. For I heard certain brothers saying: "Behold, he himself disputed that the weak must be served. The day before he disputed this, on another day he did not do it. Let us, then, be served. Why did he come down?" Why I came down, I tell your holiness. For he himself rather ought to pardon me, since I came down without his commanding it. But my counsel of coming down was such that I did not even consult him lest he forbid it. And surely if I consulted him and he forbade it, I could then only obey: it would be necessary that I not come down. I preferred, then, to ask pardon, because, without consulting him or his commanding, I came down, than not to do what I thought ought to be done.

**5.** Now then hear why I thought this had to be done. How this people has always obeyed its bishop when present, not only we know, but all Africa and perhaps the whole world, wherever the Church of this city is known. For what dissoluteness and confusion of women and men was here before, we all know, because we too in our past age were a part of this stain. It was done by the Lord through his servant that the mingled sex should not keep vigil. I, a boy keeping vigil, when I was studying in this city, so kept vigil amid women mingled with the wickednesses of men, where perhaps occasion even tempted chastity. How honorably it is now kept watch, how chastely, how holily! This diligence cannot displease even those against whom it was instituted. The wicked themselves, the wanton themselves, the very plotters against another's chastity can grieve at this diligence, but cannot blame it. But was this alone done, that we should perhaps rejoice over this alone? What of the diverse approaches and entrances? How was it provided for, with how great prudence devised, with how great insistence accomplished, that those who, when they had entered, were going to be in separate places, should also enter through separate ways, lest, entering in a narrow space, they begin what they afterward contrive to accomplish, wicked and shameless servants, those things they are wont to say to the face of passing matrons? How vigilantly these things were seen to, with what vigor abolished! In the church of Mappalia, at the memorial of the blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian, how great things were wont to be done, if we remember, perhaps we shall still grieve; if we forget, we give the less thanks to God. Let your love recall with us, brothers: I mention the benefits of God toward you through your bishop. Where then shameless little songs were resounding, now hymns ring out; where vigil was kept for luxury, vigil is kept for holiness; finally, where God was offended, God is propitiated. I ask your love that you not forget these things: they are near, they can be compared; yesterday they were, today they are not. But when could your bishop accomplish these things, unless he had an obedient people? That I may speak something, and that something not small, even of the good of your obedience, if to your bishop attempting such things your zeal did not consent, could he have fulfilled this in any way? The mercy of God was present, then, both in his diligence and in your obedience. Since, then, we knew these things, how you are wont to be subject, when we set you forth as an example to be imitated by others, so as to say to the small congregations clamoring in the country and resisting their bishops: "Go, see the people of Carthage"; since, then, we rejoiced the more abundantly over your good example, how could we yesterday be saddened, brothers, over your disobedience, as though our frequent coming had taught you disobedience?

**6.** Let your love, then, attend. That I might come down, I grieved at the misery of my ministry. You were asking to hear. What would the speaker build up, when the hearer was threatening ruin? "Why," you say, "ruin? For what great thing did we ask? What evil did we seek?" I will say what ruin, I will say it that you may fear, not that you may fall. Do you not know that from a spark a fire arises? Do you not know that the smallest drops fill rivers and drag away farms? Let disobedience not seem to you a light sin. Plainly let us say thus: it made no difference whether you heard here, or there; that there were spaces around us which could be filled by the multitude, both we know and you. What, then, remained, that you were unwilling to cross over here, except mere obstinacy: "Either this alone is done which we wish, or let not be done what you wish"? For we wished you to hear, and what we wished was useful to all. But certain ones, because they clung to the railings, when their irrational will was not consented to, even shouted: "Let the dismissal be made." Surely, how far you are and how slowly I said: "Let the dismissal be made," and behold, you all heard it, because you patiently kept silent. What if we wished to test that very obedience of yours? "But in a small matter," says someone, "how would you test it?" If in a small matter you did not obey, will you obey in a greater matter? Have you not read the Lord saying: *He who is faithful in a small matter, is faithful also in a great one, and he who is unfaithful in a small matter, is unfaithful also in a great one*[^3]?

**7.** Do you wish to know what an evil disobedience is, since I said: "The hearer was threatening ruin"? God planted all good things in his paradise. If in the whole world he made all things good, Scripture saying: *And God saw all the things that he made, and behold, they were very good*[^4], if all, how much more those things which he set more joyful in paradise. Where, then, he had planted all things good, what does it mean: *Do not touch that tree*? Do you not know that men are wont to raise a question from this, who cannot see either how great a good obedience is or how great an evil disobedience? Behold, all good things had been planted. *Do not touch*, says God. "Why may I not touch it? Did you put something evil here? If you put something evil here, take it away, and do not forbid me to touch it." "Do not", he says, "touch this tree," which surely, unless it were good, would not be in paradise. Or do you perhaps think this, that outside paradise God had filled the earth with all good things and in paradise had planted an evil? Even in the rest of the earth surely there were good things, but in paradise certainly better ones. Yet because among all the good things which were set in paradise obedience was better, God forbade something from somewhere, lest by forbidding nothing he should not have lordship. And what? Perhaps someone thinks that God, out of his pride, wished to have lordship. The lordship of God is useful not to God, but to him over whom he has lordship. He is neither lesser when we despise him, nor greater when we serve him. To be under such a Lord is expedient for us, not for him. He who wishes to have lordship over us wishes it for our benefit, not his own. He is in need of no good of ours, we are in want of all his goods, and of God himself our highest good. For our highest and best good, than which nothing is better, is God himself. See the servant confessing, hear him from the psalm: *I said to the Lord: You are my God, for you have no need of my goods*[^5]. Therefore God forbade something, that he might impose a precept, that the one having lordship might be served, that obedience might be distinguished from disobedience as virtue from vice; and that tree was called the tree of the knowledge of good and evil[^6], not because as it were good and evil fruits hung there, but it was called the tree of the knowledge of good and evil for this reason, that, if a man touched it against the precept, in that tree he would experience what the difference is between the good of obedience and the evil of disobedience. For from that tree, with the precept despised, death followed; with the precept kept, immortality would follow. You see, then, how great an evil disobedience is, my brothers: it was itself the first ruin of man.

**8.** Surely we wish to rise from this fall: why do we repeat that from which we fell? Let Adam suffice, Christ has come. *In Adam all die, in Christ all will be made alive*[^7]. From Adam into the human race the root of evil is disobedience, in Christ the root of immortality is obedience. Therefore Adam was for us the author and example of disobedience, but Christ of obedience. And how Christ for obedience? Although he is equal to the Father, he says he serves the Father. Surely you recognize with me, catholics, the catholic faith; you recognize: *I and the Father are one*[^8], you recognize: *He who sees me, sees the Father*[^9], you recognize: *And the Word was with God and the Word was God*[^10], you recognize: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped*[^11]. For to Christ equality is not a thing grasped, but his nature: he for whom it was a thing grasped, standing, fell; he for whom it was his nature, even descending, remained. But let the same apostle Paul unfold the commendation of the obedience of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. It is worth the trouble to recall and hear the whole passage. Behold, see the Son equal to the Father in the form of God, but read what follows: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped.* "But what did he do?" *But he emptied himself.* "How did he empty himself? I fear lest he lost the equality." Do not suspect this: hear what follows, hear from what he emptied himself, hear that he emptied himself not by losing what he was, but by receiving what he was not. *He emptied*, he says, *himself.* "How? I ask you, now say it." *Taking the form of a servant.* "Who took the form of a servant?" He who, *though he was in the form of God*—there *was*, here *taking*—*taking the form of a servant, made in the likeness of men*—made surely in the mother whom he had made—*made in the likeness of men and found in habit as a man.* "But we were speaking of obedience. We have said many things now, and we have not heard about obedience. *He emptied himself* I heard, *taking the form of a servant* I heard, *made in the likeness of men* I heard, but prove that he did this by obeying." At the end, hear: *made in the likeness of men and found in habit as a man, he humbled himself, made obedient unto death*[^12].

**9.** Let us behold the master of the servant: the master obeys, the servant despises? Let no one say in his heart: "But he did this, because he is the Lord." What did he do, because he is the Lord? Did I say this to you: "Light the sun, let the moon run with its monthly waning and waxing; make the stars shine from heaven, the fountains gush from the earth, the animals walk, the birds fly, the fish swim"? Finally, did I even say this: "Open the eyes of the blind, break open the ears of the deaf, drive away the sick man's fever, raise up the flesh of the dead"? I say none of these: these he did as God, but I demand of you the obedience which he dispensed beforehand as a servant. Obedience, I say, I demand. Through immortality you will be like him, through obedience he himself was made like you. He will give you his life, for he received your death. But you say: "He obeyed God the Father." For whom are you commanded to obey? For he obeyed God the Father, as an equal. Or do you think it makes a difference that he served God the Father and to you it is said: "Obey your bishop"? For who set the bishop over you, whom you should obey? Or has the Gospel slipped from you: *He who hears you hears me*[^13]; *he who receives you receives me, and he who receives me receives him who sent me*[^14]? Finally you say: "He served the Father." What if he served you too? Are you too his father and mother? Because he here deigned to have a mother, not a father, that he might show both his generations wonderful, divine and human: divine without a mother, human without a father—and yet he was a child subject to his parents (read the Gospel), in his childish age subject to his parents[^15], in divine majesty the Lord of his parents—yet you are not his mother, although he served you too. For he taught us to serve not by word only, but by example. It was too little for the Lord to say to his disciples: *Serve one another.* For the Lord commands a thing to be done: surely it ought to suffice, because the Lord commanded it. Or was it to be awaited that he should show what he commanded? Truly, would any one of us dare to demand that our Lord should first fulfill what he commanded? And yet, with none of us daring to demand it, he gave from himself an example of serving one another to his disciples: *Whoever*, he says, *wishes to be greater among you, will be your servant*[^16]. And at once, lest the disciples be saddened at the servile name and say to him: "Then, Lord, shall we be servants, whom you have redeemed? shall we be servants, for whom you shed your blood? Do we not recognize the merit of our freedom in our price, your blood?", he consoled perhaps their saddened pride not yet healed: *He will be*, he said, *your servant, just as the Son of man came not to be ministered to, but to minister*[^17]. Behold, he was made our minister too, and we are not his mother. Or perhaps we are his mother too? *These are my brothers and my mother, who do my will*[^18].

**10.** "But behold," says someone, "let my bishop take an example from my Lord, and serve me." I tell your love—let him who can, understand—: Unless he served, he would not command. For he serves who commands what is useful, he serves by vigilance, he serves by care, he serves by anxiety, he serves finally by love. For he himself too, who was made a minister here, surely commanded his disciples. Hear him commanding and them serving: *Where do you wish us to prepare the Passover for you?* And he sends whom he wishes where he wishes, he orders it to be prepared for him where he wishes. What he commanded was done, and yet he himself served more; for he did not lie when he said: *Just as the Son of man came not to be ministered to, but to minister*. How *came not to be ministered to, but to minister*? Behold, I see the disciples running, preparing the Passover, arranging the supper. How did he *come not to be ministered to, but to minister?* But what is *to minister?* It follows: *And to lay down his soul for his friends*[^19]. Do you wish to know what he ministered to us? From this we live today and are fed today from his table, which he then ministered.

**11.** *Go*, he says, *into the village which is opposite you, you will find there a colt of an ass tied on which no one has sat, lead it, and if they say to you: "What are you doing? where are you leading the colt?", answer: "The Lord has need of it," and they will let it go to you*[^20]. They heard, they went, they did it. Did anyone resist, did anyone say: "Why does he want a colt led to him? For surely he who raised the dead has not failed from walking"? Hear, servant; do what is commanded by him who surely wishes you healthy, by him who bears care for you unto salvation. To seek why he commanded is to dispute, not to obey. First be a diligent obeyer, that you may be a fitting disputer. *Wash yourselves, be clean*, are the words of one commanding. *Take away wickedness from your souls and from the sight of my eyes*, are the words of one commanding. *Learn to do good, judge for the orphan, justify the widow*—have you done what he commanded?—*and come, let us dispute, says the Lord*[^21]. The colt was loosed from the village, which was opposite them. What is the village opposite the disciples, except this world? *Go to the village, which is opposite you.* This world is opposite the disciples, falsehood opposite truth, lust opposite chastity, disobedience opposite obedience. In this village which is the world, a colt was tied, on which no one had sat. Who is this colt tied in the opposing village, on which no one had sat, who is it but the people of the nations bound by the chain of the devil, on which no one had sat, because it had carried no prophet? It is loosed, led, it carries God, is governed by the Lord, is led by the Lord along the way, is admonished by the Lord with a scourge; and in those who obeyed, to lead the colt, there was obedience, and in those who at once let the colt go, hearing that the Lord has need of it, there was obedience. Understand those men as you please. For perhaps they are the contrary powers which had tied the colt; perhaps those men who had tied the colt signify the devil and his angels[^22], through whom the people of the nations had been bound with a pernicious superstition: yet so great is the power of the one commanding, that even they do not dare to hold what the Lord says he has need of. What are you, my brothers? What do you wish to be? The loosers of the colt, or the colt? For far be it that you be those who had tied the colt, and yet not even they resisted. What, then, do you wish to be, my brothers: those who loosed the colt, or the colt itself? You do not dare to claim for yourselves the person of those through whom the colt was loosed: the apostles did this. That is the person of prelates: we ourselves, according to the strength which the Lord deigns to grant, bear it with all anxiety; from this we speak to you. You are the colt, obey those who lead you to carry the Lord. Surely, dearest ones, consider how the disciples loosed the colt and led it to the Lord. They led, it followed; for they did not drag it, and it kick back. And yet, because we speak of our service, when the disciples led the colt to the Lord, they rather served the colt; so we too serve you, when we lead you to the Lord, when we teach and admonish obedience: if your weakness were not served, you would not hear us today.

**12.** Surely, dearest ones, we too are men[^23], weak we too bear ourselves. You disturbed us yesterday, and in that very disturbance of ours we feared more for you, lest you sadden in us the Spirit of God[^24], through whom we serve you. How do you think me, brothers, now standing and about to speak to you, how do you think I could have been disturbed, attending—what I already mentioned—how obedient you are wont to be and how disobedient you showed yourselves with us present, as though we taught disobedience, as though we, according to our measure, did not hold your little rein, to lead you to the Lord? And yet, dearest ones—what you heard from him, and it is true—however great our care for you or on your behalf, can it be greater than that of him who specially serves you, who is so over you that he is under you? We seem to be over you from the higher place: yet so great is the burden of anxiety and care, that it makes us under your feet. Finally, tread and live. What is, my brothers, what is any virtue in a servant of God without obedience? For what is obedience? You love love: it is its daughter, obedience is the daughter of love. But love cannot be barren. Plainly let no one deceive you, let no one say: "I do not have obedience, but I have love." Plainly you do not have love. Wherever this mother is, she gives birth. If she is there, she has given birth; if she has not given birth, she is not there. The root is in the hidden, brothers, the fruit in the open. I do not believe that it clings in the soil, unless I see what hangs on the branch. Do you have love? Show me its fruit: let me see obedience, let me rejoice over obedience, let me embrace the offspring, that I may recognize the mother.

**13.** Behold how great goods even the feigned seem to have. Yesterday we heard the praises of a true martyr: what torments he endured, how huge, how many, how dense! If love is lacking, it is madness. Whence do we praise, whence do we proclaim, whence do we rejoice with him, except because we see in what Church, for what faith, what he resisted to the king commanding? For not because he resisted the one commanding, but because he resisted the one commanding this, where to obey is a sin, because that is not even to be called obedience, where something pernicious and sacrilegious is commanded. For just as there is no faith when something false is believed, so there is no obedience where something useless is commanded. For how could I call obedient one who believes a man, despises God? The powers are ordered in this world, and above all powers is the divine power[^25]. You would not be obedient if perhaps, by obeying as a servant your father, you despised your master. This I say: if perhaps you were a servant, and your father and your fellow-servant commanded you one thing, against that which your master had commanded you, and you obeyed your father rather than your master, would I not call you disobedient and a perverter of order? For he is rather to be heard who has the greater power, he who has the lawful one. I would not, then, call obedient one who obeyed a steward against a proconsul, a proconsul against the emperor; so I do not call obedient one obeying the emperor against God.

**14.** Whence, then, obedient, whence holy, whence the attainer of the true crown, Vincentius, whence the victor over so many sufferings and fitting to his own name? Whence? See who commanded, whom he commanded, what he commanded. The emperor commanded, he commanded a Christian, he commanded that he offer incense to idols. If you attend to the rank of the one commanding, the emperor commanded a provincial. "A certain place is granted as it were to obedience, when I hear who commanded, whom he commanded." But wait, attend to what he commanded: to offer incense to idols. "Let him who does not offer incense to idols be punished." Now the provincial obeys, if a greater power commands nothing to the contrary. But raise your ear, hear two voices: hear one from the tribunal, the other hear from heaven. What did you hear from the tribunal? "Let him who does not sacrifice to the gods be punished." What from heaven? *He who sacrifices to gods shall be uprooted*[^26]. Here let your obedience be proved, martyr. Distinguish the voices, set apart the powers. You see him who commands, fear rather him who forbids. This is the crown of martyrdom, this the triumph, with the devil thoroughly conquered and trampled under foot, whom the martyr feared when coaxing, whom he had despised when angry. He expressed this himself, as those things which were read were heard, he expressed it in his own words: when the one who raged as it were wished to have mercy, then he seemed most to be guarded against. For deceitful mercy harmed more than open cruelty. In the roaring of the angry one, he did not dread the lion; in the coaxing of the merciful one, he did not keep silent that he had feared the dragon. For he himself is the lion and the dragon of whom it was said: *You will trample on the lion and the dragon*[^27].

**15.** Let no one, then, say, dearest brothers, let no one say—for everyone who says it deceives himself—that now the Church does not suffer persecution, because the emperors are catholic, because, going to render an account to God of their empire, they command all things for the Church, keep watch for its increase; let no one then say that the Church does not suffer persecution: it does not suffer the lion, but the dragon does not sleep. Hear that lion, in the open suffering of the saints, mentioned by Peter and exhorting the martyrs to the triumph of victory: *Your adversary*, he says, *the devil, like a roaring lion goes about, seeking whom he may devour*[^28]. For then there were horrible threats and great cruelty of the pagans against the saints of God, then the onslaught of adverse commands and the great roaring of the powers: the lion was roaring, but neither did the dragon cease. You have heard Peter exhorting against the lion, hear also Paul making us cautious against the dragon: *I betrothed*, he says, *you to one husband.* For many wished to become the husbands of one woman. But consider, brothers, consider what many husbands make of one woman. They make her that which is to be considered so that it may be detested, not to be spoken so that it may be shuddered at. There were, then, many husbands wishing to be of one woman. But that one, a friend of the bridegroom and zealous for the bridegroom, not for himself: *I betrothed*, he says, *you to one husband, to present a chaste virgin to Christ. But I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve, so also your senses be corrupted away from the chastity of God, which is in Christ*[^29]. He feared lest she be corrupted not by the cruelty of the lion, but by the coaxing of the dragon. Peter admonished you, for God's sake, not to care about the lion, Paul admonishes you, for God's sake, to keep watch against the dragon and in God to trample lion and dragon[^30].

**16.** For do you wish to know what this dragon is like, how his snares are to be avoided, how great is the cunning of the enemy? Behold, now nearly six thousand years, exercised in the temptations of the saints, against the one true God he made many false gods. But one Son of God came, foretold by his heralds sent before; the Son of God came, loosed the works of the devil as it were the binding of that colt[^31], taught by word, confirmed by example; he showed that the one true God is to be worshiped, that he himself is to be adored and not the angels in his place, since the angels too, because they love God, because love especially reigns in them, wish God to be loved with them, not themselves in place of God. When, then, he had taught these things, he taught this: that the saints must die, if there is need, for this teaching. For what teaching? That which has love *from a pure heart and a good conscience and unfeigned faith*[^32]. For this he taught the saints of God to die and commended them to the Church as venerable. How venerable? *Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his just ones*[^33]. Hence precious the death of Peter, hence precious the death of Paul, hence precious the death of Vincentius, hence precious the death of Cyprian. Whence precious? From pure love and a good conscience and unfeigned faith. But that serpent saw this, that ancient serpent saw the martyrs honored, the temples deserted: cunning and venomous in his vigilance against us, because he could not give Christians false gods, he made false martyrs. But O you, catholic offshoots, compare with us for a little while these false martyrs to the true martyrs, and with pious faith distinguish what the devil tries to confound by venomous deceit.

**17.** The distinction of true and false martyrs that one wishes to darken for us, he wishes to extinguish the eye of the heart, lest we discern these things; he wished to set against them a similar appearance, but let us against the devil attend to the Apostle saying of certain ones: *Having an appearance of piety, but denying its power*[^34]. What is the power of piety? Love. It is itself that mother of obedience. Accordingly attend to the appearance by which the devil tries to join false martyrs to true martyrs. "Behold," he says, "they too suffer persecution." Still, O enemy, still you confound. They suffer persecution, you say. Still set against them robbers, murderers, parricides, adulterers, poisoners: do not all these too suffer persecution? Did not my Lord, whom I wish—and yours, willing or unwilling, because also mine, even if I had been unwilling, but to my good my Lord, because I wish it, to my evil, if I had been unwilling—did not my Lord foresee you, O ancient enemy, who, when he exhorted his disciples to the glory of the passion: *Blessed*, he said, *are those who suffer persecution for justice*[^35]? Against all your poisons, against your two-tongued or three-tongued or many-tongued attempts, one word kept watch: *for justice.* On account of this word, murderers suffer persecution and are not martyrs, adulterers suffer and are not martyrs. Now show your martyrs: you boast that they suffer, I ask why they suffer. You praise the punishment, I examine the cause. The cause, I say, I examine, the cause I seek. Tell me why he suffers, whom you toss about as suffering. For justice? Teach this: for that is the cause of martyrs. For it is not the punishment that crowns the martyrs, but the cause.

**18.** O enemy and cunning seducer, against your false martyrs the true martyrs cried out in the psalm: *Judge me, God, and distinguish my cause from a nation that is not holy*[^36]. *Judge me*, he says, *and distinguish my cause.* See, dearest ones, how many things he passed over: he wished his cause to be distinguished. For the cause is distinguished. The true martyr did not say: "Distinguish my fasts," for those people too fast; he did not say: "Distinguish my works which they do toward the poor," for those people too do them; he did not say: "Distinguish my baptism," for those people too have the same; he did not say: "Distinguish my creed," for those people too confess it; in all things he finds himself like them, he prayed only that his cause be distinguished. *Distinguish my cause.* I fast, and he fasts. But why I, why he? I for Christ. "But I too," he says, "for Christ." Really for Christ? If for Christ, I believe, surely also for the words of Christ; if against the words of Christ, certainly against Christ. "And what are the things of Christ," he says, "against which I suffer?" Do you so rage in a false passion, that your heart has fallen away from the truthful preaching? Behold the common Lord himself, whom you confess with me, even when you do not believe.

**19.** See whether Christ did not wish to show both himself and his Church, so that those invited to the wedding and clothed in the wedding garment might err in neither, neither in the bridegroom nor in the bride. How did he show the bridegroom? *It behooved Christ to suffer and to rise on the third day*[^37]. I recognize the bridegroom: this the prophets too said, this said those servants who were sent ahead, to invite to the wedding. *It behooved Christ to suffer and to rise on the third day.* Such he showed himself to them, he demonstrated this fulfilled in himself according to the prophets to those who doubted. What of the bride? Did he keep silent? At once he showed her too. For he saw that he was now consequently being desired. *It behooved*, he says, *Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day.* Behold, I see the bridegroom, behold, I recognize him. What of the bride? *And that penance and remission be preached in his name through all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^38]. This Church, expressed by the mouth of Christ, foretold by the proclamation of the prophets, recognize her, if you are a martyr, hold her; in her and for her shed your blood, render back what was dispensed to you beforehand. Hear the apostle John: *Therefore*, he says, *Christ laid down his soul for us, because we too ought to lay down our soul for the brothers*[^39]. Wake up: *for the brothers*, not against the brothers. What does it profit that you confess the bridegroom, that you honor the head of the household, and his spouse, I do not say neglect, but persecute with false charges? You, a man, having a spouse whom you have not redeemed with your own blood[^40], yet so love her that, if anyone obeyed you, kept watch at your house daily, fell at your feet, followed you with every proclamation, never and nowhere kept silent your praises, but spoke one charge about your wife, would have poured out the whole of what he had served.

**20.** Accordingly let the confessions of true and false martyrs be brought forth and compared, let us set before our eyes what we beheld yesterday. For we saw a certain most sweet spectacle: a martyr contending against the impious one compelling, true faith succumbing in no punishment, Vincentius everywhere conquering. We saw it, we were present. That reading spoke with our heart: we were delighted. That serpent envying the martyrs, that snake whom Vincentius guarded against when coaxing, because he could not blame that passion, stirred up sedition for us. Let those recognize and grieve who lent him their tongues for the service. For what did those voices mean: "Make the dismissal," "the dismissal, make the dismissal," except that that martyr should not be praised somewhat longer? Let them be compared, then, let the confessions of true and false martyrs be compared. You call yourself a martyr because you resist the power. What power commanding? Behold, I hear what was commanded the true martyrs, let me hear also what is commanded you. I see how gloriously they refused, because I see what they refused; do you show what you refuse: let me compare your voices, let me see what I ought to imitate, what to follow. "Offer incense to the gods." "I will not." We have heard the glorious voice of the true martyr, let us hear also from that side: "Be in concord with your brother." O abominable voice, worthily damnable not only by the highest God, but even by the human powers! "Be in concord with your brother." "I will not." Surely you suffer against Christ.

**21.** Open the Gospel, read: *If you offer your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave there your gift and go first to be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift*[^41]. *If*, he says, *you remember that your brother has something against you.* What is *has something against you*? You have offended him, you have done something iniquitous against him. *Go to be reconciled to your brother*: from the concordant I accept a gift. Why do you offer a gift at the altar of God, when in your heart there is the seat of the devil? Who sowed discord there? Who planted it? Who finally dwells there? Is it not he, always the sower of discords, the author of dissensions, the origin of quarrels? If Christ said this of two men, struck in so great a care, so great a fear, so exacts concord, as though he said: "You offer one thing, I require another. I take even what you have offered, if you bring what I have required," if this in two men, how much more in two peoples? If to have such a cause against one man is dangerous and ruinous, how much more against the whole world, how much more against that whole bride of Christ, spread *through all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^42]?

**22.** Or do you perhaps have a cause against me? For the Lord says this: *If you remember that your brother has something against you*[^43]. You will say to me, then: "Your brother, he says, has something against you, I have something against you, for you have nothing against me." Let us seek, then: if it be found in me, I correct it; if it be found in you, do you correct it. Set forth to me the cause you have against me, I will quickly say what I have against you. This I have against you, which my Lord has against you. You speak charges against his matron, and his matron is the whole world in his saints and faithful, against her you direct a charge. Or did you come to know anything, or did you judge it? In what tribunal were you sitting, when the whole world stood before to be heard? You do not know the cause of your neighbor: how surely did you judge about the whole world? I recite the records, I show who were the traitors, I read that those very ones sat against the innocent Caecilian who confessed their own crimes, the public archives proclaim that your elders first carried the cause of Caecilian, bishop of this city, to the emperor Constantine: yet I do not impute to you the crime of your elders. All the proceedings are for me, and I do not charge you with another's crimes. But you bring forth nothing whereby you may even convict my elders, and you accuse me who was born so long afterward. "If, then," he says, "you do not impute to me the deeds of my elders, why do you impute to me your own deeds?" What deeds of mine? "Because you are not with me." And what evil to you because I am not with you? Did I not say that I have a cause with you, which my Lord has with you? Hear him saying: *He who is not with me is against me*[^44].

**23.** And so, dearest ones, as much as we could, and perhaps beyond what we could, your zeal compelling us to heal the sadness of yesterday, we have perhaps been more talkative than the weakness of standing, whether ours or yours, required. But this especially I admonish, and to this I rather conclude my sermon: that, since I mentioned the saying of the Lord not to be heard negligently: *If you offer your gift at the altar, go first to be reconciled to your brother*[^45], our hearts may be reconciled to one another. First and especially, do not think that either yesterday or ever at all your bishop was angry out of hatred but out of love: there is never shaken out of his breast what is owed to you on account of Christ. Let the clouds of yesterday be cleared; not only love, which never withdrew, but also the former joy be recalled, so that it may be our care alike, as we have exhorted you in Christ, to serve the weak unto benefit, not unto a harmful will. For not, dearest ones, because the weak must be served, when perhaps he asks for food or when he refuses food, so that he is compelled by you with trouble and anxiety or with some importunity to take it lest he die, because the weak is so served, on that account it must be given to him, even if he asks for poison. Do not, then, grow accustomed to the great evil of disobedience. Nor let anyone again say: "What then? Did we ask for poison, because we wished to transfer the pulpit from place to place?" Disobedience is poison, it itself first killed man. Do we, brothers, blame this, that you asked? Truly we tell your love that, if you asked even somewhat longer, yet by asking you could not displease us, as you displeased us by being angry and saying: "Let the dismissal be made." This is what we wish you to grieve over. When you ask, if it seems good, it is granted you; if it does not seem good, turn yourselves from asking to obeying; but if you wish to burst out into anger, into reviling, into provocation of those who serve you in Christ with so great anxiety, that now is poison, even if it is not death itself. Do not, brothers, we ask you, we beseech you; distinguish the Church of God from the theaters. Here all those things which are done badly in the theaters are wont to be punished, are wont to be healed, are wont to be led to confession and to penance, not to be brought in here. Here to rise up, here to roar back, here to lord it, may God turn away both from your hearts and from our grief, and may we always rejoice over your obedience.
