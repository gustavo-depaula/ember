# Sermon 292: On the Birthday of John the Baptist

**[*In Which the Argument Is Directed against the Donatists*]**

**1. 1.** Today’s solemn feast, with such great expectation, calls for the customary sermon. Therefore, with the Lord’s help, we will minister to you what he gives, recalling and keeping in mind the duty of our service, so that we speak as ministers, not masters; to fellow students, not pupils; for we speak to fellow servants, not servants. We have one Teacher, whose school is on earth and whose chair is in heaven. His forerunner John was born on the day handed down as his birthday, the day we celebrate today. We have received this through the tradition of our forebears, and pass it on to posterity with a devotion worthy of imitation. Today, then, we celebrate the birthday of John, not the Evangelist but the Baptist. Once this first point is established, a question arises that we must not pass over: why do we celebrate the birthday on which John came from the womb rather than that of any apostle, martyr, prophet, or patriarch? If we are asked, what shall we answer? As far as I can see, as far as my modest powers can discern, the reason is this: the Lord’s disciples were born and, as they grew older and reached more capable years, were taken into discipleship. Their faith afterward clung to the Lord, but none of their births served the Lord. Let us also remember the prophets and recall the patriarchs. They were born human beings and, as they grew older, were filled with the Holy Spirit and prophesied Christ. They were born to prophesy afterward. But John’s very birth prophesied the Lord Christ, whom he greeted from the womb when Christ had been conceived.

**2. 2.** Having answered that question as best we could, let us approach another, according to the strength the Lord gives. For another question arises, somewhat more obscure, it seems to me, and harder to investigate. In this your attention and your prayers to the Lord for my littleness will help me greatly. This John possessed such outstanding grace that, as was said, he greeted the Lord even from the womb, not yet by speaking but by leaping for joy. His grace toward God was already manifest while his flesh was still enclosed within flesh. Yet this John is not found among the Lord’s disciples; rather, he is found having disciples alongside the Lord. What is this? Who is this man? So great a man—what man is so great? How great is such a man? Yet he did not follow the Lord among the disciples, and disciples followed him—not, far be it from me to say, against the Lord, but nevertheless as though apart from the Lord. Christ had disciples; John had disciples. Christ taught; John taught. What more shall I say? John baptized; Christ baptized. I will say still more here about baptism: Christ was baptized by John. Where are those who are swollen with the arrogance of a puffed-up spirit over the ministry of baptism? Where are those voices devoid of humility, raised up in pride: “I baptize! I baptize!”? What would you have said if you had deserved to baptize Christ? As Your Holiness can see, a great reason has already begun to appear and stand out for Christ’s being sent by the Father and John’s being sent ahead by Christ. John was sent first, but as a judge is preceded by his attendants. Christ was created as a man afterward, but Christ as God created John. John was therefore indeed a perfect man, and his great grace was so commended that the Lord himself said of him, *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist*. This man, then, so great himself, recognizes the great Lord in one who is small; the man recognizes him who had come as God made man. For if among those born of women—that is, among human beings—no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist, whoever is greater than John is not merely a man but also God. Thus this great man had to have disciples of his own and, together with his disciples, recognize Christ as the Teacher of all. For what greater testimony to the truth is there than humbly acknowledging someone whom rivalry could have led him to envy? He could have been thought the Christ, but refused; he could have been taken for the Christ, but refused. People who were mistaken about him said, “Could this be the Christ?” He answered by saying what he was not, so that he might remain what he was. For Adam fell and lost what he was precisely by claiming what he was not. This great man—yet very little in comparison with the little Christ—remembered this. He knew it, recalled it, and held fast to it, because he was intent on recovering what Adam had lost. Thus this man, the great John, as I have said, to whom the Lord bore such testimony, whom Truth so commended as to say, *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist,* could have been believed to be the Christ. Indeed, he was already believed to be the Christ by those misled by the grace of his greatness, and they would have died in that error unless corrected by his confession. So he answered those who thought this and said, *I am not the Christ*. It was as though he said, “Certainly your mistake honors me, and certainly by thinking this you add great praise to me. But I must acknowledge myself, so that he may pardon your error.” For if he were falsely taken for what he was not, he would be cut off from the one who truly was.

**3. 3.** John was therefore sent ahead to baptize the humble Lord. For the Lord wished to be baptized because of humility, not because of iniquity. Why was the Lord Christ baptized? Why was the Lord Christ, God’s only-begotten Son, baptized? Find why he was born, and there you will find why he was baptized. There you will find the path of humility, which you do not travel with a proud step. Unless you tread it with a humble foot, you cannot reach the height to which it leads. He who came down for your sake was baptized for your sake. See how great a one became so little: *Who, being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal to God.* For the Son’s equality with the Father was not robbery but nature. Had John wished to be thought the Christ, that would have been robbery for him. Thus *he did not consider it robbery to be equal to God.* For he was equal, and without robbery: he was born coeternal from the eternal one. Yet *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*—that is, taking the form of a human being. *Being in the form of God:* not having received the form of God. Therefore, *being in the form of God, he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant.* He took on what he was not in such a way that he did not lose what he was. Remaining God, he assumed humanity. He took the form of a servant, and God became man—the God by whom man was made.

**4. 3.** See, then, what majesty, what power, what sublimity, what equality with the Father came for our sake to put on the form of a servant. And learn that path of humility from so great a Teacher, for his willingness to become a man is greater than his willingness to be baptized by a man.

**4. 4.** John, then, I say, baptizes Christ: the slave his Lord, the voice the Word. Remember: *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness*; and remember that *the Word became flesh and dwelt among us*. John, then, I say, baptizes Christ: the slave his Lord, the voice the Word, the creature his Creator, the lamp the sun—but the sun who made this sun, the sun of whom it was said: *The sun of righteousness has risen for me, and healing is in his wings*. Concerning him the ungodly, repenting too late at the end, at God's judgment, will say: *What did pride profit us? Or what did boasting of riches bring us? All those things passed away like a shadow*—and with the shadows, those who followed shadows. *Therefore,* they say, *we strayed from the way of truth, and the light of righteousness did not shine upon us, and the sun* *did not rise for us*. Christ did not rise for those who did not recognize Christ. That sun of righteousness is without cloud, without night; he does not rise for the evil, the ungodly, or unbelievers. But this bodily sun in the sky he makes rise daily upon the good and the evil. Therefore, as I said, the creature baptized his Creator, the lamp the sun; and the baptizer did not exalt himself but submitted to the one to be baptized. For he said to him as he approached: *You come to me to be baptized? I ought to be baptized by you.* A great confession, the lamp's secure profession in humility! If it exalted itself against the sun, the wind of pride would quickly extinguish it. This, then, is what the Lord foresaw, what the Lord taught by his baptism. So great a one wished to be baptized by so small a one—to put it briefly, the Savior by one who needed saving. For John, great though he was, perhaps remembered some sickness of his own. Otherwise, why say: *I ought to be baptized by you?* Certainly the Lord's baptism is salvation, because *salvation belongs to the Lord*. For *human salvation is vain*. Why, then, say: *I ought to be baptized by you,* unless he needed healing? Yet what a marvelous medicine in the Lord's very humility! The one was baptizing, and the other was healing. For if Christ is *the savior of all people, especially believers*—it is an apostolic and true statement that Christ is the savior of all people—let no one say, 'I have no need of a savior.' Anyone saying this does not humble himself before the physician but perishes in his sickness. If he is the savior of all people, then of John too; for John was not something other than a human being. A great human being, certainly, but still a human being. Christ is the savior of all people; John therefore recognizes his own savior. For Christ was indeed John's savior. John himself does not deny it, but humbly confesses, saying: *I ought to be baptized by you.* And the Lord says: *Allow it now; let all righteousness be fulfilled*. What is *all righteousness?* He commended righteousness through humility. Our heavenly teacher and true Lord commended righteousness to us chiefly through humility. For being baptized pertained to humility; and therefore, about to do what pertained to humility, he said: *Let all righteousness be fulfilled.*

**4. 5.** He foresaw that many would become inflated over the ministry of baptism and say, 'I baptize,' and, 'I make the person I baptize such as I myself, the baptizer, am.' How do you prove this? 'I prove it,' he says. By what testimonies? 'From the Gospel,' he says. Let us hear this unknown new evangelist opposing the ancient Baptist. By what Gospel testimonies, then, do you prove that you make the person you baptize such as you are? 'Because it is written,' he says: *A good tree produces good fruits.* 'I recite what is written; I bring the Gospel: *A good tree produces good fruits; a bad tree produces bad fruits*.' I recognize the Gospel, but, as I think, you do not recognize yourself. And to bear with you patiently for a while, explain what you are saying; suppose for now that I have not understood. Tell me what these testimonies concern and how they help solve this question about baptism. 'A good tree,' he says, 'is a good baptizer.' 'A good tree,' he says—as they put it—'a good tree is a good baptizer; his good fruit is the one baptized by him. For the fruit will be good only if he is a good tree.' What do you say about Christ and John? Wake up, rouse yourself! The splendor of evident truth dazzles your eyes. See what has been set before us; read the Gospel: John baptized Christ. Will you say, 'John is the tree, Christ the fruit'? Will you call the creature a tree and the Creator its fruit? The Lord Christ wished to be baptized by John for this reason: not to be freed from iniquity through baptism, but to shut iniquity's mouth. See, the baptizer is inferior; shall I say the one baptized is better? Perhaps this is too much for you to understand from me. Return to human beings; consider two who are both human. Ananias baptized Paul. Paul was better than Ananias. Fruit was never better than its tree. For the tree produces the fruit; it is not produced by the fruit.

**4. 6.** Do you not see what you claim for yourself? The Lord himself says: *Many will come in my name, saying, I am the Christ*. Many who stray and deceive have come in Christ's name; we have heard none say, 'I am the Christ.' Countless heretics have come, all in Christ's name—that is, they came cloaked in Christ's name, whitewashing a mud wall with a splendid name—and we have heard none say, 'I am the Christ.' What, then? Did the Lord not know what he foretold? Or did he rather awaken us from sleep to understand these secrets, to uncover the secret, so that we might search and knock, and what is covered might be opened to us, and through the opened roof we might be lowered to the Lord, to deserve healing from the Lord like that paralytic? Certainly we find these men saying, 'I am the Christ,' not in these words, but, what is worse, in deeds. They do not dare those words. For who listens to them? Who would be deceived into admitting such fools to his ears or heart? If the man about to baptize someone told him, 'I am the Christ,' he would turn his face away, leave that man's manifest arrogance, and seek God's grace. Therefore, he does not say in that way, 'I am the Christ.' But because he says in another way, 'I am the Christ,' see how he does it. Christ heals, Christ cleanses, Christ justifies; a human being does not justify. What is justifying? Making righteous. As putting to death means making dead, and giving life means making alive, so justifying means making righteous. See, a certain baptizer approaches sideways, entering not through the door but coming down over the wall, not a shepherd and guardian but a thief and robber. Coming sideways, he says, 'I baptize.' If you mean as a minister, I allow it; add nothing. Whatever is more comes from evil. Yet he adds more without hesitation. What does he add? 'I justify; I make righteous.' This means: 'I am a good tree; let anyone who wants to be good fruit be born from me.' Listen briefly, if you will wisely receive it; these words are few and, unless I am mistaken, clear. So you justify, you make righteous? Then let the one you justify believe in you. Say it; dare to say, 'Believe in me,' you who do not hesitate to say, 'You are justified by me.' He is troubled, wavers, and makes excuses. 'What need is there,' he says, 'for me to tell him, Believe in me? I say, Believe in Christ.' You hesitated, you doubted; you have deigned to come down to us a little. You have confessed something through which you may be healed. You have said something right through which your other errors may be corrected. Now listen not to me but to yourself. Surely you do not dare say, 'Believe in me.' 'Far from it,' he says. Yet you dare say, 'I justify you.' Listen and learn: for the same reason that you do not dare say, 'Believe in me,' you ought not dare say, 'I justify you.' The Apostle speaks, to whom you will yield, to whom willingly or unwillingly you will submit—not to the Apostle as a human being, but to the one of whom the Apostle says: *Do you seek proof of Christ speaking in me?* Therefore, listen not to the Apostle but to Christ through the Apostle. What does the Apostle say? *To the one believing in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness*. Pay attention, I beg you; see how plain and open it is: *To the one believing in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness.* Anyone who believes in the one who justifies the ungodly, who makes the ungodly pious—anyone, then, who believes in the one who justifies the ungodly, who makes righteous the one who was ungodly—has his faith reckoned as righteousness. Now say, if you dare, 'I justify you.' See how I answered you from the Apostle: If you justify me, I will believe in you, because *to the one believing in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness.* Do you justify me? I will believe in you. For if you justify me, I will be someone believing in the one who justifies me, that is, who justifies the ungodly. I believe securely, because my faith is reckoned as righteousness. Therefore, if you do not dare say, 'I justify you'—rather, if you do not dare say, 'Believe in me'—take care now not to say, 'I justify you.' Lost man, find yourself, lest you lose both me and yourself.

**4. 7.** As for what you proposed about the tree and its fruit, I propose an example to you, so that you may learn to understand what was said: *A good tree produces good fruits, and a bad tree produces bad fruits.* For I understand it as the Lord himself explains it. What does *A good tree produces good fruits* mean? *A good man brings forth good things from the good treasure of his heart, and an evil man brings forth evil things from the evil treasure of his heart*. He made people the trees and their deeds the treasures. Such as a person is, such are his deeds. If he is a good person, he has good deeds; if an evil person, evil deeds. A good person cannot have evil deeds, nor an evil person good deeds. What is more evident, clearer, or more open? But now you make yourself, the baptizer, a good tree, and make the person you baptize your fruit, so that he may be such as you are. Far be it from him! See how perversely you understand. There is someone among you, or there once was, who is an adulterer, even if a hidden one. 'But what I do not know,' he says, 'does not contaminate me.' I am not dealing with that; it is another question. I want to say something about baptism, for that is our undertaking. He is a secret adulterer, and therefore a pretender—not a pretended adulterer, but a real adulterer pretending chastity. The Holy Spirit will certainly flee this adulterous pretender, all the more a pretender because he hides; for if he were openly adulterous, he would no longer be pretending. The declared sentence is evident: *The Holy Spirit of discipline will flee the deceitful*. Since this man is a secret adulterer, he nevertheless baptizes. See, I behold a man baptized by a secret adulterer. Fruit has been born; where is the good tree? He has been baptized, he is innocent, his sins have been forgiven. Therefore, an ungodly person has been justified, and good fruit has been born. I ask: from what tree? Tell me; answer me. That tree is a secret adulterer, a bad tree. If this is that tree's fruit, it is bad fruit. The Lord's sentence is: *A bad tree produces bad fruits.* To establish that this fruit is good, you will answer that it was not born from that tree. For your not knowing that the tree is bad does not make it cease to be bad; the more it goes unrecognized, the worse it is. For the more it conceals its deed with wicked cunning, the more it goes unrecognized. If it were an open adulterer, at least it could be healed through confession. A very bad tree, yet see, good fruit! From where was it born? Or perhaps it was not born? 'It was born,' you say. I ask from where; what will you say? From where was this person born? He has nothing to say except, 'From God.' I do not know whether he will say anything other than, 'From God.' If he said this about everyone, instead of pretending to show himself as a good tree when he is bad and thereby making himself worse, he would say about everyone that they are born from God. He has the Gospel's evident statement: *He gave them power to become God's children, who were born not from flesh, not from blood, not from a man's will, nor from the flesh's will, but from God*. Therefore, return to this man: was he born from God? From God. Why was this one born from God? Because good fruit could not be born from a bad tree. A chaste baptizer is a good tree, not a pretender; a truly chaste man baptized, and good fruit came from a good tree. Now this too is good fruit; from what tree was it born? Say from a bad one, if you dare. 'I do not dare,' he says. Therefore, this one too came from a good tree? From a good tree. Which good tree? From God. What about the other? From a chaste man. Pay attention a little; let us understand what we are saying. This person, baptized by a chaste man, was born as good fruit from a good tree, that is, from a good man. That person, baptized by a secret adulterer, was born as what kind of fruit from a bad tree? Good. That cannot happen. If the fruit is good, then change the tree. You acknowledge this fruit to be good and that man to be bad, because he is a secret adulterer; change the tree for this fruit. 'I have changed it,' you say; 'that is why I said, From God.' Now compare these two who have been born. An openly chaste man baptized one; a secret adulterer baptized the other. The former was born from a human being, the latter from God. Therefore, the one baptized by the secret adulterer was born more happily than the one baptized by the openly chaste man.

**4. 8.** It is better, then, to listen to John, heretic; better for you who run backward to hear the forerunner; better for you, proud man, to hear the humble; better for you, extinguished lamp, to hear the burning lamp. Listen to John when people came to him: *I indeed baptize you in water.* You too, if you recognize yourself, are a minister of water. *I,* he says, *baptize you in water; but the one who will come is greater than I.* How much greater than you? *I am not worthy to untie his sandal strap*. How much he would humble himself if he said he was worthy! Yet he did not say he was worthy even to untie the sandal strap. *He is the one who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*. Why do you substitute yourself for Christ? *He baptizes in the Holy Spirit.* Therefore, he justifies. What do you say? 'I baptize in the Holy Spirit; I justify.' Surely you are not saying, 'I am the Christ'? Surely you are not one of those of whom it was said: *Many will come in my name, saying, I am the Christ*? You have been caught; would that, having been caught, you might at least be found, you who had perished while uncaught! It is good to be caught in truth's nets for the great king's food. Therefore, no longer say, 'I justify; I sanctify,' lest you be convicted of saying, 'I am the Christ.' Say instead what the bridegroom's friend says, rather than wanting to boast of yourself as the bridegroom: *Neither the planter nor the waterer is anything, but God who gives the increase*. Listen also to the bridegroom's friend himself, whom we are discussing. Surely he had disciples alongside Christ, as it were, and was not Christ's disciple? Hear him confess that he is Christ's disciple. See him among Christ's disciples, all the more sure because more humble, all the more humble because greater. See him doing what is written: *The greater you are, the more humble yourself in everything, and you will find grace before God*. He already said: *I am not worthy to untie his sandal strap,* but in this he did not show himself a disciple. *The one who comes from heaven,* he says, *is above everyone*; *but we have all received from his fullness*. Therefore, the man who gathered disciples alongside Christ was himself among the disciples. Hear him confess himself a disciple more openly: *The one who has the bride is the bridegroom, but the bridegroom's friend stands and hears him*. He stands precisely because he hears him. *He stands and hears,* because if he does not hear, he falls. Rightly the other says: *To my hearing you will give* *exultation and joy.* What does *to my hearing* mean? To hear him, not to wish to be heard in his place. And so that we may know he commends humility in hearing him, when he said, *To my hearing you will give exultation and joy,* he immediately added, *and humbled bones will exult*. Therefore, he stands and hears him. *Humbled bones will exult,* because exalted bones are broken. Therefore, no slave should claim the Lord's power for himself. Let him rejoice to be in the household, and if he is placed over it, let him bring food to his fellow slaves at the proper time—but food by which he too lives, not so that they may live from him. For what is bringing food at the proper time except presenting Christ, praising Christ, commending Christ, and preaching Christ? This is bringing food at the proper time. For so that Christ himself might be food for his beasts of burden, he was placed in a manger at his birth.
