# Sermon 89: On the Words of the Gospel, Matthew 21:19-21, Where Jesus Withered the Tree; and on the Words of Luke 24:28, Where He Pretended to Go Farther.

**1.** The most recent reading of the holy Gospel has admonished us, has frightened us, lest we have leaves and not have fruit. This is briefly expounded: lest words be present and deeds be lacking. A great terror! Who would not fear, when in that very reading he sees with the eyes of the heart a withered tree, and so as for it to be said to it: *Never may fruit be born from you forever*[^1]. Let the terror correct, let the correction bring forth fruit. For without doubt the Lord Christ foresaw a certain tree deservedly becoming withered, because it had leaves and did not have fruit. That is the Synagogue, not called but reprobated. For from it too was called the people of God, who truly and sincerely awaited in the Prophets God's salvation, Jesus Christ. And because it awaited faithfully, it deserved to recognize him when present. For from it were the Apostles, from it the whole crowd that went before the Lord's beast of burden and said: *Hosanna to the son of David; blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord*[^2]. It was a great crowd of faithful Jews, believing in Christ before the blood was shed for them; it was a great crowd. For not without reason had the Lord himself come only to the sheep that had perished of the house of Israel[^3]. But among others he was crucified, now exalted in heaven, and he found the fruit of repentance: nor did he make them withered, but cultivated them in the field, and watered them with his word. From there were those four thousand Jews who believed, after the disciples, filled with the Holy Spirit, and those who were with them, spoke in the tongues of all nations[^4], and by that diversity of tongues in a way foretold the future Church among all nations. They believed then, and they themselves were the sheep that had perished of the house of Israel; but because *the Son of man had come to seek and to save what had perished*[^5], he found these too. But somewhere or other, as though plundered by wolves, they were hiding in the briars; and because they were hiding in the briars, he did not reach them to find them, except torn by the thorns of his passion: yet he reached them, found them, redeemed them. They had killed—not him so much as themselves. They were made safe through the one slain for them. For as the Apostles spoke, those who had pierced him with the lance were pierced with compunction, pierced in conscience: and, pierced, they sought counsel, received what was given, did penance, found grace, and believing drank the blood that, in raging, they had shed. But those who remained, with a bad and sterile offspring, until this day and until the end, are figured in that tree. Now you come to them, and find among them all the words of the Prophets. But these are leaves: Christ is hungry; he seeks fruit; but he therefore finds no fruit in them, because he does not find himself in them. For one who does not have fruit does not have Christ. But he does not have Christ who does not hold the unity of Christ, who does not have love. Therefore, by this connection, he does not have fruit who does not have love. Hear the Apostle: *But the fruit of the Spirit is love*[^6]; about to commend, as it were, the cluster, that is, the fruit. *But the fruit,* he says, *of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering*[^7]. Do not marvel at what follows, where love has begun.

**2.** Therefore, as the disciples marveled at the withering of the tree, he commended faith, and said to them: *If you have faith, and have not doubted*—that is, if in all things you have believed God: if you have not said, This God can do, this he cannot; but have presumed upon the omnipotence of the Almighty—*not only will you do this, but if you say to this mountain, Be taken up and cast into the sea, it will be done. And whatever you ask, believing, you will receive*[^8]. We read of miracles done by the disciples—rather, done by the Lord through the disciples: *For without me,* he says, *you can do nothing*[^9]. The Lord could do many things without the disciples, the disciples nothing without the Lord. He who could make the disciples themselves was surely not helped by them to make them. We read of the miracles of the Apostles, but nowhere do we read of a tree withered by them, or a mountain transferred into the sea. Let us seek, then, where it was done. For the Lord's words could not be empty. If you attend to these usual and familiar trees and these mountains, it was not done. If you attend to the tree of which he spoke, and the very mountain of the Lord of which the prophet said: *In the last days the mountain of the Lord will be manifest*[^10]: if you attend to these, if you understand these, both it was done, and it was done through them. The tree is the Jewish nation, but again I say, reprobated, not called: that tree which we have mentioned is the Jewish nation. The mountain, as the prophetic testimony taught, is the Lord himself. The withered tree is the Jewish nation without the honor of Christ: the sea, this world with all its nations. See now the Apostles speaking, to wither the tree, and casting the mountain into the sea. They speak, in the *Acts of the Apostles,* to the Jews who contradict and resist the word of truth—that is, having leaves and not having fruit—and they say to them: *To you first it was right that the word of God be announced, but since you have rejected it*[^11]: you speak the words of the Prophets, and do not recognize him whom the Prophets foretold—that is, having leaves: *behold, we turn ourselves to the gentiles*[^12]. For this too was foretold through the prophet: *Behold, I have set you for a light of the gentiles, that you may be salvation to the ends of the earth*[^13]. Behold, the tree has withered; and Christ to the gentiles, the mountain transferred into the sea. For how would the tree not wither, set in the vineyard of which it had been said: *I will command my clouds not to rain rain upon it*[^14]?

**3.** That the Lord commended himself to have done this prophetically—namely, that concerning this tree he willed not only to display a miracle, but to commend by the miracle something future—there are many things that admonish us and persuade us, nay rather, extort it from us though unwilling. First, what had the tree sinned, that it had no fruit? For if in its own time—that is, of those fruits—it had no fruit, there would surely be no fault of the wood; for the wood, without sense, had no fault. To this is added that, as is read in another evangelist who narrates this very thing: *It was not the time of those fruits*[^15]. For it was that time when the fig tree puts forth its tender leaves, which we know are born before the fruits: this we prove from the fact that the day of the Lord's passion was drawing near, and we know at what time he suffered; and if we did not attend to this, we ought surely to believe the Evangelist saying: *It was not the time of figs.* Therefore, if a miracle had only to be commended, and not something prophetically figured, much more mercifully would the Lord, and more worthily of his mercy, if he found any withered tree, have made it green; as he healed the languishing, as he cleansed lepers, as he raised the dead. But then, on the contrary, as though against the rule of his mercy, he found a green tree, not yet having fruit out of season, yet not denying fruit to its farmer, and made it withered: as though he said to a man, The withering of this tree did not delight me, but I wanted to suggest to you that I did not will to do this without cause, except that I wanted to commend to you something you should attend to more. I did not curse this tree, I did not inflict punishment on a tree without sense; but I frightened you, if you notice anything, that you should not despise the hungering Christ, and that you should love rather to be enriched with fruit than to be overshadowed with leaves.

**4.** This is the one thing by which the Lord commends that he willed to signify something. What else? Hungering, he approaches the tree, and seeks fruit. Did he not know it was not yet the time? What the cultivator of the tree knew, did the Creator of the tree not know? He seeks fruit, then, on a tree that did not yet have it. Does he seek, or rather pretend to seek? For if he truly sought, he erred. But far be it that he erred. Therefore he pretended. Fearing lest he pretend, you confess that he erred. You shun error, you run into pretense. We are in a fever in the middle. If we are in a fever, let us wish for rain, that we may grow green, lest, by saying something unworthy of the Lord, we rather grow dry. The Evangelist indeed says: *He came to the tree, and found no fruit on it*[^16]. Surely *he found no fruit* would not be said of him, except of one who either truly had sought, or had pretended to seek, what he knew was not there. Hence we do not doubt: let us by no means say that Christ erred. What then? Do we say he pretended? Do we say this? How shall we get out of here? Let us say what the Evangelist said of the Lord in another place, which we would not dare to say of ourselves on our own. What the Evangelist wrote, let us say; and when we have said it, let us understand it. But that we may understand, let us first believe. For *unless you believe, you will not understand*[^17], says the prophet. After the resurrection the Lord Christ was walking on the road with two of his disciples, by whom he was not yet recognized, whom he accompanied as though a third traveler. They came to the place to which they were heading, and the Evangelist says: *But he pretended to go farther*[^18]. But they held him, after the manner of humanity, saying that it was now growing toward evening, asking that he remain there with them: received as a guest, he breaks bread, and by the blessed and broken bread he is recognized. Let us not now fear, then, to say that he pretended to seek, if *he pretended to go farther.* But another question arises. Yesterday at length we commended truth in the Apostles; how do we find pretense in the Lord himself? It must be said, then, brothers, and conveyed to you according to our modest powers, which the Lord grants us for your sake, and commended to you, that in all the Scriptures you hold this as a rule. Everything that is said or done is either recognized by its own proper sense, or signifies something figuratively; or certainly has both, both proper recognition and figurative signification. I have proposed three; examples must be given, and from where but the holy Scriptures? Something is said which we take properly: that the Lord suffered, that he rose again and ascended into heaven, that we are to rise again at the end of the age, that with him we are to reign forever, if we do not despise him. Take this saying properly, do not seek figures: as it is said, so it is. So too with deeds. The Apostle went up to Jerusalem to see Peter[^19]: the Apostle did this, it was done, it is his own proper deed. He narrates to you a thing done: the deed itself according to its proper sense. Said figuratively: *The stone that the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner*[^20]. If we understand the stone properly, what stone did the builders reject, and it became the head of the corner? If we take the corner properly, at the head of what corner was this stone set? If we admit it to be said figuratively, and you take it figuratively: the cornerstone is Christ; the head of the corner, the head of the Church. Why is the Church a corner? Because from here he called the Jews, from there the gentiles, and bound together, as it were two walls coming from different directions and meeting in him, by the grace of his peace. *For he himself is our peace, who has made both one*[^21].

**5.** You have heard a proper saying, a proper deed, a figurative saying: you await a figurative deed. There are many, but meanwhile what occurs from the admonition of the cornerstone: when Jacob anointed the stone that he had placed at his head when he slept, in which sleep he had seen a great dream—a ladder rising from earth to heaven, and angels ascending and descending, with the Lord resting upon the ladder—he understood what he ought to figure, by which he might prove to us that he was not a stranger to the understanding of that vision and revelation; he figured the stone for Christ[^22]. Do not marvel, then, that he anointed it, for Christ received his name from anointing. Now this Jacob was called in Scripture a man without guile. This same Jacob, you know, was named Israel[^23]. Therefore the Lord in the Gospel, when he saw Nathanael, said: *Behold, truly an Israelite, in whom there is no guile*[^24]. And that Israelite, not yet knowing who was speaking with him, answered: *Whence do you know me*[^25]? And the Lord to him: *When you were under the fig tree, I saw you*: as though he said: When you were in the shadow of sin, I predestined you. And he, because he remembered that he had been under the fig tree, where the Lord was not, recognized the divinity in him, and answered: *You are the Son of God, you are the king of Israel*[^26]. He under the fig tree was not made a withered fig: he recognized Christ. And the Lord to him: *Because I said, When you were under the fig tree, I saw you, therefore you believe; greater things than these you will see*[^27]. What are these greater things? *Amen I say to you*[^28]. Because that Israelite is one in whom there is no guile, look to Jacob in whom there is no guile; and recall whence he speaks: the stone at his head, the vision in sleep, the ladder from earth to heaven, descending and ascending; and see what the Lord says to the Israelite without guile: *You will see heaven opened.* Hear, Nathanael without guile, what Jacob without guile saw: *You will see heaven opened, and the angels ascending and descending;* upon whom? *upon the Son of man*[^29]. Therefore that one anointed at the head was the Son of man; because *the head of the woman is the man, the head of the man is Christ*[^30]. Yet he did not say: Ascending from the Son of man and descending to the Son of man, as though he were only above: but, *ascending and descending upon the Son of man.* Hear the Son of man crying out from above: *Saul, Saul.* Hear the Son of man below: *Why do you persecute me*[^31]?

**6.** You have heard a proper saying, that we are to rise again: a proper deed, that, as was said, Paul went up to Jerusalem to see Peter[^32]. A figurative saying was: *The stone that the builders rejected*[^33]; a figurative deed, the anointed stone that was at Jacob's head. There is owed to your expectation what is of both kinds, both done in its proper sense and signifying something figurative. We know that Abraham had two sons, one from the slave woman, the other from the free woman; a thing done in its proper sense, not only narrated but also done: do you await something figurative there? *These are the two Testaments*[^34]. What, then, is said figuratively is in a way feigned. But because it has an outcome of signification, and that very signification holds the faith of the truth, it avoids the charge of falsehood. Behold, a sower went out to sow; and in sowing some fell on the road, some fell on rocky places, some fell among thorns, some fell on good ground. Who went out to sow, or when did he go out, or among what thorns, or upon what stones, or upon what road, or into what field? If you hear it as feigned, you understand it significantly: it is feigned. For if a sowing man truly went out, and cast the seeds, as we heard, among these various places, it would not be feigned, but neither would it be a lie. Yet though feigned, it is not a lie. Why? Because it signifies something; what is feigned does not deceive you. It seeks one who understands, it does not make one who errs. Wishing to commend this, Christ sought fruit; there he was commending a figurative, not a deceptive feigning; and thereby a praiseworthy, not a culpable feigning; one by which you, examining it, do not go into falsehood, but, searching it through, find the truth.

**7.** I see what he is going to say: Expound to me this which it signified, that he pretended to go farther. For if it did not signify, it is deceit, it is a lie. By our expositions and most careful rules we ought to say what that feigning of going farther signified: he pretended to go farther, and he is held back, that he may not go farther. As far, then, as pertains to bodily presence, the Lord Christ was thought to be absent; he was thought to be absent, as though he would go farther. Hold him faithfully, hold him for the breaking of bread. What shall I say? Have you recognized him? If you have recognized him, there you have found Christ. There is no need to speak longer of the sacrament. For those who defer to know this sacrament, Christ is farther from them. Let them hold it, let them not let it go; let them invite him to lodging, and they are invited to heaven.
