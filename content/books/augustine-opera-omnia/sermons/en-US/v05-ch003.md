# Sermon 150

**1. 1.** Your Charity noticed, along with us, when the book of *the Acts of the Apostles* was read, that Paul spoke to the Athenians, and was called a sower of words by those who mocked the preaching of the truth[^1]. It was said, indeed, by mockers, but it is not to be rejected by believers. For he was truly a sower of words, but a reaper of morals. And we too, though so small and by no means to be compared with that excellence, sow the words of God in the field of God, which is your heart, and look for a rich harvest from your morals. Yet as to the reason we are admonished to speak to your Charity, which is contained in this very reading, we urge you to attend more closely, in case, with the help of the Lord our God, we may say something which cannot easily be understood by all unless it be said, nor, once it has been understood, ought to be despised by anyone.

**1. 2.** He was speaking at Athens. The Athenians were renowned with great fame above other peoples in all literature and learning. It was the very homeland of the great philosophers. From there a varied and manifold learning had spread itself through the rest of Greece and the other lands of the world. There the Apostle was speaking; there he was proclaiming *Christ crucified, to the Jews indeed a stumbling block, but to the gentiles folly; yet to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^2]. How great a danger it was to proclaim this among the proud and the learned, it is for you to consider. And so, when his discourse was finished and the resurrection of the dead had been heard—which is the chief faith of Christians—some mocked, but others said, *We will hear you again about this*. Nor were there lacking those who believed, and among them is named a certain Dionysius the Areopagite, that is, a leading man of the Athenians (for the Areopagus was the name of the council of the Athenians); and a certain noble woman, and others[^3]. So as the Apostle spoke, that crowd became threefold, arranged by certain degrees in a remarkable distinction: of mockers, of doubters, of believers. *Some*, as we heard written, *mocked, and some said, "We will hear you again about this"*—these were the doubters; some believed. Between the mockers and the believers, the doubters are in the middle. He who mocks falls; he who believes stands; he who doubts wavers. *We will hear you again about this*, they say: it is uncertain whether they were going to fall with the mockers or stand with the believers.

**2. 2.** Yet did that sower of words labor in vain? Truly, if he had feared the mockers, he would not have reached the believers; just as that gospel sower whom the Lord mentions (for surely Paul was that sower), if he were afraid to scatter seed, lest some fall on the path, some among the thorns, some on rocky ground[^4], could never have brought the seed even to the best soil. Let us too sow, let us scatter; prepare your hearts, give fruit.

**2. 3.** This too, if your Charity remembers, we heard when it was read, that some of the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers were conferring with the Apostle[^5]. Who the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers are or were—that is, what they thought, what they held to be true, what they pursued in their philosophizing—many of you doubtless do not know; but since we are speaking at Carthage, many do know. Let them therefore now help us as we are about to speak to you. For it pertains very much to the matter, what I think must be said. Let both those who do not know and those who do know hear us; let those who do not know be instructed, those who do know be reminded; let the one learn, the other recognize anew.

**3. 4.** First hear, in general, the common pursuit of all the philosophers, in which common pursuit they had five divisions and differences of their own opinions. In common, all the philosophers, by studying, inquiring, disputing, and living, sought to lay hold of the happy life. This was the one cause of philosophizing; but I think that even this the philosophers have in common with us. For if I should ask you why you have believed in Christ, why you have become Christians, every person truly answers me: For the sake of the happy life. The desire of the happy life, then, is common to philosophers and Christians. But where so harmonious a thing can be found—there is the question, and then the discernment. For to desire the happy life, to will the happy life, to covet, to long for, to pursue the happy life, I think belongs to all human beings. Hence I see that I have said too little in calling this desire of the happy life common to philosophers and Christians; for I ought to have said, common to all human beings, indeed to all, both good and evil. For the one who is good is good in order to be happy; and the one who is evil would not be evil unless he hoped that thereby he could be happy. About the good the question is easy, that they seek the happy life and are good for that reason. About the evil, perhaps some doubt whether they too seek the happy life. But if I could question the evil set apart and distinguished from the good, and say, Do you wish to be happy? no one would say, I do not wish it. Take, for example, a thief; I ask him, Why do you steal? "That I may have," he says, "what I did not have." Why do you wish to have what you did not have? "Because it is wretched not to have." If, then, it is wretched not to have, he reckons it happy to have. But in this he is shameless and errs, because he wishes to become happy by evil. For it is good for all to be happy. Whence, then, is that perverse man? Because he seeks good and does evil. What, then, does he seek? Why does the desire of the evil aspire to the reward of the good? The happy life is the reward of the good; goodness is the work, happiness is the reward. God commands the work, sets forth the reward; he says, Do this, and you shall receive this. But that evil man answers us, "Unless I do evil, I shall not be happy." As if someone should say, "I do not arrive at the good unless I am evil." Do you not see that good and evil are contraries? You seek good and do evil? You run in the contrary direction; when do you arrive?

**4. 5.** Let us therefore leave these men; perhaps it will be opportune to return to them when we have finished what we have undertaken about the philosophers. For I do not think it was without cause, by the divine providence itself disposing, that through people who did not know it something great was done: that, although there were very many sects of philosophers in the city of Athens, none conferred with the apostle Paul except the Stoics and the Epicureans. For when you have heard what they hold in their sects, you will see how not in vain it came about that of all the philosophers these alone conferred with Paul. For he could not choose for himself those to whose disputing he should reply; but the divine wisdom that governs all things set these before him, in whom nearly the whole cause of the philosophers' dissension consisted. Briefly, then, I say: let the unlearned believe us, let the learned judge of us. I think that I dare not lie when the unlearned are my hearers and the learned my judges—most of all because I am saying something about which both the learned and the unlearned can judge truly alike. This, then, I say first: that a human being consists of soul and body. Here I do not ask that you believe, but I ask you too to judge. For I do not fear that in this saying anyone may judge ill of me who knows himself. A human being, then, as no one doubts, consists of soul and body. This substance, this thing, this person who is called a human being seeks the happy life; and this you know—I do not insist that you believe, but I admonish you to recognize. A human being, I say—that is, this thing which is not small, surpassing all cattle, all birds, all swimming creatures, and whatever bears flesh and is not a human being; a human being, then, consisting of soul and body—but not of just any soul, for cattle too consist of soul and body; a human being, then, consisting of a rational soul and mortal flesh, seeks the happy life. Unless, when a human being has come to know what makes the happy life, he holds to it, follows it, claims it for himself, takes it if it is in his power, asks for it if it is hard to obtain, he cannot be happy. The whole question, then, is what makes the happy life. Now set before your eyes the Epicureans, the Stoics, and the Apostle—which I could also put thus: the Epicureans, the Stoics, the Christians. Let us first question the Epicureans: what makes the happy life? They answer: The pleasure of the body. Here now I ask that you believe, because I have judges. For whether the Epicureans say this, hold this, you do not know, because you have not read those writings; but there are those here who have read them. Let us return to the questioning. What do you say, Epicureans—what makes the happy life? They answer: The pleasure of the body. What do you say, Stoics—what makes the happy life? They answer: The virtue of the soul. Let your Charity attend with me: we are Christians; we are disputing among philosophers. See why only those two sects were procured to confer with the Apostle. There is nothing in a human being that pertains to his substance and nature except body and soul. Of these two, in the one—that is, in the body—the Epicureans placed the happy life; in the other—that is, in the soul—the Stoics placed the happy life. So far as concerns the human being, if the happy life is to be his from himself, nothing remains but body and soul. Either the body is the cause of the happy life, or the soul is the cause of the happy life; if you seek more, you depart from the human being. These, then, who placed the happy life of the human being within the human being, could place it nowhere else but either in the body or in the soul. Of those who placed it in the body, the Epicureans held the chief place; of those who placed it in the soul, the Stoics held the chief place.

**5. 6.** See, here they are, conferring with the Apostle; whether the Apostle has anything more, or whether he had necessarily to agree with one of these two sects, so that he too should place the cause of the happy life either in the body or in the soul. Paul would never place it in the body, for this is no great thing—since even they place the cause of happiness least of all in the body, who think better of the body. For the Epicureans think this same thing of both body and soul, that each is mortal. And what is graver and more detestable, they say that the soul is dissolved after death sooner than the body. For "still," they say, "after the spirit is breathed out, while the corpse remains and the lineaments of the limbs last for a while, the soul, as soon as it has gone out, like smoke scattered by the wind, is dissolved." Let us not wonder, then, that they placed the highest good—that is, the cause of happiness—in the body, which they felt themselves to possess better than the soul. Would the Apostle do this? Far be it that he should place the highest good in the body. For the highest good is the cause of happiness; rather, the Apostle grieved that some of the number of Christians had chosen the opinion of the Epicureans—not of men, but of pigs. For from this number were those who were corrupting good morals by evil conversations and saying, *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*[^6]. The Epicureans conferred with the apostle Paul; there are Christian Epicureans too. For what else are they saying who say daily, *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*? To which this belongs: "There will be nothing after death, for our life is the passing of a shadow." For they said within themselves, reasoning not rightly, among other things, *Let us crown ourselves with roses before they wither; let there be no meadow that our luxury does not pass through; let us everywhere leave the signs of our merriment, for this is our portion, and this is our lot*[^7].

**6. 7.** If we rebuke this more sternly, if we resist these desires more vehemently, they will say also what follows: *Let us oppress the poor righteous man*[^8]. And yet, even set in this place, we do not fear to say: Do not be Epicureans. Consider indeed that which was said by these who spoke not rightly, *For tomorrow we shall die*; but we shall not die in every way, for there remains after death what follows death. The companion of the dying will be either life or punishment. Let no one say, Who has returned here from there? That rich man in purple wished to return late, and could not be permitted. Thirsting, he sought a drop, who had despised the hungry poor man[^9]. Let no one, then, say, *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*. If you wish to say, *For tomorrow we shall die*, I do not forbid it; but say something else first. The Epicureans, indeed, as though they were not going to live after death, as though they had nothing else but what delights the flesh, say, *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*. But Christians, who are going to live after death—and rather to live happily after death—let them not say, *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*; but hold to what is said, *For tomorrow we shall die*, and say: Let us fast and pray, *for tomorrow we shall die*. I add plainly something else, I add a third thing, nor do I pass over what must especially be observed: that from your fasting the hunger of the poor man may be satisfied, or, if you cannot fast, rather feed the one by whose being filled pardon may be granted you. Let Christians, then, say: Let us fast and pray and give, *for tomorrow we shall die*. Or if they wish to say two things, I choose that they say, Let us give and pray, rather than, Let us fast and not give. Far be it, then, that the Apostle should place the highest good of the human being—that is, the cause of happiness—in the body.

**7. 8.** But with the Stoics perhaps the struggle is not unbecoming. For see, when they are asked where they place that which effects the happy life—that is, what makes the happy life in a human being—they answer, not the pleasure of the body, but the virtue of the soul. What of the Apostle? Does he agree? If he agrees, let us agree. But he does not agree; for Scripture calls back those who trust in their own virtue[^10]. The Epicurean, therefore, placing the highest good of the human being in the body, places his hope in himself. But the Stoic too, placing the highest good of the human being in the soul, placed it indeed in the better thing of the human being; yet he too placed his hope in himself. But both the Epicurean and the Stoic are human beings. *Cursed, then, is everyone who places his hope in man*[^11]. What, then? With the three now set before our eyes—the Epicurean, the Stoic, the Christian—let us question each one. Tell me, Epicurean, what makes a person happy. He answers: The pleasure of the body. Tell me, Stoic. The virtue of the soul. Tell me, Christian. The gift of God.

**8. 9.** And so, brothers, as it were before our eyes the Epicureans and Stoics conferred with the Apostle, and by their conference taught us what we ought to reject and what we ought to choose. The virtue of the soul is a praiseworthy thing: prudence, discerning evil and good; justice, distributing to each his own; temperance, restraining the lusts; fortitude, bearing hardships with an even mind. A great thing, a praiseworthy thing; praise it, Stoic, as much as you can; but say: From where do you have it? It is not the virtue of your soul that makes you happy, but he who gave you virtue, who breathed into you the willing and bestowed the being able[^12]. I know that perhaps you are going to mock me, and you will be among those of whom it is written that they mocked Paul[^13]. If you are the path, I sow, for I am a sower of words according to my small measure. What was your reproach is my office. I sow; what I sow falls on you as on hard ground. I am not slothful; and I find good ground. What shall I do to you? You have been rebuked, and rebuked by a divine oracle. You are among those who trust in their own virtue; you are among those who place their hope in man. Virtue delights you; a good thing delights you; I know, you thirst; but you cannot make virtue flow for yourself. You are dry; if I show you the fountain of life, perhaps you will deride it. For you say within yourself, "Am I going to drink from that rock?" The rod approaches, and water flowed[^14]. *For the Jews*, indeed, *ask for signs*; but you, Stoic, are not a Jew; I know, you are a Greek; *and the Greeks seek wisdom. But we preach Christ crucified*[^15]. The Jew is scandalized, the Greek scoffs. *For to the Jews a stumbling block, but to the gentiles folly; but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks*—that is, to Paul himself from Saul, and to Dionysius the Areopagite, and to such as these and such as those—*Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^16]. Now you do not mock the rock; recognize the rod, the cross; Christ, the fountain; and if you thirst, drink virtue. Be fed at the fountain; perhaps you will belch forth thanksgivings; what you have from him you will no longer give to yourself, but in your belching you will cry out, *I will love you, O Lord, my strength*[^17]. Now you will not say, The virtue of my soul makes me happy. You will not be among those who, knowing God, *did not glorify him as God or give thanks, but became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened; for claiming to be wise, they became fools*[^18]. For what is it, *claiming to be wise*, but to have from oneself, to suffice for oneself? *They became fools*; deservedly *fools*. True folly is false wisdom. But you will be among those of whom it is said, *O Lord, they will walk in the light of your countenance, and in your name they will rejoice all the day, and in your righteousness they will be exalted; for you are the glory of their strength*[^19]. You were seeking virtue; say, *O Lord, my strength*. You were seeking the happy life; say, *Blessed is the man whom you instruct, O Lord*[^20]. For blessed is the people whose joy is not the pleasure of the flesh, whose strength is not its own; but *blessed is the people whose God is the Lord himself*[^21]. This is the homeland of happiness, which all desire; but not all rightly seek it. But let us not contrive for ourselves a way to such a homeland, as it were out of our own heart, and devise wandering paths; from there came also the Way.

**8. 10.** For what does the happy man will—what does he will but not to be deceived, not to die, not to grieve? And what does he seek? To hunger more and to eat more? What if it is better not to hunger? No one is happy except the one who lives forever without any fear, without any deception. For the soul hates to be deceived. How much the soul naturally hates to be deceived can be understood from this: that those who laugh in a deranged mind are wept over by the sane; and a man surely chooses to laugh rather than to weep. If these two are set before him—Do you wish to laugh or to weep?—who is there who answers anything but: To laugh? Again, if these two are set before him—Do you wish to be deceived or to hold the truth?—every person answers: To hold the truth. And he chooses both to laugh and to hold the truth; of those two, laughter and weeping, To laugh; of these two, deception and truth, To hold the truth. But truth, most invincible, so prevails that a man chooses to weep in a sane mind rather than to laugh in a deranged one. There, then, in that homeland, truth will be, deception and error nowhere. But there will be truth, and there will be no weeping. For there will be a true laughing too, and a rejoicing in the truth, because there will be life. For if there is grief, there will not be life; nor is everlasting and immortal torment to be called life. Therefore the Lord, not calling the existence the ungodly are going to have life—although they are going to live in fire, and do not end their life, lest they end their punishment, for *their worm shall not die, and their fire shall not be quenched*[^22]—yet was unwilling to call it life, but called this life which is happy and eternal. Hence, when that rich man asked the Lord, *What good thing shall I do that I may attain eternal life*[^23]? he himself indeed named eternal life as nothing but happy life. For the ungodly will have eternal life, but not happy, because it is full of torments. So that man said, *Lord, what good thing shall I do that I may attain eternal life*? The Lord answered him concerning the commandments. The man said, All these I have done. But when he answered concerning the commandments, what did he say? *If you wish to come to life*[^24]. He did not say to him: happy; because a wretched existence is not even to be called life. He did not say to him: eternal; because where there is fear of death, it is not even to be called life. Therefore that life which is worthy of this name, to be called life, is none but the happy; and it is not happy unless it is eternal. This all desire, this we all desire—truth and life; but to so great a possession, to so great a happiness, by what way does one go? The philosophers built for themselves ways of error; some said, This way; others, Not this, but this. The way lay hidden from them, because God resists the proud[^25]. It would lie hidden from us too, had it not come to us. Therefore the Lord says, *I am the way*. Slothful traveler, you were unwilling to come to the way; the Way came to you. You were seeking by what way you might go: *I am the way*. You were seeking where you might go: *I am the truth and the life*[^26]. You will not go astray when you go to him, through him. This is the teaching of Christians, by no means to be compared with, but incomparably to be preferred to, the teachings of the philosophers—to the uncleanness of the Epicureans, the pride of the Stoics.
