# Sermon 293/A: Augm. (Dolbeau 3)

**1.** Since the Lord has willed on this day to restore to your love our voice and presence—and this he did, not according to our arrangement, but according to his own will—we give him thanks with you, and render you the service of a sermon, which is our ministry, in which it is both fitting and proper for us to serve you. But it is yours, dearly beloved, to receive the dispensing of God's servants, of whatever sort, with love, and to give thanks with us to him who has granted us to spend this day together with you.

**2.** Of whom, then, shall we speak today, but of him whose birthday it is today? John, then, holy, born of a barren woman, the forerunner of the Lord born of a virgin, was made the greeter of his Lord from the womb, his announcer after the womb. There was no barren woman to give birth, no virgin to give birth from: the barren woman bore the herald, the virgin bore the judge. But the Lord himself, Jesus Christ, about to come to men from the womb of the virgin, sent many heralds before him. All the prophets were sent before by him, but he himself spoke in them, he who came after them; yet he was before them. Since, then, the Lord sent many heralds before him, what did this one merit so greatly, what greater excellence did he have, whose birthday is commended to us today? For it is not without the sign of a certain greatness that his birthday is not hidden, just as the birthday of his Lord is not hidden. When the other prophets were born, we do not know; of John it was not permitted to be ignorant. This too is great in him, that the others foretold the Lord and desired to see him and did not see, and if they saw in spirit, they saw what was to come; but they were not here present to see him present. And the Lord himself says of them to his disciples that *many prophets and righteous men desired to see what you see, and did not see it, and to hear what you hear, and did not hear it*[^1]. Was it not he who sent them? But there was in all a desire, if it could be, to see Christ here in the flesh. But because they went before in dying, as they went before in being born, Christ did not find them here, yet Christ nonetheless redeemed them unto eternal life. And that you may know what kind of desire there was in all to see Christ here, remember that old man Simeon, to whom the Spirit of God announced, as no small good, that he would not depart from this world unless he had seen Christ. Christ was born: he recognized him as an infant in his mother's hands, received him, held in his hands the one by whose divinity he himself was carried; and holding in his hands the infant Word, he blessed God, saying: *Now you dismiss your servant, Lord, in peace, for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^2]. The other prophets, then, did not see him here: Simeon saw the infant; John recognized and greeted him when conceived, announced and saw him as a young man. This one, then, is more excellent than all the rest.

**3.** Hear also the Lord's testimony about him: he set himself before him, no one else. Very great was he, before whom none but Christ could be set. Accordingly the Lord himself says thus: *Among those born of women none has arisen greater than John the Baptist.* And to set himself before him: *But he who is least in the kingdom of heaven,* he says, *is greater than he*[^3]. He called himself both less and greater: less in being born, greater in being Lord. For the Lord was born after him, but in the flesh, but of a virgin; while before him *in the beginning was the Word*[^4]. A great thing: John before Christ. For *all things were made through him, and without him was nothing made*[^5]. Why, then, did John come? To show the way of humility, that the presumption of man might be diminished, the glory of God increased. John came, then, a great man commending the Great One; John came, the measure of man. What is the measure of man? No man could be more than John; whatever was more than John was now more than man. For if the measure of human greatness had been bounded in John, you would find no man greater, and yet you have found one greater: confess God, whom you could find greater than the perfect man. John was a man and Christ was a man, but John only a man, Christ God and man. As God, he made John; as man, he was born after John.

**4.** But yet, see how greatly that forerunner of his Lord, of God and man, humbles himself. He than whom none greater arose among those born of women is asked whether he himself were the Christ. So great was he that men could be mistaken. It was doubted of him whether he himself were the Christ; and it was so doubted that he was asked. Now if he were a son of pride, not a teacher of humility, he would not set himself against erring men, nor act so that they should think it, but would accept what they already thought. Was it perhaps too much for him to wish to persuade men that he himself was the Christ? If he tried to persuade and were not believed, he would have remained abject and cast down, despised among men and damned before God. But he had no need to persuade men; he already saw that they thought this: he might accept their error and increase his own honor. But far be this from a faithful friend of the bridegroom, that he should wish himself to be loved by the bride in his stead. He confessed that he was not what he was not, lest he lose what he was. For John was not the bridegroom. For, when he was asked, he said this: *He who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the friend of the bridegroom stands and hears him, and rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom's voice*[^6]. *I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance; but he who comes after me is greater than I*[^7]. How much greater? *Whose sandal-strap I am not worthy to loose*[^8]. See how much less he would be, if he were worthy; how much he would humble himself, if he said this: *He is greater than I, whose sandal-strap I am worthy to loose*; for he would have said himself worthy to bend down to his feet. But now how high he commended him, when he said himself unworthy even of his feet, indeed even of his sandals! He came, then, to teach the proud humility, to announce the way of repentance.

**5.** The voice came before the word. How is the voice before the word? What is said of Christ? *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; this was in the beginning with God*[^9]. But that the Word might come to us, *the Word was made flesh,* that it might dwell among us[^10]. Therefore, that Christ is the Word, we have heard; that John is the voice, let us hear. When it was said to him, *Who are you?*, he answered: *I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness*[^11]. A little, then, dearly beloved, a little, as much as the Lord grants, let us treat of voice and word. Christ is the Word, the word that does not sound and pass; for what sounds and passes is a voice, not a word. The Word of God, then, through which all things were made, is our Lord Jesus Christ; *the voice of one crying in the wilderness* is John. Which is prior, the voice or the word? Let us see what a word is and what a voice is, and there we will see which is prior.

**6.** What do you think a word is, brethren? Let us set aside that Word of God; let us speak a little about our own words, if we can, from the lowest things, take up some steps of likeness to the highest. The Word of God, through which all things were made, who comprehends it? Who is worthy even to think it, let alone to utter it? A little, then, let us set aside its majesty, its ineffable eternity and co-eternity with the Father; let us believe what we do not see, that by believing we may merit to see. See, let us treat of this word, as of a thing that is daily turned over in our hearts or in our ears or in our mouths. What is a word? Do we now suppose that this is a word, what sounds to your ears? That is the word which you wish to say. You have conceived something in your heart to say: that very conception has already been made a word in your heart, but this word, that is, what you wish to say, the conception made in your heart, what you have taken up to utter, you already know, and it has been spoken within you. Let that Word that is the Son of God be present, that I may be able to bring forth to your ears, as is fitting, what he himself has deigned to give our heart to conceive. But if perhaps, unequal and too weak for the greatness of the matter, I fall short and do not speak as is fitting, you have one to whom you may turn from me: let that same Son of God, the Word of God, preside over your hearts and fill there, by speaking within you, what I, as a man, have not been able to fill in your ears. Yet aid my effort by your attention and by your prayer for me, that I may be able to speak, and for you, that you may merit to hear.

**7.** A word, then, is, as we said, what you have conceived in your heart in order to say it: the very thing you wish to say, conceived in the heart in order to be said, is called a word. When, therefore, you have conceived the thing you wish to say, and the thing itself, the very thought, has been made a word in your heart, you will attend to the one to whom you are speaking and to whom you wish to say it; and if you see a Greek, you look for a Greek word in which to call forth the word; if you see a Latin, you look for a Latin word in which to bring forth the word; if you see a Hebrew, a Hebrew word; if you see a Carthaginian, a Carthaginian word, if you know those languages. But if you do not know them, when you see standing before you one who alone knows the very language you do not know, you do not fail in the word, you fail in the voice. That word, then, which you had conceived in your heart went before all voices and was before all voices, before the Greek, before the Latin, before the Hebrew, before the Carthaginian, and whatever languages and whatever voices there are throughout the world, that conception went before them, and the offspring of the soul was carried by the soul in travail, and how it might be brought forth was sought, since what was already held in the heart could not be brought forth to another except through some voice. But that voice, unless it were distinct, whence could it be recognized? And the voice had to be distinguished by the diversity of languages, so that you speak Greek to a Greek, Hebrew to a Hebrew, Carthaginian to a Carthaginian. But that word which you had conceived before all voices was neither Latin nor Greek nor Carthaginian nor anything of the sort. See, then, the great mystery. Accordingly, if you were silent altogether, would the word therefore not live in your heart, and, even if there were no one to whom to speak, would what you had conceived in your heart not lie open within you? It would lie open without any difference of language, in simple knowledge.

**8.** For example, let us say something, that it may become plainer. God is a certain thing above all that he has made—if indeed he is to be called a thing; therefore God is something pre-eminent over all that he has made, from whom and in whom and through whom are all things. Is all this that I have said, which God is, two syllables, and is all that great power bounded by these two syllables? But God existed even before I conceived this in my heart. How was I able to say this very thing, "God"? But what is called in Latin "Deus," what is called in Greek "theos," what is called in Punic "ylim"—I have named three languages—what I conceived in my heart was none of those languages; but when I wished to bring forth what I had conceived in my heart concerning God, if I found a Carthaginian, I said "ilim"; if I found a Latin, I said "Deus"; if I found a Greek, I said "theos"; before I found any of them, that which was in my heart was neither Greek nor Punic nor Latin. That, then, which I conceived to be brought forth was the word; that which I employed to bring it forth was the voice.

**9.** We have discussed voice and word: the word before all languages, the voice in some language. Which, then, is prior, the word or the voice? With me the word is prior. For unless I conceived the word in my heart, I would not employ the voice by which to bring forth the word. The word, then, was conceived before the voice, and it employed the voice as a kind of vehicle, through which it might come to you, not in which it might exist in me. For I know what I am going to say, even if I do not say it. See, before I speak, I have not employed a voice, and the word is with me. But that I may make it known to you, I employ a voice, so that, when you have heard the voice, the word may be in you too. Therefore with me, that I may teach, the word goes before, and the voice follows. But with you, that you may learn, the voice goes before, and the word follows. Attend diligently and understand, with the Lord's help. For I know that I am speaking of hidden things, set in the secret place of mysteries, but I speak, such as I am, to faithful Christians whose faith anticipates what I am going to say. With me, then, the word went before, and to the preceding word a voice was joined. With you, my voice went before, and then you understood the word that was in my heart.

**10.** If, then, Christ is the Word, John the voice, the Word, Christ, went before with God; but to us the voice went before, that the Word might come to us. The Word, then, was with God, and John the voice was not yet. For before John was the voice, was the Word not with God? It was there; but that the Word might be spoken to us, John was taken up as a voice. And that the Word might come to us, the voice went before. Therefore Christ too was before John from eternity; and yet he ought not to be born first, but John, so that the voice might go before the Word to us. Blessed be the Lord our God, for I have spoken as I could, and you have understood what you could. May he increase and multiply your understanding, and may the Word that sent the voice before it shine clear to you.

**11.** But see, my brethren: the voice sounds and passes, the word remains. See what I am saying. See, I said "God"; first I conceived in my heart what I would say, then those two syllables sounded and passed. Does what I conceived in my heart pass away with them? Again, when I said "God," it was brought about in your heart that you thought of God; and in my heart it went before, that I might say it, and in your heart the thought of God was brought about, when you heard those two syllables. Those two syllables fulfilled their ministry and passed away; yet that which I had conceived in my heart did not pass away: it was in me, and after those two syllables were spoken it remained in me; and what was brought about of thought in your heart, when those two syllables touched your ear, remained in your heart even after they passed away. Therefore, brethren, the ministry of the man John was, like a voice, to pass away. Rightly he received baptism too, but the baptism of John was transitory and was called the baptism of John. There is the baptism of Christ and the baptism of John, but the baptism of John, like a voice, was transitory; the baptism of Christ remains and remains forever, just as the word remains.

**12.** And the more we advance in God, the more the voices are diminished and the word grows in us. But why do we have voices, except that we may understand something? If there were fullness of understanding in us, there would be no need of voices. If we could see our thoughts, would there be any need of a tongue, that we might speak with one another? There will be a time, then, when we shall see the Word, as it is seen by the angels, and there will be no need of your voices. For there will be no need of preaching the Gospel then, when we shall see the Word itself. All temporal things will pass away, because the voice is of the flesh, is of the hay; but *the splendor of the flesh is as the flower of the hay: the hay has withered, the flower has fallen, but the word of the Lord remains forever*[^12]. Because, then, the more we advance toward understanding, the less there will be need of the voices through which we are led to understanding, therefore John himself said: *He must increase, but I must decrease*[^13]. As the word increases, the voice is diminished. What is it, as the word increases? For the Word itself does not increase, but we increase in it, we advance in it, we are enlarged in it, so that we no longer have voices as necessary. This appeared also through the very nativities of the word and the voice. The Word was born on the eighth day before the Kalends of January, from which the day begins to increase; the voice was born before the Word of God, when the day begins to decrease. *He must increase, but I must decrease.* And their very passions showed this: John was diminished, struck in the head; Christ increased, raised up on the cross.

**13.** And so let us celebrate the birthday of the voice in honor of the Word, and let us not attend to or be deceived by the quibbles of empty men, who do not understand what they say. For because John had a baptism, and it is found in the Acts of the Apostles that those who had only John's baptism were baptized—for certain disciples were found having John's baptism, and were bidden by the apostle Paul to be baptized, because they had only a transitory baptism, because they had the baptism of the voice, not yet of the Word; for now you seek John's baptism and do not find it: for the voice sounded and passed away, but the baptism of Christ remains today—because, then, for the sake of that very mystery the apostle Paul bade those who had John's baptism to be baptized, from this the heretics wished to draw an argument for rebaptism, whose error we lament and at whose deliverance we rejoice. Let us, then, answer something to this briefly.

**14.** You think, then, that a man who has received the baptism of Christ ought to be rebaptized, because the apostle Paul bade men who had John's baptism to be baptized, and you argue thus: *If after John the Baptist, of whom the Lord said,* *Among those born of women none has arisen greater than John the Baptist*[^14], *if after him the apostles baptized, how much more ought one to be baptized after the heretics!* I answer: You think an injury is done to John the Baptist, because there was baptizing after him, if there is no baptizing after the heretics. And I too lament that very injury, but I reply to you thus: If there was baptizing after John, ought there not to be baptizing after Optatus? Why do you say this to me? Who was John? *Among those born of women none shall arise greater than John the Baptist.* You have some presbyter, even a drunkard—I will not say "usurer," I will not say "adulterer"—but for now, what abounds and is done publicly, you have at least a drunkard for a presbyter. Very well. Why do you not rebaptize after him? If you baptize after John, who drank no wine, ought you not to baptize after a drunkard? Here surely he is confounded and has nothing to say. What then? Hear from me.

**15.** Paul too bids those to be baptized who had John's baptism and did not have Christ's. But why do you not baptize after a drunkard? Because he gave no baptism but Christ's. For it is the baptism of Christ: let a sober man give it, let a drunkard give it, it is Christ's; it is neither the sober man's nor the drunkard's. Peter gave this baptism: it is Christ's; Judas gave it: it is Christ's. For it is not Peter's baptism because Peter gave it. Why? Just as that was called John's baptism, it was not so called in those whom Peter baptized, in those whom Paul baptized, in those whom John the Evangelist baptized, in those whom Judas baptized; it was not called Peter's baptism, Paul's baptism, Judas's baptism; but in those whom Peter baptized, in those whom Paul, John, Judas baptized, it is the baptism of Christ. But those whom John's disciples baptized, it was John's, because John had received this dispensation and fore-ministry: the voice before the word. You, then, are unwilling to baptize after a drunkard; nor will I baptize after a heretic.

**16.** And if perhaps you think that a heretic does not enter the kingdom of heaven but a drunkard does, the apostle Paul made it clear, saying: *Now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornications, uncleanness, wantonness, idolatry, sorcery, enmities, strife, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envies, drunkenness, carousings, and the like; which I tell you beforehand, as I have told you before, that those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God*[^15]. There he put heresies where he put drunkards, and concluded: *Those who do such things shall not possess the kingdom of God.* Set before me a heretic and a drunkard: if he persists in heresy, he does not enter the kingdom of heaven; so too the drunkard, if he persists in drunkenness, does not enter the kingdom of heaven. Teach me. Whose baptism do they both give? Both are outside the kingdom of heaven, but they give the thing of the kingdom of heaven. The herald pronounces the sentence, but he does not free the innocent. The judge frees, who commissioned the herald to say what he says. Sometimes the herald is a scoundrel, and through him the innocent man is freed. The scoundrel herald says, "I have ordered him released," and through the scoundrel the innocent man is freed. Why? Because the voice of the herald is the sentence of the judge. But the drunkard baptizes: it is a ministry; the heretic baptized: it is a ministry. The gift of baptism is the gift of almighty God. Clearly, if he baptized in the name of Donatus, he must be baptized again. But if I recognize there the baptism of Christ, if I recognize the words of the Gospel, if I recognize the stamp and mark of my king, even though you were a deserter and could be convicted by the mark and put to death, come to the camp with the mark of the Lord: you can merit pardon; the mark cannot be changed in you.
