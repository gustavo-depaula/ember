# Sermon 142

**1.** The divine readings lift us up, lest we be broken by despair; and again they alarm us, lest we be puffed up by pride. But to keep to the middle way, the true and straight way, as it were between the left hand of despair and the right hand of presumption, would be most difficult for us, were it not that Christ said, I am the way. *I am*, he says, *the way, the truth, and the life*[^1]. As if he were saying: Where do you wish to go? *I am the way*. Where do you wish to arrive? *I am the truth*. Where do you wish to abide? *I am the life*. Let us walk securely on the way, then, but let us fear ambushes beside the way. The enemy does not dare to lie in wait on the way, because Christ is the way; but beside the way he certainly does not stop. Hence it is said in the Psalm, *beside the path they set snares for me*[^2]. Another Scripture also says, *Remember that you walk in the midst of snares*[^3]. These snares, in the midst of which we walk, are not on the way, yet they are beside the way. What do you dread, what do you fear, if you walk on the way? Then fear, if you abandon the way. For the enemy is even permitted to set snares beside the way precisely so that the way may not be abandoned through the security of exultation, and one fall into the ambushes.

**2.** Christ the way is the humble Christ; Christ the truth and the life is the exalted Christ and God. If you walk in the humble one, you will arrive at the exalted one; if, being weak, you do not spurn the humble one, you will abide most strong in the exalted one. For what was the reason for Christ's humility, except your weakness? For weakness was besieging you grievously and beyond remedy; and this was the reason so great a physician came to you. For if you were ill in such a way that you could go to the physician, the illness itself might seem bearable; but because you could not go to him, he came to you—he came teaching the humility by which we might return. For pride would not let us return to life, and pride itself had made the human heart, exalting itself against God and neglecting the saving precepts in its very health, fall into weakness; the soul fell sick. Let it, now sick, learn to listen to the one it scorned when sound; let it hear so as to rise, the one it spurned so as to fall. Let it at last hear, taught by experience what, taught by precept, it refused to gain. For its misery taught it—the misery that prosperity made it neglect—alas what an evil it is to play the harlot from the Lord by presuming on oneself; oh how good it is to cleave to the Lord by always thinking humbly of oneself. For to withdraw from that simple and unique good into the multitudes of these pleasures, and to approach the love of the world and earthly corruptions, this is to play the harlot from the Lord. To such a soul it is cried out, *You have made yourself a harlot's face, and you have become utterly shameless*[^4]. Let us now see the purpose of the rebuke.

**3.** For he does not rebuke in order to insult, but he wishes to bring presumption to confusion, that he may heal it. Scripture cried out vehemently, and did not soothe by flattering those it wished to restore by healing. *Adulterers, do you not know that friendship with this world is set up as enmity with God?*[^5] Love of the world makes the soul an adulteress; love of the world's Maker makes the soul chaste; but unless it has blushed for its corruption, it does not long to return to those chaste embraces. Let her who boasted so as not to return be put to confusion so that she may return. Pride, then, was hindering the soul's return. But he who rebukes does not commit sin; he points out sin. What the soul refused to see is set before its eyes; and what it wished to keep behind its back is brought to its face. See yourself in yourself. *Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but do not see the beam in your own eye?*[^6] The soul that was going away from itself is recalled to itself. As it had gone away from itself, so it had gone away from its Lord. For it had looked back at itself and had been pleased with itself, and had become a lover of its own power. It withdrew from him and did not remain in itself; therefore it is both driven from itself, and shut out from itself, and slips away into outward things. It loves the world, it loves temporal things, it loves earthly things. And if it loved itself, neglecting the one by whom it was made, it would already be less, already failing, by loving what is less; for the soul itself is less than God, and far less, and the less so as a thing made is less than its maker. Therefore God is to be loved, in such a way that, for love of him, if it can be, we forget ourselves. What, then, is this passing over? The soul forgot itself—but by loving the world; now let it forget itself—but by loving the Maker of the world. Driven, then, from itself, it lost itself in a manner; and because it cannot see even its own deeds, it justifies its iniquities. It is carried away and grows proud in wantonness, in extravagance, in honors, in powers, in riches, in the power of vanity. It is reproved, it is corrected, it is shown to itself, it is displeased with itself; it confesses its foulness, it desires beauty; and she who went out poured forth returns confounded.

**4.** He seems to be praying against her who says, *Fill their faces with shame*. *Fill their faces*, he says, *with shame, and they will seek your name, O Lord*[^7]. Did he hate those whose faces he longed to have filled with shame? See how he loves those whom he wants to seek the Lord's name. Does he love only, or hate only? Or does he both hate and love? Indeed he both hates and loves. He hates what is yours, he loves you. What is this: He hates what is yours, he loves you? He hates what you have made, he loves what God has made. For what is yours, except your sins? And what are you, except what God has made? You neglect what you were made, you love what you have made; you love your own works outside yourself, you neglect the work of God in yourself. Rightly do you fall, rightly do you slip, rightly do you go even away from yourself; rightly do you hear, *A wind that passes and does not return*[^8]. Rather, hear him who calls and says, *Turn to me, and I will turn to you*[^9]. For God is not turned away and turned back; he reproves while remaining, he reproves while unchangeable. He is turned away because you turned yourself away; you brought about a fall from him, he did not bring about a setting from you. So hear him say to you, *Turn to me, and I will turn to you*. For this is what "I turn to you" means: that you turn to me. He pursues the back of the one who flees, he gives light to the face of the one who returns. For where will you flee, fleeing from God? Where will you flee, fleeing from him who is contained in no place and absent from nowhere, who sets free the one who turns, and punishes the one who turns away? The one you have as judge while fleeing, have him as father while returning.

**5.** But it had swollen with pride, and because of that very swelling it could not return through the narrow place. He who became the way cries out, *Enter by the narrow gate*[^10]. It tries to enter, the swelling hinders it; and the more the swelling hinders, the more perniciously it tries. For the narrow place distresses the swollen one; and being distressed, it will swell the more. Swelling the more, when will it enter? Therefore let it subside, if it longs to enter. But how is it to subside? Let it take the medicine of humility. Let it drink against the swelling a bitter but healthful cup; let it drink the cup of humility. Why does it cramp itself? It is not bulk that blocks it—not greatness, but swelling; for greatness has solidity, swelling has inflation. Let the swollen one not think itself great; let it subside, that it may be great, that it may be sure and solid. Let it not desire these things; let it not glory in this pomp of fleeting and corruptible things. Let it hear him who said, *Enter by the narrow gate*, saying, *I am the way*[^11]. As if the swollen one were asking: By what shall I enter? *I am*, he says, *the way*. Enter by me; walk only by me, that you may enter by the door. For as I said, *I am the way*, so, *I am the door*[^12]. Why do you seek by what to return, where to return, by what to enter? Lest you go astray anywhere, he himself has become all things for you. So he says briefly: Be humble, be meek. Let us hear him say this most plainly, so that we may see by what is the way, what is the way, where the way leads. Where do you wish to come? Surely, perhaps out of avarice you wish to possess all things. *All things have been handed over to me by my Father*[^13], he says. Perhaps you will say: These were handed over to Christ; surely not to me? Hear the Apostle saying—hear, as I said a while ago, lest you be broken by despair; hear how you were loved though not lovable, hear how you were loved though base and foul, before there was in you anything worthy to be loved. You were loved first, so that you might become worthy to be loved. *For Christ*, as the Apostle says, *died for the ungodly*[^14]. Or perhaps the ungodly deserved to be loved? I ask what the ungodly deserved. To be damned, you answer. Yet *Christ died for the ungodly*. See what has been bestowed on you while ungodly; what then is kept for you now that you are godly? What was bestowed on the ungodly? *Christ died for the ungodly*. But you were desiring to possess all things. Do not seek it through avarice; seek this through godliness; for if you seek it so, you will possess it. For you will hold him through whom all things were made, and with him you will possess all things.

**6.** We do not say these things as if by mere reasoning. Hear the Apostle himself saying, *He who did not spare his own Son but handed him over for us all, how will he not also with him give us all things?*[^15] Miser, see, you have all things. Despise all the things you love that hinder you from Christ, and hold him in whom you can possess all things. So the physician himself, though needing no such medicine, nevertheless, in order to encourage the sick man, drank what there was no need to drink, as though addressing one who refuses and raising up the fearful, drank first. *The cup*, he says, *which I am about to drink*[^16]. I, who have in me nothing to be healed by that cup, will nevertheless drink it, lest you disdain to, you who need to drink. Now see, brothers, whether the human race ought to be sick any longer, after receiving so great a medicine. God is now humble, and man is still proud. Let man hear, let him learn: *All things*, he says, *have been handed over to me by my Father*. If you desire all things, you will have them with me. If you desire the Father, you will have him through me, you will have him in me. What good will it be, you say, for me to have all things, if I do not have him? You say rightly. If you wish to have him too, hear what follows. For when he had said, *All things have been handed over to me by my Father*, as if exhorting and saying: Come to me, if you wish to possess all things—and lest you say: I do not want all things, but I want him who made all things—he goes on and says, *No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son*. Do not despair, hear the rest: *and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him*[^17]. *To whomever he chooses*, he says: perhaps he will not choose me? He would not have come to you humble, if he, the exalted one, did not wish to be made known to you. Perhaps here too you say: Even if he himself is made known to me, I would wish to learn the Father. Do you wish to know the Father? Hear the voice of Philip; he was the first to utter this word, and very well, as was fitting; for he thirsted for blessedness, and seeking it everywhere, he remained everywhere thirsting; nowhere did he find that by which he might be satisfied. Thirsting, he says to the Lord, *Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us*. What is, *it is enough for us*? Resting there, I will seek no more. And the Lord: *Have I been with you so long a time, and you have not known me? Philip, he who sees me sees also the Father*[^18]. This, then, is what is needed, that the Son show himself. For you will not find the Son less than the Father, or else he said in vain, *I and the Father are one*[^19]. But he who in himself is one with the Father, nevertheless for your sake *emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^20]. That *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*, he gave this to you while you were turned away from him; but to you turned toward him he has kept *I and the Father are one*.

**7.** And see how plainly he says this. *He who loves me keeps my commandments; and he who loves me will be loved by my Father, and I will love him*. He holds us in suspense, as though about to give something, because he loves us. Just as, if a man says to you, I love you, you attend to nothing but what he will give you, what he will bestow on you, what profit, in what he is useful. So the Lord says, *and I will love him*. Ask what he will give you. Hear what follows: *and I will show him my very self*[^21]. What is this, brothers? You see where the flame of love sweeps. We are to see something; and that something is the Word of God; and that something is God with God, through whom all things were made. A gift is announced, a gift is set forth. How great and how vain is the joy of men! On every side the tumult seethes of people running together, telling one another of something as if great, rousing themselves with exhortation and trifling enticements to go, to see. Where to go? What to see? Whither the corrupted soul presses on, and returns more corrupt, and unworthy of the embrace of so lawful a husband of hers. Thither men go, thither they run. And Christ sets forth a vision to come, an expectation to come. For he says, *I will love him, and I will show him*—not what I made, not the bosom of the depths, not the secrets of the earth, not the varieties of plants, not the multitude of kinds of animals, not, finally, the number of the stars, not the courses of the heavenly bodies, not the measures of the seasons. For why do you wish to see these? If you were to see them, they certainly would not be great. I made these.—*I will show him my very self*.

**8.** But when will this be, someone says, when will he show it? He defers, he does not take away. And in deferring, what does he do? By desire he enlarges the bosom of the soul. He is about to give something great, brothers; and why do I say even "great"? If it is even to be called that. See how much he gives to the ungodly, and how much he grants to the unworthy. Why even to these? Because *he makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil*[^22]. See how creation serves blasphemers; look around at the very gifts of nature. They have health, they have soundness of senses, they have a spirit quickening their earthly members, they have the enjoyment of this air, the sight of light, a rational mind by which they are more excellent than the other animals. All these lie open to rich and poor, good and evil, as common riches. All are nourished, all are illumined by this light, they live as though from a common store. He gives all these things. From this, brothers, ought we not to consider what he keeps for his own, what he prepares for the faithful, that they who do not see may see, even by believing? To believe before you see is the merit of the vision to come. So your capacity is exercised by the deferral of good things, that, enlarged by desire, you may be fit to receive what he promises and what you desire.

**9.** For he has already given something; for he has given his Spirit as a pledge. What is this: He has given a pledge? As if saying: See, because I will give something, from it I now give what may lift you up, what may kindle love, what, once tasted, may sweep you toward satiety. *Taste and see how sweet the Lord is*[^23]. From the source of your tasting, from there you will be satisfied. *They will be inebriated*, he says, *by the abundance of your house*, and the rest. *For with you is the fountain of life, and in your light we shall see light*[^24]. Is the fountain of life one thing and the light another? In many ways is that spoken of which, whatever is said of it, will not be told. So let us be lifted up, be kindled, blaze, desire. And if we are deferred, we are not deprived. Let us only hold to the way; there will be our satiety, there will be the fountain of life. The fountain of life itself came, was clothed in flesh, made us desire. He bestowed his death on the unworthy, he keeps his life for the worthy.

**10.** But because, as we said, by the swelling of pride we were not returning, therefore he bestowed his death on the unworthy; he became humble, that he, the exalted one, might be exalted. He did this so that your disease might subside. Those two disciples who were swelling wished to enter; through their mother—because they did not dare on their own—they asked. The very shamefacedness ought to have warned them what they were asking. They did not dare on their own: they made their aged mother, as one of retired years, speak to the Lord. They desired the kingdom, which is entered only by the narrow road. But they, still swelling with desire of honor, the more tightly they pressed themselves, the more grievously they were distressed. The Lord humbles them, and against the swelling gives the bitter cup of which I spoke a little before. You were saying: I cannot; you call me through the narrow place, I cannot enter through the narrow place. *Come to me*, he says, *all who labor and are burdened*. Your burden is your swelling. *Come to me, all who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me*[^25].

**11.** The Master of angels cries out, the Word of God cries out, by whom all rational minds are fed without failing, a food that refreshes and remains whole, he cries out and says, *Learn from me*[^26]. Let the people listen to him saying, *Learn from me*. Let them answer: What do we learn from you? For from so great a craftsman we are to hear something or other, when he says, *Learn from me*. Who is it who says, *Learn from me*? He who formed the earth, who divided the sea and the dry land, who created the birds, who created the earthly animals, who created all swimming things, who set the stars in heaven, who distinguished day and night, who made firm the firmament itself, who separated light from darkness; he says, *Learn from me*. Is he perhaps going to tell us this, that we should do these things with him? Who can do this? God alone does it. Do not fear, he says, I do not burden you. Learn this from me, that I was made for your sake. *Learn*, he says, *from me*, not to form the creation that was made through me. Nor indeed do I say that you should learn those things which I granted to some, to whom I willed, but not to all: to raise the dead, to enlighten the blind, to open the ears of the deaf; do not wish to learn these from me as something great. The disciples came back rejoicing and exulting, saying, *Behold, in your name even the demons are subject to us*. The Lord says to them, *Do not rejoice in this, that the demons are subject to you; rather rejoice that your names are written in heaven*[^27]. To whom he willed, he granted to cast out demons; to whom he willed, he granted to raise the dead. These miracles were done even before the Lord's incarnation; the dead were raised, lepers were cleansed. We read these things: and who did them, except he who afterward was the man Christ after David, but God Christ before Abraham? He himself granted all these things, he himself did them through men, yet he did not give this to all. Surely those to whom he did not give it ought not to despair and say they do not belong to him, because they did not deserve to receive these gifts? In the body there are members; one member can do one thing, another another. God fitted together the body: he did not grant to the ear that it see, nor to the eye that it hear, nor to the forehead that it smell, nor to the hand that it taste. He did not give these; but to all the members he gave health, he gave fitting-together, he gave unity. By the Spirit he gave life to all alike, he united them. So, then, he did not give to one to raise the dead, to another he did not give to debate; yet what did he give to all? We heard him say, *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*[^28]. My brothers, this is our whole medicine: *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*. What does it profit if one works miracles and is proud, is not meek and humble of heart? Will he not be counted in the number of those who are to come at the end and to say, *Did we not prophesy in your name, and in your name do many mighty works?* But what will they hear? *I never knew you; depart from me, all you who work iniquity*[^29].

**12.** What, then, does it profit us to learn? *For I am*, he says, *meek and humble of heart*[^30]. He implants love, and the most genuine love, without confusion, without inflation, without elation, without deceit. This he implants, who says, *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*. When can one who is proud and inflated have the most sincere love? It is necessary that he envy. Or perhaps one who envies does love, and we are mistaken? Far be it that anyone should be so mistaken as to say that an envious person has love. So what does the Apostle say? *Love is not jealous*. Why is it not jealous? *It is not puffed up*[^31]. He at once added the cause from which he took jealousy away from love: because it is not puffed up, it is not jealous. First he said this: *Love is not jealous*; but, as if you were to ask why it is not jealous, he added, *It is not puffed up*. If, then, it is jealous because it is puffed up, then if it is not puffed up, it is not jealous. If love is not puffed up, and therefore is not jealous, he implants love who says, *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*.

**13.** Now let anyone have what he wants, let him boast of whatever he wants: *If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become as sounding brass, or a clanging cymbal*. What is loftier than the gift of diverse tongues? It is brass, a clanging cymbal, if you take away love. Hear of other gifts. *If I know all mysteries*; what is more excellent, what more magnificent? Hear yet another. *If I have all prophecy and all faith, so as to move mountains, but have not love, I am nothing*. He has come to greater things. Brothers, what else did he say? *If I distribute all my goods to the poor*[^32]. What can be done more perfect? Since for the sake of perfection the Lord commanded this to the rich man, saying, *Do you wish to be perfect? Go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor*. Is he, then, already perfect who has sold all his goods and given to the poor? No: for he added, *and come, follow me*[^33]. *Go*, he says, *give all to the poor; and come, follow me*. Why should I follow you? Having now sold all and distributed it to the poor, am I not perfect? What need is there to follow? Follow, that you may learn, *for I am meek and humble of heart*. For can a person sell all his goods and give to the poor, while not yet meek, not yet humble of heart? Certainly he can. *For if I distribute all my goods to the poor* . . . And hear yet more. For some, having left all that they had, had already followed the Lord, but had not yet followed to the point of perfection—for to follow to perfection is to imitate—they could not bear the temptation of the passion. For Peter, brothers, was one of those who had left all and had followed the Lord. For when that rich man went away in sorrow, where the disciples, troubled, asked who then could be perfect, and the Lord consoled them, they said to the Lord, *Behold, we have left all and followed you; what then shall we have?*[^34] And the Lord said what he would give them here, what he would reserve for the future. Yet this one was already of the number of those who had done these things. But when it came to the moment of the passion, at the word of a single maidservant he denied him three times, after he had promised that he would die.

**14.** Let your Charity, then, attend. *Go*, he says, *sell all your goods, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^35]. Peter is now perfect, the Lord now sitting in heaven at the right hand of the Father; then he is perfect, and made mature. So when he was following the Lord to the passion, he was not perfect; but when there began to be no one on earth whom he could follow, then was he perfect? On the contrary, you always have before you one whom you may follow. The Lord placed an example on earth, he left the Gospel there; in the Gospel he is with you. For he did not lie, saying, *Behold, I am with you all days, even to the consummation of the age*[^36]. So follow the Lord. What is it to follow the Lord? To imitate the Lord. What is it to imitate the Lord? *Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart*[^37]. Since *if I distribute all my goods to the poor, and hand over my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me nothing*[^38]. So I exhort your Charity to love itself. But I would not exhort you to love, except by some love. So what has been begun, I exhort to be fulfilled; and what has been started, I ask to be perfected; and I ask to be asked by you on my behalf, that in me too may be perfected what I urge upon you. For we are all imperfect, and we shall be perfected there, where all things are perfect. The apostle Paul says, *Brothers, I do not consider that I have laid hold of it*. He himself says, *Not that I have already received it, or am already perfect*[^39]. And does any human being dare to boast of perfection? Rather let us confess imperfection, that we may deserve perfection.
