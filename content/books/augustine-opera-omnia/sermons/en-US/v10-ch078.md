# Sermon 391: To Young Men

**1.** My word is to you, O young men, the flower of your age, the danger of the mind. Every time indeed, and every age, in which that corruptible flesh is carried, cannot be free from temptations. And so long as anyone good wrestles in the contest, lest he be overcome by the adversary, he acts amid dangers, as long as he contends with him in this mortality as in a certain sandy stadium. For as soon as a man is born into the world, and enters a life full of misery—as it were a prophet of his future toil, by the heralding of a tearful voice—even if not yet in his own soul, yet in the soul of his parents, or of any men in whose hands his weakness lies to be nourished, he can already be tempted, and seized by the devil's entrapments, whether through the bindings of execrable remedies, or through the sacrilegious rites of the Gentiles, or, if perhaps death presses, by negligence in renouncing in saving Baptism. And, to put it briefly, that age is tempted, when by its own it is loved in the world and neglected in Christ. For it draws with it the offspring of death, and is rooted in that wound of sin which was inflicted on the first man, from whom we drew the origin of our corruption, by the venomous tooth of the serpent. Whence holy Job says that no one is clean—from the filth of sin, of course—not even an infant whose life upon the earth is of one day. But why do I speak of one already born, when David cries out with a sorrowful voice and says: *In iniquities I was conceived, and in sins my mother conceived me*[^1]? For the baptisms of newly born little ones could seem superfluous, unless all died in Adam, and original sin ran through the parents' loins down to the fruit of the offspring along the channel of mortality: since the almighty Lord both forms the mortal creature by the law of his order, and the best Father supplies the immortality of renewal by the grace of mercy.

**2.** But if not even the infancy of mortal man is free from temptations, because of the bond of corruption, what shall I say of the other ages? Or is old age perhaps excepted, and have the blood and limbs of unlawful concupiscence grown cold in flesh now near to a corpse, and has the matter of temptation withered away from a body now wearied and almost dead? Rather, in wicked old men there is generally so great a whirlpool of greed and so insatiable a chasm of belly and gullet, that, as much as good old men are made serene by prudence, so much are these buried in winebibbing: as though to this end their dry and sap-exhausted innards are bent over, that, to repair their former vigor, they must be irrigated by the inundation of drunkenness. What of avarice, which is the root of all evils—does it not, in cold old men, blaze the more fervently for acquiring, the more quickly it is about to leave what it acquires? a marvelous madness indeed. For it hastens to burden itself with heavier expenses, when it has already arrived where it was tending.

**3.** If, then, the age of boyhood and the age of old age are not free from temptations—of which the one, that is, boyhood, has scarcely yet entered, the other is already departing this life; and the one a little before was not, the other a little after will not be—what is to be thought, what is to be said of the burning of the age of youth, which, set in the middle of both, has already withdrawn from the weakness of boyhood and not yet drawn near to the torpor of old age? This is shaken by more and greater storms of temptation, this is overwhelmed by the more frequent onset of the waves of the overflowing world. It presumes on its strength, it boasts in the dignity of its form, it either longs or rejoices to shine forth in the pomp of temporal things. And so to youth whatever the truth has commanded is the poison of evils, whatever the devil suggests is food: but the bitterness of righteousness is the medicine for the ulcer of that age; while the sweetness of injustice is the snare of rashness. To this pertains what is written: *Sweeter are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy*[^2]; and that which David says: *The just man will rebuke me in mercy, and reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head*[^3]. Let the truth burn, and yet heal: for the oil of the sinner, the flattery of the fawner, soothes, but deceives. For there pride is soothed; but life slips away. For the prophet speaks in the person of one who now seeks the physician, who bears the hand of the one curing even with the sharpness of pain, who desires his sickness to be healed rather than praised. But dangerous is the ulcer of youth, which catches fire with desires, swells with hope, wastes away with pleasures. But this is the hope of the desperate, the hope of perishable things, which does not loose but inflates the affection of the wretched soul, and makes it unable to bear the touch of truth; so that, despairing even of its own immortality, it loves to say, and loves those too who say these things to it: *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*; but it hates to say and to hear: *Be sober, you just, and do not sin*[^4]. Let it love the pernicious gentleness of the enemy, let it hate the healthful harshness of the physician. This perversity, this frenzy, is most to be feared in the age of youth.

**4.** Hence is born that talk of men flowing away into outrages, and from there growing fierce into crimes: *For they said, reasoning with themselves not rightly: Short and full of weariness is the time of our life, and there is no refreshment in the end of a man, and there is none known to have returned from the lower world*. And a little after: *Come, then, let us enjoy the good things that are, and let us use the creation quickly as in youth. Let us fill ourselves with precious wine and ointments*; and the rest woven together in that same place, the words of exulting luxury, of men despairing of eternal life, and placing their hope, as it were in the sand of a torrent, so in the temporal corruption of the flesh. But from this immoderation of lusts and outrages rotting with worms, see into what crimes and into what monstrous deeds they leap forth. For, exhausted and overthrown by the corruptions of immoderate base sins, while they hate the severity of the truth contradicting them: *Let us, then, oppress*, they say, *the poor just man, and let us not spare the widow, nor revere the gray hairs of the aged of much time. But let our strength be the law of justice; for what is weak is found useless. Let us, then, ensnare the just man, since he is useless to us, and is contrary to our works*. Such things the impiety of the Jews thought about our Lord Jesus Christ. Which is found more manifestly in the words that follow: for a little after they say: *He promises to have the knowledge of God, and calls himself the Son of God*. Then again a little after they say: *Let us question him with insult and torment, that we may know his reverence. Let us condemn him to a most shameful death; for there will be a regard from his words*. But attend to what sentence the Holy Spirit bears against them, when he immediately adds: *These things they thought, and they erred; for their malice blinded them*[^5]. See, then, from what filth all the horrendous crimes arise that are feared in wicked men. For what is softer than that luxury? What is harder than that cruelty? A little before they were saying: *Let us enjoy the good things that are, and let us use the creation quickly as in youth; and let not the flower of the time pass us by, and let us everywhere leave signs of gladness*: now they say: *Let us oppress the poor man, let us not spare the widow, nor revere the aged. Let us ensnare the just man, let us question him with insult and torment, let us condemn him to a most shameful death*. Wine is followed by fury, ointments by torments, blood by roses, anger by gladness. By such men the Lord was bound, beaten, killed. Who would dread bloody bonds from flowering crowns? Who would foresee most savage pains from sweet cups? Who would foreknow from soft meadows the so dire wood of the cross? And yet no age in that luxury, except youth, is compared to the flower of the time. For thus they said: *Let us use the creation quickly as in youth*.

**5.** You, then, O young men, we above all admonish and exhort, that you be captivated by the beauty of true virtue. Let no earthly form, no gleam of metals, no pleasantness of groves, no purple of flowers, no adornment of the flesh whether natural or applied, no sound of any strings or flutes, no delight of odors, no sweetness of flavors, no embraces, be compared to the beauty, the inspiration, the sweetness, the comforts of wisdom. For we forbid you, not love itself, but the things that are basely loved. Do you wish to love? Love wisdom, strive to come to her. That her aspect may not make you shudder, compose yourselves in the inner man. As wanton eyes seek the adornments of the body, so wisdom seeks those of the heart. And do not bring forth these adornments out of your riches: because she hates the proud and those who as it were wish to boast of their own. *But what have you that you did not receive?*[^6] She, then, gives whereby you may please her. Only love her, and she will keep you; embrace her, and she will exalt you; honor her, and she will embrace you: that she may give to your head a crown of graces. *Bright and unfading is wisdom, and is easily found by those who love her*[^7]. Propose to join her to yourselves, sigh for her, burn for her, perish for love of her. Deny yourself to yourself; lest you deny yourself to her while you please yourself. *Her converse has no bitterness*[^8]. If you are lovers, love her; if you are handsome, please God; if you are young men, conquer the devil. Daniel was called by the angel a man of desires. What were those desires of his, except those by which he ardently gaped after the beauty of wisdom; because both in the age of youth he trod down wantonness, and a captive he pressed down the pride of a kingdom, and shut in he closed the mouths of lions?

**6.** Nor do you, young women, think this sermon foreign to you. For to you I say these things—yet not to confound you, but to admonish you as my dearest daughters: flee the desires of youth. Think of Susanna, you who are married; of Anna, you who are widows; of Mary, you who are virgins. And do not go out in public to this end, that, desiring to display the flower of your hay to the eyes of men, you seek death in the house of life. *For all flesh is hay, and the glory of man as the flowers of hay*. What, then, will you do when the hay dries up, when the flower falls? But the word of the Lord, which abides forever[^9]—do you think it will not easily find your ashes, you who now despise it in the proud greenness of your age? Behold, again I say and solemnly charge: flee the desires of youth. If you hear this, if you obey, if you receive it as the word of God with honor and fear, you will be not only beautiful before the eyes of God, but also sound. But if out of this admonition of ours you perhaps make for yourselves even amorous jests, you will inflict deadly wounds on yourselves from the very instruments of the physician. Surely the Jews who crucified the Lord—when we hear it, we shudder and pursue it with great execration—yet they too, when they thought of their luxury, mused on the solitudes of delightful fields, saying: *Let there be no meadow that our luxury does not pass through*[^10]. How, then, would he spare Christ, if he had found him on earth, who indeed chose, not solitary meadows for the provoking of his lusts, but the most thronged churches, of the one reigning in heaven? Behold, a third time I solemnly charge and say, flee the desires of youth. Burn with the desires of Daniel. Children, from your youth choose teaching, and even to gray hairs you will find wisdom.
