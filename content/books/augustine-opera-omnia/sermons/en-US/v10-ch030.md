# Sermon 355: On the Morals of the Clergy, First Sermon

**1.** Why I wished and asked yesterday that your love gather more numerously today, this is what I am going to say. We live here with you, and we live for your sake: and our intention and wish is that we may live with you without end with Christ. But I believe that our manner of life is before your eyes; so that we too perhaps may dare to say, although much unequal to him, what the Apostle said: *Be imitators of me, as I too am of Christ*[^1]. And therefore I do not wish that anyone find in us an occasion of living badly. *For we provide good things*, says the same Apostle, *not only before God, but also before men*[^2]. For our own sake, our conscience suffices us: for your sake, our reputation ought not to be polluted, but to be strong among you. Hold what I have said, and distinguish it. Conscience and reputation are two things. Conscience for yourself, reputation for your neighbor. He who, trusting in his conscience, neglects his reputation, is cruel: especially one placed in this position, of which the Apostle says, writing to his disciple: *Showing yourself in all things an example of good works*[^3].

**2.** Therefore, that I may not keep you long, especially because I speak sitting while you labor standing: you all, or nearly all, know that we live in that house which is called the bishop's house in such a way that, as much as we can, we imitate those saints of whom the book of the Acts of the Apostles speaks: *No one called anything his own, but all things were common to them*[^4]. Because perhaps some of you are not such diligent scrutinizers of our life as to know this in the way I wish you to know it, I say what it is that I briefly stated. I, whom by God's favor you see as your bishop, came as a young man to this city: many of you know it. I was seeking where I might establish a monastery, and live with my brothers. For I had left all hope of the world, and what I could have been I refused to be: yet I did not seek what I am. *I chose to be cast down in the house of my God, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners*[^5]. I separated myself from those who love the world: but I did not make myself equal to those who are over peoples. Nor at the banquet of my Lord did I choose the higher place, but the lower and cast-down one: and it pleased him to say to me: *Go up higher*[^6]. But I so feared the episcopate that, since my reputation had begun to be of some weight among the servants of God, I would not approach a place where I knew there was no bishop. I avoided this, and groaned as much as I could, that I might be saved in a humble place, and not be in peril in a high one. But, as I said, a servant ought not to contradict his Lord[^7]. I came to this city to see a friend, whom I thought I could win for God, that he might be with us in the monastery; as though secure, because the place had a bishop. I was seized, made a presbyter, and through this step came to the episcopate. I brought nothing, I came to this Church, except with those garments with which at that time I was clothed. And because I was arranging this, to be in a monastery with the brothers, the old man Valerius of blessed memory, knowing my institution and will, gave me that garden in which the monastery now is. I began to gather brothers of good purpose, my fellow-poor, having nothing, as I had, and imitating me: that just as I sold my slender little poverty and gave it out to the poor, so should they too do who wished to be with me, that we might live from the common store; but the common store for us was the great and most rich estate, God himself. I came to the episcopate: I saw that a bishop is bound to show constant kindness to all who come or pass by: which if the bishop did not do, he would be called inhuman. But if this custom were brought into the monastery, it would be unseemly. And I wished to have in this bishop's house a monastery of clerics. Behold how we live. It is lawful for no one in our community to have anything of his own. But perhaps some have. It is lawful for no one: if any have, they do what is not lawful. But I think well of my brothers, and always, believing well, I refrained from this inquiry: because to inquire into these things too seemed to me as it were to think ill. For I had known, and I know, that all who lived with me knew our purpose, knew the law of our life.

**3.** There came to us also the presbyter Januarius. What he seemed to have honorably, by giving out he as it were consumed, and did not consume: there remained to him a certain money, that is, silver, which he said belonged to his daughter. His daughter, by God's favor, is in the monastery of women, of good hope. May the Lord govern her, that she may fulfill what we hope of her, by his mercy, not her merits. And because she was under age, and could do nothing with her own money—for although we saw the brightness of her profession, yet we feared the inconstancy of her age—it came about that that silver was as it were kept for the girl, so that when she came to the lawful years, she might do with it what would befit a virgin of Christ, when she could already do it best. While this was being awaited, he began to draw near to death: and as long as it was, as though of his own right, not his daughter's, he made a will. A will, I say, the presbyter our associate made, remaining with us, living from the Church, professing the common life. He made a will, he appointed heirs. O grief of that fellowship! O fruit born, not from the tree which the Lord planted! But he wrote the Church as heir. I do not want these gifts, I do not love the fruit of bitterness. I sought him for God, he had professed fellowship, let him keep this, let him show this. Had he nothing? Let him not make a will. Had he something? Let him not feign himself our associate, as a poor man of God. Great is my grief from this, brothers. I tell your love, on account of this grief I have determined not to receive that inheritance into the Church. Let what he left be his children's, let them do with it what they wish. For it seems to me that if I receive it, in that deed which displeases me, and which I grieve over, I shall be a partaker of his. This I wished not to be hidden from your love. His daughter is in the monastery of women; his son is in the monastery of men. He disinherited both: her with praise, him with a notice, that is, with reproach. But I commended to the Church that they not accept those little portions, which belong to the disinherited, until they come to lawful age. This the Church keeps for them. Then he sent a lawsuit between his children, in which I labor. The girl says: It is mine, you know that my father always said this. The boy says: Let my father be believed, because dying he could not lie. And what an evil is this contention! But if the children themselves are servants of God, we quickly end this lawsuit between them. I hear them as a father, and perhaps better than their own father. I will see what is between them, and as the Lord wills, with faithful, honored brothers, by God's favor, from your number, that is, from this people, I hear the case between them, and as the Lord grants, I settle it.

**4.** Yet I ask you, let no one reproach me because I do not wish the Church to receive that inheritance. First, because I detest his deed; then, because it is my institution. Many praise what I am going to say, but some also reproach it. To satisfy both is very difficult. You heard just now, when the Gospel was read: *We sang to you, and you did not dance; we mourned to you, and you did not lament. John*—the Baptist—*came neither eating nor drinking, and they say: He has a demon; the Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say: Behold a glutton, a wine-drinker, a friend of publicans*[^8]. What, then, do I do among those who prepare to reproach me, and to fix their teeth in me, if I receive the inheritances of those who, angry, disinherit their children? Again, what shall I do with those to whom I sing, and they will not dance? who say: Behold why no one gives anything to the Church of Hippo; behold why those who die do not make it heir: because Bishop Augustine out of his goodness—for by praising they bite, with their lips they soothe, and fix their tooth—gives it all away, does not receive it. Plainly I receive, I profess that I receive good offerings, holy offerings. But if anyone is angry with his son, and dying disinherits him, if he were alive, would I not appease him? Ought I not to reconcile his son to him? How, then, do I wish him to have peace with his son, whose inheritance I crave? But plainly, if he does what I have often exhorted: he has one son, let him think Christ another; he has two sons, let him think Christ the third; he has ten, let him make Christ the eleventh, and I receive it. Because, then, I have done this in certain matters, now they wish to turn my goodness, or the commendation of my reputation, into something else, so as to reproach me in another way, who am unwilling to receive the offerings of devoted men. Let them consider how many I have received. What need is there to count them? Behold, I say one: I received the inheritance of the son of Julianus. Why? Because he died without children.

**5.** The inheritance of Bonifatius, that is, of Fatus, I was unwilling to receive: not out of mercy, but out of fear. I was unwilling that the Church of Christ be a shipowner. Many indeed there are who acquire even from ships. Yet if there were one trial, if the ship went and was wrecked: were we going to hand men over to torture, that according to custom an inquiry might be made about the sinking of the ship, and that those who had been freed from the waves be tortured by the judge? But should we hand them over? In no way would it befit the Church to do this. Should it then pay the fiscal burden? But from where would it pay? It is not lawful for us to have a reserve. It is not for a bishop to keep gold back, and to push away the hand of the beggar from himself. Daily so many ask, so many groan, so many needy ones appeal, that we leave more of them sad, because we have not what we may give to all; and should we keep a reserve on account of shipwreck? Therefore, I did this by avoiding, not by giving. Let no one there praise me, but let no one blame me either. Plainly, when I gave to the son what the angry father dying took away, I did well. Let those praise who wish, let those spare who do not wish to praise. Why say more, my brothers? Whoever wishes to make the Church heir, with a son disinherited, let him seek another to receive it, not Augustine: rather, by God's favor, let him find no one. How praiseworthy was the deed of the holy and venerable bishop Aurelius of Carthage—how he filled the mouth of all who know it with the praises of God! A certain man, when he had no children, nor hoped for any, gave all his goods to the Church, keeping the usufruct for himself. Children were born to him, and the bishop returned, to one not expecting it, what that man had given. The bishop had it in his power not to return it, but by the right of the forum, not by the right of heaven.

**6.** Let your love also know that I have said to my brothers who remain with me, that whoever has anything, let him either sell it and give it out, or give it, or make it common: let the Church have it, through which God feeds us. And I gave a delay until the Epiphany, on account of those who either have not divided with their brothers, and have left what they have with their brothers, or have not yet done anything about their property, because the lawful age was being awaited. Let them do with it what they wish: provided only that they be poor with me, and together let us await the mercy of God. But if they are unwilling, those who perhaps are unwilling: surely I am the one who had determined, as you know, to ordain no cleric, except one who wished to remain with me: so that if he wished to depart from the purpose, I would rightly take from him the clerical office, because he would desert the promised and begun consortium of holy fellowship; behold, in the sight of God and of you, I change my counsel: those who wish to have something of their own, for whom God and his Church do not suffice, let them remain where they wish and where they can, I do not take from them the clerical office. I do not wish to have hypocrites. It is an evil, who does not know it? it is an evil to fall from a purpose; but it is worse to feign a purpose. Behold I say: he falls who deserts the fellowship of the common life already undertaken, which is praised in the Acts of the Apostles: he falls from his vow, he falls from his holy profession. Let him watch for the judge, but God, not me. I do not take from him the clerical office. How great the peril is, I have set before his eyes: let him do what he wishes. For I know that if I should wish to degrade anyone doing this, patrons will not be lacking to him, supporters will not be lacking to him, even here among the bishops, who will say: What evil has he done? He cannot endure this life with you: he wishes to remain apart from the bishop, to live from his own, should he therefore lose the clerical office? I know how great an evil it is to profess something holy, and not fulfill it. *Vow*, it says, *and render to the Lord your God*, and: *It is better not to vow, than to vow and not render*[^9]. She is a virgin: if she was never in a monastery, and is a consecrated virgin, it is not lawful for her to marry. She is not compelled to be in a monastery; but if she has begun to be in a monastery, and has deserted it, and yet is a virgin, she has half collapsed. So too a cleric has professed two things, both holiness and the clerical office: meanwhile holiness—for the clerical office God imposed on his neck through his people: it is more a burden than an honor, but *who is wise and understands these things?*[^10]—so then he has professed holiness: he has professed the fellowship of living in common, he has professed *how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell in unity*[^11]. If he has fallen from this purpose, and remaining outside is a cleric, he too has half fallen. What is it to me? I do not judge him. If outside he keeps holiness, he has half fallen; if within he has pretense, he has wholly fallen. I do not wish him to have the necessity of pretending. I know how men love the clerical office: I take it from no one unwilling to live in common with me. He has God, who wishes to remain with me. If he is prepared to be fed by God through his Church, to have nothing of his own, but either to give out to the poor, or to put into the common store, let him remain with me. He who does not wish this, let him have liberty: but let him see whether he can have the eternity of happiness.

**7.** Let these things suffice now for the present to your love. What I shall do with my brothers—for I hope good things, that all will gladly obey me, nor shall I find any to have anything, except by some necessity of religion, not by occasion of greed—what, then, I shall do, after the Epiphany I will announce to your love in the Lord's will; and how I shall settle the lawsuit between the two brothers, the sons of the presbyter Januarius, I will not keep silent from you. I have spoken many things, pardon a talkative old age, but a timid weakness. I, as you see, have only now grown old by age; by the weakness of my body I have long been old. Yet if it pleases God what I have said just now, he gives the strength, I do not abandon you. Pray for me, that as long as soul is in this body, and whatever strength is available, I may serve you in the word of God.
