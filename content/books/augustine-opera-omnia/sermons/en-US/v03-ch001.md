# Sermon 51: On the Agreement of the Evangelists Matthew and Luke in the Genealogies of the Lord

**1. 1.** May he who stirred up the expectation of your love also fulfill it. For although we presume that what is to be said to you is not ours but God's, yet all the more do we say what the Apostle says in humility: *We have this treasure in earthen vessels, so that the surpassing power may be God's and not from us*[^1]. We do not doubt, then, that you remember our promise. We made our promise in him through whom we now make it good. For when we promised, we were asking of him; and when we make it good, we receive from him. Now your love remembers that on the morning of the Lord's Nativity we put off the question we had set out to answer, because many were with us who, even though the word of God is usually burdensome to them, were keeping the solemnity owed to that day. But now I think no one has gathered except the one who longs to hear. So we are not speaking to deaf hearts, nor to disdainful minds. And this expectation of yours is a prayer on my behalf. Something more has come about, because the day of the Games has scattered many away from here; and just as we are taking pains for their salvation, so, brothers, we exhort you to take pains, and with intent mind to plead with God for those who are not yet bent on the spectacles of truth but are given over to the spectacles of the flesh. For I know, and I am quite certain, that there are now among your number some who held this day in contempt; yet they break with what they have grown used to. For people change, both for the better and for the worse. By such daily experiences we are by turns gladdened and saddened: gladdened by the corrected, saddened by the corrupted. And so the Lord did not say that he who begins will be saved, but: *He who perseveres to the end will be saved*[^2].

**1. 2.** And what more wonderful thing could the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, who is also the Son of man (for he deigned to be this too), grant us; what more magnificent thing could he do than to gather into his sheepfold not only the spectators of frivolous games but even some of those who are usually watched there? For he hunted to salvation not only the lovers of hunters but the hunters themselves, because he too was watched as a spectacle. Hear how. He himself said it, he himself foretold it before he was made a spectacle, and as though it had already happened he announced beforehand in prophetic speech what was to come, saying in the Psalm: *They have pierced my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones*[^3]. See how he was made a spectacle, so that his bones might be numbered. And he speaks more plainly of the spectacle itself: *They themselves considered and looked upon me*[^4]. He was watched to be mocked, watched by those who would not even favor him in that spectacle but raged at him; just as he first made his martyrs to be watched, as the Apostle says: *We have become a spectacle to the world, to angels and to men*[^5]. But two kinds of people watch such spectacles: one of the carnal, the other of the spiritual. The carnal watch and think those martyrs wretched who were thrown to the beasts, beheaded, or burned with fire, and they detest and shudder at them. But others watch as the holy angels also do, not attending to the mangling of bodies but marveling at the integrity of faith. A mind whole within, while the body is torn apart, offers a great spectacle to the eyes of the heart. When these things are read in church, you gladly watch with the eyes of the heart. For if you watched nothing, you would hear nothing. You see, then, that today you have not despised the spectacles, but chosen them. So may God be present, granting that to your friends, whom you grieve to see today run to the amphitheater and unwilling to come to church, you may sweetly report your own spectacles, so that what they have made cheap by loving it may begin to grow cheap to them, and that they may love God with you, of whom no lover need ever blush, because he loves one who cannot be conquered. May they love Christ with you, who by the very thing in which he seemed conquered conquered the whole world. For he conquered the whole world as we see, brothers: he subjected all powers, he subdued kings, not with a proud soldiery but by a derided cross; not raging with the sword but hanging on the wood; suffering bodily, accomplishing spiritually. His body was lifted up on the cross; he was subjecting minds to the cross. And what gem, in short, is more precious in the diadem than the cross of Christ on the brow of those who reign? In loving him you are never put to shame. For how many return conquered from the amphitheater, conquered by those for whom they rave? They would be conquered all the more if they won. For they would be subjected to an empty joy, subjected to the exultation of a depraved desire; they are conquered by the very thing in which they run there. For how many, brothers, do you think hesitated today whether to come here or to go there? And those who, in that very hesitation, considered Christ and ran to the church, conquered not just any man but the devil himself, the worst hunter of the whole world. But those who in that hesitation chose rather to run to the amphitheater were conquered by the one whom these conquered. And they conquered in him who said: *Rejoice, for I have conquered the world*[^6]. For the Commander allowed himself to be tempted in order to teach the soldier how to fight.

**2. 3.** So that our Lord Jesus Christ might do this, then, he was made the Son of man by being born of a woman. But if he had not been born of the Virgin Mary, what would have been lacking? Someone says: He wished to be a man; let him be a man, yet not be born of a woman; for he did not make the first man, whom he made, from a woman either. Consider what is answered to this. You say: why did he choose a woman for his birth? You are answered: rather, why should he have shunned a woman for his birth? Suppose I cannot show what he chose in being born of a woman: you show what he ought to have shunned in a woman. But these things have already been said at times: if he had shunned a woman's womb, he would have signified, as it were, that he could have been contaminated by her. But the more incapable of stain he was through his own substance, the less he ought to have dreaded the womb of flesh, as though he could be stained by it; rather, born of a woman, he ought to show us something of a great mystery. For in truth, brothers, we too confess that if the Lord had willed to become man in such a way as not to be born of a woman, this was surely easy for his Majesty. For just as he could be born of a woman without a man, so he could be born without a woman at all. But he showed us this, namely, that no human creature should despair of itself in either sex. For the human sex is of males and females. If, then, existing as a man, which he certainly had to be, he were not born of a woman, women would despair of themselves, mindful of their first sin, because the first man was deceived through a woman; and they would think they had no hope at all in Christ. He came, then, as a man, to single out the male sex, and born of a woman, to console the female sex, as if addressing it and saying: That you may know that God's creature is not evil, but that depraved pleasure perverted it, in the beginning when I made the human being, I made male and female. I do not condemn the creature I made. Behold, I am born a man, behold, born of a woman. I do not, then, condemn the creature I made, but the sins I did not make. Let each sex see its honor, and let each confess its iniquity, and let each hope for salvation. To the man being deceived poison was given to drink through a woman; to the man being restored let salvation be given to drink through a woman. Let a woman make up for the sin of the man deceived through her, by bringing forth Christ. Hence too it was women who first announced to the apostles that God had risen. A woman announced death to her husband in paradise; and women announced salvation to men in the Church. The apostles were to announce the resurrection of Christ to the nations; women announced it to the apostles. Let no one, then, slander Christ for being born of a woman, by whose sex the Liberator could not be stained and which sex the Creator was bound to commend.

**3. 4.** But, they say, how are we to believe that Christ was born of a woman? Let me answer: from the Gospel that has been and is preached to the whole world. But, blind, they try to force a question upon a matter already believed throughout the whole earth, eager to blind others, and not seeing what ought to be seen while they try to tear down what ought to be believed. For they answer and say: Do not press us with the authority of the whole world; let us consider Scripture itself. Do not appeal to the crowd: a deceived multitude favors you. First I answer this: A deceived multitude favors me? This multitude was once a tiny few. Whence grew this multitude that was announced so long beforehand by these very increases? For it would not have been seen to grow, had it not been foreseen. I do not say, There was a tiny few: there was one, Abraham. Consider, brothers, there was one Abraham in the whole world at that time, in the whole earth among all men, among all nations, to whom this man it was said: *In your seed shall all nations be blessed*[^7]. What one man believed concerning his own singleness was displayed to many through the multitude of his seed. Then it was not seen, and it was believed; now it is seen, and it is attacked; and what was then said to one and believed by one is now opposed by a few, since it is displayed in many. He who made his disciples fishers of men enclosed every kind of authority within his nets. If the multitude is to be believed, what is more abundant than the Church spread throughout the whole world? If the rich are to be believed, let them note how many rich it has caught; if the poor are to be believed, let them note the thousands of the poor; if the noble, almost all the nobility is now within; if kings, let them see all made subject to Christ; if the more eloquent, the more learned, the more prudent, let them behold how many orators, how many experts, how many philosophers of this world have been ensnared by those fishermen, that they might be drawn from the deep to salvation; thinking of him who, descending to heal by the example of his humility the great evil of the human soul, that is, pride, *chose the weak things of the world to confound the strong, and the foolish things of this world to confound the wise; not those who were such, but those who seemed to be such; and chose the lowly things of this world, and the things that are not, that he might bring to nothing the things that are*[^8].

**4. 5.** Say what you like, they say; we have caught, where you read that Christ was born, that the Gospels disagree among themselves, and both cannot be true where they disagree. For when I have shown the disagreement, one of them says, I rightly reject the faith; but you who accept the faith, show the agreement. What disagreement, I ask, will you point out? An obvious one, he says, which no one can contradict. How securely you hear this, because you are faithful! Attend, dearly beloved, and see how wholesomely the Apostle admonishes us, saying: *As therefore you have received Christ Jesus our Lord, so walk in him, rooted and built up in him, and confirmed in the faith*[^9]. For by that very simple and certain faith we ought to remain firmly in him, so that he may open to the faithful what is hidden in himself; because, as the same Apostle says: *In him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge*[^10]. He does not hide them in order to deny them, but in order to rouse desire by what is hidden. This is the usefulness of the secret. Honor in him what you do not yet understand; and honor it all the more, the more veils you discern. For the more honored a man is, the more veils hang in his house. Veils make for the honor of the secret; but for those who honor, the veils are lifted. Those who mock the veils, however, are driven away from the neighborhood of the veils. Because, then, we pass over to Christ, the veil is taken away[^11].

**5. 6.** Certain ones bring forward their slanders and say: Is Matthew indeed an evangelist? We answer, yes, with devout mouth, with devoted heart, in no way doubting; we answer plainly, Matthew is an evangelist. Do you believe him? they say. Who would not answer, I believe? How that resounded from your devout murmur! So, brothers, if you believe securely, there is nothing for you to blush at. I am speaking to you, I who was once deceived, when as a boy I first wanted to bring to the divine Scriptures the keenness of dispute before the piety of seeking; I myself, by my perverse habits, was closing the door of my Lord against myself; when I ought to have knocked for it to be opened, I was adding instead to its being closed. For in my pride I dared to seek what only the humble can find. How much more blessed are you now! How securely you learn, how safely, all you who are still little ones in the nest of faith and receive spiritual food! But I, wretched, when I thought myself fit to fly, left the nest; and I fell before I flew. But the merciful Lord, lest I be trampled by passersby and die, lifted me up and put me back in the nest. For these were the things that troubled me, which now I both set before you and explain, secure in the name of the Lord.

**5. 7.** So, as I had begun to say, they slander thus: Matthew, they say, is an evangelist, and you believe him? Consequently, the one whom we confess to be an evangelist we necessarily believe. Attend to the genealogy of Christ that Matthew set down: *The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham*[^12]. How *son of David*, how *son of Abraham*? It cannot be shown except through the succession of the line. For it is established that when the Lord was born of the Virgin Mary, neither Abraham nor David was among the living. And you say that the same one is the son of David, and the same the son of Abraham? As if we were to say to Matthew, Prove, then, what you say. For I await the succession of the line of Christ. *Abraham*, he says, *begot Isaac, and Isaac begot Jacob. And Jacob begot Judah and his brothers. And Judah begot Perez and Zerah by Tamar. And Perez begot Hezron. And Hezron begot Ram. And Ram begot Amminadab. And Amminadab begot Nahshon. And Nahshon begot Salmon. And Salmon begot Boaz by Rahab. And Boaz begot Obed by Ruth. And Obed begot Jesse. And Jesse begot David the king*[^13]. Now from here attend to how one comes from David to Christ, who was called the son of Abraham and the son of David. *And David*, he says, *begot Solomon by her who had been Uriah's wife. And Solomon begot Rehoboam. And Rehoboam begot Abijah. And Abijah begot Asa. And Asa begot Jehoshaphat. And Jehoshaphat begot Joram. And Joram begot Uzziah. And Uzziah begot Jotham. And Jotham begot Ahaz. And Ahaz begot Hezekiah. And Hezekiah begot Manasseh. And Manasseh begot Amon. And Amon begot Josiah. And Josiah begot Jechoniah and his brothers, at the deportation to Babylon. And after the deportation to Babylon, Jechoniah begot Shealtiel. And Shealtiel begot Zerubbabel. And Zerubbabel begot Abiud. And Abiud begot Eliakim. And Eliakim begot Azor. And Azor begot Zadok. And Zadok begot Achim. And Achim begot Eliud. And Eliud begot Eleazar. And Eleazar begot Matthan. And Matthan begot Jacob. And Jacob begot Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ*[^14]. So, then, through the order and succession of parents and forebears, Christ is found to be the son of David, the son of Abraham.

**5. 8.** Against this matter, faithfully recounted, they raise this first slander, that the same Matthew goes on and says: *All the generations from Abraham to David are fourteen generations; and from David to the deportation to Babylon, fourteen generations; and from the deportation to Babylon to Christ, fourteen generations*[^15]. Then he added, to recount how Christ was born of the Virgin Mary, going on to say: *Now the generation of Christ was in this way*[^16]. For by the order of the parents he enumerated why Christ is called the son of David, the son of Abraham.

**6. 8.** But how he was born and appeared among men must now be recounted; and the account itself follows, by which we believe our Lord Jesus Christ to have been not only born of the eternal God, coeternal with him who begot him before all times, before every creature, through whom all things were made, but also now born of the Holy Spirit from the Virgin Mary, which we likewise confess. For you remember and know (since I am speaking to my Catholic brothers) that this is our faith, that this is what we profess and confess. For this faith thousands of martyrs have been slain throughout the whole world.

**6. 9.** For what follows is what those wish to ridicule who wish to detract from the faith due to the books of the Gospels, so as to make it seem that we have rashly believed what is said: *When his mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, before they came together she was found to be with child of the Holy Spirit. But Joseph her husband, being a just man and unwilling to expose her, wished to put her away quietly*[^17]. For because he knew she was not pregnant by him, he now reckoned her, as it seemed to follow, an adulteress. *Being a just man*, as Scripture says, *and unwilling to expose her*, that is, to make it public, for many codices have this too; *he wished to put her away quietly*. The husband is indeed troubled, but being just he does not rage. For so great a justice is attributed to this man that he neither wished to keep an adulteress nor dared to punish a woman publicly exposed. *He wished*, he says, *to put her away quietly*; because not only did he not wish to punish her, but neither to expose her. Attend to his sincere justice. For he did not wish to spare her because he desired to keep her. For many spare adulterous wives out of carnal love, wishing to keep them even as adulteresses, that they may take their full pleasure of them through carnal desire. But this just man does not wish to keep her: therefore he does not love carnally. And yet he does not wish to punish her: therefore he spares her mercifully. What kind of just man is this? He neither holds on to an adulteress, nor does he seem to spare her because he loved her with lust; and yet he neither punishes nor exposes her. Deservedly, then, he was chosen as a witness to the virginity of his wife. He who was troubled by human weakness was strengthened by divine authority.

**7. 10.** For it follows, and the Evangelist says: *As he was thinking these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in his sleep, saying: Joseph, do not fear to take Mary your wife. For what is born in her is of the Holy Spirit. She will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus*[^18]. Why *Jesus*? *For he*, he says, *will save his people from their sins*[^19]. It is understood, then, that "Jesus" in the Hebrew tongue is in Latin rendered "Savior," which we notice from the very explanation of the name. For as if it were asked why *Jesus*, he at once added, disclosing the reason for the name: *For he*, he says, *will save his people from their sins*. This we devoutly believe, this we hold most firmly: that Christ was born of the Holy Spirit from the Virgin Mary.

**7. 11.** What, then, do they say? If I find a lie, one says, you surely do not believe the whole. I have found one, let us see. I count the generations. For by their slanders they invite and lead us to this. If we live devoutly, if we believe Christ, if we do not desire to fly from the nest before our time, they lead us to this, that we may come to know the mysteries. Let your Holiness, then, attend to the usefulness of heretics; a usefulness that is according to God, who uses well even the wicked. According to themselves, indeed, what is returned is what they intended; not what God brings about well from them. As in the case of Judas, how much good he did! By the Passion of the Lord the nations were saved. But that the Lord might suffer, Judas betrayed him. God therefore both frees the nations by his Son's suffering and punishes Judas for his crime. For the mysteries that lie hidden there no one would examine, content with a simpler faith; and so no one would find them, because no one would examine them, were it not for the slanderers knocking. For when heretics slander, the little ones are troubled; when they are troubled, they inquire; their inquiry is like a knocking from the head against the mother's breasts, that as much may flow as is enough for the little ones. Those troubled ones seek; but those who know and have learned, because they have searched and God has opened to them as they knocked, themselves open to the troubled. And so it comes about that the heretics are useful for finding the truth, while they slander to seduce into error. For truth would be sought more negligently if it had no lying adversaries. *For there must*, he says, *be heresies too.* And as if we were asking the reason, he at once added: *That those who are approved may be made manifest among you*[^20].

**8. 12.** What, then, do they say? Behold, Matthew counts the generations, and says there are fourteen from Abraham to David, and fourteen from David to the deportation to Babylon, and fourteen from the deportation to Babylon to Christ. Multiply fourteen by three, and they make forty-two. But they count, and find forty-one generations, and raise their slander, and insult with mockery. What, then, does it mean, when the Gospel says there are three times fourteen, and yet all counted are found to be not forty-two but forty-one? Without doubt it is a great mystery. And we rejoice, giving thanks to the Lord, because even through the occasion offered by the slanderers we have found something which, the more it was covered up to be sought out, the more it delights when found. For as we said beforehand, we are putting on a spectacle of minds. From Abraham, then, to David there are fourteen. There the count begins from Solomon; for David begot Solomon. The count begins from Solomon and reaches Jechoniah, in whose lifetime the deportation to Babylon took place; and there are another fourteen generations, with Solomon counted at the head of the second interval, and with Jechoniah also counted, at whom the count itself is closed, so that fourteen are completed. But the third interval begins from that same Jechoniah.

**8. 13.** Let your Holiness attend to a mystical and sweet matter: I confess to you the taste of my heart, whence I believe that when I have brought it forth and you have tasted it, you will report the same back. Attend, then. From Jechoniah himself, with whom the count of the third interval begins, to the Lord Jesus Christ there are fourteen; because that Jechoniah, both the last of the previous interval and the first of the following interval, is counted twice. But someone may say: Why is Jechoniah counted twice? Nothing was done in the people of Israel of old that was not a mystery of things to come. Jechoniah is indeed counted twice not without reason; because if there be a boundary between two fields, whether a stone or some division of a wall, both he who is on this side measures up to that wall, and he who is on the other side takes from it again the beginning of his measuring. But why this was not done in the first joining of the interval, where from Abraham we count fourteen generations to David, and another fourteen not by repeating David but by beginning to count from Solomon, the reason must be given, which contains a great mystery. Let your Holiness attend. The deportation to Babylon took place when Jechoniah was set up as king in the place of his deceased father. The kingdom was taken from him, and another set up in his place. Yet the deportation to the nations took place while Jechoniah was living. For no fault of Jechoniah is named, on account of which he was deprived of the kingdom; rather, the sins of those who succeeded him are brought in. The captivity therefore follows, they go off to Babylon. The wicked do not go alone; but with these the saints also go. In that captivity was the prophet Ezekiel, in it was Daniel; there were the three young men ennobled amid the flames. And they went off according to the prophecy of the prophet Jeremiah.

**9. 14.** Remember that Jechoniah, condemned without any fault, thereupon ceased to reign and made the crossing to the nations, when the deportation to Babylon took place; and attend to the image, shown beforehand, of things to come in the Lord Jesus Christ. For the Jews were unwilling that our Lord Jesus Christ, in whom they found no fault, should reign over them. He was rejected in himself, rejected also in his servants; and the crossing was made to the nations, as though to Babylon. For this too Jeremiah prophesied, that the Lord commanded them to go to Babylon. And whatever other prophets told the people not to go to Babylon, Jeremiah convicted as false prophets. Let those who read the Scriptures recall this with us; let those who do not read believe us. Jeremiah, then, in the person of the Lord, threatened those who were unwilling to go to Babylon; but to those who would go he promised rest there, and a certain happiness in planting new vineyards and laying out gardens and in an abundance of fruits[^21]. How, then, does the people of Israel now pass over to Babylon, no longer in figure but in truth? Where were the apostles from? Were they not of the people of the Jews? Where was Paul himself from? *For I too*, he says, *am an Israelite, of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin*[^22]. Many of the Jews, then, believed in the Lord. From there the apostles were chosen; from there were more than five hundred brothers who were granted to see the Lord after his resurrection[^23]; from there were the hundred and twenty in the house when the Holy Spirit came[^24]. And what does the Apostle say in the Acts of the Apostles, when the Jews spurned the word of truth? *To you*, he says, *we were sent; but since you have spurned the word of God, behold, we turn to the nations*[^25]. The deportation to Babylon, then, took place according to the spiritual dispensation of the time of the Lord's Incarnation, which was prefigured then in the time of Jeremiah. But what does Jeremiah say to those being deported among these Babylonians? *Because in their peace*, he says, *will be your peace*[^26]. When, therefore, Israel was being deported through Christ and the apostles to Babylon—that is, the Gospel was coming to the nations—what does the Apostle say, as if in the voice of Jeremiah then? *I urge, then, first of all, that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and for all who are set in high places; that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life, with all godliness and chastity*[^27]. There were not yet Christian kings, and he prayed for them. Praying, then, in Babylon, Israel was heard. The voices of the Church were heard, they became Christians; and you see fulfilled what was said figuratively: *In their peace will be your peace*. For they received the peace of Christ and ceased to persecute Christians, so that now in the security of peace churches might be built, and peoples planted in the husbandry of God, and all nations bear fruit in faith, hope, and love that is in Christ.

**9. 15.** The deportation to Babylon took place then through Jechoniah, who was not allowed to reign in the people of the Jews, as a type of Christ, whom the Jews were unwilling to have reign over them[^28]. Israel made the crossing to the nations, that is, the preachers of the Gospel passed over to the peoples of the nations. Why, then, do you marvel that Jechoniah is counted twice? For if he bore the figure of Christ passing from the Jews to the nations, attend to what Christ is between the Jews and the nations. Is he not that cornerstone? Attend, in the corner, to the boundary of one wall and the beginning of another. Up to that very stone you measure one wall, and from it the other. The cornerstone, then, which joins both walls, is counted twice. Jechoniah, therefore, bearing the figure of the Lord, presented the type as a cornerstone. And just as Jechoniah was not allowed to reign over the Jews, but the going was to Babylon, so Christ, *the stone which the builders rejected, has become the head of the corner*[^29], that the Gospel might reach the nations. Do not, then, hesitate to count the head of the corner twice, and the written number comes out for you; and so there are fourteen, and fourteen, and fourteen, and yet they are not forty-two generations but forty-one. For just as, when a row of stones runs in a straight line, they are all counted singly, but when the row is turned aside to make a corner, that stone from which it turns must be counted twice, because it belongs both to that row which ends at it and to that which begins from it: so the order of the generations, as long as it remained in that people, in the twice-seven, that is, the fourteenth interval, made no corner; but when the order was turned aside, so as to be deported to Babylon, a kind of corner was made, as it were, from Jechoniah, so that he had to be counted twice, in the type of that venerable cornerstone.

**10. 16.** Another slander of theirs is this. The generations of Christ, they say, are counted through Joseph, and not through Mary. Let your Holiness attend a little. They ought not, they say, to be counted through Joseph. Why not through Joseph? Was not Joseph the husband of Mary? No, they say. Who says this? For Scripture says, by angelic authority, that he was her husband. *Do not fear*, he says, *to take Mary your wife. For what is born in her is of the Holy Spirit*[^30]. He is also commanded to give the boy a name, although born not of his seed. *She will bear*, he says, *a son, and you shall call his name Jesus*[^31]. But Scripture means this, that he was not born of Joseph's seed, when, to Joseph anxious about whence she was heavy with child, it is said: *It is of the Holy Spirit*. And yet paternal authority is not taken from him, since he is commanded to give the boy a name. Finally, the Virgin Mary herself, well aware in her own conscience that she had not conceived Christ from his embrace and intercourse, nevertheless calls him the father of Christ.

**10. 17.** Attend to how. When the Lord Jesus Christ was twelve years old according to the man—he who according to God is before the ages and without time—he remained behind them in the temple, and was disputing with the elders, and they marveled at his teaching. But they, returning from Jerusalem, sought him in their company, namely among those who walked with them; and not finding him, troubled, they returned to Jerusalem and found him disputing in the temple with the elders, being—as I said—twelve years old[^32]. But what wonder? The Word of God is never silent, but is not always heard. He is found, then, in the temple, and his mother says to him: *Why have you done so to us? Your father and I were sorrowing as we sought you.* And he: *Did you not know that I must be about the things that are my Father's?*[^33] He said this because the Son of God was in the temple of God. For that temple was not Joseph's, but God's. Behold, someone says, he did not grant himself to be the son of Joseph. Attend a little more patiently, brothers, because of the shortness of the time, that it may suffice for the sermon. When Mary had said, *Your father and I were sorrowing as we sought you*, he answered, *Did you not know that I must be about the things that are my Father's?* For he did not wish to be their son in such a way that he would not be understood to be the Son of God. For the Son of God is always the Son of God, creating these very persons. But the Son of man is so in time, born of a virgin without marital seed, yet he had both as parents. Whence do we prove this? Mary has already said: *Your father and I were sorrowing as we sought you.*

**11. 18.** First of all, brothers, the holy modesty of the Virgin Mary is not to be passed over, especially for the instruction of women, our sisters. She had borne Christ, the angel had come to her and had said to her: *Behold, you will conceive in your womb and bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus. He will be great, and will be called the Son of the Most High*[^34]. She had merited to bear the Son of the Most High, and she was most humble: she did not put herself before her husband, neither in the order of naming, so as to say, I and your father; but: *Your father*, she says, *and I.* She did not attend to the dignity of her womb, but attended to the conjugal order. For the humble Christ would not have taught his mother to be proud. *Your father and I were sorrowing as we sought you. Your father*, she says, *and I*; because the head of the woman is the man[^35]. How much less ought other women to be proud? For Mary herself was called a woman, not with her virginity corrupted, but by the proper appellation of her people. For the Apostle too said of the Lord Jesus Christ: *Made of a woman*[^36]; yet he did not break the order and texture of our faith, by which we confess him born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary. For she as a virgin conceived, as a virgin bore, and remained a virgin. But they called all females women, by the property of the Hebrew tongue. Hear a most evident example. The first woman, whom God made, taken from the man's side, before she lay with the man—which is written to have happened after they went out of paradise—was nevertheless already called woman, as Scripture says: *He formed her into a woman*[^37].

**11. 19.** So when the Lord Jesus Christ answers: *I had to be about the things that are my Father's*[^38], he does not so point to God as Father that he denies Joseph to be father. Whence do we prove this? From Scripture, which speaks thus: *And he said to them: Did you not know that I must be about the things that are my Father's? But they did not understand what he had spoken to them. And when he had gone down with them, he came to Nazareth, and was subject to them*[^39]. He did not say: He was subject to his mother; or, He was subject to her; but, *He was*, he says, *subject to them.* To whom was he subject? Was it not to his parents? Both were parents to whom he was subject, by that condescension by which he was the Son of man.

**12. 19.** Women received their precepts a while ago; now let the children receive theirs, to obey their parents and be subject to them. The world is subject to Christ, Christ is subject to his parents.

**12. 20.** You see, then, brothers, that he did not say, "I must be about the things that are my Father's," in such a way that we should understand it as if he said, You are not my parents. But they were his parents in time, that Father his eternally. They were the parents of the Son of man, that Father the Father of his Word and Wisdom, the Father of his Power, through whom he formed all things. If through her all things are formed—she who *reaches from end to end mightily and orders all things sweetly*[^40]—then through the Son of God those too were formed to whom the same one would afterward, as the Son of man, be subject. And the Apostle calls him the son of David: *Who was made for him*, he says, *of the seed of David according to the flesh*[^41]. Yet the Lord himself sets a question before the Jews, which the Apostle resolves in these very words. For when he said, *Who was made for him of the seed of David*, he added to this, *according to the flesh*, so that it might be understood that according to his divinity he is not the son of David but the Son of God, the Lord of David. For in another place too the Apostle speaks thus, when commending the stock of the Jews: *Whose are the fathers*, he says, *and from whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all things, God blessed forever*[^42]. In that he is *according to the flesh*, he is from there the son of David; but in that he is *God over all things, blessed forever*, from there he is the Lord of David. The Lord, then, says this to the Jews: *Whose son do you say that Christ is?* They answered: *David's*[^43]. For this they knew, which they easily grasped from the preaching of the Prophets. And truly he was *of the seed of David*, but *according to the flesh*, through the Virgin Mary betrothed to Joseph. So when they answered that Christ was the son of David, Jesus said to them: *How then does David in spirit call him Lord, saying, "The Lord said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool under your feet"? If then David in spirit calls him Lord, how is he his son?*[^44] And the Jews could not answer. So we have it in the Gospel. He did not deny that he was the son of David, so that they should not be ignorant that he was the Lord of David. For they held in Christ what was made in time; in him they did not understand what is in eternity. Therefore, wishing to teach them his divinity, he raised a question about his humanity: as if he said, "You know that Christ is the son of David; answer me how he is also the Lord of David." But lest they say, "He is not the Lord of David," he set David himself as a witness. And what does he say? He certainly speaks the truth. For you have this too in the Psalms, where it is said to David: *Of the fruit of your womb I will set upon your throne*[^45]. Behold the son of David. How is he also the Lord of David, who is the son of David? *The Lord*, he says, *said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand*[^46]. Do you marvel that David has his own son as Lord, when you see that Mary bore her own Lord? The Lord of David, because God; the Lord of David, because the Lord of all; but the son of David, because the Son of man. The same is Lord, the same is son: the Lord of David, *who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal with God*; but the son of David, because *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^47].

**13. 21.** Joseph, then, was not any less a father because he did not lie with the mother of the Lord; as though it were lust that makes a wife, and not conjugal love. Let your Holiness attend. The Apostle of Christ was going to say, after some time, in the Church: *It remains that those who have wives should be as though not having them*[^48]. And we know many of our brothers, bearing fruit in grace in the name of Christ, who by mutual consent restrain the desire of the flesh from each other, yet do not restrain conjugal love from each other. The more the one is repressed, the more strongly the other is confirmed. Are they not spouses who live thus, not seeking from each other the fruit of the flesh, not exacting from each other the debt of bodily desire? And yet she is subject to her husband, because so it is fitting; and the more subject, the more chaste; and he loves his wife truly, as it is written, *in honor and sanctification*[^49], as a coheir of grace, *as Christ*, he says, *loved the Church*[^50]. So if there is union, if there is marriage, if it is not therefore no marriage because that is not done which can be done even outside marriage, but unlawfully: would that all could; but many cannot. Let no one, then, separate those who can, and on that account deny that the one is a husband or the other a wife, because they do not mingle carnally with each other, but are joined in their hearts.

**13. 22.** From this, my brothers, understand what Scripture meant about those parents of ours who were so married that they sought from their spouses only offspring. For so chastely did those keep them—those who, for their time and the custom of their people, even had several—that they did not approach carnal intercourse except for the sake of procreation, truly holding them in honor. But the man who craves the flesh of his wife more than the bounds prescribed by the cause of procreating children acts against the very tablets by which he took her as wife. The tablets are read out, and read out in the sight of all who attest them, and there is read out, *For the sake of procreating children*; and they are called *marriage tablets*. Unless wives are given and taken for this end, who with a sound face gives his daughter to another's lust? But so that the parents need not blush when they give her, the tablets are read out, that they may be fathers-in-law, not procurers. What, then, is read from the tablets? *For the sake of procreating children*. The father's brow is wiped clean and made serene on hearing the voice of the tablets. Let us look at the brow of the man taking a wife. Let the husband too blush to take her otherwise, if the father blushes to give her otherwise. But if they cannot (we have already said these things at times), let them exact the debt; let them not go beyond their debtors. Let both woman and man console their weakness in each other. Let him not go to another woman, nor she to another man; whence it is called adultery, as it were "to another" (ad alterum). And if they go beyond the bounds of the marriage compact, let them not go beyond the bounds of the conjugal bed. Is it not a sin to exact the debt from a spouse beyond what the necessity of procreating children compels? It is indeed a sin, but a venial one. The Apostle says: *This I say by way of indulgence*; speaking on this matter: *Do not*, he says, *defraud one another, except by consent for a time, that you may be free for prayer; and come together again, lest Satan tempt you because of your incontinence*[^51]. What is this? Do not lay upon yourselves anything beyond your strength; lest, by abstaining from each other, you fall into adulteries. *Lest Satan tempt you because of your incontinence.* And lest perhaps what he was permitting should seem to be commanded (for it is one thing to command for virtue, another to permit for weakness), he at once added: *This I say by way of indulgence, not by way of command. For I would that all men were as I myself*[^52]. As if he said: I do not command that you do it, but I pardon it if you have done it.

**14. 23.** So, my brothers, attend. As for the great men who had wives for the sake of procreating children, such as we read the Fathers were, and find by many proofs, the holy pages crying out altogether without doubt: if, then, any men had wives only for the begetting of children, and if it could be granted them to have children without intercourse, would they not embrace so great a benefit with ineffable joy? Would they not receive it with vast gladness? For there are two carnal works by which the human race subsists; into these two works the prudent and the holy go down out of duty, but the imprudent rush into them through desire. For it is one thing to go down to something out of duty, another to fall into something through desire. What are these by which the human race subsists? In ourselves the first is what pertains to taking nourishment (which surely cannot be taken without some delight through the flesh), eating and drinking: if you do not do it, you will die. By this one support, then, the human race stands, according to the measure of its nature, of eating and drinking. But by this support men are sustained in what pertains to themselves; they do not provide for succession by eating and drinking, but by marrying. For thus the human race subsists, first that men may live; but because, however much care is given to the body, they cannot live forever, the next provision is that those being born succeed those dying. For the human race is, as it is written, like leaves on a tree—but on a tree of olive, or laurel, or some such kind, which is never without foliage; yet it does not always have the same leaves. For as it is written, it puts forth some and casts off others[^53]; because those that spring up succeed the others that fall. For it always casts off leaves, it is always clothed with leaves. So too the human race does not feel the loss of those dying daily, through the replacement of those being born: and thus, in its own measure, the whole species of the human race stands; and just as leaves are always seen on a tree, so the earth is seen full of men. But if men only died and were not born, then, just as some trees of all their leaves, so the earth would be stripped of all its men.

**14. 24.** Since, then, the human race so subsists that two supports are necessary, of which enough has been said, to each the wise and prudent and faithful man goes down out of duty, he does not fall through lust. To eating and drinking, how many rush greedily, placing there their whole life, as though it were the very cause of living! For though they eat in order to live, they think they live in order to eat. These the wise man reproves, and most of all divine Scripture: the gluttonous, the drunken, the wastrels, *whose god is the belly*[^54]. To the table nothing leads them but the desire of the flesh, not the need of refreshment. And so these fall into food and drink. But those who go down out of the duty of living do not live in order to eat, but eat in order to live. And so, if it were offered to these prudent and temperate men that they should live without food and drink, with what joy would they embrace this benefit, that they need neither fall, where they were not accustomed to fall, nor be compelled to go down; but might always be suspended in the Lord, the necessity of propping up the body's ruins not bringing down their intentions! How do you think holy Elijah received it, when he received a cup of water and a small loaf of bread, that it might be enough for him as nourishment for forty days[^55]? Surely with great gladness, because he ate and drank for the duty of life, not for the slavery of desire. Try to offer this to a man who has set his whole blessedness and happiness in feasting, like a beast at the stall. He hates your benefit, he thrusts it from him, he thinks it a punishment. So too in that conjugal duty, lustful men seek wives for nothing else, and so they are scarcely ever content even with their wives. And would that, if they cannot or will not take away their lust, they would not let it go beyond what the conjugal debt prescribes, even what is conceded to weakness. But plainly, if you said to such a man, "Why do you take a wife?" he would answer you, perhaps abashed, "For the sake of children." If someone said to him, one whom he would believe without any doubt, "God is able to give you, and will surely give you, children, even though you do not do that work with your wife": there at last he would be cornered and confess that it was not for the sake of children that he sought a wife. Let him confess his weakness, then; let him receive what he pretended to receive out of duty.

**15. 25.** So those former saints, men of God, sought children, wished to receive children. For this one thing they were joined to women; for this they had intercourse with women, that they might procreate children. Therefore it was permitted them to have several. For if immoderate lust pleased God, it would have been permitted in that time for one woman to have several husbands, just as one husband had several women. Why did all chaste women have no more than one husband, but a man several women, unless because for one man to have several women pertains to the multiplying of offspring; whereas one woman, however many husbands she had, could not bear the more children for that? Therefore, brothers, if our Fathers were joined and had intercourse with women for no other reason than to procreate children, it would have been a great joy to them if, apart from that carnal work, they could have the children for whose sake they did not rush into it through lust, but went down to it out of duty. Was Joseph not a father, on this account, because he received a son without carnal desire? Far be it that Christian chastity should hold this, which not even Jewish chastity held. Love your wives, but love them chastely. Seek the carnal work only up to the point of procreating children. And because you cannot otherwise have children, go down to it with sorrow. For it is the punishment of that Adam from whom we have sprung. Let us not be exalted over our punishment. It is the punishment of him who deserved to beget mortally; because by sin he was made mortal. God did not take away the punishment itself, that man might remember whence he is called back, and to what he is called; and might seek that embrace where there can be no corruption.

**15. 26.** In that people, then, because an abundant propagation had to take place up to Christ, through the multitude of the populace in which would be prefigured all the lessons that had to be prefigured for the Church, they had the duty of taking wives through whom the people might grow, the people in which the Church was to be foreshadowed.

**16. 26.** But when the very King of all nations was born, the dignity of virginity began from the Mother of the Lord, who merited both to have a son and not to suffer corruption. So, then, that was a marriage, and a marriage without any corruption: as the wife chastely bore, why should the husband not chastely receive? For as she was chastely a wife, so was he chastely a husband; and as she was chastely a mother, so was he chastely a father. He, then, who says, He ought not to be called father, because he did not so beget the son, seeks lust in the procreating of children, not the affection of love. He was the better man, who fulfilled in mind what another desires to fulfill in flesh. For those too who adopt children beget them more chastely in heart, whom they cannot in flesh. See, brothers, see the law of adoption, how a man becomes the son of one of whose seed he was not born; so that the will of the one adopting has more right in him than the nature of the one begetting. Thus, then, Joseph not only ought to have been a father, but most especially ought to have been. For men beget children from women who are not their wives, and they are called natural children; and the children of marriage are preferred to them. As far as the work of the flesh is concerned, they were born equally: whence are these preferred, unless because the love of the wife, from whom children are procreated, is more chaste? It is not the carnal mingling that is regarded there, which is equal in either woman. Where does the wife win out, except in the affection of fidelity, the affection of marriage, the affection of a more sincere and chaste love? If, then, anyone could receive children from his wife without intercourse, ought he not to have done so the more gladly, the more chaste she is whom he loves the more?

**17. 27.** From this now see also that it can come about that one man has not only two sons but also two fathers. For now that adoption has been named, let it occur to your thoughts that it can come about. For it is said: A man can have two sons, but he cannot have two fathers. On the contrary, he is found able to have even two fathers, if one begets him by seed, the other adopts him by love. If, then, one man can have two fathers, Joseph too could have two fathers, being begotten by one, adopted by the other. And if he could, why do those slander, who say that Matthew followed one genealogy and Luke another? And indeed we find that the one followed one, the other another. For Matthew said Jacob was the father of Joseph[^56], but Luke said Heli[^57]. And it can indeed seem that one man, whose son Joseph was, had two names. But because they enumerate different grandfathers and great-grandfathers and other forebears further up, and in that very count one has more, the other fewer, it is plainly shown that Joseph had two fathers. Now that the slander of the question has been removed, since clear reason has shown it can come about that one is the father who begot, another who adopted, with two fathers established, it is no wonder if the grandfathers and great-grandfathers and the rest of the forebears upward are counted differently from the different fathers.

**18. 28.** Nor let the law of adoption seem to you foreign to our Scriptures, and, as something observed in the custom of human laws, unable to fit the authority of the divine books. For it is an ancient thing, and customary in the ecclesiastical sayings themselves, that not the origin of seed alone should beget a son, but also the grace of the will. For women too used to adopt as sons those born of the seed of their husbands by handmaids, if they themselves had not borne children; indeed, they would even command their husbands to beget them, as Sarah did[^58], as Rachel[^59], as Leah[^60]. In which duty the husbands did not commit adultery, because they obeyed their wives in that matter which pertains to the conjugal debt, according to what the Apostle says: *The woman does not have power over her own body, but the husband; likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^61]. Moses too, born of a Hebrew mother and exposed, was adopted by Pharaoh's daughter[^62]. There were not indeed the very legal formulas that exist now; but the decision of the will was held in place of the norm of law; just as the Apostle says in another place, that *the nations, not having the law, do by nature the things of the law*[^63]. But if it was lawful for women to make those their sons whom they had not themselves borne, why not also for men those whom they had not themselves begotten of carnal seed, but by the love of adopting? For the patriarch Jacob himself, the father of so many sons, we read nevertheless made his own grandsons, the sons of Joseph, his sons, saying thus: *These two shall be mine, and shall receive the land with their brothers; the others whom you beget shall be yours*[^64]. Unless perhaps someone says that the very word of adoption is not found in the holy Scriptures. As though it mattered at all by what word it is called, when the thing itself exists, that a woman has a son whom she did not bear in the flesh, or a man someone whom he did not beget in the flesh. And, for my part, I do not object if he does not call Joseph adopted, provided he grant that he could have been the son even of one of whose flesh he was not born. Although the apostle Paul too constantly mentions the name of adoption, and in a great mystery. For though Scripture testifies that our Lord Jesus Christ is the only Son of God, it says that the brothers and coheirs whom he deigned to have come about by a kind of adoption of divine grace. *But when*, he says, *the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law; that he might redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^65]. And in another place: *In ourselves*, he says, *we groan, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^66]. And again, when he spoke of the Jews: *I myself could wish to be anathema from Christ for my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh, who are Israelites; whose is*, he says, *the adoption, and the glory, and the Testament, and the giving of the Law; whose are the fathers, and from whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all things, God blessed forever*[^67]. Where he shows that among the Jews of old there was either the name of adoption or the thing itself, like the Testament and the giving of the Law, which he mentions together.

**19. 29.** To this is added that there was another mode proper to the Jews, by which someone became the son of one of whom he was not carnally born. For relatives used to take the wives of their relatives who had died without children, in order to raise up seed for the deceased[^68]. Thus the one who was born was both the son of the man of whom he was born and the son of the man in whose succession he was born. These things have been said lest anyone, thinking it impossible that two fathers of one man be rightly named, should suppose that any of the Evangelists who recounted the genealogies of the Lord ought to be charged, by a sacrilegious slander, as it were with a lie: especially since we see ourselves admonished by their very own words. For Matthew, who is understood to mention the father by whom Joseph was begotten, enumerates the generations thus, This one begot that one, so that he can arrive at what he says at the end: *Jacob begot Joseph*[^69]. But Luke, because one who becomes a son either by adoption or by being born of her who had been his wife in the succession of the dead is not properly said to be begotten, did not say, "Heli begot Joseph"; or, "Joseph whom Heli begot"; but, *Who was*, he says, *the son of Heli*[^70]; whether by adoption, or by being born of a relative in the succession of the dead.

**20. 30.** Now as for the fact that it ought not to disturb anyone why the generations are counted through Joseph and not through Mary, enough has been said: because, just as she was a mother without carnal desire, so he was a father without carnal mingling. Through him, then, let the generations descend, and through him let them ascend. Nor let us separate him on this account, that carnal desire was lacking. Let a greater purity confirm his fatherhood, lest holy Mary herself reprove us. For she was unwilling to put her own name before her husband's, but said: *Your father and I were sorrowing as we sought you*[^71]. Let not the perverse murmurers do, then, what the chaste spouse did not do. Let us count, then, through Joseph: because as he is chastely a husband, so he is chastely a father. But let us put the man before the woman, by the order of nature and of God's law. For if, setting him aside, we set her up, he says, and rightly says: Why have you separated me? Why do the generations not either ascend or descend through me? Is it said to him, Because you did not beget by the work of your flesh? But he will answer, Did she then bear by the work of her flesh? What the Holy Spirit wrought, he wrought for both. *Being*, it says, *a just man*[^72]. A just man, then, a just woman. The Holy Spirit, resting in the justice of both, gave a son to both. But in that sex which it was fitting should bear, he wrought this, that the son should be born to the husband too. And so the angel tells both to give the boy a name; whereby the authority of the parents is declared. For Zechariah too, while he was still mute, the mother gave the name to the son being born. And when those who were present made signs to the father what he wished him to be called, taking writing tablets he wrote what she had already said[^73]. It is said to Mary too: *Behold, you will conceive a son, and you shall call his name Jesus*[^74]; it is said also to Joseph: *Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife. For what is born in her is of the Holy Spirit. She will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus: he will save his people from their sins*[^75]. It is said also: *And she bore him a son*[^76]; where the father is confirmed altogether not by flesh, but by love. So, then, he is a father just as he is. For most carefully and most prudently the Evangelists count through him, whether Matthew descending from Abraham to Christ, or Luke ascending from Christ through Abraham to God. The one counts descending, the other ascending, both through Joseph. Why? Because he is the father. Why a father? Because he is the more firmly a father, the more chastely a father. He was thought, indeed, to be the father of our Lord Jesus Christ in another way, namely as other fathers who beget in the flesh, not receiving children by spiritual affection alone. For Luke too said: *Who was thought to be the father of Jesus*[^77]. Why thought? Because human reckoning and estimation was carried to what is usually done by men. The Lord, then, was not of Joseph's seed, although this was thought; and yet, by the godliness and love of Joseph, a son was born to him of the Virgin Mary, and the same was the Son of God.

**21. 31.** But why does the one count descending, why does the other count ascending? Hear this, I ask, attentively, as far as the Lord helps, now with an untroubled mind and free from all the annoyance of knotty slanders. Matthew descends through the generations, to signify our Lord Jesus Christ descending to bear our sins, that in the seed of Abraham all nations might be blessed. Therefore he does not begin from Adam: for the whole human race is from him. Nor from Noah: because from his family too, after the flood, the whole human race arose. Nor could the man Christ Jesus pertain to the fulfillment of the prophecy as one from Adam, from whom are all men, or from Noah, from whom again are all men; but from Abraham, who was then chosen, in whose seed all nations would be blessed, when the earth was already full of nations[^78]. But Luke ascends, not beginning to enumerate the generations from the very beginning of the Lord's birth, but from that place where he recounts that he was baptized by John. And just as in the Lord's Incarnation the sins of the human race are taken up by him to be borne, so in the consecration of Baptism they are taken up to be cleansed. And so the one, signifying him descending to bear sins, enumerates the generations descending; but the other, ascending, signifying the cleansing of sins—not his own, indeed, but ours—enumerates the generations ascending. But the one descends through Solomon, in whose mother David sinned; the other ascends through Nathan, another son of the same David, through whom David was cleansed of his sin. For we read that Nathan was sent to him to rebuke him, and that he was healed through penance[^79]. Both met each other in David, the one descending, the other ascending; and from there up to Abraham, or from Abraham to David, they differ in no generation. Thus Christ, both the son of David and the son of Abraham, passes over to God. For we must in Baptism be led back, renewed, to God by the abolition of sins.

**22. 32.** In the generations, indeed, that Matthew enumerates, the number forty stands out. For the divine Scriptures have this custom, that what exceeds certain articles of numbers they sometimes do not reckon. So too four hundred years are spoken of, after which the people of Israel went out of Egypt[^80]; though they are four hundred and thirty. So too one generation that exceeds the number forty does not take from this number its primacy. Now this number signifies the life by which one labors on this earth, as long as we are pilgrims away from the Lord[^81], in which the dispensation of truth temporally preached is necessary. For the number ten, by which the perfection of blessedness is signified, multiplied fourfold, on account of the fourfold time and the fourfold world, makes the number forty. Therefore there was a fast of forty days, both by Moses[^82] and by Elijah[^83] and by the Mediator himself, our Lord Jesus Christ[^84]: because in this time abstinence from bodily enticements is necessary. The people too were pilgrims forty years in the desert[^85]; in forty days the flood took place[^86]. For forty days after the resurrection the Lord conversed with the disciples, persuading them of the truth of his risen body[^87]: where he signified that in this life, in which we are pilgrims away from the Lord[^88], the number forty—as has been said—mystically conveys the memory of the Lord's Body, necessary for us, which we make in the Church until he comes[^89]. To this life, then, because our Lord descended, and the Word was made flesh[^90], that he might be handed over for our offenses and rise for our justification[^91]; Matthew followed the number forty: so that the one generation which there exceeds the number forty either does not hinder it, just as those thirty years do not hinder the perfection of the four hundred; or this very thing too signifies, that the Lord himself, joined to whom they make forty-one, so descended to this life to bear our sins that nevertheless, by his own and singular excellence—by which he is so a man that he is also God—he is found to be an exception from this life. For of him alone is it said what could never be said, nor ever can be, of any holy man, however perfect in wisdom and justice: *The Word was made flesh*[^92].

**23. 33.** But Luke, who ascends through the generations from the Lord's baptism, completes the number seventy-seven, beginning to ascend from our Lord Jesus Christ himself through Joseph, and arriving at God through Adam: that is, because in this number is signified the abolition of all sins, which takes place in Baptism—not because the Lord himself had anything to be forgiven in baptism, but because by his humility he commended what was useful for us. And although that was the baptism of John, yet in it there sensibly appeared the Trinity of Father and Son and Holy Spirit, by which the baptism of Christ himself was consecrated, with which future Christians were to be baptized: the Father, in the voice that came from heaven; the Son, in the man himself, the Mediator; the Holy Spirit, in the dove[^93].

**23. 34.** But why the number seventy-seven should contain all the sins that are forgiven in Baptism, this probable reasoning seems to present itself: that the number ten has the perfection of justice and blessedness, since the sevenfold creature clings to the Trinity of the Creator; whence too the decalogue of the Law was consecrated in ten precepts. But the transgression of the ten is signified by the number eleven: and it is understood that sin is transgression, when a man, desiring something more, exceeds the rule of justice. Whence the Apostle calls avarice the root of all evils[^94]. And to the soul that fornicates away from God it is said, in the person of the same Lord: You hoped that, if you departed from me, you would have something more. Because, therefore, he who sins refers transgression, that is, sin, to himself, since he wishes to rejoice in a certain private good of his own; whence those too are reproved who seek their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ[^95], and love is praised that does not seek its own things[^96]: therefore that same number eleven, by which transgression is signified, is not multiplied tenfold, but sevenfold, and they make seventy-seven. For transgression pertains not to the Trinity of the Creator, but to the creature itself, that is, to man himself, which creature the number seven shows. Three on account of the mind, where there is a certain image of the Trinity of the Creator; for there man was made to the image of God; and four on account of the body. For the four primal elements of which the body consists are most well known. And he to whom they are not known can easily notice that the very body of the world, in which our body is moved through places, has as it were four principal parts, which divine Scripture too constantly mentions: East and West, South and North.

**24. 34.** And because sins are committed either in the mind, as in the will alone, or also in the works of the body, now visibly: therefore the prophet Amos constantly mentions God threatening and saying: *For three and four impieties I will not turn away*[^97], that is, I will not overlook. Three, on account of the nature of the mind; four, on account of the nature of the body: of which two man consists.

**24. 35.** So eleven times seven, as has been said, the transgression of justice referred to sinful man, makes the number seventy-seven; by which all sins are signified to be contained, which are forgiven through Baptism. Whence Luke ascends through seventy-seven generations to God, showing that man is reconciled to God through the abolition of all sins. Hence the Lord himself, to Peter asking how often he ought to forgive his brother: *I say to you*, he says, *not seven times, but seventy times seven*[^98]. And whatever else can be drawn out of these secrets and treasures of God's mysteries by the more diligent and the more worthy. We, however, according to our capacity, as far as the Lord has helped and granted, and given the shortness of the time too, have said what we could. If any of you grasps more, let him knock at the door of him from whom we too receive what we can grasp, what we can say. Above all, hold this: that you be not troubled by the holy Scriptures not yet understood; and when you understand, be not puffed up; but both put off with honor what you do not understand, and hold with love what you do understand.
