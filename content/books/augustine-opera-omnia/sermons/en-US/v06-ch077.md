# Sermon 229/H: A Tractate on the Tuesday of Easter

**1.** The resurrection of the Lord Jesus Christ is the pattern of the Christian faith. For the fact that he was born of a human being as a man at a fixed time, but as God from God without any time; the fact, then, that he was born in mortal flesh, in the likeness of the flesh of sin[^1], that he bore infancy, passed through boyhood, came to youth, and led this on to death—all this was at the service of the resurrection. For he would not rise unless he had died; he would not have died unless he had been born; and thus, in that he was born and died, it served the resurrection. That the Lord Christ was born a man from a human being, many even strangers and impious men believed; though they did not know that he was born of a Virgin, yet that Christ was born a man both friends and enemies believed; that Christ was crucified and died both friends and enemies believed; that he rose again only friends knew. But on account of what? Now the Lord Christ, in that he willed to be born, in that he willed to die, intended to rise; therein he defined our faith for us. For in our kind, that is, in the human kind, we knew two things: to be born, and to die. That he might teach us also what we did not know, he took up what we did know. It was customary in the earthly region, in the condition of our mortality, altogether customary, to be born and to die; so customary that it cannot be in heaven, it does not cease to be on earth. But to rise again, and to live forever, who had known this? This new thing he brought to our region, who came from God. He was made a man for the sake of man, a great compassion; he who made man was made man. It was no great thing for Christ to be what he was, but he willed it a great thing to become what he had made. What is it, to become what he had made? To become a man, who had made man. This is his compassion.

**2.** The whole of what is done in this life, in which men wish to be happy and cannot . . . For it is a good thing that they greatly wish for, but they do not seek what they desire in its own region. For certain things are born in their own regions. And on earth gold is not born everywhere, nor silver everywhere, nor lead everywhere; the crops themselves come forth differently in different places; as each region bears or refuses, one fruit in this place, another in that and that, diverse things in diverse places; and nothing is everywhere, except to be born and to die. And yet this very being born and dying is not of the whole universe, but of this lowest part of the world; for in the heavens there is no being born and dying, from the time when all things were established there. The prince of the angels indeed could fall from there with his companions; but in place of those angels who fell, men are to come there, and will fill the place of those who fell. Because, then, the devil saw that man would ascend to the place from which he himself had fallen, he saw, and he envied; he had fallen, and he cast down. But what is it, that the devil fell? What is it, that he cast down man? He conquered the whole, who did not fall, but descended. Man fell: God descended, and was made man. Where, then, being born and dying abound, that is a region of misery; men wish to be happy in a region of misery, they seek eternity in a region of death. The Lord says to us, the Truth says to us: What you seek is not here, because it is not from here. You seek a good thing, which every man wants; you seek a good thing, because to live is good; but for this we were born, that we might die. Do not look at what you wish, but consider on what condition you came. For this we were born, that we might die. To those who desire life, it is not obtained by those who are to die, and therefore they are the more wretched. For if we were to die, and wished to live, we would not be greatly wretched; but we are greatly wretched for this reason, that we wish to live and are compelled to die. Do you not know that any man cannot stay awake always, but wants also to sleep? It is not against his will that he sleeps; he cannot stay awake always, but because he wants also to sleep. Yet a man cannot exist unless he both stays awake and sleeps. One comes to life, and every man says: I want to live, and no one wants to die; and though no one wants to die, he is forced to die. As much as he can he acts—by eating, drinking, sleeping, providing himself the means to live, sailing, walking, running, taking precautions—he wants to live. Often, freed from many dangers, he lives; but let him hold on, if he can, to his lifetime; let him not arrive at old age. A dangerous day passes, and a man says: I have escaped death. How have you escaped death? Because a dangerous day has passed. Yet a day has been added to you: you have lived one more, you have one less, if I reckon. For if you were going to live, say, thirty years, when this day has passed, it withdraws from the sum of what you are to live, and adds to the weight of what you are to die. And yet they say: Years are added to a man. I say that they are withdrawn: for I attend to that sum which has remained, not to what has passed. Added, why? Because he who has lived fifty years now has fifty-one. In which is he living, or which is he to live? Say he was going to live eighty years; he has of these fifty, thirty remain. He has lived one: he has what he has lived, fifty-one; what he is to live, twenty-nine remain; and from this side one has withdrawn, to that side another has been added; but when it has been added, you do not hold what has withdrawn. Fearing, he lives also another, twenty-eight remained; he lives also a third, twenty-seven remained. By living, that whereby one lives is taken away, and by the leading of life, life is diminished and is not; for there is no way to escape the last day.

**3.** But our Lord Jesus Christ came, and as it were addressed us: Why were you afraid, O men whom I created, and whom I did not abandon? O men, the ruin is from you, the creation from me; O men, why were you afraid to die? Behold, I die, behold, I suffer; behold, do not fear what you feared, because I show you what you may hope for. Behold, thus he did, he showed us the resurrection forever; the Evangelists set it down in their writings, the Apostles preached it throughout the world. For the faith of that resurrection the holy martyrs did not fear to die, and yet they feared to die; for the more would they die, if they feared to die, and by fear of death denied Christ. Yet what is it to deny Christ, but to deny life? How great is the madness, by loving life to deny life! The resurrection of Christ, then, is the very determination of our faith. Hence it is also written, both in the Old and in the New Testament, that we are to do penance and receive the forgiveness of sins, in the man in whom he has defined faith for all, by raising him from the dead[^2]. This is the definition of faith, the resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ. You live, if you live; that is, you live forever, if you have lived well. Do not fear to die ill: fear, rather, to live ill. Wondrous perversity! Every man fears what no one will escape, and does not do what he can do. To keep from dying, you cannot do; to live well, you can. Do what you can, and you will not fear what you cannot. For nothing is so certain to man as death. Begin from the beginning. A man is conceived in the womb: perhaps he is born, perhaps he is not born. Now he is born: perhaps he grows, perhaps he does not grow; perhaps he learns his letters, perhaps he does not learn his letters; perhaps he takes a wife, perhaps he does not; perhaps he will have children, perhaps he will not; perhaps he will have good ones, perhaps bad ones; perhaps he will have a good wife, perhaps a bad one; perhaps he will be rich, perhaps poor; perhaps he will be lowborn, perhaps honored. Among these things, can one say: Perhaps he will die, perhaps he will not die? Therefore every man born has fallen into a sickness from which no one born will escape. He dies from that, as the saying goes. He has dropsy: he must die, no one escapes it. He has elephantiasis: he must die, no one escapes it. He is born: he must die, no one escapes it. Because, then, it is necessary that he die, since not even the life of man is permitted to be at least long-lasting—from infancy to decrepit old age—there is nothing left but to flee to him who died for us, and by rising again gave hope; so that, because in this life, in which we run, we can only die, nor can we make eternal that which we so greatly love, we may flee to him who promised us the eternal. For see what the Lord has promised us, brothers: both eternal and blessed. Certainly this life is wretched: who does not know it, who does not confess it? How much we have, how much we suffer that we do not want, in this life! Quarrels, dissensions, temptations, ignorance of one another's heart, so that sometimes, unknowing, we embrace an enemy, and fear a friend; where there is hunger, where nakedness, where cold, where heat, where weariness, where sicknesses, where jealousies. Certainly this life is wretched. Yet, if this very wretched life could be given us as eternal, who would not be glad? Who would not say: I want to be just as I am, only I do not want to die? If you want to have an evil life, what sort is he who will give both an everlasting and a blessed one? But if you want to come to the everlasting and the blessed, have a good temporal one. It will be good in the work, blessed in the wages. But if you refuse the work, with what face do you seek the wages? If you cannot say to Christ: I have done what you commanded, how will you dare to say: Render what you promised?
