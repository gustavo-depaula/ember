# (Erfurt 2 = Sermon 350/D) (Erfurt 2)

**1.** The fruit of faith is to do good to one in need, for faith is fruitless if a person believes in God in such a way that the works of mercy are neglected. For just as a barren tree is tended in vain, a hard flint rained upon in vain, the dry sand plowed in vain, so it profits a person nothing not to deny what is true if he is unwilling to provide what is good. Rightly is it written that *faith without works is dead in itself*[^1], and that is why such people are compared to demons; for to certain ones who boasted of their faith yet kept clear of good works the apostle James speaks thus: *You believe that God is one; you do well. Even the demons believe and tremble*[^2], so that there seems to be no difference between the fear of the trembling demon and the grace of the faithful person, except that the deeds of the one are evil and those of the other good, since both proceed from the same believing, just as from the same water there sprout both the sharpness of thorns and the fruit of grapes.

**2.** Furthermore, the first mercy of the faithful person is toward himself; this Scripture enjoins when it says, *Have mercy on your own soul, pleasing God*[^3]. From there, growing, it reaches out toward the neighbor, so that the precept may be fulfilled, *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^4]. True mercy, then, which is spent on the neighbor must be spent to this end, that he too may please God: to this he must be called, to this exhorted, to this trained and instructed. For even the very almsgivings that are provided for bodily needs and for this temporal life are to be done with this mind and intention, that those for whom they are done may love the God by whose gift they are done. This too the Lord admonishes when he says, *Let your good works shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^5]. The man of God, therefore, *a vessel sanctified unto honor, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work*[^6], whatever he does in his life, does nothing but a work of mercy, whether toward himself or toward his neighbor. And toward himself he is indeed merciful, as we mentioned above, when he pleases God; and he pleases God when, in the good he does, God is pleased with him, and in the evil he suffers, God is not displeased with him. For the Apostle too, when he had said, speaking of his own good works, *I have labored more than all of them*, at once adds, *yet not I, but the grace of God with me*[^7]; and Job in his trial and tribulation says, *As it has pleased the Lord, so has it been done. Blessed be the name of the Lord*[^8]. And toward his neighbor he is merciful when he gives all the effort he can, that the other too may likewise delight in the sweetness of pleasing God.

**3.** I had proposed to speak of the works of mercy, so that I may now seem to some to have strayed from my purpose and gone off into something else, because I do not say, *Break your bread for the hungry, and bring the needy and homeless into your house; if you see a naked person, clothe him*[^9], and other things of this kind. For these are, as it were, properly thought of and called almsgivings, as though the works of mercy pertained to these alone; they do pertain to them, but they are so far from being the only ones that they are themselves the least of them, unless perhaps people are so foolish as to suppose that those who provided carnal goods to the apostles to be reaped were more merciful than the apostles themselves, who were sowing spiritual goods. Far be it from anyone to think this who listens with understanding to the Apostle saying, *If we have sown spiritual goods for you, is it a great thing if we reap your carnal goods?*[^10] And in sowing spiritual goods, see what kind of giver he shows himself to be where he says, *So, longing for you, we are pleased to share with you not only the gospel of God, but even our own souls*[^11], and in another place he says, *I will most gladly spend and be spent for your souls*[^12]. Now compare the one who breaks his bread for the hungry with the one who shares his soul with the believer; compare the one who spends gold for the temporal life of a needy person with the one who spends himself for the eternal life of a brother. If he is rightly called and held merciful who brings a stranger lacking a roof into his house, ministers fullness at his table and rest in his bed, how much more merciful is the one found to be who, calling back one wandering astray along the ways of iniquity and taking him up, brings him into the house of God, incorporates him into the members of Christ, where he is restored, refreshed by the food of righteousness, made secure by the forgiveness of sins!

**4.** But these works of mercy, by which it is brought about that God is pleased, are by the truthful law of wisdom so preferred to those works by which the necessary support is supplied to bodily need, that for the most part the more prudently the former are done, the more mercifully the latter are withheld. For first, the man who is merciful toward himself, mindful of the divine precept that says, *Have mercy on your own soul, pleasing God*[^13], often fasts in order to please God; and although he is commanded to love his neighbor as himself, he ministers bread to his hungry neighbor while denying it to himself, namely chastising his body and bringing it into subjection, lest perhaps, while preaching to others, he himself be found a castaway. But is this perhaps an act of mercy when done to oneself, yet of cruelty when done to another? What then? When food is often not provided by parents to their hungry little children, whether for reasons of health or for the strictness of discipline or to render some great humiliation of fasting before all, are not the parents then too more merciful toward their children? Unless perhaps the Ninevites, when, believing God and turning to him, they not only feared him as he threatened but also hoped in him as he showed mercy, and observed the fast appointed even by the king's command with such humility that they kept even the cattle from pasture and drink, were being cruel to their little ones, whom that totality embraced which had not even passed over the dumb animals? How many indeed, who through their age did not know what was being done and were demanding food with weeping, were refused it, so that from this too a greater grief might more deeply humble the afflicted minds of their elders, and that, pouring out to the Lord the groan and prayer pressed forth by this pressure, they might obtain salvation not only for themselves who fasted willingly but even for their little ones whom they compelled! Yet this was not only no cruelty of fathers or mothers toward their children, but even a great mercy; a mercy, however, which did not break bread for the hungry but withheld it even from one weeping. Finally, what is more merciful than the Lord God himself, *who makes his sun rise on the good and the evil and sends rain on the just and the unjust*[^14], who nevertheless not only often chastises sinners with the scourge of famine but also exercises the patience of the just by such a test of their proving? Or was he not merciful toward the apostle Paul when he strengthened him with a father's chastisement as he fought in hunger and thirst and cold and nakedness? Or did he not become angry with the people of Israel because, when they had craved the indulgence of the flesh, he heaped their camp with birds? Therefore these temporal and earthly goods are often given for the punishment of those to whom they are given, and often withheld for their benefit; often God takes them away in mercy and grants them in anger, and again often grants them in mercy and takes them away in anger, according to the most wise judgment of him who governs. And so the Apostle came to visit the people of God not only in the gentleness of a meek spirit but also with a rod, mercifully; not only did he, moved by mercy, command the spirit of divination to go out of a certain woman, but he also mercifully handed certain men over to Satan himself, that they might learn not to blaspheme; for to the Apostle himself too the Lord mercifully provided not only food and clothing, by which the weakness of the flesh might be sustained under the strength of the mind, but also mercifully gave the angel of Satan to buffet him, that the strength of the mind might be perfected in the weakness of the flesh.

**5.** Food, then, and drink and clothing, and indeed the whole well-being of the mortal body, is in due season withheld out of mercy; but that mercy by which it is brought about that we please God ought to be perpetual, unceasing, and continuous, varied by no alternation of time. For it cannot be said, just as one may say, "Sometimes it is expedient to take food, sometimes it is not expedient," so also, "Sometimes it is expedient to please God, sometimes it is not expedient"; nor, just as it is said, "Now break bread for the hungry person because this profits him, now compel him to fast because this is healthful for him," can it likewise be rightly said, "Now deal with this person so that he pleases God, for this is expedient for him; now so that he displeases God, for this too is sometimes expedient." This kind of error is surely most absurd and altogether to be detested, and it is not to be believed that any sacrilegious person, however great, could be ensnared by it. That mercy, then, by which the work is spent so that we may please God is itself in a manner the cardinal one: the rest that are shown mercifully are rightly done if they never depart from regard for this.
