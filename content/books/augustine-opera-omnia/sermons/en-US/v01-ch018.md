# Sermon 15/A: A Sermon Delivered at Hippo Diarrhytus in the Basilica of Margarita

On the Tenth Day Before the Kalends of October, on a Thursday

On the Response of Psalm 32:

"Rejoice in the Lord, You Just; Praise Befits the Upright"

**1.** To rejoice in the praise of God and to be in harmony with the praise of God by our living—this is what we are admonished by what we have just sung: *Rejoice in the Lord, you just; praise befits the upright*[^1]. For if it befits the upright, it does not befit the crooked. But those who are upright, they are also the just, to whom it is said that they should rejoice in the Lord: for praise befits them. But who are the crooked, except sinners and the unjust, who cannot rejoice in the Lord, since praise does not befit them? Rightly it is said in another psalm, *But to the sinner God says: Why do you recount my justices and take up my covenant through your mouth?*[^2] Since praise befits the upright, and certainly the justifications of the Lord and the covenant of the Lord are there where the praise of the Lord is, rightly it is also said elsewhere, *Praise is not comely in the mouth of a sinner*[^3]. For where it does not befit, it is not comely; and where it is comely, there it befits.

**2.** But who, properly, are upright men—that each one may recognize whether the praise of God is comely in his mouth—seeking the Scriptures we find in this way. A certain psalm says, *How good is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart!* And it follows, *But my feet were almost moved, because I was envious of the sinners, beholding the peace of sinners*[^4]. This man confesses, not indeed his aversion and fall, but yet his danger. For he does not say that he fell, but that his feet wavered so as to fall. For he says this: *How good is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart! But my feet were almost moved*. When by this word "but" he distinguishes himself from those upright in heart, he confesses that he himself was at one time not upright in heart, and therefore his feet were almost moved. *Good,* then, he says, *is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart*. But to me at one time he did not seem good, because I was not upright in heart. But he did not dare to say, "God did not seem good to me," yet he said this. For when he says, *Good is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart; but my feet were almost moved*, he shows that his feet were moved for this reason, that he did not seem good to him. But whence did God not seem good to him? *A little more and my steps had been poured out*. *A little more*, what is it? *They had almost been poured out*. Whence? *Because I was envious of the sinners, beholding the peace of sinners*. "I attended," he says, "to the sinners not worshiping God, blaspheming God, provoking God. I saw that they abound in peace, abound in happiness. And it seemed to me that God did not judge rightly, granting happiness to his blasphemers." When, then, he beheld this, that is, the happiness of the wicked, he said that his feet wavered so that God did not seem good to him. But because afterward he came to know, as he says in that very psalm, *I undertook to know*, and he added, *This is a labor before me*, why the unjust are happy, *this is a labor before me,* he says, *until I enter into the sanctuary of God, and understand in their last things*[^5], that for those wicked to whom happiness is now given for a time, everlasting punishment is reserved at the last. When he had come to know this, he became upright in heart, and began to praise God in all things, both in the labors of the devout and in the very happinesses of the wicked, attending that God is a just rewarder in the last things, but now gives to some a temporal happiness for whom he reserves everlasting unhappiness at the end, and exercises some devout people in present unhappiness for whom in turn he reserves eternal happiness; and that the turns ought to be changed, as of that rich man who feasted splendidly every day, and that poor man full of sores lying at the rich man's door, desiring to be filled with the crumbs that fell from the rich man's table. But when both had died, the one began to be in punishment in hell, the other rested in the bosom of Abraham. And when it seemed unworthy to the rich man, and he wished a drop of water to be dripped on him from the little finger of Lazarus—desiring in turn a drop from the finger of him from whose table he had desired a crumb—he heard from Abraham the sentence of the upright God: *Son,* he says, *remember that you received good things in your life, but Lazarus evil; but now he rests here, and you are tormented*[^6]. To these last things he directed his gaze, that man entering into the sanctuary of God, to whom God did not seem good because he was envious of the sinners, beholding the peace of sinners. And recognizing the true and just judgment of God, which indeed is now carried out, but hiddenly, to be in the future at the end in manifestation, and as though with his heart corrected from its own crookedness, the rule, as it were, of God's justice being applied by which the twisted heart might be straightened, he broke out into this voice and said, *How good is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart!* Now I understand him good, because I have become upright in heart. But before he did not seem good to me, because my feet wavered: *For I was envious of the sinners, beholding the peace of sinners*[^7].

**3.** If, then, God now seems good to you even when he gives happiness to the wicked, about which you used to murmur against God, you have become upright in heart, praise befits you: for *praise befits the upright*[^8]. But if you are crooked, praise does not befit you. Why does it not befit you? The praise by which you praise God will not be persevering. For you praise God when it is well with you; you blaspheme God when it is ill with you. For then he pleases you when he gives happiness; but he displeases you when he chastises. You are not upright in heart, you will not be able to say that song of another psalm, *I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise always in my mouth*[^9]. For how *always*, if you praise then when it is well with you, but not also when it is ill with you? Because even that which is said to be ill with you is well, if you understand the Father to be a chastiser. A senseless boy generally loves the master who flatters, hates the one who strikes. But the understanding boy understands the master to be good, both when he flatters and when he strikes. For he flatters lest the boy fail, he strikes lest he perish. And so, when anyone has such a heart, that is, an upright one, so that God does not displease him, and when he does what perhaps seems to oppose him for a time, he securely praises God, because he will always praise, and truly praise befits him, and truly he faithfully sings, *I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise always in my mouth*[^10]. *But he scourges every son whom he receives*[^11]. What, then, do you choose: to be scourged and received, or not to be touched and not received? See what kind of son you are. If you desire the paternal inheritance, do not refuse the scourge. If you reject the scourge, renounce the inheritance. For to what does he instruct you, except that he may give you the inheritance? That you might be the heir of your father, did he not reprove you, did he not rebuke you, did he not chastise you, did he not beat you? For what purpose this? That you might succeed him in a house someday to fall and an estate someday to pass away, and gold that will not remain in this world longer than you yourself who possess the gold. For either, living, you will lose what you have, or, dying, you will leave it. For the sake of that temporal inheritance you endured the scourges of your father, and do you murmur against God instructing you, that he may give you the kingdom of heaven?

**4.** When, then, you are such that God pleases you, and chastising he pleases you—for either there is in you something to be corrected by the scourge, or your very uprightness is proved in the scourge—when, then, you are such, praise. For you praise securely. Why do you praise securely? Because you praise becomingly, because you praise perseveringly. For I do not fear lest you praise now and after a little while blaspheme. I do not fear lest, healthy, you praise, and, sick, you blaspheme. I do not fear lest from the mouth of the healthy the praise of God proceed, and from the tongue of the sick an astrologer or diviner be sought, an enchanter and a binder-on of diabolical remedies. I do not fear, because you have now understood that God is good even when he chastises, and you know that he who strikes a son knows also when to spare. It will befit you to praise, because you will always perseveringly praise, and the praise of God will always be in your mouth. You gladly receive the flattering Father, you gladly receive also the scourging Father. You do not run to him when he flatters, and flee from him when he scourges. For if you do this, you will be like the boy who, when he fled from his scourging father, ran into a flattering slave-dealer, and thought him good, but his own father evil, and preferred the deceits of flatteries to the truth of scourges; but by preferring he lost the inheritance, ran into bondage. Change your counsel, and make your heart upright. For God is not changed because he scourges you, but you are changeable. He does something by the change, that you, changed for the better, may receive the inheritance. For if he leaves you and neglects you, when he seems good to you, then he is greatly angry. Let your Charity attend to what the Scripture of God says in another psalm. *The sinner has provoked the Lord,* it says. Why has he provoked? See where it cried out about the provoked God. Certainly the sinner has provoked the Lord to greater anger: *In the greatness,* it says, *of his anger he will not seek it out*[^12].

**5.** Holy Job, on the contrary, blessing the Lord at all times, in whose mouth was always his praise, when he was rich, blessed the Lord in his riches, doing with them all the good works that are enumerated in his book: breaking bread to the hungry, clothing the naked, taking in the stranger, and the rest, which the rich alone receive from their riches, which the rich alone gain. For they do not gain, or presume upon, the gains which they leave to their children. For they do not know who, after their death, will possess their labors. And Scripture called this vanity: *But truly every living man is altogether vanity; he lays up treasure and does not know for whom he gathers it*[^13]. Therefore the whole gain that is made from riches is the treasure of the kingdom of heaven, whence the Lord gave counsel, not that you should lose, but that you should change the place of your gold. For he does not say to you, "When you give, you lose," but, "You hold it badly on earth; in heaven I will keep it for you. Why do you fear to lose? You put it in heaven, with Christ as keeper. If the place troubles you, it will be heaven; if the keeper, it will be Christ. Why do you fear to lose?" Therefore, when Job did these things with such riches, he certainly was working, and in his very works God was praised, and he blessed the Lord in the things he had received. For truly, brothers, riches are not accused. Do you think, when you see wicked rich men, that riches are evil? Riches are not evil, but they are; riches, however, are gifts of God. Give them to a just man, and see what good he does with them. Is wine therefore evil, because someone gets drunk? Give it to a sober man using it well, and see the gift of God. So give gold to an avaricious man. That he may make more of what he has, he seeks to commit all crimes. Give gold to a just man, and see how he distributes, how he shares, how he relieves the necessities of men, by whatever means he can. Therefore not riches, but the one using riches badly, is himself evil. When, then, Job used them well, as Abraham used them well... For certainly, brothers, that beggar full of sores lying before the rich man's door was so destitute that the dogs licked his sores. So certainly we read, so it is written. And yet whither was he carried? *Into the bosom of Abraham*[^14]. Review the Scriptures. See whether Abraham was poor here. You will find that he had here much gold, much silver, much cattle, many slaves and estates. Therefore a poor man is lifted into the bosom of a rich man. If poverty had made his merit for him, Abraham himself would not have gone before him to rest and received him as a successor. But because that was in the poor Lazarus which was in the rich Abraham, that is, humility, devotion, the worship of God, the observance of God, neither did riches hurt the one nor poverty the other, but the merit of the one was devotion. Hence in that rich man who changed the turns badly, his riches are not reproved but his mind. *For he was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day*[^15], and suffered a beggar full of sores to lie before his door and, proudly despising him, did not satisfy his want. What do you think the rich man, disdaining the beggar, said? "Why does this man lie here?" But rightly that tongue which despised the poor man desired a drop from his finger.

**6.** When, then, holy Job, as I said, had many riches, he praised God, tempted that he might be proved, proved that he might be shown. For he lay hidden not only from men, but even from the devil himself, who inspects more diligently than any man. He lay hidden, then, from him, who Job was, but he did not lie hidden from the Lord. The Prover permitted the tempter—the Prover, however, not for his own sake but for ours, that he might make manifest to us what we ought to imitate. For he did not wish to show Job to the devil himself, but through the devil to us, so that, the devil being conquered, we might have what to imitate. Therefore, all things lost, not gradually but suddenly, he said, *The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away. As it pleased the Lord, so it has been done. Blessed be the name of the Lord*[^16]. *As it pleased the Lord, so it has been done.* That cannot be crooked which pleased the upright. That cannot be evil which pleased the good. For *good is the God of Israel to those who are upright in heart*[^17]. Job was upright in heart, therefore praise befitted him. *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away. As it pleased the Lord, so it has been done.* He confessed, praising, *Blessed be the name of the Lord. The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away.* There was abundance, now there is want. With me things are now changed, he is not changed. I am at one time rich, at one time poor; he always rich, always upright, always Father. *Blessed be the name of the Lord.* For the name of the Lord was not blessed in my abundance, and to be cursed in my want. Far be this from me. This Job said, enriched with inner riches. He had lost his whole house, but his breast was full. He had lost house, gold. He had filled his breast. God, in place of all these things he had given, was himself present. *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away.* See how he understands the most preeminent power. Lest perhaps you wish to worship God for the kingdom of heaven, O Christian, and fear the devil for earthly goods, the whole and highest power is entirely with him. The devil wished only to harm; unless permitted, he could not. With him, then, is the power. Otherwise, if as much were permitted the devil as he wishes, which of the Christians would remain? Which worshiper of God would be left on earth? Do you not see his temples falling, his images broken, his priests turned to God? Do you not think that the devil grieves and is tormented at this? If, then, in proportion to his grief he had also the power, what Church would remain on earth? Accordingly, when holy Job, through the snares of the devil, had lost all things, he gives him no power. When he praises God he does not say, "The Lord gave, the devil has taken away," but he says, "The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away." Let the devil arrogate nothing to himself. Both that I was rich was God's, and that I am poor is God's." And if he was permitted to tempt, he was not permitted to choke; but he would choke, not by holding and crushing the throat, but by shutting off the spirit. If perhaps Job, suffering the straits of tribulation, sent forth one blasphemous word from his mouth, then, choked, he would breathe his last, the spirit of life being shut off from him. This he did neither in that sudden poverty nor in that last wound.

**7.** For when it had been too little for the devil to have taken away all that Job possessed, he took away also the children for whom he possessed it, only his wife being left. For her alone he did not take away, and this surely knowing what he was doing. He knew Adam had been deceived through Eve. He kept her, then, rather as his own helper than as a consoler of her husband. When, then, he had taken away all those things and left one through whom he might tempt again, it was too little for him. He seeks to take away also the health of his body. And this too he was permitted to take away, so that even placed in that wound Job might praise God, upright in heart, varying in nothing, he whom praise befitted. She who was left for this came to him, and persuaded—rather, urged—blasphemy. For she said, "How many evils we suffer! *Say something against God, and die.*" First Eve, as though invited to life by the devil, was seduced, and found death. For the devil said, You shall not die the death. And thinking she would have life, she found death, because she did contrary to the command of God, and persuaded her husband contrary to the command of God. Now the contrary: *Say something against God, and die.* Let it suffice Eve that she persuaded to act contrary to the command of God. She is still Eve, he is now not Adam. She filled by the devil, he corrected by the example. Job is better on the dunghill than Adam in paradise. That you may know how great a thing it is to have an upright heart, how did Job lay the devil low in that destitution, in that wound? For he answered the woman and said, *You have spoken like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, shall we not endure evil things?*[^18] He blessed the Lord at all times, his praise was always in his mouth. For he was upright in heart, therefore praise befitted him. If, then, you wish that praise befit you too, be upright in heart. But if you wish to be upright in heart, let God not displease you in anything. For either you see the cause why he does what he does, and, beholding the cause, you do not reprove. Or, if the cause lies hidden from you, know that he does it who can in nothing be displeasing.

**8.** Someone, I know not who, demolishes his house; he is reproved. If you knew the cause, perhaps you would not reprove the man doing it. Certainly we are now in this basilica, and it is narrow and has something small about it. And it pleased the master that another be made, and this is to be destroyed. Whoever perhaps sees those demolishing it, when it begins to be demolished, says, "Was there no prayer here? Was the name of God not invoked here? What do these people suffer that they demolish it?" The work displeases, because the counsel is hidden. So, then, God too does something. Why he does it, either you know and praise, or you do not know and believe, if you are upright in heart. For he himself is upright in heart who both in the very causes that he knows praises God, and in those that he does not know does not attribute folly to God. Unjustly and foolishly are you reproved, O man, governor of your own house, by one ignorant of the causes and ignorant of your counsels. And do you dare to reprove the governor of the whole world, and the creator of heaven and earth, because the wind blew and the vines withered, or a cloud arose and poured down hail? Do not reprove. He knows all his works, both to govern and to number them. You certainly could not fabricate heaven and earth, and yet, if it were permitted you, you would say to God, "Oh, if I governed, I would not do what you do." For when something done by God displeases you, would you not wish yourself to be the governor? Be ashamed. See whom you wish to succeed: a mortal the Immortal, a man God. It is better for you to yield to him than to seek to succeed him. Yield to God, because he is God. And if perhaps he has done something against your will, perhaps it is not against your advantage. How many things physicians do against the will of the sick, and yet they do not do them against their health! And a physician sometimes errs, God never. If, then, you commit yourself to a physician who sometimes errs, you commit yourself to human counsel. Not that he may apply a poultice which is mild, or a plaster from which he causes you no pain, but generally that he may burn, that he may cut, that he may take away a member belonging to you and born with you, you commit yourself to him. You do not say, "Here perhaps he errs, and I will have one less finger." You permit him to take away one finger, lest perhaps from it the whole body rot. And do you not permit God to cut, that he may take away something of your fruits, in case perhaps in that very restraint you may have discipline?

**9.** Therefore, brothers, be upright in heart, that is, that God may displease you in nothing. I do not say that you should not ask. Ask as much as you can in affliction. He withholds the rain, he is to be asked. But both if it rains he is to be praised, and if it does not rain he is to be praised, yet he is to be asked. For we do not say that you should not ask. For sometimes he is bent and grants to those who ask, and he wishes to grant only to those who ask. Is God arrogant, that he grants only to one who asks? But then the little soul will advance toward the greatness of God, if he comes to its aid in tribulation, so that to us, asking and in tribulation, he may give consolation. He wishes to grow sweet to us for our good, not his own. For see what an evil it is, that the world has grown sweet to you, and God who made the world is bitter. Must you not be changed? Must you not be corrected, that you may have an upright heart? Rather let the world be bitter, and God be sweet. So let the Lord our God mix bitternesses into this world, let him mix them indeed. Here to flow, to overflow, to abound with delights, to forget God, delights one. If one has anything more of money, one wishes to trifle with it, does not wish to make anything useful of it, to procure anything heavenly. One wishes to lose both the money and oneself and the others on whom one spends the money. Do you not wish, then, that God cut away the superfluous, lest from the rotten part the whole rot? He knows, then, what he does. Let us leave it to him. Only let us give ourselves to be cured, let us not offer counsel to the physician. Turned to the Lord.
