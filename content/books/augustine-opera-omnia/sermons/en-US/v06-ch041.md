# Sermon 218: On the Passion of the Lord, on Good Friday

**1.** The passion of him by whose blood our sins were blotted out is solemnly read, solemnly celebrated; that by the yearly devotion our memory may be more joyfully renewed, and by the very thronging of the peoples our faith may be more clearly illuminated. The solemnity, then, demands of us that we render to you, concerning the Lord's passion, such a sermon as he himself grants. And indeed, for our salvation and for the usefulness of getting through this life, in the things he suffered from his enemies our Lord deigned to offer us an example of patience; that, for the sake of the gospel's dignity, if he himself should will it, we may not refuse to suffer anything of the kind. Yet because not even in that mortal flesh did he suffer anything by necessity, but all by will; it is rightly believed that he also willed to signify something in each and every deed which was done and recorded concerning his passion.

**2.** And first, that, handed over to be crucified, he himself carried his own cross[^1]; he gave a sign of self-restraint, and showed, going before, what he ought to do who wishes to follow him. This too he admonished by a word, when he says: *He who loves me, let him take up his cross, and follow me*[^2]. For he in a certain manner takes up his cross, who governs his own mortality.

**3.** That he was crucified in the place of Calvary[^3]; he signified in his passion the forgiveness of all sins, of which it is said in the Psalm: *My iniquities are multiplied above the hairs of my head*[^4].

**4.** That two were crucified with him, one on each side[^5]; he showed that some would suffer on his right, others on his left: those on the right, of whom it is said: *Blessed are they who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness*[^6]; but those on the left, of whom it is said: *And if I hand over my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me nothing*[^7].

**5.** That a title was set above his cross, on which was written: *King of the Jews*[^8], that showed that not even by killing him could they bring it about that they should not have him as king, who, by his most evident and most eminent power, will requite them according to their works. Whence it is sung in the Psalm: *But I am set as king by him over Zion, his holy mountain*[^9].

**6.** That the title was written in three languages, Hebrew, Greek, and Latin[^10]; it was declared that he would reign not only over the Jews, but also over the Gentiles. Accordingly, in the same psalm, when he had said: *But I am set as king over Zion, his holy mountain*, where, namely, he reigned in the Hebrew tongue; he at once subjoins, as it were Greek and Latin: *The Lord*, he says, *said to me: You are my Son, today I have begotten you: ask of me, and I will give you the nations for your inheritance, and your possession the ends of the earth*[^11]. Not because Greek and Latin are the only languages of the nations: but because they especially excel, Greek, on account of the study of letters; Latin, on account of the skill of the Romans. Although in those three languages the totality of all the nations to be subjugated to Christ was shown: yet it was not written there *King* of the Gentiles, but only *of the Jews*; that the origin of the seed might be commended in the propriety of the name. For *the Law will go forth from Zion*, it is said, *and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem*[^12]. For who are those who say in the Psalm: *He has subjected peoples to us, and the nations under our feet*[^13]; except those of whom the Apostle says: *For if the Gentiles have shared in their spiritual things, they ought to minister to them also in carnal things*[^14]?

**7.** That the chief men of the Jews suggested to Pilate that he should not write absolutely that he is king of the Jews, but that he himself said he was king of the Jews[^15]; with those branches broken, Pilate so figured the wild olive to be grafted in[^16]: because he was a man of the Gentiles, writing the confession of the Gentiles, of whom the Lord himself rightly said: *The kingdom will be taken from you, and given to a nation doing righteousness*[^17]. Nor for that reason, however, is he not the king of the Jews. For the root bears the wild olive, not the wild olive the root[^18]. And although those branches were broken off through unbelief; not for that reason did God reject his people, whom he foreknew. *For I too*, he says, *am an Israelite*[^19]. And although the sons of the kingdom, who were unwilling that the Son of God should reign over them, go into the outer darkness; yet many will come from the East and the West, and will recline, not with Plato and Cicero, but with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven[^20]. Pilate indeed wrote king of the Jews, not king of the Greeks or Latins; although he would reign over the Gentiles. And what he wrote, he wrote, nor did he change it at the suggestion of the unbelieving[^21]: to whom so long before it had been foretold in the Psalms: *Do not corrupt the inscription of the title*[^22]. All nations believe in the king of the Jews: over all nations he reigns, but yet *King of the Jews*. So great was the power of that root, that it could change into itself the grafted-in wild olive, but the wild olive could not take away the name of the olive.

**8.** That the soldiers took away his garments, divided into four parts[^23]; they signified that his sacraments would spread through the four parts of the world.

**9.** That for the one seamless tunic, woven from above, they cast lots rather than divided it[^24]; it was sufficiently shown that the visible sacraments, although they too are the garments of Christ, yet any persons whatever can have these, whether good or evil; but the most sincere faith, which through love works the integrity of unity (*because the love of God is poured out in our hearts, through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^25]), does not pertain to just anyone, but is given, as it were by lot, by the hidden grace of God. Whence to Simon, who had baptism and did not have this, it was said by Peter: *You have no lot nor part in this faith*[^26].

**10.** That on the cross he commended his recognized mother to the beloved disciple[^27]; he fittingly showed human affection then, when as man he was dying. That hour had not yet come, when, about to turn water into wine, he had said to the same mother: *What is it to me and to you, woman? My hour has not yet come*[^28]. For he had not taken from Mary what he had in his divinity, as he had taken from Mary what hung on the cross.

**11.** That he said: *I thirst*[^29]; he sought faith from his own: but because *he came into his own, and his own did not receive him*[^30]; for the sweetness of faith, they gave the vinegar of faithlessness, and this in a sponge. Truly to be compared to a sponge, not solid, but swollen; not opened by the straight approach of confession, but full of holes by the tortuous windings of treacheries. Indeed, that drink had also hyssop[^31], which, a humble herb, is said to cling with a most strong root to a rock. For there were in that people some for whom this crime was kept, to humble the soul by repenting, after casting it away. He himself knew them, who received the hyssop with the vinegar. For he prayed even for them, as another evangelist witnesses, when, hanging on the cross, he said: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^32].

**12.** That he said: *It is finished, and gave up the Spirit*[^33]; he showed not the necessity, but the power of his own death, waiting until all that had been prophesied of him were fulfilled: because this too was written: *And in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink*[^34]: as though having the power to lay down his soul, as he himself also witnessed[^35]. And he gave up the Spirit by humility, that is, with his head bowed; about to take it back by the resurrection, with his head raised up. That this death and bowing of the head were of great power, that patriarch Jacob, foretelling it in the blessing of Judah: *You went up, lying down, you slept like a lion*[^36]: by the going up signifying death, by the lion signifying power.

**13.** But now that the legs of those two were broken, but not his, because he had died; why this was done, the Gospel itself declared. For it behooved that by this sign too he should show that in the prophecy foretold concerning him the Passover of the Jews had been commended, where this was commanded, that they should not break the bones of the sheep[^37].

**14.** That his side, struck by a lance, poured forth blood and water onto the earth[^38]; without doubt these are the sacraments by which the Church is formed; as Eve was made from the side of the sleeping Adam[^39], who was a figure of the one to come.

**15.** That Joseph and Nicodemus bury him[^40]; as some have interpreted the names, Joseph is interpreted "Increased"; but Nicodemus, because it is a Greek name, is more widely known to be composed of "victory" and "people"; because νῖκος is victory, δῆμος people. Who, then, is the one increased by dying, except he who said: *A grain of wheat, if it does not die, remains alone; but if it dies, it is multiplied*[^41]? And who too, by dying, conquered the persecuting people, except he who, by rising, will judge them?
