# Sermon 61: On the Words of the Gospel of Matthew 7:7-11: "Ask, and It Will Be Given to You," and What Follows; Exhorting to Almsgiving

**1. 1.** In the reading of the holy Gospel the Lord exhorted us to pray. *Ask*, he says, *and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks, receives; and the seeker finds, and to the one who knocks it will be opened. Or what man is there among you, of whom his son asks bread, will he hand him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will he hand him a serpent? Or when he asks for an egg, will he hand him a scorpion? If you, then*, he says, *being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children; how much more will your Father, who is in heaven, give good things to those who ask him?*[^1] *Being*, he says, *evil, you know how to give good gifts to your children.* A wondrous thing, brothers: we are evil, and we have a good Father. What is more evident? We have heard our name: *Being*, he says, *evil, you know how to give good gifts to your children.* And those whom he called evil, see what kind of Father he shows them: *How much more your Father?* Father of whom? Surely of the evil. And what kind of Father? *No one is good, except God alone*[^2].

**2. 2.** So, brothers, for this reason, being evil, we have a good Father, that we may not always remain evil. No evil man does good. If no evil man does good, how does an evil man make himself good? He who is always good makes good out of the evil. *Heal me, Lord*, he says, *and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved*[^3]. What do the vain say to me vainly: "You make yourself well, if you wish"? *Heal me, Lord, and I shall be healed.* We were made good by the Good: for God made man upright[^4]; but by our own choice we were made evil. We could be made evil from good, and we shall be able to be made good from evil. But he who is always good himself makes good out of evil: because man himself by his own will could not heal himself. You do not seek a physician to wound yourself: but when you have wounded yourself, you seek one to heal you. Good things, then, according to time, temporal, bodily, carnal goods we know how to give our children, even though we are evil. For these too are good, who would doubt? A fish, an egg, bread, fruit, grain, this light, this air which we breathe, these are good things: the very riches in which men exalt themselves, and do not acknowledge other men, their equals; in which, I say, men exalt themselves, loving more a gleaming garment than thinking of their common skin; the very riches, then, are good: but all these good things which I have mentioned can be had by the good and the evil; and although they are good, yet they cannot make good.

**3. 3.** There is, then, a good that makes good: and there is a good whence you may do good. The good that makes good is God. For no one makes a man good, except he who is always good. So, that you may be good, call upon God. But another good is that whence you may do good: that is, whatever you have. There is gold, there is silver; it is good, not because it makes you good, but whence you may do good. You have gold, you have silver; and you crave gold and you crave silver. And you have, and you crave; and you are full, and you thirst. It is a disease, and not opulence. There are men in a disease, full of fluid, and they always thirst. They are full of fluid, and they thirst for fluid. How, then, do you delight in opulence, you who have the dropsy of craving? You have gold, then, it is good: you have, not whence you may be good, but whence you may do good. What good, you say, am I going to do with the gold? Have you not heard the Psalm? *He dispersed, he gave to the poor; his justice abides forever and ever*[^5]. This is the good, this is the good whence you are good, justice. If you have the good whence you may be good, do good with the good whence you are not good. You have money, dispense it. By dispensing money, you increase justice. *For he dispersed*, he distributed, *he gave to the poor; his justice abides forever and ever.* See what is diminished, and what is increased. The money is diminished, the justice is increased. That is diminished which you were going to leave behind, that is diminished which you were going to leave; that is increased which you will possess forever.

**4. 4.** I give counsel of gains, learn to trade. For you praise the merchant who sells lead and acquires gold; and do you not praise the merchant who dispenses money and acquires justice? But I, you say, do not dispense money, because I do not have justice. Let him dispense money who has justice: I do not have justice, let me have at least money. So because you do not have justice, for this reason you are unwilling to dispense money? Rather dispense money, that you may have justice. For from whom will you have justice, except from God, the fountain of justice? So if you wish to have justice, be a beggar of God, who a little while ago, from the Gospel, was admonishing you to ask, to seek, to knock. He knew his beggar, and behold, the householder and great rich man—rich, that is, in spiritual and eternal riches—exhorts you, and says to you: "Ask, seek, knock. *He who asks receives: the seeker finds: to the one who knocks it will be opened*[^6]." He exhorts you to ask: will he deny what you ask?

**4. 5.** Attend to a likeness or comparison from the contrary, as concerning that unjust judge, the Lord exhorting us to prayer, when he says: *There was a certain judge in a city, who neither feared God nor reverenced man. A certain widow kept appealing to him daily, and saying, Avenge me. He was unwilling for a time*: she did not cease to appeal; and she accomplished by wearying him what he was unwilling to do as a benefit[^7]. For thus from the contrary he admonished us to ask.

**5. 6.** *He came*, he says, *to his friend*, to whom a guest had come; *and began to knock, and to say: A guest has come to me, lend me three loaves. That one answered: Now I am at rest, and my servants are at rest with me*[^8]. The other does not cease, stands by, presses on, knocks; and as a friend from a friend he begs. And what does he say: *I tell you, that he rises, and not on account of his friendship, but on account of his importunity, gives him as many as he wishes*[^9]. *Not on account of friendship*, although he is a friend, *but on account of importunity.* What is, *on account of importunity?* Because he did not cease to knock: because, even when it was refused, he did not turn away. The one who was unwilling to give did what was asked, because the other did not fail in asking. How much more will the good one give, who exhorts us to ask, whom it displeases if we do not ask? But when sometimes he gives more slowly, he commends his gifts, he does not deny them. Things long desired are more sweetly obtained: but things quickly given grow cheap. Ask, seek, press on. By asking and seeking you grow, that you may receive. God keeps for you what he does not wish to give quickly; that you too may learn to desire great things greatly. Hence *one must always pray, and not faint*[^10].

**6. 7.** If, then, my brothers, God has made us his beggars, by admonishing us and exhorting and commanding us to ask, seek, knock; let us too attend to those who ask of us. We ask. Of whom do we ask? who are we who ask? what do we ask? Of whom, or who, or what do we ask? We ask of the good God: we ask, evil men: but we ask for justice, whence we may be good. So this we ask, that we may have it forever: with which, when we have been filled, we may further be in need of nothing. But that we may be filled, let us hunger and thirst; by hungering and thirsting let us ask, seek, knock. *For blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice*[^11]. Why blessed? They hunger and thirst, and they are blessed? Is want ever sometimes blessed? Not for this reason are they blessed, because they hunger and thirst; but *because they shall be filled*. There will be the blessedness, in fullness, not in hunger. But let hunger precede the fullness, lest disdain not reach the bread.

**7. 8.** So we have said of whom we ask, who we are who ask, what we ask. But it is asked of us too. For we are beggars of God: that he may acknowledge his beggars, let us too acknowledge ours. But there too let us consider, when it is asked of us, who they are who ask, of whom they ask, what they ask. Who ask? Men. Of whom do they ask? Of men. Who ask? Mortals. Of whom do they ask? Of mortals. Who ask? The frail. Of whom do they ask? Of the frail. Who ask? The wretched. Of whom do they ask? Of the wretched. Except for the substance of means, those who ask are such as those of whom they ask. With what face do you ask of your Lord, you who do not acknowledge your equal? I am not, you say, such a one: far be it from me to be such. Inflated, clad in silk, he says these things of one in rags. But I question naked men. I do not question what you are in your clothes, but what you were when you were born. Both naked, both weak, beginning a wretched life, therefore both weeping.

**8. 9.** Behold, recall, rich man, your beginnings: see whether you brought anything here. You have now come, and found so much. Tell me, I beg you, what did you bring? Tell what you brought. Or if you blush to tell, hear the Apostle: *We brought nothing into this world*[^12]. *Nothing*, he says, *we brought into this world.* But perhaps, because you brought nothing, and here you found much, are you going to take something away from here with you? And perhaps this too, from love of riches, you tremble to confess: hear this too. Let the Apostle say this too, who does not flatter you. *We brought nothing into this world*, surely when we were born; *but neither can we take anything away*[^13], surely when we go out of the world. You brought nothing, you will take nothing away from here: why do you inflate yourself against the poor man? When infants are born, let the parents, servants, clients withdraw from the midst; let the crowds of attendants withdraw from the midst, and let the rich babies be recognized weeping. Let the rich and the poor give birth together, let the rich woman and the poor woman give birth together: let them not attend to what they bring forth, let them withdraw a little, return and recognize. Behold, rich man, you brought nothing into this world: but neither can you take anything away from here. What I said about the newly born, this I say about the dead. Surely, when by some chance old tombs are broken open, let the bones of the rich man be recognized. So, rich man, hear the Apostle: *We brought nothing into this world.* Acknowledge it, it is true. *But neither can we take anything away.* Acknowledge that this too is true.

**9. 10.** What, then, follows: *Having food and clothing, let us be content with these. For those who wish to become rich, fall into temptation and many harmful desires, which plunge man into ruin and perdition. For avarice is the root of all evils: which some pursuing, have wandered from the faith*[^14]. Attend to what they left behind. You grieve that they left this behind: see into what they entangled themselves. Hear: *They have wandered from the faith, and entangled themselves in many sorrows*[^15]. But who? *Those who wish to become rich.* It is one thing to be rich; another, to wish to become rich. He is rich who was born of the rich: not because he wished it, is he rich, but because many left him inheritances. I see the means, I do not inquire into the pleasures. Here craving is accused, not gold, not silver, not riches, but craving. For those who do not wish to become rich, or who do not care, or do not burn with cravings, are not kindled by the torches of avarice, but are rich—let them hear the Apostle. Today it was read: *Charge the rich of this world*[^16]. *Charge.* What? *Charge* above all, *not to be proud-minded.* For there is nothing that riches so beget as pride. Every apple, every grain, every wheat, every wood has its own worm. And the worm of the apple is one, of the pear another, of the bean another, of the wheat another. The worm of riches is pride.

**10. 11.** So *charge the rich of this world not to be proud-minded.* He excluded the vice, let him teach the use. *Not to be proud-minded.* But whence not to be proud-minded? From what follows: *Nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*[^17]. Those who do not hope in the uncertainty of riches are not proud-minded. If they are not high-minded, let them fear. If they fear, they are not high-minded. How many rich yesterday, today poor? How many go to sleep rich, and, when robbers come and take everything away, wake poor? So, *not to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy*[^18]: temporal and eternal things. But rather the eternal to enjoy; the temporal to use. The temporal, as to travelers; the eternal, as to dwellers. The temporal, whence we may do good; the eternal, whence we may be made good. So let the rich do this: let them not be proud-minded, nor hope in the uncertainty of riches, *but in the living God, who furnishes us all things abundantly to enjoy*: let them do this. But from what they have, what should they do? Hear what: *Let them be rich in good works, let them give readily.* For they have whence. Why do they not do it? Poverty is the difficulty. *Let them give readily*, they have whence. *Let them share*, that is, acknowledge their fellow mortals, their equals. *Let them share, let them lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future*[^19]. For not because I say, he says: *Let them give readily, share*, do I wish to strip them, to make them naked, to leave them empty. I teach gain, when I show: *Let them lay up for themselves.* For I do not wish them to remain poor. *Let them lay up for themselves.* I do not say, that they may lose; but I show whither they may migrate it. *Let them lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^20]. This, then, is the false life: *let them lay hold of the true life. Vanity* indeed *of vanities, and all is vanity. What such great abundance has a man in all his labor, by which he labors under the sun?*[^21] The true life, then, must be laid hold of, our means must be migrated to the place of the true life; that we may find there what we give here. He changes them, who changes us too.

**11. 12.** So give to the poor, my brothers. *Having food and clothing, let us be content with these*[^22]. The rich man has nothing of his riches except what the poor man asks of him, food and clothing. Of all you have, what more do you have? You received food, you received necessary clothing. Necessary, I say, not empty, not superfluous. What more do you take of your riches? Tell me. Surely all your things will be superfluous. What are your superfluities, let them be the necessities of the poor. But I, you say, take precious feasts, I feed on precious foods. What does the poor man? On cheap ones. The poor man feeds on cheap foods; I, he says, on precious ones. I ask you, when both of you have been filled: precious food enters into you; what happens when it has entered? If we had glass windows in our belly, would we not blush at all the precious foods with which you have been filled? The poor man hungers, the rich man hungers: the poor man seeks to be filled, the rich man seeks to be filled. The poor man is filled with cheap foods, the rich man is filled with precious foods. The fullness is equal: the possession is one, to which both wish to come; but the one by a short cut, the other by a roundabout way. But precious things, you say, are more savory to me. With difficulty are you, the fastidious, filled. You do not know how savory is what hunger kindles. Nor did I say this so as to compel the rich to feed on the feasts and foods of the poor. Let the rich use the custom of their weakness: but let them grieve that they cannot do otherwise. For they could do better, if they could do otherwise. If, then, the poor man is not exalted by his beggary, why are you exalted by your weakness? Use choice, precious foods, because you are so accustomed, because you cannot otherwise; because if you change the custom, you fall ill. It is granted you: use the superfluous, give the poor the necessary; use precious things, give the poor cheap things. He awaits from you, you await from God: he awaits the hand that was made with him, you await the hand that made you. But he made not only you, but also the poor man with you. He gave you both one road, this life; you have found yourselves companions, you walk one road: he carries nothing, you are too heavily laden: he carries nothing with him, you carry with you more than is needed. You are laden: give him of what you have; and you both feed him and lessen your burden.

**12. 13.** So give to the poor: I ask, I admonish, I charge, I command. Whatever you wish, give to the poor. For I will not hide from your love why I had need to bring forth this sermon to you. Ever since we have been here, going to church and returning, the poor appeal to us, and say that we should tell you to give them something. They have admonished us to speak to you: and when they see that they receive nothing from you, they think we labor among you in vain. They expect something even from us. We give as much as we have, we give as we can: but are we sufficient to fill their need? Because, then, we are not sufficient to fill their need, we are at least their envoys to you. You have heard, you have praised: thanks be to God. You received the seed, you returned words. These praises of yours rather weigh us down, and put us in peril: we bear them, and tremble amid them. Yet, my brothers, these praises of yours are the leaves of the trees: the fruit is sought.
