# Sermon 87: Preached on the Lord's Day, on What Is Written in the Gospel, Matthew 20:1-16: "the Kingdom of Heaven Is Like a Man, a Head of a Household, Who Sent Laborers Into His Vineyard."

**1. 1.** From the holy Gospel you have heard a comparison harmonizing with the present season, about those who work in the vineyard. For now is the time of the bodily vintage. But there is also a spiritual vintage, where God rejoices at the fruit of his vineyard. For we cultivate God, and God cultivates us. But we do not cultivate God so as to make him better by our cultivating. For we cultivate him by adoring, not by plowing. But he cultivates us as a farmer cultivates a field. In that he cultivates us, he renders us better; for the farmer too, by cultivating the field, makes it better. And he seeks the very fruit in us, that we may cultivate him. His cultivation upon us is that he does not cease, by his word, to root out the bad seeds from our hearts, to open our heart as with the plow of his discourse, to plant the seeds of his precepts, to await the fruit of godliness. For when we have so received this cultivation into our heart that we cultivate him well, we do not turn out to be ungrateful to our farmer, but render fruit in which he may rejoice. And our fruit does not make him richer, but us more blessed.

**1. 2.** Behold, hear, that, as I said, God cultivates us. For that we cultivate God, there is no need that it be proved to you. For every man has this in his mouth, that men worship God. But that God cultivates men—the hearer, as it were, takes fright when he hears it; because it is not in men's usage that it be said that God cultivates men, but that men worship God. We ought, then, to prove to you that God too cultivates men, lest perhaps we be thought to have said an ill-disciplined word, and someone dispute against us in his heart, and, not knowing what we said, reproach us. This, then, is settled, to demonstrate to you that God too cultivates us; but, as I already said, as a field, to make us better. The Lord says in the Gospel: *I am the vine, you are the branches*[^1], *my Father is the farmer*[^2]. What does a farmer do? I ask you who are farmers: what does a farmer do? I think he cultivates a field. If, then, God the Father is a farmer, he has a field, and he cultivates his field, and awaits fruit from it.

**2. 3.** Finally, he planted a vineyard, as the Lord Jesus Christ himself says, and let it out to farmers, that they might render him fruit in due seasons. And he sent his servants to them, to ask for the produce of the vineyard. But they treated them with insults, killed some of them too, and refused to render the fruit. He sent others; they suffered the like. And that head of the household, the cultivator of his field, both the planter and the leaser of his vineyard, said: *I will send my only son; perhaps they will at least respect him. And he sent,* it says, *even his son*[^3]. They said among themselves: *This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours*[^4]. And they killed him, and cast him outside the vineyard. When the lord of the vineyard comes, what will he do to those bad tenants? The answer was given: *He will destroy the wicked wickedly, and let out his vineyard to other farmers, who will render him fruit in its season*[^5]. The vineyard was planted when the law was given in the hearts of the Jews. The Prophets were sent, seeking fruit, their good life: the Prophets were treated with insults by them, and killed. Christ too was sent, the only Son of the head of the household; and him too, the heir, they killed, and therefore they lost the inheritance. Their evil counsel turned out contrary to them. To possess, they killed; and because they killed, they lost.

**3. 4.** And just now you heard a comparison from the holy Gospel, that *the kingdom of heaven is like a head of a household, who went out to hire laborers for his vineyard*[^6]. He went out in the morning, and hired those he found; and he agreed with them on a denarius as their wage. He went out also at the third hour, found others, and brought them to the work of the vineyard. And at the sixth hour he did this, and at the ninth he did this. He went out even at the eleventh hour, near the end of the day, and found some idle and standing about; and he said to them: Why do you stand here? Why are you not working in the vineyard? They answered: Because no one has hired us. Come, he said, you also, and what is just I will give you. He agreed on a denarius as their wage. These who were going to work one hour—when would they have dared to hope for a denarius for themselves? But still they were glad to be going to receive something. They too were brought in for one hour. When the day was finished, he ordered the wage to be paid to all, from the last to the first. He began, then, to pay from those who had come at the eleventh hour; he ordered a denarius to be given them. Those who had come at the first hour, seeing those receive a denarius, the very one that had been agreed with them, hoped they would receive something more: it came to them too, and they received a denarius. They murmured against the head of the household, saying: Behold, you have made us, who bore the burning of the day and the heat, equal and on a par with those who worked one hour in the vineyard. And the head of the household, rendering a most just answer to one of them, said: Friend, I have done you no wrong; that is, I have not defrauded you; what I agreed on I have rendered you. I have not defrauded you, because I have rendered what I bargained for. To this one I will not render, but give. Is it not lawful for me to do what I want with my own? Or is your eye evil, because I am good? If I took from someone what was another's, I would rightly be reproached, as a defrauder and unjust man; if I did not render to someone what was due, I would rightly be reproached, as a defrauder and a denier of another's due: but when I render the due, and also give to whom I will, neither can the one to whom I owed reproach me, and the one to whom I have given ought to rejoice the more. There was nothing that could be answered: and all were made equal, and the last were made first and the first last—by being equalized, not by being put in reverse order. What does it mean, The last were made first and the first last? Because both the first and the last received exactly as much.

**4. 5.** What is it, then, that he began to pay from the last? Are not all, as we read, going to receive together? For we read in another place of the Gospel that he is going to say to those he sets on his right: *Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the kingdom prepared for you from the beginning of the world*[^7]. If, then, all are going to receive together, how do we understand here that those who worked at the eleventh hour received earlier, and those who worked at the first, later? If I can say it so that it reaches your understanding, thanks be to God. For you ought to give thanks to him who pays you out through us; for we do not pay out from what is our own. If you ask about two men, for example—which received first, the one who received after one hour, or the one who received after twelve—every man answers that the one who received after one hour received earlier than the one after twelve. So, then, although all received in one hour, yet because some received after one hour and others received after twelve hours, those are said to have received first who received after a short time. The first just ones, like Abel, like Noah, called as it were at the first hour, are going to receive with us the happiness of the resurrection. Other just men after them—Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and whoever were of their age—called as it were at the third hour, are going to receive the happiness of the resurrection with us. Other just men—Moses and Aaron, and whoever were with them—called as it were at the sixth hour, are going to receive the happiness of the resurrection with us. After them the holy Prophets, called as it were at the ninth hour, are going to receive the same happiness with us. At the end of the age all Christians, called as it were at the eleventh hour, are going to receive the happiness of that resurrection with them. All are going to receive together; but see those first ones, after how much time they receive. If, then, those first ones receive after much time, and we after a little time; although we receive together, we seem to have received earlier, because our wage will not be delayed.

**4. 6.** We shall, then, all be equal in that wage, as the first the last and the last the first: because that denarius is eternal life, and in eternal life all will be equal. For although they will shine with a diversity of merits, one more, another less, yet as far as it pertains to eternal life, it will be equal for all. For it will not be longer for one and shorter for another, what is alike everlasting: what has no end will have none for you, nor for me. In one way there will be conjugal chastity there, in another way there will be virginal integrity there; in one way there will be the fruit of good work, in another the crown of suffering. That in one way, that in another; yet as far as pertains to living forever, neither will the one live more than the other, nor the other more than the one. For they live equally without end, though each lives in his own glories; and that denarius is eternal life. Let not the one who received after much time murmur, then, against the one who received after a little time. To the one it is paid, to the other it is given; yet to both the one thing is given.

**5. 7.** There is also something similar in this life. And apart from that solution of this comparison, by which those called at the first hour are understood to be Abel and the just men of his age, at the third Abraham and the just of his age, at the sixth Moses and Aaron and the just of their age, at the ninth the Prophets and the just of their age, at the eleventh, as it were at the end of the age, all Christians—apart from that solution of this comparison, this comparison can be noted in this life of ours too. For as it were at the first hour are called those who, fresh from their mother's womb, begin to be Christians; as it were at the third, boys; as it were at the sixth, young men; as it were at the ninth, those declining into old age; as it were at the eleventh, the wholly decrepit: yet all are going to receive the one denarius of eternal life.

**6. 8.** But attend and understand, my brothers, lest anyone for this reason put off coming to the vineyard, because he is secure that, whenever he comes, he is going to receive that same denarius. He is indeed secure that that same denarius is promised him; but he is not commanded to delay. For surely even those who were hired to the vineyard, when the head of the household went out to them, to hire those whom he found at the third hour, and hired them—did they, for example, say to him, Wait, we are not going there except at the sixth hour? Or did those whom he found at the sixth hour say, We are not going except at the ninth? Or did those whom he found at the ninth say, We are not going except at the eleventh? For he is going to give just as much to all: why are we wearied more? What he is going to give, and what he is going to do, is within his own counsel: you, when you are called, come. For an equal wage is promised to all; but about the very hour of working, there is a great question. For if those, for example, who are called at the sixth hour, set in this age of the body where the youthful years are fervent, as the sixth hour itself is fervent—if those young men called were to say: Wait, for we have heard in the Gospel that we are all going to receive one wage; when we have become old men, we will come at the eleventh hour; going to receive just as much, why should we labor more? It would be answered them and said: You, who do not know whether you will live until old age, do not want to labor? You are called at the sixth hour, come. The head of the household has indeed promised a denarius to you coming even at the eleventh; but whether you will live until the seventh, no one has promised you. I do not say, until the eleventh, but until the seventh. Why, then, do you put off the one who calls you, sure of the wage, unsure of the day? See lest perhaps what he is going to give you by promising, you take away from yourself by delaying. If this is rightly said of infants, as pertaining to the first hour; if it is rightly said of boys, as pertaining to the third hour; if it is rightly said of young men, as set in the blazing of the sixth hour: how much more rightly is it said of the decrepit, Behold, it is now the eleventh hour, and you still stand, you are lazy to come?

**7. 9.** Or has the head of the household perhaps not gone out to call you? If he has not gone out, what is it that we are saying? Surely we servants are of his household, sent to hire the laborers. Why, then, do you stand? You have now finished the number of your years; hasten to the denarius. For this is the going out of the head of the household: to become known; since one who is in the house is in concealment, and is not seen by those who are outside; but when he has gone out of the house, he is seen by those who are outside. Christ, when he is not understood and not acknowledged, is in secret; but when he is acknowledged, he has gone out to hire. For from the hidden he has come forth into recognition: Christ is known, Christ is preached everywhere; all things whatsoever that are under heaven proclaim the glory of Christ. In a way he was laughable and reproachable among the Jews, he seemed lowly, he was despised. For he was concealing his majesty, he had his weakness ready to hand. What was ready to hand in him was despised, and what was hidden was not recognized. For if they had recognized him, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory[^8]. Is he still to be despised, sitting in heaven, if he was despised when he hung on the wood? Those who crucified him wagged their heads, and standing before his cross, and as though arriving at the fruit of their savagery, insultingly said: *If he is the Son of God, let him come down from the cross*[^9]. *He saved others, can he not save himself? Let him come down from the cross, and we believe in him*[^10]. He did not come down, because he was hiding. For he who could rise from the tomb could much more easily have come down from the cross. For our instruction he showed forth patience, he deferred his power, and he was not recognized. For he had not then gone out to hire laborers; he had not gone out, he had not become known. On the third day he rose, he showed himself to the disciples, he ascended into heaven, and he sent the Holy Spirit on the fiftieth day after the resurrection, the tenth after the ascension. The Holy Spirit, sent, filled all who were in one room, a hundred and twenty men. Filled with the Holy Spirit, they began to speak in the tongues of all nations[^11]; the calling was made plain, he had gone out to hire. For then the power of truth began to become known to all. For then even one man, having received the Holy Spirit, even one man spoke in the tongues of all nations. But now in the Church that very unity, as it were one man, speaks in the tongues of all nations. To what tongue has the Christian religion not come? To what ends does it not extend? Now *there is no one who can hide himself from his heat*[^12]; and still there is delay from the one who stands at the eleventh hour!

**8. 10.** It is manifest, then, my brothers, altogether manifest—hold it, be certain—that our God Jesus Christ, when anyone has converted to faith in him, from his way, whether superfluous or most worthless, all his past is forgiven him, and, altogether, as though the debts were remitted, a new account is made with him. All things whatever are forgiven. Let no one be anxious that something is not forgiven him. But again, let no one be perversely secure. For these two things kill souls, either despair or perverse hope. About these two evils, hear a little. For as good hope, and right hope, sets free, so perverse hope deceives. First attend to how despair deceives. There are men who, when they begin to think of the evils they have done, do not think they can be forgiven; and while they think they cannot be forgiven, they give up their soul to perish already, they perish by despair, saying in their thoughts: Now there is no hope for us; for those great things we have committed cannot be given or forgiven us; why, then, do we not satisfy our desires? Let us at least fulfill the pleasure of the present time, since we have no wage in the future. Let us do whatever we please, even if it is not lawful, so that we may have at least temporal sweetness, since we do not deserve to obtain the eternal. Saying such things, they perish by despairing, whether before they believe at all, or as Christians already and lapsed into some sins and crimes by living badly. The Lord of the vineyard comes forth to them, and, as to despairers and ones turning their backs on the one who calls, he knocks and cries out through the prophet Ezekiel: *On whatever day a man is converted from his worst way, I will forget all his iniquities*[^13]. When this voice is heard and believed, they are restored from despair, and emerge from that deepest and most profound abyss in which they had been submerged.

**9. 11.** But these must beware lest they run into another abyss, and die by hoping perversely, who could not die by despairing. For they change their thoughts, far different indeed, but no less ruinous; and again they begin to say in their hearts: If on whatever day I am converted from my worst way, the merciful God, as he truly promised through the Prophet, forgets all my iniquities, why am I converted today, and not tomorrow? Why today, and not tomorrow? Let today go like yesterday, let it be in most worthless pleasure, let it be in the whirlpool of disgraces, let it wallow in deadly delight: tomorrow I will be converted, and that is the end. It is answered you: The end of what? You say: Of my iniquities. Well, rejoice, because tomorrow there will be an end of your iniquities. What if before tomorrow there will be an end of you? So you indeed rejoice well, because for the sake of your iniquities God has promised pardon to you when converted; but no one has promised you tomorrow. Or if perhaps an astrologer has promised it, he is far other than God. Astrologers have deceived many, since they often promised even themselves gains, and found losses. Therefore for the sake of these too, who hope badly, the head of the household comes forth. As he came forth to those who had despaired badly, and were perishing by despairing, and recalled them to hope, so he comes forth also to these who, by hoping badly, want to perish; and he says to them through another book: *Do not delay to be converted to the Lord*[^14]. As he had said to those: *On whatever day the wicked man is converted from his worst way, I will forget all his iniquities*[^15], and took from them the despair by which they had already given their soul to perdition, despairing of pardon in every way: so he comes forth also to these who, by hoping and deferring, want to perish; and he speaks to them and rebukes them: *Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day. For suddenly his wrath will come, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you*[^16]. Therefore do not delay, do not shut against yourself what lies open. Behold, the giver of pardon opens the door to you; why do you tarry? You ought to rejoice if he opened at some point to one knocking: you did not knock, and he opens, and you remain outside? Therefore do not delay. About the works of mercy Scripture says in a certain place: *Do not say, Go and return, tomorrow I will give; when you can do good at once*[^17]: for you do not know what may befall on the following day. You heard the precept not to delay being merciful to another, and by delaying are you cruel to yourself? You ought not to delay when you are going to give bread, and do you delay when you are going to receive pardon! If by being merciful you do not delay toward another, have mercy also on your own soul by pleasing God[^18]. Show alms to your own soul too. We do not say that you should give to it, but that you should not repel the hand of the one who gives.

**10. 12.** But sometimes men do themselves the greatest harm in this matter, when they fear to offend others. Both good friends are of great power for good, and bad friends for evil. Therefore the Lord, that for the sake of our salvation we might despise the friendships of the powerful, refused to choose senators first, but fishermen. Great is the mercy of the craftsman. For he knew that if he chose a senator, the senator would say: My rank was chosen. If he first chose a rich man, the rich man would say: My wealth was chosen. If he first chose an emperor, the emperor would say: My power was chosen. If he first chose an orator, the orator would say: My eloquence was chosen. If he chose a philosopher, the philosopher would say: My wisdom was chosen. Meanwhile, he says, let those proud ones be deferred, they swell much. Now there is a difference between greatness and swelling: both are big; but not both are healthy. Let those proud ones be deferred, then, he says, they are to be healed by some solid means. Give me first, he says, that fisherman. Come, you poor man, follow me; you have nothing, you know nothing, follow me. Poor unlettered man, follow me. There is nothing in you to take fright at, but there is much to be filled in you. To so generous a fountain an empty vessel must be brought. The fisherman left his nets, the sinner received grace, and was made a divine orator. Behold what the Lord did, of whom the Apostle says: *God chose the weak things of the world, to confound the strong; and God chose the ignoble things of the world, and the things that are not, as though they were, that the things that are might be brought to nothing*[^19]. Finally, now the words of the fishermen are read, and the necks of orators are bent beneath them. Let the empty winds, then, be taken out of the way; let the smoke be taken out of the way, which in growing vanishes; let them be utterly despised for the sake of this salvation.

**10. 13.** If someone were sick in body in a city, and there were there some most skilled physician, an enemy to the sick man's powerful friends: if, then, someone in the city were sick with some dangerous bodily disease, and there were in that same city a most skilled physician, an enemy, as I said, to the sick man's powerful friends, who would say to their friend, Do not engage him, he knows nothing—saying it, however, not with a judging mind, but with an envious one: would not that man, for the sake of his own health, set aside the talk of his powerful friends, and, that he might live a few more days, with whatever offense to them, engage that physician whom report had commended as most skilled, to drive off the disease of his body?

**11. 13.** The human race is sick, not with diseases of the body, but with sins. Throughout the whole world, from east to west, there lies a great sick man. To heal the great sick man, the almighty physician came down. He humbled himself even to mortal flesh, as though to the bed of the sick man. He gives the precepts of health, he is despised: those who hear are set free. He is despised, when powerful friends say, He knows nothing. If he knew nothing, his power would not fill the nations. If he knew nothing, he would not be before he was among us. If he knew nothing, he would not send the Prophets before him. Are not the things now being fulfilled that were foretold beforehand? Does not this physician prove the power of his art by fulfilling his promises? Are not ruinous errors overthrown throughout the whole world, and the desires of the world subdued by the world's threshing? Let no one say, The world was better before than now: ever since this physician began to exercise his art, we see many horrible things here. Do not marvel. Before anyone was cured, the physician's station seemed clean of blood: nay rather, now that you see this, shake off your empty delights, come to the physician; it is the time of health, not of pleasure.

**11. 14.** Let us be cured, then, brothers. If we acknowledge the physician, let us not rage against him like delirious men, let us not turn away from him like lethargic men. For many have perished by raging, many by sleeping. Delirious are those who go mad by not sleeping. Lethargic are those who are weighed down by too much sleeping. People are altogether of this kind. Against this physician some want to rage; and since he himself now sits in heaven, the faithful, his members on earth, they persecute. He cures even such men. Many of them, converted, were made friends from enemies, made preachers from persecutors. Even those same Jews, raging against him when he was here, he healed as delirious men, for whom, hanging on the wood, he prayed. For he said: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^20]. Yet many of them, their fury calmed, their delirium as it were suppressed, recognized God, recognized Christ. After the ascension, when the Holy Spirit was sent, they were converted to him whom they had crucified, and, believing, drank in the Sacrament his blood, which by raging they had shed.

**12. 15.** We have examples. Saul was persecuting the members of him who already sits in heaven: he was persecuting grievously, in delirium, his mind lost, in excessive disease. But he, crying out to him with one voice from heaven: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me*[^21]? struck down the delirious man, raised up the healthy man; killed the persecutor, gave life to the preacher. Many lethargic men too are healed. For like to them are those who do not rage against Christ, nor are malicious against Christians; but only languish by deferring, with drowsy words, and are too lazy to stretch their eyes toward the light, and those who want to rouse them are troublesome. Withdraw from me, says the languishing, lethargic man, I beg you, withdraw from me. Why? I want to sleep. But you will die from it. He, in his love of sleep, answers, I want to die. And love from above: I do not want it. Often a son shows this affection of love even to his old father about to die after a few days, his age indeed already at its end. If he sees him lethargic, and recognizes from the physician that his father is weighed down by lethargic disease, the physician saying to him: Rouse your father, do not let him sleep, if you want him to live: the boy is at the old man's side, he knocks, he plucks at him, he pricks him, he is troublesome out of devotion; nor does he let him die quickly, though by his very old age he is to die quickly; and if he lives, the son rejoices that he may live somewhat more days, the one who is to follow with the one who is to depart. With how much greater love ought we to be troublesome to our friends, with whom we are to live not a few days in this world, but with God forever? Let them love us, then, and do what they hear through us, and worship him whom we too worship, that they may receive what we too hope for. Turned to the Lord, etc.
