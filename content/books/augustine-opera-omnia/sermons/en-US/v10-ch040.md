# Sermon 360/B

**1.** The word of God does not cease to exhort and console us with faithful promises and wholesome threats. For it is expedient for us neither not to love nor not to fear. But just as God the promiser is to be loved, so the threatener is to be feared. In neither does he deceive the hearer, in neither does he beguile the believer. Let no one say in his mind: "He promises true things, but he threatens false things." Both are true. Love and fear. Without any doubt he is going to come who has already come[^1]. But he came to teach you patience, he is going to come to crown patience. He will surely crown, when he comes, what he taught when he had come. And what he threatened when he had come, he will inflict when he comes. And these are two: the promise of God, eternal life; the threat of God, eternal punishment. If you do not yet know how to love what he promises, begin to fear what he threatens. For so it is written: *The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^2]. But the apostle John says: *Fear is not in love, but perfect love casts out fear*[^3]. When, then, we have heard: *The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*, let us begin to fear. But because fear has the torment of the heart, you will not be long in torment, if love grows in you and is perfected. Yet it cannot even be begun in you, unless you offer your heart by fear for it to be sown. But love, once born, the more it grows, the more fear is lessened. And if it is lessened while love grows, it is consumed when love is consummated.

**2.** For what God promises, dearest ones, exceeds not only our words, but even the thoughts of all men. For so it is commended too: *What eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man, which God has prepared for those who love him*[^4]. If there were some color as it were of the light which the bodily eyes know, it would not be said: *Nor has eye seen it*; if there were some sweet sound such as human ears are wont to be delighted by, as of organs and any musical things, it would not be said: *Nor has ear heard it.* And because men cannot turn over good things in their minds, except those which they have perceived by the bodily senses, it was added: *Nor has it ascended into the heart of man.* For you cannot, O man, think of a good thing, except such as you have been accustomed to see or hear or touch by some such sense. Whatever has not entered through the sense of your body, cannot even be thought by your mind.

**3.** And so when it has been said to us that we shall be in paradise[^5], we think of some pleasant garden. And if we think of a larger one than we have been accustomed to see, yet we enlarge this very kind of thing. If, for instance, we are accustomed to see small trees, we think of them large; and if we are accustomed to such or such fruits, we think of more ample ones. If we are accustomed to see meadows of some extent, we turn over in mind ones immense without end: yet we enlarge by thinking the same things which we know by the eyes. Again, when we hear: God *dwells in inaccessible light*[^6], from this light which we know by the bodily eyes we measure that one and amplify it to an immense degree, yet by enlarging what we know, although that light is of a far different kind: for that is the light not of the eyes, but of minds. And just as the eye of the flesh must be cleansed that it may be able to bear this bodily light, which shines either from heaven above and gleams from the nocturnal lights, and if the eyes are wounded and disturbed by some inner humor or by some thing rushing in from without, they will have that very light by which they were wont to be invigorated as a punishment and will be tortured by that which they were wont to rejoice in, so for that intelligible and immortal light, not the eye of the flesh, but the eye of the heart must be cleansed. For just as rheum, which causes bleariness, disturbs the eye of the flesh, so iniquity disturbs it. It too has its weakness, it has its uncleanness, coming not from dust, but from sin. As, then, this bodily eye must be cleansed to see its bodily light, so that inner eye must be cleansed to see that light which *neither eye has seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^7].

**4.** Why *has it not ascended into the heart of man*? For it is beheld by the heart, when it is beheld. But why *has it not ascended into the heart of man*? Because *of man.* What does *of man* mean? Those who know the Scriptures understand, and anticipate by thought what I am going to say. Men, sometimes by a certain definite signification, our Scripture calls those who still savor carnally[^8]. For they are men, that is, they are Adam. But you know that Adam sinned, and from there draws the origin of carnal concupiscence whoever is born mortally. He bears with him, then, the wound of the eye, and as long as he is a man, as long as there is in him that which was injured and disturbed by the first sin. Whence a certain one cries out in the psalms and says, sighing and groaning to God: *And the light of my eyes is not with me*[^9]. As long, then, as a man is thus, savoring carnally, he cannot think nor grasp with the mind that light, and therefore it is said: *What eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^10]. What is *of man*? Of one savoring carnally. What is *of man*? Of one still bearing Adam. Whence those who were men, what did he wish to make them, to whom it was a reproach that they were men? For he says to them: *For when each one of you says: I am of Paul, but I of Apollos, but I of Cephas*[^11]. For they had divided among themselves the ministers of God and made parties for themselves out of the Church of Christ, the evil of schisms beginning, which afterward were confirmed by the error of men. These things they said savoring carnally and placing their hope not in God, but in man[^12], not singing with the heart what we sang a little before: *In you our fathers hoped*[^13].

**5.** Rebuking these, then, the Apostle says, since they said such things: *Are you not men and do you not walk according to man?*[^14] Again in the psalm it is said in the person of God speaking: *I said: You are gods and sons of the Most High, all of you. But you shall die like men and fall like one of the princes*[^15]. As you know, he calls the ancient one of the princes the devil. For when he was an angel, fallen by pride he was made the devil. He who then, fallen, envied the one standing, he himself now too envies the one returning. Men were therefore made mortal, that by the greater affliction of punishment they might be trained to humility, and, scourged in a certain way by their mortality and considering that they cannot live here longer and that, if they lived longer, yet they would not live forever and that this life would have to be ended at some time, they might humble themselves to God and try to grasp the future, the present fleeing. For what flees or slips away cannot be held. Or can any one of us now, when either we stand and speak or you stand and hear, hold his ages, so that either the boys do not grow or the youths do not grow old? You see that from the time we are speaking it is time. And if from the time we are speaking it is time, and by length of time it comes about that we incline toward old age, now by this very time in which we have spoken we have grown a little old. But these changes of ours are grasped by reason, but cannot be seen by the eyes. For neither are your hairs seen when they grow, and yet unless they were perpetually growing, you would not after a few days seek a barber: for what the barber is going to cut off tomorrow does not grow in one night. But just as the hairs grow even now, but cannot be seen, so the age itself grows old even now, but cannot be grasped by the eyes.

**6.** Men, then, love the present life, which they cannot hold as it flees, and which slips away both as they grow and as they fail. How much better, then, do they hold something firm, to which, these things ended, they will come. To this is added that, since the life of man is small, it is also uncertain. For if old age itself were certain for every man, he ought to consider that life is small, even if it were permitted to all to be brought even to its limits. For what is long that is ended? To this, then, is added that death is the companion of mortality, and, while it in a certain way walks with you on the way, when it may overtake you, you surely do not know. Since, then, both life itself is small, and death is uncertain for every age, men thinking thus ought to humble themselves to God, supplicate the Creator, confess, groan in their sins, allege their sickness to the physician, that they may be healed within and change that eye, whence that light can be seen, which is not seen as long as the inner eye of man is still of man. Let them wake up, then, when they hear from God: *I said: You are gods and sons of the Most High, all of you*[^16]. What is *I said*? I call to this, I wish to do it. Hear the Gospel: *He gave them the power to become sons of God*[^17]. When, then, I say: *You are gods and sons of the Most High, all of you, but you shall die like men*, and not even that very mortality profits you for correcting yourselves, but as though you were immortal, so *you fall like one of the princes*, that is, you are so proud as the angel dared to be proud. But if pride cast down the angel, what will it do with man? You shall, then, be gods. And if you do not worship false gods, you yourselves shall be gods. And how shall you be? With him making it who made men too. For he who made us men wishes to make us gods, not that we should be worshiped in his place, but that he himself should be worshiped in us.

**7.** There is, then, as I had begun to say, dearest ones, an inner eye which sins, carnal concupiscences and earthly desires wound and disturb, so that the man himself sinning has heard: *You are earth and into earth you shall go*[^18]. If, then, the proud, iniquitous man deserved to hear: *You are earth and into earth you shall go*, why does not the humble, pious man hear: "You are heaven and into heaven you shall go"? For by humility and piety one is made the seat of God. Now when he has been made the seat of God, is he not heaven? It is said in the Scriptures: *Heaven is my seat, but the earth is the footstool of my feet*[^19]. If, then, heaven is the seat of God, be heaven that you may carry God. When you have begun to carry God, you will be heaven. But that you may carry him fully, he himself will cleanse you, when he begins to dwell there, until he brings that eye of the heart to so great cleanness that it can see the face of him in whom, unseen, you believed[^20]. Believe, then, before you see, so that with the heart cleansed by faith you may merit even to see what you have believed. For there is promised to you a light which *neither eye has seen*, because it is not a color, *nor ear heard*, because it is not a sound, *nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^21], because the man who is properly called man, carnal, weak, animal, cannot think except those things which he has drawn in by the senses of the body. That light is not such. Let the soul dare to form for itself nothing as it were in fantasy about God: him whom it wishes to find, let it first learn not to find.

**8.** What is it that I said: let it first learn not to find? So that when perhaps it thinks of God and there begins to occur to it what it has seen—the beauty of the earth occurs, let it spurn it from its mind; the appearance of the waters occurs, the tranquility of the airy serenity occurs, let it spurn this too by its thought; let it say to itself: "This is not my God, it is the work of my God." This is not, I say, my God, it is the work of my God: what has been made, you think of. O my soul, seek him who made it. But when your thought has come even to the heavenly bodies, let not the heavenly light entice you, not even that which is the highest. For the highest heavenly light is in the sun. The primacy of brightness of the heavenly bodies is found in the sun, which suffices for the day. Do not think your God to be any such thing, nor increase that very brightness into a greater one and wander for yourself through the spaces of your phantasm; neither is this God which in your mind is so figured as is wont to shine to the eyes of the body. This is not God.

**9.** Come to the soul itself, because it too is not seen. The soul is not seen, and great is the power of the incorporeal nature. For the soul is not a body, it is something invisible, and it is something great: it cannot be seen, but from its works marvel at what you do not see. What delights you in human affairs? Look around at the order of things, the beauty of cultivated fields, of cleared forests, of grafted fruit-trees, and whatever in the fields we see and love, the very order of the state, the masses of buildings, the varieties of arts, the abundance of tongues, the depth of memory, the richness of eloquence. All these are the works of the soul. How great works of the soul you see, and you do not see the soul itself! When, then, some such nature has begun to occur to you, is it now your God whom you were seeking? It is not seen, it is now something incorporeal, it is something spiritual, it is something great, which invigorates even mortal members, which in a certain way restrains and holds the flowing decay of the body. But this even the soul of a beast can do. Something great indeed is even the soul of a beast, it too is something invisible. But pass over to the soul of man: behold where man was made to the image of God[^22]. For he was made to the image of God not in the body, but in the mind, in that thing by which he governs all these things, in that thing by which he surpasses the beasts. For how many beasts surpass us both in firmness of body and in keenness of the carnal senses! Both in speed and in strength, in many bodily gifts we are surpassed by beasts. Whence are we better than the beasts, except because we understand, except because we have reason by which we can even tame a wild beast? But by a wild beast we cannot be tamed. But just as man is fit to tame a beast, so to tame man none is fit except God. When, then, some such thing has occurred to you, so that you think of the human mind even free from bodily bonds, dare to suspect nothing of the kind to be God. You seem now indeed to be in the neighborhood, but at a long interval. In the neighborhood, so that there is nothing higher that is near to God, but yet between your mind and God who created your mind there is very much distance. No nature or place lies between, but by unlikeness it is far off, since this was made, he made it, and in no way can what was made be compared to its maker. Yet the image of your God is in some measure in your mind.

**10.** Transcend, if you can, even your mind, if indeed you have arrived by knowledge at your mind. Consider, brothers, what I shall say. For perhaps even when you think of your mind, you think of something corporeal by the habit of the carnal senses, so that your mind seems to you to be either air or fire or this light which you behold. You think of some such thing, when you think of your mind. Do not think of any such thing. As soon as that which you understand occurs to you, "what is it that I understand?", say to yourself. For surely unless some light were there, you would not understand. For so you behold a certain light within, as you behold a certain light without. For the lamp of your body is your eye[^23]. But with the light absent, what does your open eye profit? You have indeed whole lights, but you are helped by another light to see. So too when you understand, you have something or other of the kind, which can enjoy an inner light, which is not this which you see with the eyes. Think of your mind as some such thing, if you can; but if you cannot, what is that one above your mind who terrifies your mind, who exhorts your mind, who forms your mind? You cannot worthily think of the mind: from where can you think of him whom you are going to see with a pure mind? If, then, you cannot do even this, neither earth, nor heaven, nor air, nor this starry light, nor the so marvelous power and nature of the rational soul itself can rightly be called your God, but everywhere you ought to say: "This is not my God." You cannot, then, know what he is, unless you first learn what he is not. Therefore first think what he is not, that you may find what he is.

**11.** This is what I said a little before: learn to be ignorant of God, that you may merit to find him. For you learn to be ignorant of him by a better ignorance than a false knowledge. For better is an ignorance that does not err, than a knowledge that is so called and is not. For you will say to me: "I know God." I ask what God is. You begin to wish to explain, and first by that very thing you do not know by which you think you explain what you cannot think. You are, then, going to tell me your thoughts, you are going to tell me those things which have ascended into your heart. But see that you are a man, and into the heart of man has ascended what you are going to tell me. But he who promises himself to be enjoyed by his lovers surely does not promise what eye has seen nor what ear heard nor what has ascended into the heart of man[^24]. But how do they love whom they do not see, except because they believe before they see[^25]? What, then, does he promise to his lovers? *What eye has not seen nor ear heard.* Can it perhaps be thought? Do not deceive yourself: *nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^26].

**12.** What, then? How do you prepare yourself? Say. "I wish to see my God." Say it to him: "I wish to see," say it to him who says: *Ask and you shall receive, knock and it shall be opened to you*[^27]. Knock, set before the door, knock vehemently. He who closes does not drive away: he wishes to exercise the one knocking. Knock, then, knock, not with a bodily hand, but with the affection of the heart. Say to the Lord your God what you sing in the psalms: *To you my heart has said: I have sought your face, your face will I seek*[^28]. Say also what is said in another psalm: *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, that I may contemplate the delight of the Lord*[^29]. Desire to contemplate and say to him: "I wish to see you." But whence shall I see? If with carnal eyes, then you are a corporeal light. Now my heart already reports back to me that you, my God, are not a corporeal light. What, then, are you? I have transcended all these things, I have now come to my mind, and even it is not my God. And yet the nature of the mind transcends all corporeal things whether earthly or heavenly, but it is not yet my God. For my mind is changeable: but my God is unchangeable. I seek something unchangeable, when I seek my God. But from where do I recognize the mind to be changeable? Now it remembers, now it forgets, now it is wise, now it is foolish, now it wills, now it does not will, now it is angry, now it is calmed. I seek something unchangeable, when I seek my God. So my God spoke to me in the Scriptures, that I might be able somehow to think what I believe, not yet to have what I may see. I seek something which always remains unchangeable.

**13.** But from where shall I see? The Gospel will answer you: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^30]. If, then, *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*, but we bear hearts unclean from the weight of sins, what shall we do? From where shall we cleanse our inner eye, by which the face of our God may be seen? From where shall we cleanse it? You have this too in Scripture: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^31]. Let us, then, hold these two testimonies: one from the Gospel, the other from the Acts of the Apostles. What from the Gospel? *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* You have looked at yourself, you have found a certain uncleanness of your heart; desiring to see God and hearing that he is not seen except by clean hearts, inflamed with the desire of seeing, you surely seek to cleanse your heart. From where, then, will you cleanse it? Attend to the one saying in the Acts: *Cleansing their hearts by faith.* Hold these two: one in the promise, the other in the action. In the promise what? *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.* In the action what? *Cleansing their hearts by faith.* Believe, then, before you see, that you may be able to rejoice when you see.

**14.** Let no vain thought ascend into your heart: "What is it that the Christians say: Believe, believe?" The physician says this, who knows what is happening in your eye. Now both resist the hands of the physician and say: "I do not believe, unless you show me." The physician will answer you: "There is no one to whom I may show it; I wish to heal in you that by which you can see what you now desire me to show you." Suppose a physician saying to a man blinded by some haze, and perhaps from the beginning of his life, so that he does not even know what is seen by those who see: "There is something I may show you; behold, by a certain sense you know yourself to be blind, but others to be seeing: because either you need a guide which they do not need, surely there is some difference between them and you. Those, then, see something which you do not see; which if you saw, you would rejoice much." He rouses in him the desire of seeing what he does not know, wishing to heal him, that he may be able to see what he does not see. But if he is so absurd and averse from all reason of truth that he says to the physician: "You will not heal me, unless you show me what I am going to see," what do you think the physician will answer? "You must be healed that you may see, not see that you may be healed. You are perverse, you are preposterous. First let that be done which you do not wish, that you may be able to come to that which you wish. If you had eyes to which I could show what I say, there would be no need for you to be healed." Perhaps he will answer: "And what am I to do? Heal as you wish." And the physician: "I am going to apply certain sharper salves, which may wipe away the haze for you, from whose sharpness you are going to feel some pain. But you must endure the healing pain patiently and not, anxious and impatient of the pain, repel my hands. For I know what I am doing in your eyes, that there may be eyes which now are not even to be called eyes. I know what I am doing, and therefore I forewarn you that you are going to suffer something troublesome with the fruit of illumination." He, perhaps terrified lest he be bitten by those medicines which the physician is going to apply, returns again to that worn-out and rejected thing: "Am I going to suffer such great pains, which you are going to apply to me? Unless I first see what you are going to show me, I will not bear it." Against this the physician: "You cannot. This is what I seek; I ask you: be healed. You will see, the haze will be driven out, that light will shine again for you which you hear those who see name and you do not see. For you hear: light, color, brightness; these names you hear. These names are of certain things, the things themselves you do not see. Those who see are happier than you. Bear, then, something of pain in compensation for great joys." If he consents, he will be healed and will see. If he does not consent, wishing first to see rather than to be healed so that there may be that whence he may see, most absurd and an enemy of his own salvation, he will abandon the physician.

**15.** Attend now: a saving physician came to us, our Lord Jesus Christ, he found the blindness of our heart, he promised the light which *eye has not seen nor ear heard nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^32]. This the angels see, over it they rejoice. For just as those healthy men see what the blind man does not see, so the angels see what man does not see. Why does man not see? Because he still wishes to be a man. Let the man himself, then, begin to be healed, that from a man he may become among the sons of God, because *he gave them the power to become sons of God*[^33]. This is: *he gave them the power*, that they may be healed, that the haze of the heart may be wiped away, because *blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^34]. And hear that there is in the Gospel too what is said elsewhere: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^35]. When the Lord had said: *He gave them the power to become sons of God*, he at once subjoined: *to those believing in his name*[^36]. If, then, to those believing he gave the power to become sons of God—but the sons of God will be able to see what has not ascended into the heart of man—by faith he cleanses their hearts that they may be able to be pure in heart, for they shall see God[^37].

**16.** Blessed, then, are you, brothers, who believe; pray for those who do not believe, that they too may merit to see. Blessed are you who believe: you do not see, but you believe[^38]; you are not yet healthy, but you are still being healed. Your health is in hope, not yet in reality. Persevere under the hands of the physician, bear his precepts as sharp salves, abstain yourselves from the pernicious pleasures of the world: let not the unlawful things of the nations lead you, not the theatrical trifles, not the luxury of drunkenness, not the poison of unlawful curiosity. From all these abstain yourselves. But you have grown accustomed to rejoice in these things, and when you begin to abstain from these, the greed of broken habit will grieve. But these are the sharp salves by which the eyes are healed. Receive the precepts of the physician. Whatever he imposes on you to bear, he himself first endured. And in him indeed there was nothing to be healed, because he was sick with nothing, but in the office of healing he endured what he imposed on the sick. He gave a certain bitter cup to the one swollen and puffed up with pride; he, coming humble, suffered all humble things from proud men.

**17.** The humility of Christ is the medicine of your pride. Do not mock that by which you are healed. Deign to be humble, on account of him for whom God was made humble. For he judged that you must be healed by this medicine, who knows well both from what you are sick and from what you are to be healed. In all the members of the body good physicians seek the cause from which are drawn all those things which are most difficultly endured. And therefore many unskilled ones, treating the accidental causes and not treating the original one, seem to heal for a time, but with the source as it were of the diseases remaining, again into the streams of calamity flows out what perseveres in the source. Hear, then, from what man is sick, from what he has not only the eyes but no members healthy. Hear from what he is sick, receive from the Scriptures where the art of the physician is written. For he is not more certain who reads to you the disease from Hippocrates, than he who demonstrates to you from the divine Scripture from what you are sick within. Hear, then, the Scripture saying: *The beginning of all sin is pride*[^39]; and yet you run for the health of your body, for your soul you are slothful. If a straw rushes into your eye, it is not deferred to take it out. Iniquity presses the eye of your heart, you do not run to the physician. Although, while you could not run to the physician, the physician himself came to you and, what is graver, this very thing that he came to you you mock, you count his mercy as nothing. He came, he wishes to help, he knows what to apply. Therefore God came humble, lest he should not be imitable by man. For how would you imitate the lofty one? Not having imitated him, how would you be healed? He came, then, humble, because he knew what cup he might give you. A little bitter indeed, but wholesome. But you, mocking the one bearing the cup, still say to yourself: "What sort of God am I going to have? One born, suffering, smeared with spittle, crowned with thorns, hung on a cross?" Unhappy soul! You see the humility of the physician, you do not see the swelling of your pride. Hence the humble one displeases you, displeases your pride, the medicine which the physician gives you displeases your disease.

**18.** If you still mock, you are frenzied. The frenzied often even strike the physicians, and the merciful ones not only are not angry at those who strike them, but even seek the health of those who strike them. But sometimes they prevail so that they can even kill the physician. But he vehemently avoids being killed by the frenzied one, because he cannot rise again and heal the frenzied one. But that physician of ours did not fear to be killed by the frenzied, and from his very death he made medicines for the frenzied one. For he died and rose again. And see that the true physician is not angry at the frenzied who strike him, but rather pities them and from what he suffers wishes to heal the raging. Hear the physician hanging on the cross; the crowd of raging frenzied looked around, he says: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^40]. Nor was his voice empty. For after he rose again and was glorified in the eyes of his disciples, so that he showed them even the scars of the same risen flesh, and offered himself not only to be seen but also to be handled, he ascended into heaven, he sent the Holy Spirit: miracles began to be done in the name of the one slain, in the name of the one crucified, and then those who killed him were more pricked than when they saw him hanging on the cross. For they considered such things being done in the name of him who was thought by them to have been slain among their hands. They perceived him living, whom they had mocked dying. *They were pricked in heart*[^41], as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles; many of those very Jews, the crucifiers of the Lord, sought counsel from the apostles; they received counsel too, because not in vain did he say, hanging: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.* The apostle said to them: {*Do penance, and let each one be baptized in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and your sins are forgiven you*[^42]. It was done: they were baptized and believed} in the one whom they crucified. This is what I said, brothers, that from his very death too he made medicines for the frenzied.

**19.** From there it went to the nations. The apostles were sent to the nations: they found the whole world given over to images. And the disciples themselves of that physician, in whom the physician himself presided, because they had been made heaven and carried God, began therefore to preach him who was crucified and *died for our offenses and rose for our justification*[^43]. With signs and so great miracles attesting it, the whole world began to be filled. And first, that it might be filled, the disciples of the physician too were killed, just as the physician himself had been slain. But when would they fear to be killed, since they beheld even the flesh risen in their head? For whom would souls that would never die fear, since they had already risen in body in the Lord? But he made all believers as it were a body to a head, so that he himself was their head, but all who believe in him clung as it were as members. From the beginning of the world to the end Christ has been believed in and will be believed in, because even before he was born of the virgin Mary, many believed him going to come, just as now they believe in him who has already come. And through that very faith all are healed, nor is there any other salve for the haze of the spiritual eye, except as it is written: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^44]. He therefore made all the saints a body for himself, of which body he himself is the head[^45]. Nor would he be the head of this body, unless he had something from the body itself. For from where did he assume flesh, which in him could die? For if the soul of man cannot die, when could the divinity of the Word die? Therefore thousands of martyrs too were killed, and as it were with their blood sown the harvest of the Church arose throughout the whole world.

**20.** Therefore, brothers, these things were foretold thousands of years ago and, as they were foretold, so they are seen expressed and displayed. A few things remain which we read and believe, for more things we now both read and see. But from those things which we read and see, it is no great matter to believe the fewer things which remain. It was a great matter to believe for those who saw none of these things which we see. Now it is no longer praiseworthy to believe, but damnable not to believe. Let those wake up at some time and be healed who still were unwilling to be healed. Let them believe: they will see. Let them not be so perverse as to say to us: "Let me first see and then I will believe." What is "let me see and then I will believe"? {For he who sees, does he believe? He believes who does not see. It is one thing to believe, another to see. Believe because you do not see, that by believing what you do not see you may merit to see what you believe. The merit of vision is faith. The reward of faith is vision. Why do you seek the reward before the work? Believe, then, and walk in faith: your salvation is in hope. For the best physician has begun to heal you, to whom no disease is incurable. Do not fear your past crimes, however monstrous, however incredible, which perhaps you committed: they are great diseases, but greater is the physician. Do not, then, care about past things: in one moment of the sacrament they will be forgiven, and altogether all will be forgiven}. Nothing of past things will remain to trouble your care. You will be secure not in your own strength, but in the hand of the physician. Be, then, secure under him, because he will heal even what remains: the very weakness of our mortality, from which lesser sins indeed creep in while we live here. He will heal the whole, he will cleanse the whole. The whole haze will be taken away, but let such an eye of the heart be given that you may be blessed when you see, because believing you heard: *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^46]. Let those who have not yet believed look, O my brothers, let them look how great things God has displayed. All the things which are seen done throughout the whole world in the name of Christ were foreseen, foretold, prescribed. In our hands are the books, before our eyes the deeds.

**21.** And if we think truly, brothers, he has done a more difficult thing than what he is going to do. What is it that I say: he has done a more difficult thing? He has justified {the impious one, from an idolater he has made a believer, from a drunkard a sober man, from a prodigal a frugal man, from a greedy man one giving away his goods, not giving to huntsmen with the devil applauding, but giving to the poor with Christ crowning, and acquiring for himself that which cannot pass away. The thing he has done was more difficult. For he who from an impious man made a pious one, will he not render a reward to the pious one? Attend, my brothers. What is more incredible? To make a pious man out of an impious one, or to make an angel out of a pious man? Impious and pious are contraries. Pious and angel are not contraries. Will he not fill you from the neighboring state, who changed you from the contrary one? For now, when you have begun to be pious, you begin to imitate the life of the angel. But when you were impious, you were removed from the choir of the angels. But faith came and justified you. You humble yourself to God, you who blasphemed God. And you who had turned to the creature, now desire the Creator. Behold what he has bestowed on you: he displayed his Church in the whole world; as he promised her, he displayed her. It was foretold that idolatry would at some time be slain and taken away: our elders read these things and did not see them, but we both read and see them}. Heresies were foretold, schisms were foretold: these too exist. Whence Christians are not disturbed when they see heresies and schisms. For they more surely hope that the things which were promised will come to pass, when the things which were foretold come to pass.

**22.** Flee, then, the crimes of all heresies and schisms, flee the sacrileges of the nations, the curiosities of demons, the adorations of idols, the sacrileges of remedies, the consultations of astrologers. Flee these, brothers. What you may hope for in them, you have not. For the future life they never promised; for the present, if they say so, they lie to you. Let someone come forward who says: "To me, I know, the astrologer told the truth, and to me I know that the diviner told the truth, and I had that remedy, and it availed." Let your love know this alone, which can easily be seen. The happiness and unhappiness of the present time, which is neither true happiness nor full unhappiness, is scattered intermingled through all men. If those alone were happy according to time who do these things, you, brothers who have believed, ought, on account of the future happiness, to despise the present. But since you see intermingled the healthy both of those who do them and of those who do not, intermingled the dying both of those who do not and of those who do, intermingled the rich and poor both of those who do and of those who do not, intermingled the honored and ignoble both of those who do and of those who do not—since, then, you see temporal happiness or unhappiness intermingled in the human race, why do you not beware eternal unhappiness, when it will be said: *Go into the eternal fire*[^47], and procure true happiness, when it will be said: *Receive the kingdom*[^48]?

**23.** "Juno," he says, "provides for those giving birth, and Mercury for huntsmen or men of letters, and Neptune for those sailing." These things are false. For if they are true, let not women give birth well who blaspheme Juno. What great thing is it, my brothers, to open the eyes to see these things? Did the prophets foretell these things too? Question yourselves: let the human race answer. Do all who do not worship Neptune suffer shipwreck? Do all merchants who mock Mercury suffer loss? But if these things are false, they never promised you a future life, for the present life they profit nothing. Why are they worshiped, except to bind the feet of those going the way of the Lord[^49], lest they seek immortality and some rest after the labors and difficulties of this life? For that devil sets himself with his angels against you, and makes himself as it were necessary, that he may reduce you to slavery. Use rather your freedom; greater is he who redeemed you, than he who assails you. Whoever shall consent to this one, will be condemned with him; whoever shall believe in Christ, will condemn with him. These things are to come, but from those which have already come, conjecture those which remain.

**24.** Future persecutions of Christians by the kings of the world were foretold: they came to pass, the slaughter of martyrs was made, and those who did these things thought they could by killing put an end to the Christians. The Church grew by its own blood, the persecutors were conquered, those who suffered persecution conquered. This too was foretold. We find in the holy Scriptures that the kings themselves too would submit their necks to the yoke of Christ[^50], by whom at first the persecution of the Church seemed as it were to be feared. And this has been done, brothers: now the cross of Christ is on the foreheads of kings, kings adore what the Jews mocked. And because it was said thus: *God chose the weak things of this world to confound the strong, and the ignoble things of this world he chose, and the things that are not as though they were, that the things that are might be brought to nothing*[^51], our Lord Jesus Christ came for the salvation not only of the poor, but also of the rich, not only of the common people, but also of kings. Yet he was unwilling to choose kings into discipleship, was unwilling to choose the rich, was unwilling the noble, was unwilling the learned, but chose the poor, the simple, the ignoble, fishermen, where his grace might more shine forth. For he came to give the cup of humility and to heal pride. And if he first called a king, the king would say his dignity was chosen in him; if he first called a learned man, he would say his learning was chosen. Those who were called to humility ought to be called through the humble. And so Christ did not gain a fisherman from an emperor, but an emperor from a fisherman.

**25.** Kings now come to Rome. A great thing, brothers, how it has been fulfilled. When it was being said, when it was being written, none of these things existed. It is wonderful: attend and see, rejoice. Let those be curious who are unwilling to attend to it; we wish them to be curious about these things: let them leave the crimes and trifles of vain curiosities, let them at some time be curious about the divine Scriptures, let them find that the things which they now see were foretold so long ago. For they marvel that in the name of the crucified the human race runs together and flows together, from kings down to those clothed in rags. No age is passed over, no sect, no teaching. For it is not that the unlearned believed and the learned did not believe, or the ignoble believed and the noble did not believe, or women believed and men did not believe, or boys believed and old men did not believe, or slaves believed and free men did not believe. Every age has been called to salvation, every age has now come, every dignity, every abundance and means of man. Now let all be within. Now few have remained outside and still dispute; let them wake up at some time at least at the noise of the world: the whole world cries out.

**26.** Kings come, as I had begun to say, to Rome. There are the temples of the emperors who by their pride exacted divine honors for themselves from men and, because they could—for they were kings and rulers—extorted rather than merited them. From whom could a fisherman extort any such thing? There is the tomb of the fisherman, there is the temple of the emperor. Peter is there in the tomb, Hadrian is there in the temple. The temple of Hadrian, the memorial of Peter. The emperor comes. Let us see where he runs, where he wishes to fix his knees: in the temple of the emperor, or at the memorial of the fisherman? With his diadem laid aside, he beats his breast where the body of the fisherman is, whose merits he considers, in whose crown he believes, through whom he desires to come to God, by whose prayers he feels and finds himself helped. Behold what that crucified and mocked-on-the-cross one has done, behold whence he laid low the nations, not with raging iron, but with mocked wood. Let the proud, then, drink the cup of humility, with Christ humbled. Let them deign to be humble, let them now recognize their medicine, let them come and believe.

**27.** Exhort them, brothers, not only with words, but also with your morals, and we exhort them not to defer any longer. For perhaps some think and say: "Tomorrow I will be a Christian." If tomorrow it is good, today too it is good. For to be a Christian he is not going to ask the astrologer for a day. God made every day. That is a good day for you, on which you have done something good. If, then, it is good to believe in Christ, that the heart may be cleansed by faith and that eye healed which is going to see so great a light, why is it deferred, why has the crow's voice remained in men? "Tomorrow, tomorrow," says the crow, who, sent out from the ark, did not return; the dove returned[^52]. The crow cries "tomorrow," the dove daily groans. Let there not, then, be in you the voice of the deferrer, but the groan of the confessor. Whoever are wearied with hearing, let them pardon the eager. Whoever still wish to hear, let them pardon the wearied, because we too are pressed by time to end the sermon. For we see your so great eagerness in Christ, that you could hear more, but we cannot hold time. Whoever here are who have not believed, behold, we are here, behold, here is the Church: if they wish, let them believe. If they think it must be deferred, which, I think, they ought now no longer to think, let them make room for those going to perform the divine mysteries.

**28.** Now, brothers, both yesterday we said to you and now we say and always we ask, that by living well you gain those who have not yet believed, lest you too have believed in vain. We ask you that, just as the word of God pleases you, so it may please also in your morals, not in the ear alone, but also in the heart, not in the heart alone, but also in your life, that you may be the family of God fit and pleasing to his eyes unto every good work[^53]. Altogether, brothers, we do not doubt that, if you live worthily of God, soon none of those who have not yet believed will remain in unbelief.
