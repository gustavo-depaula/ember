# Sermon 299: On the Birthday of the Apostles Peter and Paul

**(*In Which the Heresy of the Pelagians Is Refuted*)**

**1.** For praising preachers, and such great preachers, of whom we have heard and sung that *their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world*, no words of ours are sufficient, without a doubt. We owe devotion; we do not fulfill your expectation. For today you expect us to praise the apostles Peter and Paul, whose solemn feast this is. I recognize what you expect; and where I recognize it, I falter. For I see what is expected, and from whom. But because their God deigns to be praised by all of us, may his servants not disdain to be praised, however inadequately, by those who serve you.

**2.** As you know, all of you who know the holy Scriptures, the apostle Peter was the first chosen among the disciples whom the Lord chose while present in the flesh. Paul, however, was not among them or chosen with them, but much later, though not unequal to them. Peter, then, is the first of the Apostles, Paul the last; but God, whose servants, heralds, and preachers they are, is the first and the last. Peter is first among the Apostles, Paul last among the Apostles; God is both first and last, with nothing before him and nothing after him. Therefore God, who commended himself as first and last in eternity, joined the first and last Apostles in suffering. Their suffering agrees in its feast day; their lives harmonize in love. *Their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words* *have sounded to the ends of the world*. Where they were chosen, where they preached, where they also suffered, we all know. But how do we know these men themselves, unless because *their sound has gone out into all the earth?*

**3.** When his Epistle was read, we heard Paul foretelling his suffering, already approaching and close at hand: *For I am already being offered in sacrifice, and the time of my release is at hand. I have fought the good fight, completed the course, kept the faith. Henceforth the crown of righteousness is laid up for me, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day; and not only, he says, to me, but to all who love his appearing*. Let us say something about this, for their words, which have gone out to the ends of the world, help us. First, see his holy devotion. He said he was being offered in sacrifice, not dying—not because one who is offered in sacrifice does not die, but because not everyone who dies is offered in sacrifice. To be offered in sacrifice, then, is to die for God. For the word is drawn from sacrifice. Everything sacrificed is slain for God. The Apostle understood to whom his blood was owed in his suffering: he had become a debtor for his own blood, since his Lord's blood had been poured out for him. That one shed his blood and bound everyone in obligation. All of us who receive that faith owe what we receive; and this is because he deigned to make us both debtors and repayers. For who among us, in such need and poverty of lowliness, is able to repay so great a creditor? But, as it is written, *The Lord will give the word to those who proclaim good news with great power:* the word by which they may spread it, the power by which they may suffer. He himself therefore made victims for himself, consecrated sacrifices for himself, filled the martyrs with the Spirit, and equipped confessors with strength. For he said to them, *It is not you who speak*. Therefore, although about to suffer, although about to shed his blood for Christ's faith, he still rightly says, *What shall I repay the Lord for all he has repaid me?* And what occurs to him? *I will receive the cup of salvation and call upon the Lord's name*. You were considering repayment, searching for something to repay; and as if you were about to repay, this occurred to you: *I will receive the cup of salvation and call upon the Lord's name.* Surely you were going to give back? Look, you receive. So what you receive, because you received what you owe, you receive as the means of repayment: a debtor when you receive, a debtor when you repay. For *what,* he says, *shall I repay?* *I will receive the cup of salvation.* So you receive this too, the cup of suffering, the cup of which the Lord says, *Can you drink the cup that I am going to drink?* But see, the cup is already in your hand; suffering already approaches. What do you do to avoid trembling? What do you do to avoid wavering? What do you do so that you can drink what you already carry? What shall I do, he says? There too I will receive; I will be a debtor, because I will call upon the Lord's name. *I,* he says, *am already being offered in sacrifice.* This had been confirmed to him by revelation, for human weakness would not dare promise itself this. His confidence came not from himself, but from the one who gave everything, whom he understood when he said earlier, *For what do you have that you have not received?* *I* *therefore,* he says, *am already being offered in sacrifice, and the time of my release is at hand. I have fought the good fight.* Question his conscience: it does not hesitate, because it glories in the Lord. *The good fight,* he says, *I have fought; I have completed the course, kept the faith.* Rightly you completed the course, because you kept the faith. *Henceforth,* he says, *there remains for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day.*

**4.** And so that he might not seem to boast excessively as though he alone were involved, and to claim the Lord as his own private possession, he says: *Not only, however,* *to me, but to all who love his appearing.* He could not have conveyed better or more briefly what people should do to deserve that crown of righteousness. For we should not all expect to pour out our blood: there are few martyrs, but many faithful. Can you not be offered in sacrifice like Paul? You can keep the faith; by keeping the faith, you love his appearing. For if you fear the Lord's coming, you do not love his appearing. The Lord Christ is hidden now; he will be manifested in his own time, to be a judge justly, having been an accused man unjustly before a judge. He will come—and how will he come? To judge. For he will not be judged again; rather, as we know, as we believe, he will now judge the living and the dead. I question any person attending to me in order to hear me; I question him—let him answer not me, but himself: Do you want this judge to come? I do, he says. Consider what you say: if you speak truly, if you want him to come, consider how he will find you. For he will come as judge: humility has already been extended to you; power is to come. For he will not come in such a way as to be clothed in a body, born of a mother, suck at her breasts, be wrapped in a cradle, placed in a manger; finally, as a young man, to be mocked by a human being, seized, scourged, hung up, and silent while being judged. Do not perhaps await his coming because you still think he will come in humility. He was silent when he was to be judged; he will not be silent when he is to judge. He was hidden here, so that he would not be recognized: *For if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory*. Since, then, he was here hidden in his own power and silent under another's, what we await will be the opposite of that concealment and that silence. For *God* *will come manifestly.* He who first came hidden will come manifestly. There you have the opposite of his concealment; see the opposite of his silence: *Our God will come and will not be silent.* He was silent when hidden, because *he was led like a sheep to sacrifice.* He was silent when hidden, because *like a lamb voiceless before its shearer, so he did not open his mouth.* He was silent when hidden, because *in his humiliation his judgment* *was taken away*. He was silent when hidden, because he was thought to be only a man; but *God will come manifestly, our God, and he will not be silent.* What then of you who were saying, I want him to come? I want him to come, he says; let him come! Are you still not afraid? *Fire will go before him*. If you do not fear the judge, do you not fear the fire?

Indeed, the apostle Paul himself demands it as a debt: *Which,* he says, *the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day.* He will repay because he is just: by his promise he has made himself my debtor. **6.** Look at the Apostle himself........................................  
*and worthy of all acceptance...........................................................................  
into the world sinners.............................................................................  
of whom I am the first. Christ,* he says, *Jesus,* that is, Christ the Savior. For that is what Jesus means in Latin. Let grammarians not ask how Latin it is, but Christians how true it is. For “salvation” is a Latin word. “To save” and “savior” were not Latin words before the Savior came; when he came to Latin speakers, he made these words Latin too. Therefore *Christ Jesus,* Christ the Savior, *came into the world.* And as though we asked, Why?, he says: *To save sinners.* This is why Jesus came. For we read in the Gospel that this very name was interpreted and explained in a certain way: *They will call his name Jesus, for he himself will save his people from their sins*. A *saying,* then, *worthy of all acceptance,* worthy of belief: namely, *that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the first.* Not because he sinned earlier, but because he sinned more than the others. Just as in the professions we call a doctor first, though younger than many in age but superior in skill, or a craftsman first, or an architect first: we commonly speak this way. In this way the Apostle called himself the first sinner. For no one persecuted the Church more grievously. Therefore, if you ask what was owed to the sinners to whom Jesus came, you find nothing owed to sinners but punishment. If you ask what was owed, it is punishment; if you ask what was repaid, it is salvation: salvation in place of punishment. Punishment was owed, salvation was repaid; a penalty was owed, a crown was repaid. Nothing was owed to Paul, formerly Saul, the first sinner, surpassing others in cruelty—nothing was owed to him but punishment, and great punishment. Yet he is called from heaven: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?* He is restrained so that he may spare, so that he may be spared. The wolf is changed into a sheep—into a sheep is too little; rather, into a shepherd. By the voice from above he is killed and made alive, struck and healed. The persecutor is thrown down; the preacher is raised up. What is this grace, if not grace? For what good merit preceded it? It is called grace because it is given freely. *Jesus came,* he says, *into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the first. But for this reason I obtained mercy.* Could he then have said, *The Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day*? If the Lord, the just judge, repays the first sinner on that day, what will he repay but what is owed to the first sinner—great punishment, an eternal penalty? This was owed earlier, and was not repaid. *For this reason,* he says, *I obtained mercy.* I did not receive a debt: *rather, I obtained mercy,* the first sinner, *so that in me Christ Jesus might display all his patience, as a pattern for those who will believe in him for eternal life*. What does *as a pattern* mean? So that any wicked person, anyone entangled in crimes, may not despair of the pardon Saul received. The great physician, that is, Jesus, the great physician, coming to a region of sick people, chose to cure someone whose case was deeply despaired of, so that his medicine might become widely known. Thus the man now like this, who was formerly like that, says, *I am already being offered in sacrifice, and the time of my release is at hand. I have fought the good fight, completed the course, kept the faith.* Were you the one rushing headlong, dragging Christians to death, guarding everyone's clothes when Stephen was stoned, so that you could stone him through everyone's hands? Were you that man? I was, he says, but I am not now. Why were you, and why are you not now? Because I obtained mercy. So you received, Paul, what was not owed to you. Now say confidently what is owed to you; say it now. *Henceforth there remains for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day.* How confidently he demands a debt, when it was granted to him that he should no longer owe punishment! Now say to your Lord, say confidently, say with certainty, say with the fullest trust: Previously I was in my wickedness; I enjoyed your unowed mercy; crown your gifts as a debt. Let this suffice. Let us come to Peter and offer him, too, not a worthy ability but our solemn devotion: from the last to the first, because we too strive from the last things toward the first.

**7.** In the Gospel we have just heard read, the Lord Jesus himself foretold his suffering to Saint Peter, the first Apostle, saying, *When you were younger, you used to gird yourself and go wherever you wished; but when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go.* The Evangelist himself then explained to us what had been said: *The Lord said this,* *to indicate by what death he would glorify God*. The Lord Christ himself foretold his suffering and his cross to him, but now that he loved, not when he denied. For the physician observed both moments in him: sick, he denied; healed, he loved. The Lord showed Peter to that same Peter when, with a certain rash confidence, he had promised to die for Christ, although Christ had come to die for Peter. *Your life,* he says, *will you lay down for me? Truly I tell you, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times*. I will heal you, but first you must recognize that you are sick. So in that foretold denial the Lord showed Peter to Peter; but in that love the Lord showed Christ to Peter. *Do you love me,* he says? *I love you. Feed my sheep*. This once, this again, this a third time. Three times love confessed; three times fear was condemned. And because he loved, his suffering was made known to him. For this was love: to reach the point of suffering through love of Christ.

**8.** But what is that saying, brothers? Whom would it not trouble? *Someone else,* he says, *will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go.* Did Peter, then, reach so great a grace of suffering unwillingly? Look at Paul: *For I am already being offered in sacrifice, and the time of my release is at hand*. In these words he seems to hurry toward suffering with exultation; but to Peter it is said, *Someone else will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go.* Was Paul willing and Peter unwilling? Rather, if we understand, Paul was willing and Peter was willing, and Paul was unwilling and Peter was unwilling. While I explain this as best I can, I need your attention. Death cannot be loved; it can be endured. For if it is loved, those who underwent it for the faith did nothing great. If we saw them rejoicing at banquets, would we call them great men, would we call them brave men? If we saw them surrounded by pleasures, would we praise courage or patience in them? Why? Because they were doing something contrary to pain, contrary to trouble, and were amid joys, pleasures, and delights—would they be praised on that account as great, brave, and most patient? But this is not how we praise the martyrs. Great men, brave men, patient men! Do you want to know that it is to be endured, not loved? Ask its name: it is called suffering. By nature, then, not only human beings but absolutely all living creatures refuse and fear death. The martyrs are therefore great because for the kingdom of heaven they bravely underwent something very hard, and endured troubles while thinking of the promises. See the Lord saying, *No one has greater love than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends*. If this is not hard, what great thing does love do—because it loves pleasures for my sake? No. Rather, because it endures death. *Because of the words of your lips*—this is the martyrs' voice—*because of the words of your lips,* that is, because of your admonitions and promises, *I have kept to hard roads*. Therefore, as far as the condition of nature and the force of habit go, death is refused. But because what will come after death is loved, what we do not want is undertaken so that we may reach what we do want. This is the source of *He will take you where you do not wish to go.* He expressed nature, not devotion. The Lord himself represented this nature of our weakness in himself when, about to suffer, he said to the Father, *Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me*. *For I am already being offered in sacrifice* are the words of someone suffering, not enjoying himself. Our death, then, comes from punishment, handed to us to drink. We, the spreading branches of the human race, received it from the root. The first Adam earned it by sinning. *Sin took its beginning from a woman,* as Scripture says, *and through her we all die*. And: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all people, in whom all sinned*. Therefore both guilt and punishment are in our nature. God made nature without guilt; had it persisted without guilt, punishment would certainly not have followed. From there we came, from there we drew both, and from here we contracted many things. In our nature, then, there are both guilt and punishment; in Jesus' flesh there was punishment without guilt, so that both guilt and punishment might be healed. *Someone else,* he says, *will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go.* This is punishment, but through punishment one advances to the crown. Paul despised the punishment; therefore, looking toward the crown, he despised the punishment and said: I am already being offered in sacrifice; the crown of righteousness is owed to me. The way through is hard, but the destination is great. Peter too knew where he was heading; therefore he too undertook suffering with full devotion. Yet he endured suffering; he did not love it. He endured the suffering and loved what followed; because he loved where he was going, he endured the way he was going.

**9.** We said that both were unwilling and both willing: both, if it could be so, did not want the punishment; yet both alike loved the crown. But let us show that Paul too did not want the punishment. For Peter, the Lord himself became a witness; he also represented you in himself when he said, *Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me.* So the Lord bore witness for Peter, while Paul bore witness for himself. For in one place he says of this mortal body, *We groan, weighed down:* in accordance with what Scripture says elsewhere, *The corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that thinks of many things*. So he says, *We groan, weighed down:* beneath the burden, that is, of a corruptible body. *We groan, weighed down.* If you groan, gladly lay down that burden. Certainly he said that he groaned under this load, that he was weighed down under this burden of a corruptible body. See whether he wants to be stripped of this load beneath which he is weighed down, beneath which he groans. That does not follow; what does he say? *For we do not want to be stripped.* O voice of nature, confession of punishment! The body is heavy, the body is burdensome, the body is corruptible; one groans beneath it, yet does not willingly abandon it or willingly lay it down. *We do not want,* he says, *to be stripped.* Will you remain groaning like this? And if you groan, weighed down, why do you not want to be stripped? No, he says. See what follows: *We do not want to be stripped, but to be clothed over.* Beneath an earthly tunic I groan; toward a heavenly one I hurry. I want to receive that one; I do not want to lay this one aside. *For we do not want to be stripped, but to be clothed over.* So, Paul, let me understand you: what are you saying? Will that great heavenly garment be insulted by coming over these rags of mortality and corruption, with this underneath and that above, this inside and that outside? Far from it, he says; that is not what I mean. I do not want to be stripped, but clothed over. Yet not so that corruption may lie hidden beneath incorruption, but so that *what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.* You who know the Scriptures have rightly cried out. But lest anyone ignorant of the Scriptures think the words that followed were mine, they are Paul's words; all these are the Apostle's words: *We groan, weighed down, for we do not want to be stripped but to be clothed over, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life*. You rightly hold to what you say elsewhere, speaking of the body's resurrection: *This corruptible thing must put on incorruption, and this mortal thing must put on immortality. But when this corruptible thing has put on incorruption, then the saying that is written will be fulfilled: Death has been swallowed up in victory.* What he says in that passage, *so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life,* he says here as *Death has been swallowed up in victory.* Death nowhere: not below, not above; not within, not outside. For *death has been swallowed up* *in victory. Where, death, is your struggle?* This will be said to death at the future resurrection of the body, and at such a transformation that death is swallowed up in victory. *When this corruptible thing has put on incorruption,* death will be told, *Where, death, is your struggle?* That very struggle makes you be taken where you do not wish to go. *Where, death, is your struggle? Where, death, is your sting? Now the sting of death is sin*.

**10.** Surely death does not come from sin? Of what other death was he speaking when he spoke of the body's resurrection? This corruptible thing will be clothed with incorruptibility; death will be swallowed up in victory. This is the body's resurrection. There it will be said, *Where, death, is your struggle?* To what death, if not the death of the body? For the discussion concerns the body's resurrection. *Where, death, is your struggle? Where, death, is your sting? Now the sting of death is sin.* Sin is death's sting, the sting by which death was brought about, not a sting death brought about—just as poison is a cup of death because it causes death, not because it is produced by death. Therefore the Lord ends this punishment in the resurrection; yet he leaves death even to the faithful and the saints as a struggle. Death has been left to you for a contest. For God could have removed death from you once you were justified, but he left it for combat, so that you would have something to despise for the faith. For he did this for those he wished. Enoch was taken away, and Elijah was taken away, and they live. Did their righteousness earn this? Or was it God's grace, God's kindness, and a special grant? To show his power over all things, the Creator has commended to us what he can do.

**11.** Therefore those who say that we do not die because of sin, as regards bodily death, but that our dying belongs to nature and Adam would have died even if he had not sinned, vainly set Enoch and Elijah against us. They speak very thoughtlessly; if they pay attention, they are speaking against themselves. For what do they say? If death comes from sin, why did Enoch and Elijah not die? Do you who say this not see that you say death belongs to nature when you deny that it comes from sin? You say, From nature; I say, From sin. It does belong to nature, but to nature already corrupted, already condemned to this punishment. Thus you say that bodily death belongs to nature; I say it comes from sin. And you ask me: If it comes from sin, why did Enoch and Elijah not die? I answer: Rather, if it belongs to nature, why did Enoch and Elijah not die? Enoch and Elijah live; they have been taken away, and wherever they are, they live. And if a certain inference of faith from God's Scripture is not mistaken, they will die. For *Revelation* mentions two wonderful prophets who will die, rise again in human sight, and ascend to the Lord; they are understood to be Enoch and Elijah, although their names are not given there. And if perhaps you who hold these views have not accepted this Scripture, or if you accept it but disregard it and say, They are not expressly named, let them live, as you think, never to die. Tell me again: If death comes from sin, why have they not died? I reply to you: If death belongs to nature, why have they not died? I say that for them to live, guilt has been ended; you say, if you can, that nature has been ended.

**12.** We have indeed passed from one thing to another as occasion arose, yet what we said concerns the stability of our faith against certain disputants who are becoming harmfully numerous. But let them not overcome our patience, nor yet overthrow our faith. Let us be cautious and watchful against novelties in arguments, human ones, certainly, not divine. Today we celebrate the Apostles' solemn feast; let us hear the Apostle's warning: *Avoid profane novelties of words, for they advance greatly toward impiety*. *But we want you to be wise in good and untainted by evil*. Adam is dead, but that serpent is not dead yet. He whispers and does not cease hissing. The ultimate punishment is reserved for him, but he seeks companions in his condemnation. Let us hear the bridegroom's friend, jealous for the bridegroom, not for himself: *For I am jealous for you with God's jealousy; I have fitted you to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. And I fear,* he says, *that just as the serpent deceived Eve by his cunning, so your minds may be corrupted from the chastity that is in Christ*. We have all heard the Apostle's words; let us all observe them, let us all beware the serpent's poisons. For we cannot say, We did not hear, we did not know, when we have just sung, *Their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world*. The words running to the ends of the earth have come to us: we received them, wrote them down, and appointed readers. The reader is not silent, the expositor labors in childbirth; why does the ambusher not cease?
