# Sermon 374: (Augmented)

**1.** The yearly celebration of this day requires of us a yearly sermon, owed to your ears and to your hearts and, if you listen devoutly, owed also to your conduct. For the whole fruit of our life is righteousness, the wage is eternal life, and the beginning of righteousness is faith. For we have not been called to what we already see, nor do we already hold what has been promised. But because the one who promises is truthful, we must first live in hope, so that we may deserve to live in the reality; what God has promised he will without doubt give. But because what he is to give is great, by delaying he stretches out our desire, so as to make us capable of so great a gift. For he is not going to give the perishing, changing, fragile goods that are in this world, which, when they abound, fill us with fears, and when they depart, fill us with sorrows. But he is going to give—what do you suppose? Something earthly? Far from it! Something heavenly, of the sort we see with the eyes of the body? Even this is cheap in comparison with the promised gift, for the receiving of which hearts are cleansed. For if God were now to give you something belonging to the present time, he would require your eyes for seeing what he gives. But because he will give that *which eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*[^1], as the Apostle says, what is he preparing for you, to whom he may give it? *Blessed*, he says, *are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^2]. Our whole reward, then, will be to see God. Or is it a cheap reward to see the one by whom you were made? You may stretch wide the lap of your desire, you may be greedy in longing for what so great a one has promised: he can give you nothing better than himself. For whatever God has made is cheaper and lower than its maker.

**2.** And where does God's creation begin? With the angels. And where does God's creation end? With earthly, mortal things. Upward there is an end of creation, beyond which is God. Downward there is an end of creation, beyond which is nothing. Begin, then, to count meanwhile the gifts you have, before you have received those for which you have been called. Begin. Look at earthly goods: light, a certain nourishment of the air without which we can never exist, then the fruits of the earth, the springs, the very health of the body, and whatever such things can be mentioned—which had to be touched on briefly. They are gifts of God, they are divine bounties. But do not covet these as something great from the one to whose image you were made. For you see that you still have these in common with the beasts, nor will you find anyone from whom to receive them except from that true God, the creator and bestower of all things; yet not because he gives these too should you now be content with them: he reserves something for his own image which he does not give to the beast. Let us seek, then, what it is, and having been reminded of it, let us desire it; having desired it, let us await it; and that we may be able to attain it, let us do what the one who promised commands.

**3.** There is the wage, here the work. The promiser of the wage is also the giver of the command: the one who foretells what you are to receive commands what you are to do. If you love the wage of faith, do not flee the labor of the work. He will give it; for God is truthful. Whoever promises and does not give fails to give in one of two ways, either because he is a liar or because he cannot. What such thing can we say of God? Is the truth a liar? Is there anything the Almighty cannot do? If, then, he is both truthful and has the means to give, why would he deceive you? Lift up your mind, hope without anxiety: the one who promised is powerful, is eternal. Or do you fear that, after you have done your part, before you receive, a successor will replace the one who promised you? Let us rather see what has been promised. Let us pass over these goods we share with the beasts; let us see what man has as his own, more than the beast, which you already possess: to speak, to understand, to discern the true from the false, the just from the unjust, and finally the very one by whom he was made.

**4.** It is a great work to seek the one whom it is a great reward to find. So man differs from the beasts by eloquence—indeed, he differs from the beasts by speaking, and from certain men by eloquence. This gift of eloquence is great, a great good, but even the wicked have it. It is not common to the beasts, but it is common to the wicked. Many have eloquence and are wicked, and by that very eloquence they ensnare many and seek temporal power for themselves. All such men are to be condemned, but they are nonetheless men. Seek something from God that you do not have in common with the beast, nor even with the wicked man. Now, if I have spoken of eloquence, take this of riches too. For he gave riches not to the beasts but to men. To men it belongs to rule, to command, to govern. Yet many wicked men too have riches: still this earthly good both the good have and the wicked have, and the good do not have and the wicked do not have. Seek something higher. Why do you fear that, when you have found something choice that can be given neither to the beasts nor to wicked men, the Almighty cannot give it to you—he who has called you to do the work and to receive the gift? "I have found something." What are you going to find? The wicked too have talent, the wicked too have memory, the wicked too—as I have said—have riches, the wicked too have temporal honors and power. What are you going to find?

**5.** Oh, if you would listen to me, you would seek nothing from God except God. Surely the one to whom he gives this, he also makes good. What is received in such a way that it sets one apart from the wicked cannot be common to the wicked. For there is one kind of good with which you may do something good, and another kind of good by which you may become good. All goods other than God are such that you may do well with them; God is such a good that you become good by him. You have gold: it is good, but as something with which you may do good. You have eloquence: it is good, but as something by which you may work well. You have bodily health: use it well. For many have been corrected through sickness and cast headlong through health. Many have grown sick by their good and recovered by their ill. So even bodily health, which is the poor man's patrimony, unless it is turned to good use, is harmful too. A sharp mind is a great good, but still of the sort that the good can use well and the wicked badly: it is not yet the good by which you become good. All the perversities of all errors, all the sects of false dealing and impiety, have had men of great talent as their authors. Men of no account did not give them birth; they were established by sharp men. None of the theatrical trifles were invented, to the ruin of the human race, except by ingenious men. "These are all hostile, certainly base." They are base, you say. How then is something honorably watched that is basely performed? You confess that this is base, and yet you support its being able to exist. I do not know which I should call worse: the seller of baseness or the buyer. And yet these things were invented and established by the sharpest minds. So talent too is good, but only if you use it well. So far I have mentioned goods with which you may do good, not goods by which you may become good. If that good is added by which you become good, you use the other goods well. But if that good by which you may be made good is not added, how will you, being wicked, be able to use the other goods well? What is the good by which you become good? It is God himself.

**6.** There is a certain reality that is beheld by the mind. For the eyes do not have what they may see, while the mind does not have it. Which is better: the body or the mind? I think that even lifeless bodies, if they could, would answer that the mind is better. But the body will not tell me that the mind is better than the body. So I ask in this way: Which is better, the one that rules or the one that is ruled? Here, I think, not even the beasts would hesitate to answer that the ruler is better. Who, then, rules? Surely the mind, which is better than the body. If the mind is absent, the windows of the body, though they stand open, have no one to see through them. The eyes stand open, the ears stand open: if the dweller is away, of what use are the unbarred doors? So the mind, through the eyes, perceives certain things: light, colors, shapes; the mind, through the ears, certain things: voices and sounds; the mind, through smell, certain things: all odors; the mind, through the sense of taste, all flavors; the mind, through the whole body, the hard and soft, the rough and smooth, the cold and hot, the light and heavy. The eye does not hear and the ear does not see, yet the mind both sees through the eye and hears through the ear. It cannot do both through either, yet it alone can do what it does through either. Does the mind do nothing of itself? Can it act through the body, and not of itself? It sees white and black through the body; can it not, of itself, see the just and the unjust? And yet there are many who think that these alone exist which are seen through the body, doing wrong to their own minds, ungrateful to God, by whom they were made to his image. Within is the dweller; it has other eyes of its own.

**7.** If I wished to display gems, if I wished to display gold and silver, finely wrought vessels, precious garments, and whatever is counted precious and most beautiful among the riches of men—if, finally, I wished to display the sky, not a paneled ceiling but the starry vault of the poor—what would I require? Eyes. When I want to show the just and the unjust, what do I require? To whom do I show it? But perhaps I have nothing to show. For some have thought this too: that justice does not exist by nature, but is established by opinion—that is, that this is just (or rather, is called just) which men have willed to be just by a certain compact of human society, and not by any nature of justice. Is there then, after all, no nature of justice? We labor to show it—and yet we ought above all to know it, since it is by the mind alone that we know it—we labor to show what the nature of justice is, and we do not labor to show that gold exists, that earth exists, that the sky exists. Do I labor to show these things? Every man cries out that what can be seen through the body exists, and many deny that what cannot be seen except through the mind exists.

**8.** It would be a wonder if this mind were not sick, a wonder if its eyes were not wounded or even extinguished! Whence are they to be restored? Whence healed? The doorway of healing is faith. For in order that he may see what he does not see, he must believe that there is something he may see, but that he himself is not yet such as to see it. "Perhaps I can: show it." But to whom shall I show it? Indeed, not even I can; but he can who has shown it even to me, if I see anything in this matter. The physician comes to the sick man who has wounded eyes, or perhaps already lost—he has forgotten that he once saw, or perhaps from his birth has never seen—but the physician is nonetheless so great that he can by treatment drive out the old blindness: he promises that there is something he can show, if the man will let himself be cured. But further, if the man, before he sees, does not believe, how will he be cured? "There is," the physician says, "something for you to see, but you will see it then, when your eyes have been made sound." And the man says: "Unless I see, I will not be cured." How absurd and perverse a reply: to want to see first in order to be cured—a man who, if he could see, would have no reason to be cured. Believe, then, the physician who is going to show it, so that you do not resist the one who is going to cure you. "Show me," he says. What shall I show you? "God." To whom? "To me," he says, "if you want me to believe. For I do not believe except what I see." Whom do you ask to have shown, to whom do you ask it shown, by whom do you ask it shown? God is to be shown, he is to be shown to a man, by a man he cannot be shown: he shows himself. I can admonish you what to do, that you may deserve to see.

**9.** "There is no God at all," he says. Everyone shudders. Who would say this: "There is no God"? And yet the psalm was not silent about it: *The fool said in his heart, There is no God*[^3]. Because it is the sort of saying that everyone shudders at, *he said it in his heart*; he did not entrust it to tongue or voice. Yet I too ask something. "Show me God," he says. Brother, let us seek together. Still, even if I try to show it and am not able—for it perhaps takes time, it takes some effort, it takes some instruction—still I too ask something. You say, "Show me God"; I say, Show me your soul. You ask of me the loftiest thing, I a lowly one; you ask the one from whom you are absent, I the one that is present; you ask one to be sought, I the one who is seeking. See: if I were to say that you have no soul, what would you do? For if we must believe the eyes alone, you have no soul; unless I see your soul, I will not believe it exists. You will say: "The soul indeed cannot be seen with the eyes, but it can be shown through its works. You see me walking, you hear me speaking; I speak, you answer, and do you doubt that I have a soul?" Meanwhile I have not seen your soul, but I have seen the works of your soul. If through the works of your soul I come to know your soul, through the works of God come to know God. There is a soul, because it moves the flesh; and is there no God, who moves the world? Does the order of things not move you—earthly things subjected to heavenly, the alternations of night and day, the arrangement of the seasons, the brightness of the sun by which the clearness of day is filled, the moon and stars by which the darkness of night is tempered, the fruits springing from the earth, the flowing springs? The very animals that are born on the earth are born with life: what is made lives, and does the one who makes it not live? There is, then, a God; far be it from us to doubt it! But perhaps the one who doubts still seeks; yet let him seek in such a way that he first believes, lest by not believing he fail to deserve to find what he seeks.

**10.** There is, then, a God, and the question is rather how he is to be worshiped than whether he exists. What then? Let us say something on this point too. I think it easy for me to speak to Christians: that God is to be worshiped just as he himself commanded to be worshiped. For if a Christian asks me how God commanded that he be worshiped, I do not bring forward my own word, but I read aloud the book to which he is without doubt subject by the merit of his faith. For it is not lawful for him to doubt the divine writings: how God willed to be worshiped, he also willed to be written down; what he willed to be written down, he willed to be read aloud; and he gave to that same Scripture so high a peak and summit of authority that he set all the authors of other books beneath its feet. For there have been men who wrote what they wished, as they wished. Whose book has been set on so high a summit of authority that the world answers it "amen"? When perhaps we are dealing with someone not subject to so great an authority of the books, who contends with me and says, "Men wrote these things for themselves," what shall I do? How shall we prove that these writings are divine?

**11.** Apart from that governance of nature by which the world is ruled, men have shuddered at certain divine works only when there were marvelous deeds and words. Nature is indeed full of miracles, but everything marvelous has grown cheap through its constancy. In short, let one man who has lived among men rise from the dead, and the divine work is proclaimed by all; yet so many who did not exist are born daily, and no one marvels. Christ turned water into wine: a great miracle; but who else does this in the vine every year? Do you think it a small thing to be marveled at, that moisture is drawn from the earth, turned into the quality of that wood, runs through the branches, unfolds into leaves, brings forth even swelling clusters, ripens the unripe, gives color to the ripe? Ask the root whence all these things come to pass. What numbers, what measures, what art of working in so slight and contemptible a form? I still marvel at the workmanship of the root; the grain of seed is to be marveled at still more. How small, how almost nothing, where nonetheless are all the numbers of the future root, trunk, branches, fruits, and leaves—and the working numbers drawing up the sap, turning it into a work so beautiful and delightful. These are the stupendous works of the Creator. You have not yet ascended into heaven to see him present, and on earth you already find him at work. These things need someone to consider them. For what is more marvelous than such works? Yet because they are daily, as I said, they have grown cheap. Therefore God, wishing to rouse the minds of men, reserved for himself certain works not greater, but certainly rarer. For it is more to make a man than to raise him up. But because no one any longer marveled at the one who daily makes, he showed himself at times as the one who raises up. He granted light to the blind, hearing to the deaf, speech to the mute. The one who does these things without a miracle in seeds did them with a great miracle in men.

**12.** These things have been written down, these things are read. "But now," he says, "they do not happen, and I fear that these things too were written by men and were not done." We sometimes endure such questioners; you indeed ought not to be such, but to be able to answer such men. I know that I speak almost superfluously to believers, but let me be as if persuading unbelievers, so that you may be equipped against unbelievers. Certain men say—few indeed now, but not none: "These things were written, but they were not done." How then shall I prove that they were done? Surely every miracle is either in a deed or in a word. Marvelous deeds are those done beyond the accustomed course and order of nature; marvelous words are those by which future things are announced. Accordingly, if you will not believe the marvelous deeds that have been written down to be read, at least believe the marvelous words, which are shown to be true by their very effect. For the one who related to you the marvels of the past has shown his trustworthiness, when you behold present the things he foretold as future. Or is divination perhaps not divine—divination, which even took its name from this, and to which the curiosity of men is so given over that many today refuse for that reason to be Christians, while they want it to be permitted them to consult the possessed, the astrologers, the augurs, and—what else shall I say?—the magi? But the power of Christ extinguishes the works of this art too, the power that drew the Magi from so distant a nation to adore him.

**13.** They came to adore the infant, the Word of God. Why did they come? Because they saw an unusual star. And how did they recognize that it was Christ's? They could indeed see the star; but could it speak to them and say, "I am the star of Christ"? Without doubt it was made known in another way, by some revelation. Still, a king had been born in an unusual manner, one who was to be adored even by foreigners. Had not kings been born before in Judea, or throughout the whole earth among the various nations? Why was this one to be adored—and adored by foreigners—not with a terrifying army, but in the poverty of the flesh, with the majesty of his power hidden? When he was born, he was adored by the Israelite shepherds, to whom the angels announced him. But the Magi were not of Israel: they worshiped idols or the gods of the Gentiles, that is, demons, by whose deceptive power they were deceived. They saw, then, a certain unusual star, and they marveled: without doubt they asked whose sign it was, this thing so new and unaccustomed that they saw, and surely they heard. But you will say: "From whom did they hear?" Surely from angels, by some admonition of revelation. Perhaps you ask: "From good angels, or from evil ones?" Even the evil angels, that is, the demons, confessed Christ to be the Son of God. But why should they not have heard it from good angels too, since in the adoring of Christ their salvation was now being sought, and iniquity was not being condemned? The angels, then, could also have said to them: "The star you have seen is Christ's; go and adore him where he has been born," and at the same time make known of what sort and how great is the one who has been born. And they, having heard this, came and adored. They offered as gifts gold, frankincense, and myrrh, according to their custom; for such things they were accustomed to offer to their gods.

**14.** But before they did this, before they found him in the city where he had been born, they came asking where the king of the Jews had been born. Could they not have learned this too by revelation, just as they had learned that the star belonged to the king of the Jews who was to be adored even by foreigners? Could not the same star have led them to that city, just as it later led them to the place where Christ was, an infant with his mother? It surely could have; yet it was not done, so that they might inquire this of the Jews. Why did God will this to be inquired of the Jews? So that, while they point out the one in whom they do not believe, they might be condemned by their own testimony. Notice that even now it happens. The Magi are the firstfruits of the Gentiles: the greater the impiety from which they are freed, the greater the glory they give to the one who frees them. They ask, *Where is he who has been born king of the Jews?*[^4] Herod, hearing the name of king, trembles like a rival. He summons those learned in the Law and asks them, according to the Scriptures, to point out where Christ was to be born. They answer, *In Bethlehem of Judea*[^5]. The Magi went on and adored; the Jews who had pointed the way remained behind.

**15.** O great mystery! Today, from the books of the Jews, we convince the unbelievers, that they may become believers; through their books we show the pagans what they refuse to believe. For sometimes the pagans put this sort of question to us, when they see the things that are written being so fulfilled that they cannot deny at all that the things foretold and read aloud in the sacred books are presented through the name of Christ among all the nations—in the faith of kings, in the overthrow of idols, in the changing of human affairs; and sometimes they are so moved that they say: "You saw these things happen, and you wrote them down as if they had been foretold." A certain poet of theirs did this: those who have read it recognize it. He related that someone went down to the lower world and from there came into the region of the blessed, and that the future princes of the Romans were shown to him—princes whom the very man who wrote this already knew to have been born. For he narrated things past, but wrote them down as though they had been foretold as things to come. "So you too," the pagans say to us, "saw all these things happen, and wrote yourselves books in which they are read as though foretold as things to come." O glory of our King! Rightly were the Jews conquered by the Romans, yet not destroyed. All the nations subdued by the Romans passed over into Roman law; this nation was both conquered and remained in its own law; and, as regards the worship of God, it keeps its ancestral custom and rite. Even with its temple overthrown and its former priesthood extinguished, as was foretold by the prophets, they still keep circumcision and a certain custom by which they are distinguished from the other nations. For the sake of what else, if not for the sake of testimony to the truth? The Jews are scattered everywhere, carrying the books in which Christ has been foretold—and, just as he was foretold, presented—so that he may be shown even to the pagans. I bring forth the book, I read the prophet, I show that the prophecy has been fulfilled. The pagan doubts, lest perhaps I have invented this myself: my enemy holds this book, handed down to him of old by his forefathers. From it I convict them both: the Jew, because I have come to know that what was prophesied there has been fulfilled; the pagan, because I did not invent it.

**16.** Therefore let the demons not seduce, with a show of divination, the unwary and those who are wrongly curious about temporal things; and let them not, deceiving the impious with proud arrogance, demand for themselves the honor of their sacrifices. True divine predictions belong to the one true God. True sacrifice is owed to the one true God, the figures of which were formerly foreshadowed in frankincense and victims, so that divine providence, foretelling in many ways what was to come about in one way, might show how great it was. For this too, among the other things foretold as to come in Christian times, was prophesied: that those things which had formerly been offered to God in sacrifices were utterly to be changed. "Why, then," you say, "were they commanded, if they were to be changed?" O sick man, do not give the physician advice on how you are to be cured. One man, Adam, filled the whole earth by his offspring. The human race, like one man, a great sick man lying from east to west, is to be cured: a great sick man, but a greater physician. Let us therefore take a comparison from the very medicine of the human art. The physician comes to the sick man and says: "Take this in the morning, but that in the afternoon." And the sick man says to the physician: "Why not in the afternoon the same thing as in the morning?" Will the physician not rightly answer him: "You can be sick, you cannot cure yourself; leave the counsel of your health to the art"? "Then," says the sick man, "the art is inconsistent, ordering one thing first, another afterward." "Be quiet rather, and be healed. The art is consistent; it knows what to apply in the morning, what in the afternoon. It has not changed, but your sickness is changeable. It knows what is expedient and when—it which comes to the aid of changeable diseases according to the diversity of times." So, then, for the benefit of the human race, certain things were of use in the earlier times, and others are of use in the later. You ask why? Be the physician's friend, and perhaps you will come to know—lest perhaps even today you suppose that we ought not go to make offering to God except with a bull, with a ram, with frankincense.

**17.** "Show," someone says, "that God commanded these things and foretold that they were to be changed, and I will believe—lest perhaps you say these things to me out of yourself, and not on divine authority." Hear, then, the words of God, a few out of many: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord*[^6]. The prophet speaks. If you think this was invented by Christians, let the Jew bring forth the book. How then can it be I, when my enemy carries it from of old? Let the book, then, be brought forth from the library of the Jews, and let us read aloud from it. What are we going to read? *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new covenant*[^7]. "But perhaps he is speaking of that covenant which the Jews received, and he calls it new because, when our fathers received that which was given through Moses on Mount Sinai, it had not existed before among the human race." He has reminded you well, but wait: I ask for patience, until I read aloud the rest. Hear of the New Covenant: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new covenant*. I do not yet say the rest. Who is it that speaks? Jeremiah. When did Jeremiah prophesy? Long after Moses, through whom the first covenant was given. If, then, Jeremiah, who says, *Behold, the days are coming*, speaks of the future, *and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new covenant*, there is no reason to say that the covenant given through Moses is meant. Therefore he calls another covenant new. But still, hear the rest as well: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new covenant, not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers, in the day when I took their hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt*[^8]. What is more evident? For the first covenant, which is called the old, was then given through Moses.

**18.** Come now, brothers, attend to the divine sayings. We read what was foretold, we behold it fulfilled, and do we still doubt the divine authority of the books? Let no one, then, now say: "Behold, the Magi gave frankincense to Christ; why do we too not offer incense to Christ?" First, notice that the Magi gave frankincense; they did not burn it. "But why," you say, "did he accept this gift?" As if these men were going to forbid that any creature be offered to the Creator! God made frankincense—not the demons; God made myrrh; God made gold. The Magi sin by giving these to demons and thereby honoring them, using the creature to the injury of the Creator. Yet learn that today they gave such things as they were accustomed to give to their gods. Christ did not, however, permit them to give such things without reason: they were signs rather than gifts. He accepted the frankincense as God, the gold as king, the myrrh as one about to die, for his burial. But at that time not only frankincense but also the victims of cattle were offered, both by the pagans and by the Jews—that is, both by those who worshiped many false gods and by those who worshiped the one true God. These things, I say, were offered to God according to the Old Testament; but he changed this in the New Testament. *Behold*, he says, *the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new covenant, not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers, in the day when I took their hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt*[^9].

**19.** "But," someone says, "he did not speak of sacrifices." Do you wish, then, to hear something of sacrifices? That God foretold it would come about that all those sacrifices would be taken away and one sacrifice given that truly cleanses sins—the body of Christ—the faithful know. And what I say, I know is not understood by all; but let those who understand rejoice, and live worthily of so great a mystery. But those who do not yet understand have it in their power to change their life, to receive the sacrament of change, and to know what is offered by the faithful, what is received. For there is that which was foretold: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^10]. For the first priesthood was according to the order of Aaron, but afterward it was said to our priest—and this through a prophet, long before he came in the flesh: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*. Therefore the order of Aaron was changed, and the order of Melchizedek came. According to the order of Aaron, the victims of cattle; according to Melchizedek, the body of Christ. "You have not yet said," someone says, "whether God foretold that those sacrifices would cease." Behold, I read it to you, lest perhaps something escape my memory. For I, brothers, did not learn these letters from my youth, and other things, which is worse, superfluous things, I can recite by memory. But these things, which I did not study from boyhood, I cannot recite unless I look at the books. Or perhaps it is more useful that you hear, not from my mouth but from the divine book, what you may healthfully come to know. Hear now, and do not marvel that one does not now come to Christ with frankincense, since he instituted another sacrifice, of which all the things of the past were shadows.

**20.** Among many things the prophet Isaiah says—whose book I carry in my hands. What does Isaiah say? *There is no reason for you to remember the things of old, nor to take the former things into your mind*[^11]. Behold now, brothers, the abolition of the old things is manifest. "But still," you say, "he has said nothing about sacrifices. Perhaps he changed other things; these he did not change." Although *nor to take the former things into your mind* is general, and although I might be allowed to understand here what the apostolic teaching afterward taught me—what I should offer, what I should no longer offer (for I have an expositor of this obscurity, I have a teacher telling me in what sense I am to take this)—still, the prophet himself does not permit a man to conjecture whatever he pleases: for he says openly what you are about to hear. Hear, then: *There is no reason for you to remember the things of old, nor to take the former things into your mind. For I will make new things which shall now spring forth, and you shall understand*[^12]. What is this: *For I will make new things which shall now spring forth, and you shall understand*? That is: I will make new things which did not exist, so that you may be able to understand those which did exist. A beast was slain, blood was poured out, and through blood God was appeased. Is God truly appeased through blood, and does God desire blood, and is God delighted by the smoke of sacrifices? Or does the one who creates all things, who gives you all things, seek the odor of frankincense or of the other spices? Far be it that you should believe so! He is fed by your devotion—a devotion useful indeed to you, not to him whom you serve. Every man who is the slave of a human master serves his master for his master's benefit, and in turn that master looks after his slave for his slave's benefit. God is not like this. Whoever serves him serves him for his own benefit, not for God's. And do I perhaps say this out of myself? Hear the prophet: *I said to the Lord*—what?—*You are my Lord*. Why? *Because*, he says, *you have no need of my goods*[^13]. You have the matter settled; there is no reason for you to doubt: God has no need of your goods. Do not, then, believe that your God has need of such sacrifices, but seek what they teach, what they signify. Formerly blood was poured out in the victims, because the blood to be poured out in the one true victim was being foretold—the blood of your Lord, the blood that is your price, the blood by which the bond of your debt would be blotted out, that is, by which the old guilt of your sin would be abolished. It has come, it has been poured out, he himself is offered. *Let the day now dawn, and let the shadows be removed*[^14]. *There is no reason for you to remember the things of old, nor to take the former things into your mind. For I will make new things which shall now spring forth, and you shall understand*[^15]. Now at last we understand why those things went before which foretold the things to come. All things are interpreted with reference to Christ, all things have their end in Christ. *I will make*, he says, *new things which shall now spring forth, and you shall understand*. Before all these new things came about, the old things were carried on and were not understood.

**21.** What follows next? *I will make a way in the wilderness*[^16]. In what wilderness? Surely that of the Gentiles, where there was no worship of the true God. *I will make a way in the wilderness, and rivers in the dry land*[^17]. Nowhere among the Gentiles were the prophets read; now through all the nations their Scripture is poured out. See, *rivers in the dry land*. *The beasts of the field will bless me*. What are *the beasts of the field*, if not the Gentiles, who are understood? *The beasts of the field will bless me, and the sirens, the daughters of sparrows*[^18]. Certain impious souls, daughters of demons, *will bless me*. Whence, if not by deserting the devil, turned to Christ? "But these things are still obscure, and according to our ability they are interpreted as something rather plain. Tell us where it is shown that the sacrifices of victims displeased God." If I could not read aloud something rather plain, I would not dare to take this book into my hands. Let your love, then, listen with patience. *The beasts of the field will bless me, and the sirens, the daughters of sparrows*. Why *will they bless*? Hear what follows: *Because I have given water in the desert, and rivers in that place where there was no water, that I might give drink to my chosen race*[^19]. To what race of yours? *To my people, whom I have acquired for myself. I have not now called you Jacob, nor have I made you labor, Israel. To what end have I sheep for whole burnt offerings?*[^20] Come now, brothers. I do not know whether there is anyone who would say to me: "I do not understand. You say what you please, you interpret what you please." Now I have become not a debater but a reader. Refer this to that with which we began, where he says: *There is no reason for you to remember the things of old, nor to take the former things into your mind*[^21]. For this is apparent: that God first commanded these things to be done for the sake of a certain sacrifice, foretelling that righteous blood was to be poured out, and foreshadowing it through the likeness of the victims. For now what does he say? *To what end have I sheep for whole burnt offerings? For neither in your sacrifices have you honored me, nor have you served me in your sacrifices. For I did not make you for this, that you should labor over frankincense*[^22]. Lest perhaps someone say that he forbade victims but permitted frankincense: *For I did not make you for this*, he says, *that you should labor over frankincense, or that you should buy me incense with silver; nor have I desired the fat of your sacrifices*[^23].

**22.** Let us dare to say to the Lord our God: "And why did you first institute these things?" Here now we seek understanding. In these things there was not an abolition, but an attestation, of sins. What I have said, I know to be obscure and somewhat in need of being made clear. But I will say it briefly, since I have already said much; if perhaps I am less able to explain this because of the shortness of time, the Lord will be present, that I may be able to at another time. Yet I say this: that people was such that it had certain wise, holy, righteous men, but it also had a carnal crowd, not knowing why these things were commanded, doing rather than understanding; but now, through this prophet, he briefly shows why he commanded those things. When he had said, *nor have I desired the fat of your sacrifices*, and other such things, at once, as if it were asked why he had then commanded them, he added and said: *But in your sins and iniquities you stood before me*[^24]. For all those things availed as a testimony against the sinners. Why this? Except to lay low the neck of pride. Why this? Except because Christ was to come with grace, blotting out the bond of sins, and the Jews were going to say: "We are righteous." But what does the Apostle say to them? *For all have sinned and lack the glory of God*[^25]. "Whence," he says, "do you prove that we have sinned?" "The sacrifices you offered for sins speak as testimonies against you." This is what God says: *Neither have I desired the fat of your sacrifices; but in your sins and iniquities you stood before me*[^26]. So the sacrifices you offered convicted you; they did not cleanse you. Now, then, let the guilty people, their pride broken, confessing their sickness, seeking health, say to themselves: "What then shall I do? If my sins were not cleansed by those sacrifices, whence shall I be cleansed?"

**23.** Hear what follows: *It is I, it is I who blot out your iniquities, that you may be justified*[^27]. *It is I, it is I*—not a bull, not a ram, not a goat, not any spices, not frankincense, but *it is I, it is I who blot out your iniquities, that you may be justified*. How greatly he commended his grace! Lest anyone glory in the merits of works or in the abundance of victims, it was not enough to say once, *It is I*, but, by doubling it, he commended it more vehemently: *It is I, it is I*. He himself is the physician, he himself the medicine. The physician, because he is the Word; the medicine, because *the Word was made flesh*[^28]. He himself is the priest, he himself the sacrifice. *It is I, it is I who blot out your iniquities, that you may be justified*. Lest perhaps, because he says, *I blot out your iniquities*, it should always delight you to sin, he blots them out for this end, *that you may be justified*—that is, that, your former iniquities being blotted out, you may afterward live rightly, so that you may receive what he promised. Rightly too those Magi, the firstfruits of the Gentiles—in whom, as much as sin had abounded, so much grace abounded all the more—being divinely admonished *not to return to Herod, went back by another way*[^29]. He who then changed the way of the Magi himself even now changes the life of the wicked. His manifestation in the flesh—which in Greek is called Epiphany—the Gentiles, justified in the Spirit, celebrate together on today's festival, so that the festival may renew our memory, that devotion may keep piety vigorous, that gathering together may make love fervent, and that truth may shine out upon the envious.
