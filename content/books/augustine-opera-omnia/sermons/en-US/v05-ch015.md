# Sermon 159/B

**1.** The divine readings, which feed us spiritually, admonish what we should dispense to you as you wait and attend, and that, as it were from the Lord's storehouse, of which we are the dispensers, we should set something before the hungry. The apostolic reading has been declared to us in these words, which your Holiness recalls with us: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God, how incomprehensible are his judgments, and untraceable his ways. For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor, or who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid to him? For from him and through him and in him are all things; to him be glory unto ages of ages, amen*[^1]. But that the Apostle might exclaim, and as if terrified by a certain depth of the judgments of God might say, *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*, he had said above, *God has shut up all in sin, that he might have mercy on all*[^2]. After this sentence, then, where he says, *God has shut up all under sin, that he might have mercy on all*—because truly there is something deep in this, that men were first made manifestly guilty in their own conscience, so that they could be helped, confessing—he exclaimed, *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*. Where is *the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*? In this, that *God has shut up all in sin, that he might have mercy on all*. By what sin? Of unbelief. For he used this word: *God has shut up*, he says, *all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*. May the Lord our God himself be present, then, whose riches the Apostle, praising them, exclaims over, and deign to impart to us something of those hidden and deep riches of his, so that what we feel to be inexplicable we may in some way speak—not so as to explain it, but so as to commend the inexplicable things. For the Apostle seems, by a certain human infirmity, to have as it were failed in explaining what he rejoiced in beholding. He saw something that the tongue could not explain, he beheld in the heart that for which he was less fit in words, and he did not find how to make us attentive to what he saw, except by exclaiming and lifting up our hearts, saying, *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*; that our hearts, lifted up, might be directed to him whose riches, seeing, he exclaimed over, not to the mouth of the feeble dispenser who could not explain those riches. This, then, according to our small measure, may we have done, and may we have directed your hearts to him whose we all are, and under whom, one Master, we are all fellow-disciples in this school, where are the riches of God, where is *the depth of the riches*, and where are *his unsearchable judgments and untraceable ways*, because *he has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*.

**2.** He who *has shut up all in unbelief* seems to be angry, but he who has mercy on all is calm. Therefore the Apostle's passage agrees with the Psalm: *God, you have repulsed us and destroyed us, you were angry and have had mercy on us*[^3]. Hear the one who is angry and shows mercy. *God has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*. What did the Lord our God wish to do? First to be angry, to repulse, to humble, and afterward to help, to call back the one turned away, to hear the one turned back, to aid the one heard, to change the one aided, to crown the one changed. Join other testimonies of the Scriptures. The voice itself of a certain one man laboring on this earth—that is, of Adam himself, of the human race (which human race, nevertheless, the second man from heaven did not abandon, that, having first been earthly, afterward they might become heavenly[^4]; for because they were humbled, hence earthly; because cast down, hence earthly; because repulsed, hence earthly; but because he who repulsed and cast down and humbled had mercy on us, hence heavenly)—let us hear, then, the voice of that same man saying elsewhere, *Before I was humbled, I offended*[^5]. Groaning in his lowliness, he acknowledged his sin; he ascribed iniquity to himself, righteousness to God. For what does he say? *Before I was humbled*—which is the punishment that God inflicted—*I*, he says, *offended*. Lest God who humbled me seem unjust, my offense came first, my humiliation followed. *The Lord my God is therefore a just judge*[^6]. For I would not come to this humiliation, unless I first offended. And because his very humiliation seems indeed to sound of the wrath of God the judge, but pertains to mercy, hear the voice of the same one elsewhere: *It is good for me*, he says, *that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*[^7]. Let your charity attend to what he says: *Before I was humbled, I offended*. In his punishment he seems to groan, in his fetters to sigh, in this mortality and earthly infirmity to seek the help, by confessing, of the one he had offended by sinning. For these words sound thus: *Before I was humbled, I offended*—that is: I do not impute my humiliation to you, my God: I did what is wicked, you did what is just.

**3.** With these words agree those words that we sang. For he who says, *God, you have repulsed us and destroyed us*, the same says, *Before I was humbled, I offended*. For you heard that God repulsed; you heard that God cast down—that is, threw down from the height to the earth. You heard it. Seek the cause why God did this: *Before I was humbled, I*, he says, *offended*. You have heard your preceding offense and the consequent righteousness of God; hear that even that very righteousness of God, which humbled you, indicates not only the severity of a just judge, but also the clemency of one who has mercy. For he says, as I said a little before, *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*. What then, my brothers? Was God angry when he humbled, or did he have mercy? If the humiliation profited us nothing, let it be attributed to excessive severity of God; which, if it were so, we could not complain of his injustice. For let the sinner pay back his desert; let the proud and unjust man not flatter himself; let him first find out what he deserves, that he may so come to know what God has bestowed. Does the heart of any sinful man, then, dare to announce to itself anything but punishment, anything but most just penalty? Or if punishment has followed a man's iniquity, can it be said to the just judge, "You did wrong to condemn the sinner"? Let us, then, sinners, announce this to ourselves, let us so confess in our punishments both our offenses and the righteousness of our God: for thus we shall deserve, in our very punishment, to find the mercy of God. This, dearly beloved brothers, no one finds, except the one who has first humbled himself. And because I am going to speak as I can, I do not think any of you will understand what I am going to say, unless he first suppresses the smoke of pride, by which the eyes of the mind are darkened, so that the mercy of God in the very punishment cannot be understood.

**4.** But first see this in daily life itself—for from there you can have a way to understanding, because he did not forsake the mortality of men—by certain similitudes in their very deeds, that it may be shown to us that a punishment can be inflicted mercifully. What shall I say? You discipline your servant, and in giving discipline surely, in the very thing in which you seem to punish, you have mercy—but I do not say "to your servant": perhaps you are so angry with your servant that you hate him. You ought not to be so, if you are a Christian; you ought not to be so, if you consider that you are a man; you ought not to be so, if you consider that "servant" and "master" are indeed different names, but "man" and "man" are not different. You ought not so to persecute with hatred the sinning servant. But because men are accustomed to be so, let us reject this similitude, and let us set up a son. No one can do anything but love his sons: for a man is not to be praised who loves his son. *For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have*, says the Lord, *do not the tax collectors do this*[^8]? How much more sons, whom men beget as successors to themselves. No one at all can, by the very right of nature, hate the one he has begotten. Nor is a man to be praised in that which is found in a beast. No one praises a man for loving his sons. You find this not only in gentle cattle: the ferocity of lions grows gentle toward their young, tigers love their young, serpents warm their eggs and nourish their cubs. If, then, the things that seem savage and harsh in creation do not keep their harshness and savagery toward those they beget, what great thing does a man do in loving his son? But I said these things, brothers, that you may see, from the example of sons, that there can be a punishment of one who has mercy, from that thing which no one can hate. Someone sees his son going into pride, exalting himself against his father, claiming for himself more than he ought, wishing to dissolve into delightful trifles, wishing to squander what he does not yet possess; and that one, while he does this, is glad, laughing, rejoicing, exulting; but the father restrains him by rebuke, punishment, scourges, takes away the laughter, brings on weeping, seems to have taken away a good and brought on an evil—see what he took away: gladness; see what he brought on: groaning—and yet, if he had let that gladness go unpunished, he would be cruel; because he forced him to weeping, he is found merciful. If, then, the father who forces to weeping is found merciful, why do we not understand that our Creator too could do what we sang: *God, you have repulsed us and cast us down*? But why this? Surely not unto destruction, not unto perdition? Hear what follows: *You were angry and have had mercy on us*[^9]. Why is he justly angry with you? Join it, since we said, *Before I was humbled, I offended*. What does it profit you that you were repulsed and cast down? *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*[^10].

**5.** Let us now turn our mind back to that apostolic saying: *God has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*[^11]. The first sin of man was pride: so we read in Genesis, so we find in another Scripture. What do we read in Genesis? That man, created and formed, was placed in paradise under a certain law, under a certain commandment[^12]; the commandment imposed on him showed him this: that he was made great in such a way that he had a greater above himself. God, therefore, commanded that humility always be retained by man subject to him—that is, that the humility of man, established under God, be kept. Man was indeed made in the image of God and, as is written in another place, *he gave him the power of holding all things*: all things were under him, but above him was the one who made all things. So, then, man ought to have attended to the things that were under him in such a way that he attended more to the one who was above him; for cleaving to the higher, he would possess the lower more securely; but departing from the higher, he would be made subject to the lower. Just as if we set up three men: one man having a servant, having also a master—as it often happens that servants with savings have servants. Attend: he has a servant, he has a master; he is under one, he is over another; he is higher than the servant, lower than his master. We set up a third, the servant of the servant; the first, the master of the master; the middle, both servant and master: the master of his servant and the servant of his master. That third is nothing but a servant, that first is nothing but a master, that middle is both servant and master. But he securely possesses his servant, if he has not offended his master. And we have indeed spoken of three men: all are of the same kind, all subsist from the same substance and nature. Not so those three: God, and man, and the creature lower than man. For of another kind, and not of the same substance, are the founder and the founded, the maker and the made, the craftsman and the work, the creator and the creation. But the things that have been created are indeed all called, in general, created; but they differ in natures and orders and merits and places. For first are spiritual things and afterward carnal—the very things that God founded, that God made. Spiritual things have the first place, corporeal the last. But the human mind is something spiritual, where the likeness and image of God is imprinted; but corporeal are all the things that we perceive to lie under the senses of the body: they are known to all, they are seen, heard, smelled, tasted, touched; hard, soft, hot, cold, rough, smooth—all these are called corporeal, they are lower. Above all these man is established, but according to the mind, according to the intellect, according to that which is made in him in the image and likeness of God. For God is not circumscribed and enclosed by a corporeal form, so as to have a back on one side and eyes on another; but he is a certain light—nor such as we see with the eyes, nor if you enlarge this which we see with the eyes, spreading it out through the fancy of your thoughts, and make fields of light and mountains of light and trees of light, flitting about through the vanities of your thoughts. Do you wish to understand spiritual light? Seek out whereby you understand.

**6.** Understand, I say, that very light by which you understand. What did I say? You see white and black with the bodily eyes, you are helped from without by the light either of the sun, or the moon, or a lamp, or some little fire. Moreover, if that light from without did not help your eyes, your eyes would lie open in vain and be called eyes without cause. But what in you lies open and is sound—that is, the eye—and what is brought to its aid from without—that is, the light—and what it is for the seeing of which you are helped—that is, colors and shapes—you know and distinguish. This we have said of the eyes. You hear voices, you know whereby you hear. The eyes do not hear, but neither do the ears see. Something is lacking to the eyes for perceiving voices, and something is lacking to the ears for perceiving colors. But to you none of these is lacking, because through the eyes you see, through the ears you hear. You know, then, the things that smell, and you know what member to bring near whereby you may perceive an odor. For you do not bring the ear near to perceive the sweetness of an odor, but you bring near that which God created in you for smelling. Nor, when you wish to taste some dish, do you put it to the ears or the eye: you know there is no sense there distinguishing flavors. And do you wish to perceive whether something is hard or soft, cold or hot? You know that with your whole body you can perceive contact. These things you know. Good. Attend to that inner thing: who is this one within to whom all these senses report what men perceive? For these are as it were instruments, these are as it were subjected to servitude. The inner perceiver is a certain commander to whom these messengers report whatever they find outside. But that inner one, who distinguishes all these things, is surely higher than all these things. So the eye has what it may see, the ear what it may hear, the nostrils what they may smell, the mouth what it may taste, the hand what it may touch, and has the mind nothing that it can behold through itself? The mind indeed perceives white and black, but with the eyes reporting; it perceives in voices the tuneful and the harsh, but with the ears reporting; it perceives in odors the sweet-smelling and the foul-smelling, but with the nostrils reporting; it perceives sweet and bitter, but with the mouth reporting; it perceives hard and soft, but when the touching hand has reported. Can it, then, with the body reporting, perceive things so many and various, and is it through itself not fit to perceive anything, with no member of the body reporting to it? Seek, then, what it perceives through itself, and you will find where the image of God is. White and black it perceived through the eyes, tuneful and discordant the ears reported. And, not to run again through each of these things that lie near the body, the members of the body reported. The just and the unjust—do the eyes report it? The mind distinguishes the just and the unjust and says, "This is just, this is unjust." Seek who reported it. If righteousness is a color, the eyes reported it; if righteousness is a sound, the ears reported it; if it is an odor, the nostrils reported it; if a flavor, the mouth reported it; if it is hardness or softness, the hand reported it. If it is none of these, who reported it, but the inner light? This nature, then, this substance which you see to be excellent—of which, if I wished to speak more fully, time would not suffice—a certain inner thing, a certain divine thing, was made in us in the image and likeness of God, is above all corporeal things, and was so made that all corporeal creation, subject to it, should serve it; but yet the mind itself is not God. For if it were God, would it have sinned? For God is unchangeable. But our mind, because it was created, because it was made, is not what God is. It is changeable. We see even now its very changes. It is wise, it is foolish; it remembers, it forgets; it wills, it does not will; it is delighted, it is saddened. These changeabilities do not fall upon God, who is above the mind, and is the creator of the mind.

**7.** But nevertheless all this which I have spoken of is above the body, is below God, below the master and above the servant. These are the three things of which I was speaking a little before. If, then, three men, though all are men, are ordered by a certain condition of this life, so that one of them is only a master, another of them is only a servant, and another of them is both servant of the master and master of the servant, how much, do you suppose, and how much more easily and more distinctly has the whole creation been ordered? The nature and substance of the mind placed under God, the nature of the whole body placed under the mind. But as I was saying: then he securely possesses his servant, if he has not offended his master; so that mind, if by a certain pride, by which it wished to be of its own power, it had not offended its master, the whole nature of the body would always be subject to it as a servant. But because by pride it offended the Lord, the creation of the body, which had been given into its service, became to it a torment of punishment, a torment of retribution. For now the mind is tortured by the difficulty of the body, when before it had dominion over the whole nature of the body. Just as if that man—for from here you better grasp the more illustrious similitude, because this very thing, that we understand with difficulty, pertains to the punishment by which we were humbled: from custom we give some illustrations. Set those three servants again before your eyes, because you understand this with difficulty, though it is more distinct; for those things are more distinct in proportion as they are more diverse: for God is far other than the mind, and the mind far other than the body. But in those three it is man, and man, and man. The nature is not diverse, but condition makes the order; yet because those are in our custom, we more easily understand them than the things that are more distinct. Now, then, understand this which we say. Make that middle one—because he is a servant in such a way that he is also a master; he is a master in such a way that he is also a servant: servant of the higher, master of the lower—make him, then, to have offended his master. By what did he offend? By a certain pride. For he considered that he too had a servant, and dared to exalt himself against his master, in the very thing by which he seemed to have his servant in his power. He raised himself against his master; his master commanded that he be beaten by his own servant. For that master of the master was the master of both. For that servant did not have his own servant in his power as much as the master of both had both. For when could he despise that master, no one's servant, so as not to beat his own master, when the more powerful master of both commanded it? Our God commanded, then, because we offended him, that we be tormented by our own body; and our body was made mortal, and we began to suffer punishments from there whence we dared to be proud against the Lord. So now we are beaten by our own servant. We are tortured in the torments of our flesh; the Lord humbled us, that we might be flogged by the servant.

**8.** But why did he humble us, that we might be flogged by the servant? Because we first offended: *Before I was humbled, I offended*[^13]. Placed, then, under the lash of your servant, cry out to the Lord your God, and say to him, *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*[^14]. What *righteous decrees of yours*? That, just as I have the body as a servant, you too have me as a servant. And just as I demand that the body obey me, so I ought to have obeyed you. Therefore from this I have learned *your righteous decrees*, as though the Lord were speaking to me from above and saying: "*O wicked servant*, at least now, set in this humiliation, acknowledge whom you offended, and to whom you were subject. Surely you are tortured by your own servant: you have a body and you wish it to obey you in all things, you wish that when you raise your hand, the hand follow; when you raise your foot, the foot follow. And although I wished you to be beaten by your servant, your servant still serves you." For when we wish to walk and to move the body to another place, we command the feet, and they obey; we command the eye to see, when we wish to behold something: it does not contradict us, it turns, it reports to us. We bring the ear near to sounds, at once it reports what sounds; we lift the hand to handle something, it does not resist. For in that the body serves us, it indicates that we are its masters; but in that it resists us, it indicates that we have a Master. But let us see in what your body does not obey you. For example, you can walk ten miles, you wish twenty: it does not obey. You can walk fifty miles, you wish sixty: it does not obey. You wish to stay awake two nights: it obeys in part, in another part it does not obey. You wish to move the hand to lift something: you lift something; you try at something else: it does not obey. Add so many tormenting difficulties of its infirmity and corruption, which cannot be numbered, and consider how the body, in that which is corrupted, weighs down the soul[^15]. In that, then, which serves you, it shows that you are its master; but in that which resists you, it admonishes you to serve your Lord. Therefore say to your Lord, *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*. How do you learn his righteous decrees? So that now you do not disdain to serve your Lord, just as you wish your body to serve you. And you now begin to serve your Lord, and your body does not yet serve you as you wish. For you believe, who were unbelieving, you follow the precepts of your Lord, you walk the way, but righteousness is not yet perfect in you: therefore obedience is not yet perfect in your servant; there still remains something of bitterness, lest this world grow sweet to you, and you not long for your Lord who made the world.

**9.** Cry out from the ends of the earth to him, O Church spread throughout the world, say in the Psalm, *From the ends of the earth I have cried to you, while my heart was in anguish*[^16]. These things are written in the Psalm: *On a rock you have exalted me, you have led me, for you have become my hope*[^17]. For God exalted us *on a rock*. On what rock? *But the rock was Christ*[^18], says the Apostle. And how was our hope made there? Because our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we were made, is himself the Word of God, through whom all things were made[^19]. He took flesh from the lump of our mortality, and took on death, which pertained to the punishment of sin—not sin, but out of the mercy of freeing us from sin he handed over his own flesh to death. For he was not handed over unwilling; he would not have been crucified, unless he had handed himself over. For even that Judas handed him over, he handed over the willing one. Yet the merit of Christ's will is not imputed to Judas, but the merit of his own greed. For when he handed over the Lord, he did not consider our salvation, but his own avarice and his own perfidy. For Judas handed him over, Christ handed himself over, the Father of Christ handed him over. They all seem to have done one thing. They did one thing, but did not do it with one intention. The Father handed over the Son out of mercy, the Son handed over himself out of that very mercy, Judas handed over the master out of perfidy. There seems to be no difference between handing over and handing over, but between mercy and perfidy there is the greatest difference. How did the Father hand him over? Hear the Apostle: *He who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^20]. How did the Son hand himself over? The same Apostle says of the Lord himself, *Who loved me and handed himself over for me*[^21]. He handed over this flesh, then, to be killed, that you might not fear anything for your flesh. He showed in his resurrection after three days what you ought to hope for at the end of the age. He leads you, then, because he has become your hope. You walk now to the hope of resurrection; but unless our head had first risen, the other members would not find what they should hope for.

**10.** What then, my brothers? Although, even before the Lord suffered, his body served him as a master—for he was not bound to the body as though for retribution, as though for punishment, so as to be beaten by the servant as we are; but if he wished to suffer anything in his body, he suffered it by will and power, not by necessity and want, as he himself said, *I have power to lay down my life, and I have power to take it up again: no one takes it from me, but I myself lay it down*[^22]. Great, then, is the power in him; yet what he wished to suffer in the flesh he showed there, that you suffer deservedly. He suffered undeservedly, you suffer deservedly. But that you may endure what you suffer deservedly, he who suffered undeservedly consoles you. Endure, then, what you suffer, until your mortality passes away. Therefore your kingdom comes by certain measures of time; he renders what he promised, because in himself he has already shown that he rose. For he rose after three days; he wished to rise first and to show us what we ought to hope for at the end. We thought the flesh was going to perish; therefore he wished to take flesh from no other source than that from which we too had flesh. For if he took it from elsewhere, we would say: "The flesh could rise, which was taken from elsewhere. Was it taken from where we were taken?" He indeed did not admit to the mother the company of a man, because he was the only Son of God. Because he had the Father above, on earth he sought nothing but a mother. He showed us that what he created is not evil: he created male and female, he created both[^23]. But because man had been seduced through a woman, women could despair of themselves, unless that sex were honored through the Virgin Mary. He chose to be born of a woman; it was fitting to take on a man, to be born a man. But not only had God created the man, he had created the woman too. Women could, as I said, despair of themselves and say that they did not pertain to the mercy of God, because through a woman the man was deceived; he deigned to be born of a woman, taking on a man, and honored the sex; he showed himself the founder of both sexes, and afterward the liberator. For because through a woman death was prepared for the man by the serpent, life was announced to the men themselves through women. For the women first saw the rising Lord and announced it to the men, the apostles. Our Lord Jesus Christ, then, showed us in his flesh what we ought to hope for at the end. He humbled us, then, that we might learn his righteous decrees.

**11.** Now then, let us return humbled, we who were cast down proud. For the whole cause of our mortality, the whole cause of our infirmity, the whole cause of all our torments, all difficulties, all hardships in this age which the human race suffers, is nothing but pride. You have Scripture saying, *The beginning of all sin is pride*[^24]. And what else does it say? *The beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^25]. If pride seems to you a small evil, at least tremble at apostatizing from God. Moreover, if you tremble at apostatizing from God, cast down the cause of apostatizing. For pride made man apostatize from God. Because, then, this is the head of all our diseases—for we are sick in this life. Just as a skilled physician, when he sees a man languishing with various diseases, does not attend to the proximate causes and leave the origin of all the causes—for if he treats the proximate causes while the source of the diseases is preserved, the derivations of calamity return, and he seems to heal for a time, but does not heal thoroughly; but that physician is found most skilled who gathers well all the causes of all the diseases, and whatever he finds to be the first cause, from which all those various things seem to have arisen like branches, he cuts the root, and the whole forest of pains is cut down—so the Lord Jesus Christ (why is he called Savior, and who said, *Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^26]) came to the sick, because the sick could not come to him; he sought those not seeking him, he turned himself to the infirm, he suffered many things, he endured to be killed by the blind, that he might heal their eyes by his very death—he did all these things, and because he saw pride to be the cause of all our diseases, by his humility he healed us.

**12.** Do not, then, deride the humility of Christ. For many pagans deride it because Christ came humble—and would that pagans alone!—and many heretics who call themselves Christians. It is foul to them that Christ was born of a woman; it is foul to them that he was fastened to the cross and wounded, and that those wounds were true that he received, and those nails were true that were driven in; it is foul to them and they say: "He simulated all those things, he feigned them and did not endure them." Did truth, then, free you by a lie? Were you laboring under a lie, and were you healed by a lie? How can it be? But whoever says these things show what kind of masters they themselves are. For if the Lord rose and offered to his doubting disciple his hands to be felt and the scars to be handled, the disciple saying, "I will not believe, unless I put my fingers into his side"[^27], he showed himself not only to be seen with the eyes but also handled with the hands; but he, when he handled the scars, found expressed the truth and exclaimed, *My Lord and my God*[^28]. If, then, Christ deceived, will you tell the truth? How shall I hear you, tell me. Do you wish me to hear you as a master? "As a master," he says to me. What do you say to me, what do you teach me? "I teach you," he says, "that Christ was not born of a woman, and did not have true flesh, and that death was not true, nor those wounds true, and if not true wounds, then not true scars." And I, on the contrary, have learned from the gospel about the Lord Jesus Christ, who, when the disciple doubted, offered him his scars. Surely he could have risen without scars, who could heal the eyes of one born blind. But why did he wish to bring the testimony of the scars? Because the testimony of the scars of the body was the medicine of the wounds of the mind. What, then, will you teach me: that those things were false, and Christ simulated all these things, and the disciple, deceived by falsity, exclaimed, *My Lord and my God*? If, then, he wished to make a man sound by falsity, how do I know whether you tell me the truth or lie? For you do not think it a crime to lie, when you try to set before me Christ as the author of a lie. For I will say to you: You lie, and you to me: "Far from it, I do not lie." You lie outright. "Far be it from me to lie." This you will say to me, that I may believe you. For tell me, "I lie," and I want to know whether I will believe you anything. But that I may believe you anything, you will say to me, "Far be it that I lie." Why did you say, "Far be it that I lie," unless you think it a crime to lie when you teach? What, then, you think a crime for yourself, this you assign to Christ? Therefore let human deceits depart: as it is written in the gospel, so Christ came. Let not the humility of Christ be foul to you; that humility is foul to pride. Do not be proud, and the humble Christ will not be foul to you.

**13.** The Apostle says, *All things are clean to the clean. But to the unclean and unbelieving nothing is clean, but both their mind and conscience are polluted*[^29]. With a chaste heart say: "A woman conceived, a virgin conceived." By faith she conceived, a virgin conceived, a virgin gave birth, a virgin remained. Believe all these things, and let those inner parts not seem unclean to you. For even if that flesh had been altogether unclean, Christ, coming to the flesh, would cleanse the unclean, and would not become unclean from the unclean. See the humility of your Lord: if it makes you shudder, you are proud. Humility makes the proud man shudder. As you are proud, so force yourself, that the cup of your swelling may not make you shudder. For when you are proud, you swell; you are not great. If you swell, drink the cup, that your inner parts may subside, that you may be able to be sound. This cup the physician has tempered for you, that you may drink. The physician himself has tempered the chalice for you: drink the bitter chalice, if you wish to be sound. Do you not see that you swell, do you not see that your inner parts are not sound? You think yourself great, and you swell. This is not greatness, but disease. Do you wish to be free of disease, do you wish to be free of swelling? Drink the chalice of humility. He tempered it for you who came to you humble. And lest you doubt to drink it, the physician drank first—not because it was necessary for the physician, but to take away the doubt from the sick. Do not, then, despise the humility by which you are healed. The head of all diseases is pride. To heal the head of all diseases came he who deigned to become the head of the Church. The head of all diseases being taken away, you will be sound. Humble yourself and you will be sound, and you will say more securely, *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your righteous decrees*[^30]. For you raised yourself up and were humbled. Humble yourself and you will be raised up, because *God resists the proud, but to the humble he gives grace*[^31]. Therefore, then, *God has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*[^32].

**14.** Man withdrew from God, followed his own covetings, loosened the reins: by wandering, roaming, he reached even the worship of idols. The very nation of the Jews, which worshiped one God, had grown proud, had grown proud and gone into iniquity. God, wishing to show them that they are weak, wishing to show them that they lie under the frailty of their own flesh, because that craving which was drawn from the lineage of their parents still remained in them, gave them the law and commandments, just and good and holy, as the Apostle says, *And so the law indeed is holy, and the commandment just and holy and good. Did that which is good*, he says, *then become death to me? Far from it! But sin, that it might appear as sin, worked death in me through what is good*[^33]. See how he called good that very law which had been given to the Jews. He called it good, because God had given it. And truly he had commanded all good things in the decalogue. Or was there perhaps something evil in: *You shall not steal, you shall not kill, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not bear false witness*, and the rest, *you shall not covet your neighbor's property*[^34]? For even if you have not stolen, but only coveted, the laws do not hold you in the forum, but God holds you in judgment. Attend, therefore, brothers: the law was given to the Jews, weak but proud. They began to try to do the righteous deeds of the law and to be cast down by their own covetings, and they were made guilty, who before were unjust, but were not guilty of the law, were not transgressors. Hence the Apostle says, *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^35]. When the law is given, the one who acts against the law, although he does what he was doing, yet when he was doing this without the law, he was a sinner, he was not a transgressor; but when he now does it with the law received, he is not only a sinner, but also a transgressor. Because, then, he is not only a sinner, but also a transgressor, that is fulfilled which the Apostle says, *But the law crept in, that the offense might abound*[^36]. But why did the offense abound? For this is, *God, you have repulsed us and cast us down*. But it follows and he says, *But where the offense abounded, grace abounded the more*. Because, then, *the offense abounded*, we rightly say, *God, you have repulsed us and cast us down, you were angry*. But because *grace abounded the more*, we rightly add, *And you have had mercy on us*[^37]. Let not the Jews, then, say, "We are something." *For God has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all*[^38].

**15.** Let us, then, dearly beloved brothers, know our life, our Lord Jesus Christ; let us hold the medicine of our pride, the humility of our Lord Jesus Christ. Let us believe in him, let us hope wholly from his mercy, *who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^39]; and when perhaps we make progress in his righteous decrees, let us not be proud and despise the rest, but let us attend, on the road of righteousness, not to how much we have traversed, but to how much remains for us to accomplish; and let us groan everywhere, and let us groan as long as we are pilgrims, because our joy will not be except in the homeland, when we have been made equal to the angels[^40]. *As long as we are in the body, we are on pilgrimage away from the Lord*. Why are we *on pilgrimage away from the Lord*? *For we walk by faith*, he says, *not by sight*[^41]. Faith is to believe what you do not see; sight is to see what you had believed. When, then, sight comes, that flame of love will be keener, because what you longed for absent, you embrace present; what you believed absent, you see present. And if God believed is sweet, what will he be when beheld? When, then, all these things are ended which still torment us on account of the remnants of our sins, then there will be the fullness of righteousness, then, joined to the angels, we shall sing the everlasting hymn "alleluia": the praise of God without ceasing will be ours, nor will hunger drive us from it, for the body does not hunger except that which is corrupted and weighs down the soul[^42]; nor will we thirst nor grow weak nor grow old nor sink into sleep nor be tired by any weariness, but such as the bodies of the angels are, such will our flesh be in the resurrection of the dead[^43]. Do not wonder that this flesh will be heavenly bodies in the resurrection of the dead. Consider that, before we existed, we were nothing, and from that believe what we shall be when we have risen. Let each one consider himself: before he was born, what was he, where was he, where did he lie hidden? All this distinction of the body—ears, eyes, face, the spirit animating the whole mass of the body—where were all these things? Surely in the secret of nature, surely where they were not seen. They came forth from there, God formed you who did not exist. What great thing is it for God to make an angel from a man, who from mud made man[^44]? What were you? and you are a man; you are a man, and will you not be an angel? An angel is made from a man more closely than a man is made from what you were. What is more wonderful, he has done in you; what remains, will he not do?

**16.** It is needful that you believe, and that your faith not forsake Christ, not forsake the gospel, not forsake his promises. Understand that all the things that are written are nearly accomplished, few are those that remain. This Church, which you see spread throughout the whole world, a little while ago did not exist. A few years ago you were pagans, now you are Christians; your parents served demons, before the temples were full of those burning incense, now the Church is full of those praising God. How suddenly has God changed human affairs! Before all these things were, the writings were read, were believed and were not seen; now we see those things that our elders read. If, then, such great things have been fulfilled, will the few that remain not come? Believe strongly that they will come, brothers, because even all these things that have already come, came in no other way than as they were written and foretold before they came. Many thousands of years ago, when it was said to Abraham, *In your seed all nations will be blessed*[^45], it was said to one man, *In your seed all nations will be blessed*; he considered himself one, and this seed, and his wife now an old woman and worn out with old age itself, and it was said to him not only, "There will be a seed from you"—which, if it alone were said, what more wonderful?—it was too little to say to one already worn out with old age, "You will have a son": *In your seed*, he says, *all nations will be blessed*. God said wonderful things, said impossible things, but easy for himself[^46]. That one man believed what he did not see, and we see it; what he believed has been displayed to us—rather, has been rendered to us, what has been displayed in us. For from the seed of Abraham came Isaac, and from Isaac Jacob, and from Jacob the people of the Jews, and from the people of the Jews David, and from the seed of David the virgin Mary, and from the virgin Mary the Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore *in the seed* of Abraham *all nations will be blessed*, because all nations are blessed in Christ. Behold, now there has been displayed to us what was promised to him. God, then, almighty and faithful, displayed what he promised to one man: will he not display what he promised to all? My brothers, let your faith be built up, let your hope be strengthened. He did not deceive one man; can he deceive the world? He has displayed to one man the world full of Christians; he will display the world living forever with Christ his Son.

**17.** Holding these things, brothers, understand that the Church is not in a part, but is in the whole. Christ bought the whole, he gave his blood for the whole: the whole world has Christians, the unity of Christ is the Church. Without cause the heretics quarrel with the Church of Christ: it is too little that they wished to be disinherited; they also slander the heirs. Lead them in unity to the whole; let them not seduce you to a part. If you follow them, you will go to a part; if they hear you, they will come to the whole: they themselves are conquered to their own gain. For Christ bought the whole when he hung on the cross, my brothers: the commerce of Christ, the passion of Christ; there he bought us where he was crucified. For there he poured out his blood, our price, there where it was foretold in the Psalms as still to come. See how many years before it was foretold: *They have pierced my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones; but they themselves looked and gazed upon me, they divided my garments among themselves and over my garment they cast lots*[^47]. All these things can scarcely be distinguished, whether they are heard in the Psalm or recited from the gospel. Are they not, just as they are sung in the Psalm, so read in the gospel: *They have pierced my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones*? There Christ bought us, where all his bones were numbered; where his hands and his feet were pierced with nails, there he bought us. For there he poured out his blood, which is our price. In that very Psalm is understood what he bought. Do you wish to know? Ask the very Psalm. What did Christ buy, hanging on the tree? For after a few verses it says, *All the ends of the earth will remember and be turned to the Lord, and all the families of the nations will worship in his sight*[^48]. Why will they *worship*? *Because the kingdom is his, and he will rule over the nations*[^49]. As though it were answered why—who is this to whom *all the ends of the earth will be turned and in whose sight all the families of the nations will worship*? *Because*, it says, *the kingdom is his, and he will rule over the nations*. Why is it *his*? Because he himself bought it.

**18.** Now the enemy possessor breaks in, and this under the name of Christ. He can divide some of the garments of Christ; that tunic no one will divide, which was *woven from the top*. *They divided*, it says, *my garments among themselves, and over my garment they cast lots*[^50]. And the evangelist says, *There was there a certain tunic woven from the top, and those who crucified the Lord said among themselves, Let us not divide it, but cast lots over it*[^51]. It was not placed in the division, that tunic was beyond division. Why was that tunic beyond division? Because *it was woven from the top*. It was signified why what was *woven from the top* did not deserve to be divided. What is it that is woven from the top? Whence it is said to us, "Lift up your hearts." And so the one who has his heart on high cannot be divided into parts, because he will belong to that tunic which cannot be divided. Therefore, my brothers, that tunic fell by lot to our Lord Jesus Christ himself, because his lot is his inheritance. And since it was his inheritance, he bought it. But those who were divided can belong to the other garments of Christ, because he is clothed with all. All who believe in him are in some way his clothing. But whoever seek earthly honors, temporal advantages, bodily fancies, are not woven from the top, because they desire worldly things. They, then, can be divided. But that tunic which is *woven from the top* cannot come into division. Rejoice that you belong to it, you who are shoots of the catholic Church. Ask your heart whether you seek from Christ nothing but the kingdom of heaven: not vain things, not temporal things, not bodily images, not the things that delight in this age and on this earth. When you have asked yourselves, your conscience answers you that you have your "heart on high." And if you have your "heart on high," you are woven from the top; if you are woven from the top, you cannot be divided.
