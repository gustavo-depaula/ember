# Sermon 24

**1** Thanks to the Lord our God, and an abundance of praise to that God, to whom a hymn is fitting in Zion. Thanks to him, to whom with the devotion of heart and mouth we have sung: *O God, who is like you?*[^1] Because we feel his holy love rooted in your hearts, because you fear him as Lord, love him as Father. Thanks to him, who is longed for before he is seen, and is felt as present, and is hoped for as to come. Thanks to him, whose fear love does not shake off, whose love fear does not hinder. Him we bless, him we honor, both for you and in you. *For the temple of God is holy, which you are*[^2]. Now see how greatly he lives, or how he lives, when the stones of his temple so live. Consider, brothers, what you say and to whom you say it: *O God, who is like you?* Temples say to their God: *O God, who is like you?* Living stones say to their indweller: *O God, who is like you?* Let the whole creation come to your hearts: the earth and whatever is in the earth, the sea and whatever is in the sea, the air and whatever is in the air, heaven and whatever is in heaven. *He himself spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created*[^3]. Therefore, *O God, who is like you?* let every faithful heart say, let every obedient tongue say, let every devout conscience say, let it say securely: *O God, who is like you?* For it says it to the one of whom it is not ashamed. This is worthy, this befits living stones.

**2** For as for the dead stones—would that they might feel within themselves the mercy of the living stones! I call them dead—not those from which these buildings rise, nor those on which the iron of craftsmen works, nor what a man has sculpted that they may be gods, or rather that a man has sculpted that they may be called gods, though they are not—I do not call those dead stones, but I call dead stones the men to whom those gods are like. Living stones are those whom the apostle Peter addresses, saying: *And you, brothers, as living stones be built up into a holy temple of God*[^4]. Would, then, my brothers, that the dead stones might feel within themselves the mercy of the living stones! For what do we busy ourselves with? What do we labor to bring forth, whether in the narrowness or the breadth of our heart? What do we care for, what do we strive for, except to free a stone from a stone? For living stones have eyes and see, have ears and hear, have hands and work, have feet and walk. And indeed they have known their maker, and they adore their craftsman and praise their sculptor. But the dead stones, the servants of stones, look to their gods and are not looked to, adore and are not acknowledged, offer sacrifices, and themselves become a sacrifice to the devil. And they themselves, brothers, if they had eyes to see and ears to hear, how great a thing it would be to see the prophecies of Christ fulfilled! How great a thing it would be to attend to the truthful codices and the oracles that do not deceive! But why do they not see? Why do they not hear? Because prophecy said this too: *Let all who make them become like them, and all who trust in them*[^5]. Are they then beyond hope, or to be despaired of? Far be it. And what is to be hoped of dead stones? What do you think, except what we already hold written: *For God is able from these stones to raise up sons of Abraham*[^6]?

**3** And so, dearly beloved, since you know to what God we have said, *O God, who is like you?*[^7]—of whom we are not ashamed, whose inscription we read not on a stone but carry in the heart, whose name is both known to all and lives in believers, dwells in the submissive, wars down the proud—since we know to whom we have said, *O God, who is like you?* let the men whom God made not move us to hatred of themselves, but let whatever man himself has done badly in the man well-made by God move us to hatred of itself. "Man" is one name, and it is the name of a creature. Of this creature I seek the craftsman. It is God. Is God the creator of man only? Is he not also the creator of cattle, and fish, and bird, and angel, and heaven, and earth, and stars, and sun, and moon, and of all things made and ordered above and below, the lowest and the highest, bound together by the bond of unity—is not God the craftsman of all these? But man he made to his own image and likeness. Man is called some likeness of God. And how great is it, compared to so great? What is it, compared to whom? Man, compared to God. *What is man, except that you are mindful of him?*[^8] Let us, then, say to our God, we too, men made to his image and likeness, let us say: *O God, who is like you?* If man, made to the image and likeness of God, rightly says it, he says truly: *O God, who is like you?*—for he added: *Remember that we are dust*[^9]—if man, made to the likeness of God, is far from the likeness of God, if that very likeness so differs that it is not fitting to compare it. Yet the heart of a man, the heart of a Christian who cannot say "to the god Man," gladly reads "to the god Hercules," reads above while attending below, questioning the inscription that said "to the god Hercules"—the inscription does not speak, but at least is read, "to the god Hercules"—of whom it was said, let him say of whom it was said. Both are mute, both senseless. Above, a lie; below, a fiction. The inscription accusing the writer, confounding the worshipper. The inscription not commending the stone as a god, but indicating the man as a fool. The inscription imposing the name of God upon a fiction, a fiction, and blotting out the name of the worshipper from the book of the living. What particle of sense does he feel in himself who does not feel that he adores something senseless?

**4** And yet God is able from these stones to raise up sons of Abraham. Let him attend there to what God himself, to whom we have said, *O God, who is like you?*[^10], made in man. Let him attend in the man himself to what he made, let him blot out what was done by the man himself against the one who made man. Let him strike and heal, kill and make alive. For to him to whom we said, *O Lord, who is like you?* we consequently added: *Do not be silent, nor grow gentle, O God*[^11]. What then? In this song, my brothers, have we provoked God to anger, to whom we said, *Do not be silent, nor grow gentle, O God*? Surely either the one who sent, or the very one who came and said, *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart*[^12]? Gentle and humble of heart is the Son of God, Christ. What then? He says, *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart*. And we have said to him, *Do not be silent, nor grow gentle, O God*. Suppose he answers us: "O man, is it too little that you do not learn from me to be gentle, and you want to teach me not to be gentle?" See, brothers, attend, help us with devout attention, with chaste prayer, help us that we may come out in his name from these straits. The divine utterances seem to quarrel. They are thought to sound contrary, unless understanding be present, and we receive it from the very one to whom we said, *O God, who is like you?* What he himself said: *I will give you understanding*[^13], let us receive. Let us know this: *My peace I give you*[^14], says Christ, that Christians may have peace among themselves. How will they imitate it? How will they hear, if the divine utterances themselves cannot have peace among themselves? Attend, see, as it were, the resounding of contraries. *Come to me*[^15], and: *Learn from me*[^16]. What? First, who calls? Whom does he call? To what does he call? Hear who calls: *I confess to you, O Lord, Father of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones. Yes, Father, for so it was pleasing before you. All things have been handed over to me by my Father*[^17]. See who calls: *All things have been handed over to me by my Father, and: No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son, and the one to whom the Son wishes to reveal him*[^18]. A vast greatness, and an ineffable height. *All things have been handed over to me*, he says, *by my Father*. I alone acknowledge, by one alone I am acknowledged. What? Are we left out? Do we not acknowledge? And where is: *the one to whom the Son wishes to reveal him*?

**5** Your spirit and zeal of faith, and the ardor of your love, and the abundance of holy zeal for the house of God, has appeared in your shouts, which you had as clear enough witnesses of your heart. Allow it also to appear—the zeal of the few faithful of God, through whom you are governed, concerning this will of yours. For you, brothers, are the people of God, as he himself said, and the sheep of his pasture. You have, in the name of God, shepherds, servants of the shepherd and members of the shepherd. The spirit and will of the multitude to do any deed will be able to appear in these shouts. But the care of the few on your behalf ought to be shown not by shouts but by deeds. And so, brothers, since you have now fulfilled what pertained to you by acclaiming, allow it to be proved to you whether we too fulfill what pertains to us by acting. We have proved you. Prove us, if, after these shouts that witness your heart and zeal, we are slack in doing what ought to be done. Far be it from us that you should be found approved, and we reprobate. But since the will to act in the matters you acclaimed is one, both ours and yours—though the manner of acting cannot be equal—we think, dearly beloved, that therefore the will ought to be received from you, the counsel for fulfilling your will be awaited from us. That the members of Christ may not be in discord, let them all fulfill their offices that are in his body. Let the eye, set on high, do what pertains to the eye, the ear what pertains to the ear, the hand what pertains to the hand, the feet what pertains to the foot, that there be no divisions in the body, but that the members be anxious for one another in the same way. And so we congratulate and rejoice with your Charity, because you obeyed the holy lord and our colleague, your bishop, in what he spoke to you this morning. Follow this, do not withdraw from this way, lest you fall. For God greatly helps what you wish, if you do what he commands. *For what is man?*[^19] as I had begun to say, every man? Or what is the life of men, which, as it is written, appears for a little while like a vapor? Consider, then, brothers, our frailty, our lowliness, the condition of the flesh, the flitting passages of this very world, and see that it will then be well with us, if our whole hope is in him in whom alone it can be firmly placed. But how will our hope be there, unless we obey his commands?

**6** Do we say, "Do not will what you will"? Rather let us even give thanks that you will what God wills. For that all the superstition of the pagans and the gentiles may be destroyed, God wills it, God has commanded it, God has foretold it, God has already begun to fulfill it, and in many places of the earth he has already in great part accomplished it. If your will were to begin from this city, that you should seek here first to abolish the superstitions of the demons, perhaps there would be some arduous labor, yet not to be despaired of. But now, if these things have been done effectively where they began to be done, where examples had not gone before, how much more effectively, in the name of the Lord, with the help of his right hand, do we believe this too can be done, when the preceding examples are now proclaimed? Surely you shouted this: "As Rome, so also Carthage." If in the head of the nations the matter went before, will the members not follow? Consider, brothers, attend in the very books of the gentiles, hear from those among whom the remnants of that very unhappiness have remained, whether by hearing or by reading come to know their writings, and see that those very gods are called Roman. Therefore these gods are called Roman. And when Christians were being forced, with the raging onslaught of the pagans, to adore them, and refusing, they endured their cruelty even to the shedding of blood. The whole fault of the martyrs whose blood was shed seemed to be this, that they would not adore the Roman gods, that they spurned the Roman ceremonies, that they did not beseech the Roman gods. And the whole onslaught, all the hatred, wept over nothing but the name of the Roman gods. If, then, the Roman gods have failed at Rome, why have they remained here? This, then, brothers, attend to this, this I have said, restrain this. The Roman gods—the Roman gods—if, then, I say, the Roman gods have failed at Rome, why have they remained here? If they could walk, they would say that they fled here from there. But they did not flee. Did they remain there, at Rome? He who was once called the god Hercules is now no longer at Rome. But here he even wanted to have a gilded beard. When he is no longer yours, here too he wanted to have a gilded beard. I was plainly mistaken, because I said he "wanted." For what does a senseless stone want? He indeed wanted nothing, could do nothing. But those who wanted him gilded blushed at his being shaved. And so some suggestion or other crept upon a new judge. What did it do? It surely did not bring it about that a stone should be honored by a Christian, but that the Christian should be angered at the superstition, to the point of shaving it. It did not incline him to comply, but moved him to take vengeance. Brothers, I think it was more ignominious for Hercules to have his beard shaved than to have his head cut off. What, then, was set up with their error has been removed with their disgrace. Hercules is usually called the god of strength. All his strength is in his beard. He shone to his own harm. What did not shine with the Lord's light shone not from light but from mourning.

**7** Let them, then, be silent; let them now see to what God the faithful read and say: *O God, who is like you? Do not be silent, nor grow gentle, O God*[^20]. I had taken up how *he does not grow gentle*—by overthrowing not men but errors. He does not grow gentle, therefore he is angry. But he is God, therefore he also has mercy. He is angry and he has mercy. He is angry, to strike; he has mercy, to heal. He is angry, to mortify; he has mercy, to make alive. In one man he does this. Not as though mortifying some and making others alive, but in those same ones he is both angry and gentle. He is angry at errors, he grows gentle at corrected habits. *I will strike, and I will heal; I will kill, and I will make alive*[^21]. One Saul, afterward Paul, he both threw down and raised up. He threw down the unbeliever, raised up the believer. He threw down the persecutor, raised up the preacher. If he is not angry, whence was Hercules' beard shaved? For he did this through his faithful, through his Christians, through the powers ordained by him and now made subject to the yoke of Christ. And so, brothers, receive these things with a willing spirit, and with the Lord's help hope now more prosperously for the rest. Amen.
