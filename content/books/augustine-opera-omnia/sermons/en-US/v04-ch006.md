# Sermon 98

**1.** The miracles of our Lord and Savior Christ Jesus move all who hear them and believe them: but they move different people in different ways. For some, astonished at his bodily miracles, do not know how to look at greater things: but others, the very deeds they hear were done in bodies, they now admire still more in souls. The Lord himself says: *For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so the Son also gives life to whom he will* [^1]. Not, of course, that the Son raises some and the Father others; but the Father and the Son raise the same ones: for the Father does all things through the Son. Therefore let no one who is a Christian doubt that even now the dead are raised. But every man has eyes with which he can see the dead rise as the son of this widow rose, who has just now been read of from the Gospel: but the eyes with which men may see the dead rise in the heart, not all have, except those who have already risen in the heart. It is a greater thing to raise up one who will live forever, than to raise up one who will die again.

**2.** Over that young man raised up, the widowed mother rejoiced [^2]: over men raised up daily in spirit, mother Church rejoices. He indeed was dead in body; but they in mind. His visible death was visibly mourned: their invisible death was neither sought out nor seen. He sought it who knew the dead: he alone knew the dead, who was able to make them living. For unless the Lord had come to raise the dead, the Apostle would not say: *Rise, you who sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ will enlighten you* [^3]. You hear of one sleeping, when he says: *Rise, you who sleep*: but understand one dead, when you hear: *And arise from the dead*. Those who are visibly dead are often called sleepers. And clearly all who can be awakened by the one who is able to awaken them are sleeping. For one is dead to you who, however much you strike him, however much you pinch him, however much you tear at him, does not wake. But to Christ that man was sleeping to whom it was said: *Rise*; and at once he rose. No one wakes a man in bed as easily as Christ wakes a man in the tomb.

**3.** We find three dead persons raised by the Lord visibly, thousands invisibly. But how many dead he raised visibly, who knows? For not all the things he did are written: John says this: *Jesus did many other things, which, if they were written, I suppose that the whole world could not contain the books* [^4]. So there are without doubt many others who were raised: but not in vain are three recorded. For our Lord Jesus Christ wanted the things he did bodily to be understood also spiritually. For he did not work miracles merely for the sake of miracles: but so that the things he did might be wonders to those who saw, and truths to those who understood. Just as one who sees the letters in a beautifully written book, and does not know how to read, praises indeed the scribe's hand, admiring the beauty of the characters; but what those characters mean, what they indicate, he does not know; and he is a praiser with his eyes, but not a knower with his mind: another, however, both praises the craftsmanship and grasps the meaning; he, namely, who can not only see, which is common to all, but also read, which one who has not learned cannot do. So those who saw Christ's miracles, and did not understand what they meant, and what they intimated in a certain way to those who understand, only marveled that they were done: but others both marveled at the deeds and attained their meaning. Such ought we to be in the school of Christ. For whoever says that Christ worked miracles only so that they should be nothing but miracles, can also say that he did not know that it was not the season for fruit, when he sought figs on the tree [^5]. For it was not the season for that fruit, as the Evangelist testifies: and yet, being hungry, he sought fruit on the tree. Did Christ not know what the countryman knew? Did the creator of the tree not know what the cultivator of the tree knew? When, then, being hungry, he sought fruit on the tree, he signified that he was hungry for something, and seeking something else: and finding that tree without fruit, full of leaves, he cursed it; and it withered. What had the tree done, by not bearing fruit [^6]? What fault is barrenness in a tree? But there are those who cannot bear fruit by the will. Barrenness is the fault of those whose fruitfulness is a matter of the will. So the Jews were those who had the words of the Law and did not have the deeds, full of leaves and bearing no fruit. I have said this to persuade you that our Lord Jesus Christ worked miracles in order to signify something by those miracles, so that, besides the fact that they were wonderful and great and divine, we might also learn something from them.

**4.** Let us see, then, what he wanted us to learn in the three dead persons whom he raised. He raised up the dead daughter of the ruler of the synagogue, on whose behalf, when she was sick, he was asked to free her from her sickness. And as he was going, she was reported dead; and as though he were now being wearied in vain, word was sent back to her father: *The girl is dead, why do you trouble the Master further* [^7]? But he went on, and said to the girl's father: *Do not fear, only believe* [^8]. He came to the house, and found the funeral rites already prepared: and he said to them: *Do not weep, for the girl is not dead, but sleeps* [^9]. He spoke truly: she was sleeping, but to him by whom she could be awakened. Awakening her, he gave her back alive to her parents. He also raised up this young man, the widow's son, of whom we have now been reminded, so that from this we might speak with Your Charity of these things which he himself deigns to grant. You have just now heard how he was raised [^10]. The Lord was drawing near the city; and behold, a dead man was being carried out already beyond the gate. Moved with mercy, because the widowed mother was weeping, deprived of her only son, he did what you have heard, saying: *Young man, I say to you, arise. That dead man arose, began to speak; and he gave him back to his mother* [^11]. He also raised up Lazarus from the tomb. And there, when the disciples with whom he was speaking knew that he was sick (for he loved him): *Lazarus*, he said, *our friend sleeps* [^12]. They, thinking it the healthful sleep of a sick man: *If he sleeps*, they said, *Lord, he is safe* [^13]. And he, speaking now more plainly: *I tell you*, *Lazarus our friend has died* [^14]. Speaking truly in both: He has died to you, he sleeps to me.

**5.** These three kinds of dead persons are three kinds of sinners, whom Christ raises even today. For that dead daughter of the ruler of the synagogue was inside in the house, not yet carried out from the inner walls into public. There she was raised inside, given back alive to her parents. But this one was indeed no longer in the house, yet not yet in the tomb; he had been carried out from the walls, he had not been committed to the earth. He who raised the dead woman not yet carried out, raised the dead man already carried out, not yet buried. The third remained, that he should raise even one buried: and this he did in Lazarus. So there are those who have sin inside in the heart, but do not yet have it in the deed. Someone has been stirred by some desire. For the Lord himself says: *Whoever looks at a woman to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart* [^15]. He has not yet approached in body, he has consented in heart: he has a dead man inside, he has not yet carried him out. And as it happens, as we know, as men daily experience in themselves, sometimes when the word of God is heard, as though the Lord were saying, Arise, the consent to wickedness is condemned, and one breathes again unto salvation and justice. The dead man rises in the house, the heart revives in the secret place of thought. That resurrection of the dead soul has taken place inside, within the hiding places of conscience, as though within the household walls. Others, after consent, go on into the deed, as though carrying out the dead man, so that what was hidden in secret appears in public. Are those, then, who have proceeded into the deed already despaired of? Was it not also said to that young man: *I say to you, arise* [^16]? Was he not also given back to his mother? So then he too who has already done it, if perchance, admonished and stirred by the word of truth, he rises at Christ's voice, is given back alive. He could go forward, he could not perish forever. But those who, by doing what is evil, also entangle themselves in evil habit, so that the very habit of evil does not let them see that it is evil, become defenders of their own evil deeds: they grow angry when they are rebuked; so much so that the men of Sodom once said to the just man who rebuked their most wicked will: *You came to dwell, not to give laws* [^17]. So great was the habit of unspeakable foulness there, that now wickedness was justice, and the one who forbade was rebuked rather than the one who did it. Such men, weighed down by malignant habit, are as it were buried. But what shall I say, brothers? Buried in such a way that, as was said of Lazarus: *He already stinks* [^18]. That mass laid upon the tomb is the very hard force of habit, by which the soul is weighed down, and is not allowed either to rise or to breathe again.

**6.** It was said: *He has been four days* [^19]. Truly, by a certain fourfold progress the soul comes to this habit of which I speak. For the first is, as it were, the tickling of delight in the heart; the second, consent; the third, the deed; the fourth, habit. For there are some who utterly cast aside the unlawful things that come before their thoughts, so that they are not even delighted. There are some who are delighted, and do not consent: death is not yet complete, but in a way begun. To delight is added consent: now there is that condemnation. After consent one proceeds to the deed: the deed is turned into habit: and there arises a kind of despair, so that it is said: *He has been four days, he already stinks*. The Lord came, then, for whom of course all things were easy; and he showed you a certain difficulty. He was deeply moved in spirit, he showed that there is need of much loud rebuke for those who have hardened in habit. Yet at the voice of the Lord crying out, the chains of necessity were broken. The dominion of hell trembled, Lazarus was given back alive. For the Lord frees even from evil habit those who have been four days dead: for he too who had been four days dead was sleeping, when Christ willed to raise him. But what does he say? See the manner of the raising. He came forth from the monument alive, and could not walk. And the Lord to the disciples: *Loose him, and let him go* [^20]. He raised the dead man, they loosed the bound man. See that something pertains to the proper majesty of God who raises. Someone set in evil habit is rebuked by the word of truth. How many are rebuked and do not hear! Who then works inwardly with the one who hears? Who breathes life within? Who is it who drives out the secret death, who gives secret life? Is it not that after the chidings, after the rebukes, men are left to their own thoughts, and begin to turn over within themselves what an evil life they lead, by what a wretched habit they are weighed down? Then, displeased with themselves, they resolve to change their life. These have risen; those have come to life again to whom what they have been is displeasing: but coming to life again, they cannot walk. These are the chains of their guilt. There is need, then, that the one who has come to life again be loosed and permitted to go. This office he gave to the disciples, to whom he said: *What you loose on earth is loosed also in heaven* [^21].

**7.** These things, then, dearly beloved, let us hear in such a way that those who live may live; that those who are dead may come to life again. Whether sin is still conceived in the heart and has not proceeded into the deed; let him repent, let the thought be corrected, let the dead man rise within the house of conscience. Or if he has already committed what he thought; let him not even so despair. The dead man did not rise inside; let him rise once carried out. Let him repent of the deed, let him come to life again soon: let him not go into the depth of burial, let him not receive from above the mass of habit. But perhaps I am now speaking to one who is already weighed down by the hard stone of his own habit, who is already pressed by the weight of habit, who already, having been four days dead, stinks. Let not even him despair: he is deeply dead, but Christ is high. He knows how to break earthly burdens by crying out, he knows how to give life inwardly through himself, to hand over to the disciples the one to be loosed. Let such men also do penance. For after Lazarus was raised, after four days, no stench at all remained in the living man. Therefore let those who live, live: but whoever are dead, in whichever of these three deaths they find themselves, let them act so as to rise again quickly.
