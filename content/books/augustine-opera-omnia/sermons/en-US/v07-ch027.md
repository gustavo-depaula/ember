# Sermon 253: In the Days of Easter

**1.** The Gospel of the holy apostle John, which is called according to John, was today finished concerning those things that he narrated, how the Lord appeared to his disciples after the resurrection. He addressed the apostle Peter, that presumer and denier, when, now alive with death conquered, he spoke to him and said: *Simon, son of John* (for thus Peter was called), *do you love me?* He answered what was in his heart. If Peter answered what he had in his heart, why did the Lord ask, who saw hearts? Finally, Peter himself marveled, and with a certain weariness heard him asking, whom he knew to know. Once it was said: *Do you love me?* The answer was: *I love you, Lord, you know it.* And again: *Do you love me? Lord, you know all things, I love you.* And a third time: *Do you love me? Peter was saddened*[^1]. Why are you saddened, Peter? Because you answer love three times? Have you forgotten the threefold fear? Let the Lord question you, it is a physician who questions you, what he asks pertains to health. Do not be affected with weariness. Wait: the number of love is fulfilled, to wipe out the number of denial.

**2.** Yet everywhere, everywhere, that is, in the very threefold number of his question, the Lord Jesus, to the one answering love, commends his lambs, and says: *Feed my lambs, feed my sheep*[^2]; as if to say: What do you repay me because you love me? Show your love in my sheep. What do you furnish me because you love me, when I have furnished you with what to love me with? But your love toward me, you have where to show it, you have where to exercise it: *Feed my lambs.* But to what extent the Lord's lambs were to be fed, the sheep bought at so great a price were to be fed with so great a love, he showed in what follows. For after Peter, the lawful number of the threefold answer being fulfilled, professed himself a lover of the Lord, when the Lord's sheep had been commended to him, he hears about his future passion. Here the Lord showed that his sheep are so to be loved by those to whom he commends them that they be ready to die for them. So the same John in his epistle: *As Christ laid down his life for us, so we too ought to lay it down for the brothers*[^3].

**3.** Peter, then, had answered the Lord through a certain proud presumption, when he had said to him: *I lay down my life for you*[^4]. He had not yet received the strength with which to fulfill the promise; now, that he may be able to do this, he is filled with love; therefore it is said to him: *Do you love me?* and he answers: *I love you*; because nothing fulfills this but love. What, then, is it, Peter? When you denied, what did you fear? All that you feared was to die. He who was alive speaks with you, whom you saw dead; do not now fear death; in him was conquered the one whom you feared to die. He hung on the cross, he was fixed with nails, he gave up his spirit, he was struck with a lance, he was laid in the tomb; this you feared when you denied, you feared lest you suffer this, and by fearing death you denied life. Now understand: when you feared to die, then you died. For he died by denying, but he rose by weeping. Why then does he say to him: *Follow me*[^5]? Knowing his ripeness. For if you remember—rather, because those who read it remembered; let those too be reminded who read it and did not remember, or let those come to know who did not read—Peter had said: *I will follow you wherever you go.* And the Lord to him: *You cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward*[^6]. *Now*, he says, *you cannot.* You promise, but I see your strength, I inspect the vein of your heart, and what is true I report to the sick man: *You cannot follow me now.* But this report of the physician is not despair, he added and said: *But you will follow afterward.* You will be healthy and will follow. Now then, because he sees what is going on in his heart, and sees what gift of love he has given to his soul, he says to him: *Follow me.* I certainly had said: *You cannot now*; I say: Now *follow me.*

**4.** But a certain question arose, not to be omitted. When the Lord said to Peter: *Follow me*, Peter looked back at the disciple whom Jesus loved, that is, that John who wrote the Gospel, and said to the Lord: *Lord, and this man?* I know that you love him; how then? shall I follow and he not follow? The Lord said: *So I want him to remain until I come, you follow me.* But the Evangelist himself, the very one who wrote, of whom this was said: *So I want him to remain until I come*, went on to add his own words in the Gospel and said: *That a report had arisen among the brothers because of this word, that that disciple would not die.* And to remove this opinion he added: *But he did not say that he would not die, but only said: So I want him to remain until I come, you follow me*[^7]. This opinion, then, by which John was thought not going to die, John himself took away by his following words; lest this be believed, he says: The Lord did not say this, but said this. But why John said that he did not expound, but left it to us, that we might knock for it, if perhaps it be opened to us.

**5.** As far as the Lord condescends to grant me, as far as it seems to me—but it seems better to the better—so I think this question is solved in two ways: either the Lord said what he said about Peter's passion, or about the Gospel of John. What is about the passion is also this: follow *me*, suffer for me, suffer what I did, for Christ was crucified, Peter too was crucified, he experienced the nails, he experienced the torments; but John experienced none of these, this is: *So I want him to remain*, without wound, without torment let him sleep and await me; *you follow me*, suffer what I did, I poured out my blood for you, pour it out for me. In this one way, then, what was said can be expounded: *So I want him to remain, until I come, you follow me*; I do not want him to suffer, you suffer. But according to the Gospel of John this seems to me to be understood, since Peter wrote about the Lord, and others too wrote, but their writing is occupied more with the humility of the Lord. For the Lord Christ is both God and man. What is man? soul and flesh. What, then, is Christ? Word, soul, and flesh. But what kind of soul? for beasts too have souls. The Word, the rational soul and flesh, all this is Christ. But about the divinity of Christ something stands out in the letters of Peter, much in the Gospel of John. *In the beginning was the Word*, he himself said. He transcended the clouds and transcended the stars, transcended the angels, transcended all creation, reached the Word, through which all things were made. *In the beginning was the Word, this was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him*[^8]. Who could see, who could think, who could worthily take it in, who could worthily declare it? Then it can be well understood when Christ comes. *So I want him to remain until I come.* I have expounded as I could, he can do it better in your hearts.
