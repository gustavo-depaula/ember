# Sermon 174

**1. 1.** We heard the blessed apostle Paul saying, *A human saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the first*[^1]. *A human saying*, then, *and worthy of all acceptance*. Why human, and not divine? Without doubt, unless this human saying were also divine, it would not be worthy of all acceptance. But this saying is human and divine in the way that Christ himself is both man and God. If, then, we rightly understand that this saying is not only human, but also divine; why did the Apostle prefer to say "human" rather than "divine"? Without doubt, since he would not lie if he said "divine," not without cause did he prefer to say "human." He chose this, then, by which Christ came into the world. For he came by that whereby he was man. For by that whereby he was God, he was always here. For where is God not, who said, *I fill heaven and earth*[^2]? Christ surely is the power and wisdom of God; of whom it is said, *It reaches mightily from end to end, and disposes all things sweetly*[^3]. Therefore: *He was in this world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^4]. He both was here, and came; he was here by his divine majesty, he came by human infirmity. Because, then, he came by human infirmity, therefore, proclaiming his coming, he said, *A human saying*. The human race would not be freed, unless the Word of God deigned to be human. For a man too is called "humane" who shows himself a man, and most of all who receives a man into his lodging. If, then, he is called humane who receives a man into his house, how humane is he who received a man into himself?

**2. 2.** Therefore: *A human saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners*[^5]. Attend to the Gospel. *For the Son of man came to seek and to save what was lost*[^6]. If man had not perished, the Son of man would not have come. Therefore man had perished, God came as man, and man was found. Man had perished through free will: God came as man through liberating grace. Do you ask what free choice avails for evil? Recall the sinning man. Do you ask what God-and-man avails for help? Attend in him to liberating grace. Nowhere could it be so shown how much the will of man, usurped through pride, avails for avoiding evil without the help of God; it could not be expressed more and more manifestly than in the first man. And behold, the first man perished, and where would he be, unless the second man had come? Because that one too was man, therefore this one too is man, and therefore *a human saying*. Plainly nowhere does the kindness of grace and the liberality of the omnipotence of God so appear as in the man, the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus. For what do we say, my brothers? I speak to those nourished in the catholic faith, or won to catholic peace. We know and hold that the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, insofar as he was man, is of that nature of which we too are. For our flesh and his flesh are not of another nature, nor our soul and his soul of another nature. He took on this nature, which he judged was to be saved. He had nothing less in nature, but he had nothing in fault. A pure nature, but not only human. There was God, there was the Word of God. And just as you, one man, are soul and flesh; so he, one Christ, is God and man. Will anyone, then, dare to say that our nature in that Mediator first earned God through free choice, and so deserved to be taken up, so as to be man and God, one Christ Jesus? Behold, we can say that by our virtues, by our morals, by the conduct of our life we earned to be made sons of God: we can say: We received a precept; if we keep it and live well, we will be received into the number of the sons of God. Did he too first live as a son of man, and by living well become the Son of God? From there he began, and there he started, and by being taken up he was made. *For the Word was made flesh, that it might dwell among us*[^7]. The Word of God, the only Son of God, assumed the soul and flesh of a man, not first earning it for himself, nor laboring by his own virtue to attain that sublimity, but wholly freely. For nothing went before that taking up: by being taken up he was made. A virgin conceived: before the conception of the virgin was there a man-mediator? Surely he was not first just. For how was he just, who did not even exist? A virgin conceived, and by the taking up of man it began there. Rightly it was said, *We saw his glory, glory as of the Only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth*[^8]. You love your free will, you are going to say to your father, *Give me my substance which belongs to me*[^9]. Why do you entrust yourself to yourself? He can better keep you, who could create you before you were. Acknowledge Christ, then; he is full of grace. This he wishes to pour into you, with which he is full; this he says to you: Seek my gifts, forget your merits; for if I sought your merits, you would not come to my gifts. Do not exalt yourself, be small, be Zacchaeus.

**3. 3.** But you are going to say: If I am Zacchaeus, I will not be able to see Jesus because of the crowd. Do not be sad, climb the tree on which Jesus hung for you, and you will see Jesus. And what kind of tree did Zacchaeus climb? A sycamore. In our regions it is born either nowhere at all, or rarely perhaps somewhere; but in those parts there is much of this kind of tree and fruit. "Sycamores" are called certain fruits like figs; yet they differ somewhat, which those who have seen or tasted can know. Yet as far as the interpretation of the name indicates, "sycamore" is interpreted in Latin "foolish figs." Now see my Zacchaeus, see him, I beseech you, wishing to see Jesus in the crowd, and not able. For he was little, the crowd was proud; and the crowd itself, as a crowd is wont, was hindering itself from seeing the Lord well; he climbed up from the crowd, and saw Jesus with the crowd not hindering. For the crowd says to the humble, to those walking the way of humility, to those forgiving God their wrongs, to those not seeking vengeance on their enemies—the crowd insults, and says: Unavenged one, who cannot avenge yourself. The crowd hinders that Jesus be seen: the crowd, glorying and exulting when it can avenge itself, hinders that he be seen who hanging said, *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^10]. Wishing to see him, then, Zacchaeus, in whom the person of the humble was figured, did not attend to the hindering crowd; but climbed the sycamore, as it were a tree of foolish fruit. *For we*, says the Apostle, *preach Christ crucified, to the Jews indeed a stumbling block*: attend to the sycamore: *but to the gentiles folly*[^11]. Finally the wise of this world insult us about the cross of Christ, and say: What kind of heart do you have, who worship a crucified God? What kind of heart do we have? Surely not yours. The wisdom of this world is folly with God[^12]. For we do not have your heart. But you say our heart is foolish. Say what you wish: let us climb the sycamore, and see Jesus. For you cannot see Jesus for this reason, because you blush to climb the sycamore. Let Zacchaeus lay hold of the sycamore, let the humble man climb the cross. It is too little if he climb; lest he blush at the cross of Christ, let him fix it on his forehead, where the seat of shame is: there entirely, there in the member where one blushes, let it be fixed whence one does not blush. I think that you laugh at the sycamore: and it made me see Jesus. But you laugh at the sycamore, because you are a man: but the foolishness of God is wiser than men[^13].

**4. 4.** And the Lord saw Zacchaeus himself. He was seen, and saw; but unless he had been seen, he would not see. For whom he predestined, these he also called[^14]. He himself it is who said to Nathanael, as it were already helping the Gospel by his testimony and saying, *Can anything good be from Nazareth*? the Lord to him: *Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you*[^15]. You know whence the first sinners, Adam and Eve, made themselves girdles. When they sinned, they made themselves girdles from fig leaves and covered their shameful parts: because what shamed them they did by sinning. Therefore, if the first sinners made themselves girdles, from whom we draw our origin, in whom we had perished, that he might come to seek and to save what had perished—they made them from fig leaves to cover their shameful parts; what else was said: *When you were under the fig tree, I saw you*; except, you would not come to the cleanser of sin, unless he had first seen you in the shadow of sin? That we might see, we were seen; that we might love, we were loved. My God, his mercy will go before me[^16].

**4. 5.** Now then the Lord, who had received Zacchaeus in his heart, deigned to be received into his house; and he said, *Zacchaeus, hurry down, for in your house I must remain*. He reckoned it a great benefit to see Christ. He who thought it a great and ineffable benefit to see him passing by, suddenly deserved to have him in his house. Grace is poured in, faith works through love; Christ is received into the house, who already dwelt in the heart. Zacchaeus says to Christ, *Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore fourfold*. As though he said: Therefore I keep half for myself, not that I may have it, but that I may have whence to restore. Truly behold what it is to receive Jesus, to receive him into the heart. For there Christ was, he was in Zacchaeus, and of him he was saying to himself what he heard from his mouth[^17]. For so the Apostle says, *That Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts*[^18].

**5. 6.** Now then, because he was Zacchaeus, because he was a chief of the tax collectors, because he was a very great sinner; that crowd, as it were healthy, which was hindering the seeing of Jesus, wondered, and reproached that Jesus had entered the house of a sinner. This was to reproach that the physician entered the house of a sick man. Because, then, Zacchaeus was mocked as a sinner—but he was mocked, when healed, by the mad—the Lord answered those who mocked, *Today salvation has been made for this house*. Behold why I entered, *salvation has been made today*. Surely, if the Savior had not entered, salvation would not have been made in that house. Why, then, do you wonder, sick man? Call Jesus, you too, do not seem healthy to yourself. He is sick with hope who receives the physician: he is sick to despair who in madness strikes the physician. What madness, then, is his who kills the physician? But how great the goodness and power of the physician, who from his own blood made a medicine for his mad slayer? For he who had come to seek and to save what had perished did not without cause say, hanging, *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^19]. They are mad, I am the physician: let them rage, I bear it patiently; when they have killed me, then I will heal. Let us, then, be among those whom he heals. *A human saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners*[^20]: the great, the small, *to save sinners. The Son of man came to seek and to save what had perished*[^21].

**6. 7.** Whoever says that infancy has nothing for Jesus to save denies that Christ is Jesus to all the faithful infants. Whoever says, I say, that infancy has nothing in it for Jesus to save, says nothing else than that Christ the Lord is not Jesus to the faithful infants—that is, to infants baptized in Christ. For what is "Jesus"? "Jesus" is interpreted "Savior." Jesus is the Savior. To those he does not save—by their having nothing in them for him to save—he is not Jesus. Now if your hearts can bear that to some baptized persons Christ is not Jesus, I do not know whether your faith can be recognized as sound by the rule. They are infants, but they become his members. They are infants, but they receive his sacraments. They are infants, but they are made partakers of his table, that they may have life in themselves. What do you tell me: He is healthy, he has no defect? Why do you run with him to the physician, if he has no defect? Do you not fear lest he say to you: Take away from here the one you think healthy? The Son of man came only to seek and to save what had perished. Why do you bring him to me, if he had not perished?

**7. 8.** *A human saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world*[^22]. Why did he come into the world? *To save sinners*. There was no other cause why he came into the world. Not our good merits, but our sins led him from heaven to earth. This is the cause why he came, *to save sinners. And you will call*, he says, *his name Jesus*. Why *will you call his name Jesus*? *For he will save his people from their sins*[^23]. You will call his name Jesus. Why *Jesus*? what is the reason for this name? Hear why: *For he will save his people*. Whence? *From their sins. His people from their sins*. Do little ones not belong to this people, whom Jesus *will save from their sins*? They belong plainly, they belong, my brothers. So have it in your heart, so believe, with this faith carry the little ones to the grace of Christ; lest, if you do not have this faith in your heart, you kill with your tongue those for whom you answer. Plainly, brothers, whoever does not run with the little one with this faith pretends. He is healthy, he has nothing evil, he has no defect; but I will take him to the physician. Why? Because it is the custom. Do you not fear lest the physician say to you: Take him away with you; *those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^24].

**8. 9.** I would commend to your Charity the cause of those who cannot speak for themselves. Let all the little ones be considered as it were orphans, even those who have not yet buried their own parents. The whole number of the predestined—the people of God—seeks the little ones a guardian, which awaits the Lord the Savior. The whole lump of the human race that poisoner struck in the first man; no one passes from the first to the second except through the sacrament of Baptism. In little ones born and not yet baptized let Adam be recognized: in little ones born and baptized and through this reborn let Christ be recognized. He who does not recognize Adam in little ones born will not be able to recognize Christ in the reborn either. But why, they say, does a man now baptized, faithful, with sin now forgiven, beget one who is with the sin of the first man? Because he begets him by the flesh, not by the spirit. *What is born of the flesh is flesh*[^25]. *And if our outer man*, says the Apostle, *is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^26]. By that which in you is renewed, you do not beget the little one: by that which in you is corrupted, you beget the little one. You have been born, and reborn, that you may not die forever: he as yet only born, not yet reborn. If you, by being reborn, live, allow him too to be reborn and live; allow, I say, that he be reborn, allow that he be reborn. Why do you contradict? why do you try by new disputations to break the ancient rule of faith? For what is it that you say: Little ones do not have any original sin at all? What is it that you say, but that they should not come to Jesus? But Jesus cries out to you, *Let the little ones come to me*[^27]. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
