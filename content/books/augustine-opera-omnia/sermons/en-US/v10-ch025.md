# Sermon 352: On the Usefulness of Doing Penance

**1. 1.** The voice of the penitent is recognized in the words with which we responded to the one chanting: *Turn your face away from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities*[^1]. Whence, although we were not preparing a sermon for your love, we recognized that from here, by the Lord's command, we must discourse. For we wished today to leave you in rumination, knowing what abundant feasts you have taken. But because you receive wholesomely what is set before you, you hunger much daily. May the Lord our God himself, then, grant both to us a sufficiency of strength, and to you a useful hearing. For we are not ignorant that we must serve your good and useful will. Let us, then, be helped by you both by prayer and by zeal; by prayer to God, by zeal toward the word; that we may say those things which he himself judges to be useful to you, who feeds you through us. The voice, then, of the penitent is recognized in these words: *Turn your face away from my sins, and blot out all my misdeeds*. Accordingly we are by divine command to say something about penance. For we did not command the reader to chant this psalm: but what he judged useful for you to hear, this he commanded even to a boyish heart. Let us say something about the usefulness of penance: especially because the holy anniversary day is now at hand, on whose approach it is fitting that souls be humbled and bodies subdued more eagerly.

**1. 2.** Now a threefold consideration of doing penance is found in sacred Scripture. For neither does anyone rightly approach the Baptism of Christ, in which all sins are blotted out, except by doing penance for his former life. For no one chooses the new life, except one who repents of the old. But this too we ought to prove by the authority of the divine Books, whether those to be baptized did penance. When the Holy Spirit was sent, having been promised beforehand, and the Lord fulfilled the faith of his promise; the disciples, having received the Holy Spirit, as you know, began to speak in all tongues, so that among those who were present each one recognized his own language. But terrified by this miracle, they sought counsel of life from the apostles. Then Peter announced to them that he must be worshiped whom they had crucified, so that they who believing might now drink his blood, which raging they had shed. But when our Lord Jesus Christ had been announced to them, and they recognized their guilt, that there might be fulfilled in them what the prophet had foretold beforehand, *I was turned to my misery, while the thorn was being fixed*; they were pierced. For they were turned to the misery of grief, when the thorn of the recollection of sin was being fixed. For they had thought they had done no evil, the thorn was not yet fixed. But as Peter was speaking, that you may recognize the thorn fixed, Scripture said: *While Peter was speaking, they were pierced in heart*. Accordingly, in the same psalm, when it was said: *I was turned to my misery, while the thorn is being fixed*; there follows: *I acknowledged my sin, and my misdeed I did not cover. I said: I will declare against myself my offense to the Lord; and you forgave the impiety of my heart*[^2]. When, then, pierced by that thorn of recollection they said to the apostles, *What, then, shall we do?*, Peter said to them: *Do penance, and let each one of you be baptized in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ; and your sins will be forgiven you*[^3]. Therefore meanwhile now, if perhaps any are present from that number who are disposed to be baptized (for we believe they are present, the more eagerly toward the word, the nearer to forgiveness), these first we address in a few words, that they may lift up their minds in hope. Let them love to become what they are not, let them hate what they were. Let them already by their wish conceive the new man to be born: whatever pricked them from their past life, whatever anguished the conscience, whatever altogether, whether great or small, whether to be told or not to be told, let them not doubt that it can be forgiven; lest perhaps what the mercy of God wishes to forgive, human doubt should hold against itself.

**1. 3.** Let everyone faithfully recall the example also displayed in that first people. For the Apostle says: *All these things were figures of us*: when he was speaking of such matters. For what had he said? *For I do not wish you to be ignorant, brothers, that our fathers were all under the cloud; and all were baptized in Moses in the cloud and in the sea; and all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank of the spiritual rock that followed. But the rock was Christ*[^4]. That these were figures of us he said, whom no faithful person has ever contradicted. And when he enumerated many things, he resolved one thing alone, because he said: *But the rock was Christ*. By resolving some one thing, he set the rest forth to be inquired into: but lest the inquirer, departing from Christ, should err, that he might seek firmly, founded on the rock, *The rock*, he said, *was Christ*. He said those things were figures of us, and all were obscure. Who would unroll these wrappings of figures? who would open them? who would dare to examine them? In certain things, as in most dense thickets and thick shade, he kindled a light: *The rock*, he said, *was Christ*. Now, then, with the light brought in, let us seek what the rest signify; what they mean, the sea, the cloud, the manna. For these he did not expound, but he showed what the rock was. The passage through the sea is Baptism. But because Baptism, that is, the water of salvation, is not of salvation unless consecrated in the name of Christ, who shed his blood for us, the water is signed with his cross. That this Baptism might signify this, it was the Red Sea. The manna from heaven is openly expounded by the Lord himself. *Your fathers ate*, he says, *manna in the desert, and died*[^5]. For when would they live, when the figure could foretell life, but could not be life? *They ate*, he says, *manna, and died*; that is, the manna which they ate could not free them from death: not because the manna itself was death to them, but because it did not free them from death. For he was going to free from death who was figured by the manna. The manna certainly came from heaven, attend to whom it figured: *I am*, he says, *the living bread, who came down from heaven*[^6]. But as eager and well-watchful ones, attend to the Lord's words, that you may profit and know how to read and to hear. *They ate*, he says, *the same spiritual food*. What is *the same*, except that he which we too eat? I see it is somewhat difficult to bring out and explain what I have undertaken to say: but I shall be helped by your benevolence, which may obtain for me from the Lord this faculty. *They ate*, he says, *the same spiritual food*. It would have sufficed for him to say: They ate spiritual food. *The same*, he says. I do not find how to understand *the same*, except him whom we too eat. What, then, says someone, was that manna which I now receive? Then nothing comes now, if it already was before. Then the scandal of the cross is emptied out. How, then, *the same*, except because he added *spiritual*? For those who so received that manna as to think only their bodily need was being satisfied, and their belly fed, not their mind; ate nothing great, satisfaction was made for their need. God fed some, to some he announced something. Such people ate bodily food, not spiritual food. Whom, then, does he say our fathers, who ate the same spiritual food? Whom do we think, brothers, except those who truly were our fathers? rather, were not our fathers, but are. For all those live. For so the Lord says to certain unbelievers: *Your fathers ate manna in the desert, and died*. For what is *your fathers*, except those whom you imitate in unbelief, whose ways you follow by not believing and by resisting God? According to which understanding he says to certain ones: *You are of your father the devil*[^7]. For neither did the devil create any man by power, or procreate him by begetting; and yet he is called the father of the impious, not on account of generation, but on account of imitation. As, on the contrary, it is said of the good: *Therefore you are the seed of Abraham*[^8]: when he is speaking to the Gentiles, who did not draw the stock of the flesh from Abraham's line. For they were sons, not by being born, but by imitating. But father Abraham is disowned and alienated from the faithless, when the Lord says to them: *If you were sons of Abraham, you would do the works of Abraham*[^9]. And that the evil trees, glorying in Abraham's fatherhood, might be uprooted, sons of Abraham are promised from stones[^10]. As, then, in this place he says: *Your fathers ate manna in the desert, and died*: for they did not understand what they ate; and so, not understanding, they received only bodily food: so too the Apostle says that our fathers, not the fathers of the unbelievers, not the fathers of the impious, eating and dying; but our fathers, the fathers of the faithful, ate spiritual food, and therefore *the same*. *Our fathers*, he says, *ate the same spiritual food, and drank the same spiritual drink*. For there were there those who understood what they ate: there were there those to whom Christ tasted more in the heart than the manna in the mouth. Why should we speak of others? From there came first Moses himself the servant of God, faithful in all his house[^11], knowing what he dispensed, and that at that time the closed things of the present, the open things of the future, ought so to be dispensed. Briefly, then, I would say: whoever understood Christ in the manna, ate the same spiritual food as we; but whoever sought from the manna only their fill, ate as fathers of unbelievers, and died. So too the same drink: *For the rock was Christ*. The same drink, then, as we, but spiritual; that is, which was received by faith, not which was drawn off by the body. You have heard the same drink: *The rock was Christ*; for Christ was not one then, another now. That rock indeed was one thing, another the stone which Jacob set for himself at his head[^12]; one thing the lamb slain that the Pasch might be eaten[^13], another the ram caught in the brambles to be sacrificed, when Abraham, commanded, spared his son, whom commanded he had offered[^14]; one sheep and another sheep, one stone and another stone, yet the same Christ; therefore the same food, therefore the same drink. Finally the rock itself was struck with wood, that water might flow down: for the rod struck it[^15]. Why with wood, not with iron, except because the cross drew near to Christ, that he might offer us grace to drink? The same food, then, the same drink, but to those who understand and believe. But to those not understanding, only that manna, only that water; that food to the hungry, this drink to the thirsty; neither that nor this to the believer: but to the believer the same as now. For then Christ was to come, now Christ has come. "To come" and "has come" are different words, but the same Christ.

**1. 4.** Since the matter has come up, I wish to say something also about the doubting of Moses the servant of God. For this too was a figure of the ancient saints. Moses doubted at the water, when he struck the rock with the rod that water might flow, he doubted. But anyone reading would perhaps pass over his doubting, and not understand it, because he would not dare even to inquire. But that doubting displeased the Lord God, and he marked it, not only by rebuking, but also by avenging. For on account of this doubting it is said to Moses: *You will not lead the people into the land of promise*. *Ascend the mountain and die*[^16]. God here surely appears angry. What, then, of Moses, my brothers? Was all that labor of his, all his ardor for the people, and that love, saying, *If you forgive them their sin, forgive; but if not, blot me out of your book*[^17], condemned by this sudden and abrupt doubting? And what is it that the reader concluded when he was reading the Apostle: *Love never falls away*[^18]? When I proposed certain things to be resolved, your zeal made me propose also something else, which perhaps you would not inquire about. Let us see, then, and still, as much as we can, let us try to penetrate the mystery. God is angry, he says Moses will not lead the people into the land of promise; he orders him to ascend the mountain and die. And yet he enjoins many things to be done by this same Moses: he orders what he should do, how he should arrange the people, how he should not leave them carelessly and negligently. He would never deign to enjoin such things on one condemned. Receive something more wonderful. Since it was said to Moses (for this pleased God for the sake of a certain mystery and dispensation) that he himself would not lead the people into the land of promise, another is chosen, Joshua son of Nun; and this man was not called by this name, he was called Hoshea[^19]. And when Moses entrusted to him the people to be led in, he called him, and changed his name for him, and named him Jesus: so that not through Moses, but through Jesus, that is, not through the law, but through grace, the people of God might enter into the land of promise. But as that Jesus was not the true one, but a figure; so too that land of promise was not the true one, but a figure. For to the first people it was temporal: but to us, which is promised, it will be eternal. But by temporal figures eternal things were promised and foretold. As, then, that Jesus was not the true one, nor that land of promise true, but a figure: so the manna was not truly heavenly food, but a figure; so that rock was not truly Christ, but a figure, and so all things. What consideration, then, does the doubting of Moses demand for itself? Lest perhaps there too some figure is expressed, and has hinted to the one who understands, and has stirred and provoked the mind to inquire. For I see both after that doubting, and after the anger of God, and after the threats of death, and after the removal from leading the people into the land of promise, that God says many things to Moses, as to a friend, just as he spoke before: so much so that to Joshua son of Nun himself the example of obedience is set forth from Moses, and God admonishes him to serve him as Moses served; and so promises to be with him, as with Moses. Evidently, dearest ones, God himself constrains us, not to reprehend rashly, but to understand the doubting of Moses. The rock lying is a figure, the rod striking a figure, the water flowing a figure, Moses doubting too a figure. And there he doubted, where he struck. From here arose the doubting of Moses, when the wood drew near to the rock. Now let the swift fly ahead, rather let them patiently await the slow. Moses doubted when the wood drew near to the rock: the disciples doubted when they saw the Lord crucified. Of these Moses bore the figure. He was a figure of that Peter who thrice denied. Why did Peter doubt? Because the wood drew near to the rock. When the Lord foretold the manner of his death, that is, the cross itself, Peter himself was terrified: *Far be it from you, Lord, this shall not be*[^20]. You doubt, because you see the rod threatening the rock. Therefore the hope which the disciples bore concerning the Lord they then lost: in a certain way it was cut off, when they saw him crucified, when they lamented him slain. He found them after the resurrection speaking among themselves about this matter, in sad conversation; and holding their eyes lest he be recognized by them, not withdrawing himself from believers, but deferring doubters, he mingled himself as a third interlocutor with their talk, and asked them what they were talking about. They wonder that he alone is ignorant of what had happened in him who was asking. *Are you alone*, they say, *a stranger in Jerusalem?* And they recount what was done concerning Jesus. And at once they open the marrow of their despair, and, though unknowing, show the wound to the physician: *But we*, they say, *were hoping that in him there was the redemption of Israel*[^21]. Behold, doubting has arisen, because the wood drew near to the rock: the figure of Moses is fulfilled.

**1. 5.** Let us see this too: *Ascend the mountain, and die*[^22]. By the bodily death of Moses was figured the death of that doubting; but on the mountain. O wonderful mysteries! This, surely, expounded and understood, how much sweeter than manna? At the rock the doubting was born, on the mountain it died. When Christ was humble in the passion, he lay as it were a rock before the eyes: deservedly there was doubt about him, that humility held forth nothing great. Deservedly by that very humility he was made a stone of stumbling: but glorified by the resurrection he appeared great, now he is a mountain. Now, then, let that doubting which had been born at the rock die on the mountain. Let the disciples recognize their salvation, let them recall their hope. Attend how that doubting dies, attend how Moses dies on the mountain. Let him not enter the land of promise: we do not want doubting there; let it die. Let Christ show it to us dying. Peter trembled: and denied thrice. *For the rock was Christ*. He rose again, was made a mountain: he strengthened Peter too. But the doubting dies. How does it die? *Peter, do you love me?* The inspector of the heart, the knower of the heart asks; and wishes to hear that he is loved, and once is too little. He asks this, hears it almost to the weariness of Peter himself: for he wonders that he is asked by one who foreknows, but also asked so many times when once would suffice to answer even one who did not know. But as though the Lord said to you: I wait, let the lawful number be fulfilled: let him confess three times through love[^23], because he had thrice denied through fear[^24]. In that the Lord asked so many times, he was slaying that doubting on the mountain.

**1. 6.** What, then, dearest ones, if these things are open? They were closed not for fraud, but for delight. For they would not be grasped so sweetly, if, being ready at hand, they grew cheap. Let him, then, look to his own business who is about to seek Baptism, whom I had begun to address. The Red Sea was Baptism, the people passing through were being baptized: the passage itself was Baptism, but in the cloud. For what was foretold was still clouded: what was promised was still hidden. Now already the cloud has withdrawn, the serenity of manifest truth has come about: because the veil too has withdrawn, through which Moses spoke. This veil hung also in the temple, lest the secrets of the temple be seen: but at the cross of the Lord the veil was torn, that they might lie open. Come, then, to Baptism; enter undaunted the way through the Red Sea; do not be anxious about your past sin as about a pursuing Egyptian. Your sins pressed you with the hard burden of slavery, but in Egypt, in the love of this world, in a distant pilgrimage; they forced you to follow earthly works, as it were to make bricks, you were working works of mud. Your sins press you, come secure to Baptism: as far as the water the enemy will be able to follow, there he will die. Fear something about your past life, believe something of your sins will remain, if any of the Egyptians remained. I hear the voice of the slothful: I, he says, do not fear about past sins, I do not doubt that all are forgiven me in the holy water through the love also of the Church; but I fear future sins. Does it please you, then, to remain in Egypt? Meanwhile escape the present enemy, who has already pressed you, and already subjugated you. Why do you meditate to yourself enemies from the future? What you have already done, even if you do not wish it, will be: what you think you will do, if you wish, will not be. But the way is dangerous: for not when I have crossed the Red Sea will I now be in the land of promise: that people was led through long deserts. Meanwhile be freed from Egypt. What then, do you think a helper will be lacking to you on the way, he who rescued you from the old captivity? Does he not restrain your new enemies, who freed you from the old enemies? Only cross undaunted, walk undaunted, be obedient: do not embitter that Moses, whose type that one bore in this obedience. I admit, enemies are not lacking. For just as there were not lacking those who would follow the fleeing, so there were not lacking those who would hinder the walking. Plainly, dearest ones, they were figures of us. Meanwhile let there not be in you that which may sadden Moses: do not be bitter water, which after the Red Sea that people could not drink. For there too was a temptation. And yet these things, when they happen, when the people grows bitter, we show them Christ, what he bore for them, how for them he shed his blood: and they grow gentle, as though we cast wood into the water. Plainly you will have Amalek as an enemy opposing your journey. Then Moses prayed, then he stretched out his hands: and where he lowered his hands, Amalek grew strong; where he stretched out his hands, Amalek failed. Let your hands too be stretched out, let Amalek the tempter and the hinderer of this journey fail: be vigilant and sober in prayers, in good works, yet not apart from Christ, because that stretching out of the hands was the cross of Christ. In it the Apostle is stretched out, when he says: *The world is crucified to me, and I to the world*[^25]. Therefore let Amalek fail, let him be conquered, and not hinder the passage of the people of God. If you lower your hands from the good work, from the cross of Christ, Amalek will prevail. Yet do not in any way think either that you will at once be strong, or that you must utterly fail by despairing. For that alternation of failing and of strength in the hands of Moses the servant of God was perhaps your alternation. For sometimes in temptations you fail, but do not succumb. He lowered his hands a little, he did not altogether collapse. *If I said: My foot has slipped; behold, your mercy, O Lord, was helping me*[^26]. Do not fear, then: there is present on the journey a helper, who in Egypt was not lacking as liberator. Do not fear, enter upon the way, presume securely. Sometimes he lowered his hands, sometimes he raised them; yet Amalek was conquered[^27]. He could rebel, he could not overcome.

**2. 7.** And so we are now admonished to speak of the second penance. For I proposed that its consideration is threefold in holy Scripture. That first is of the candidates and of those thirsting to come to Baptism: this I showed from the holy Scriptures. There is another, surely, the daily penance. And where do we show that daily penance? I have nowhere better to show it than in the daily prayer, where the Lord taught us to pray, showed what we should say to the Father, and set it in these words: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^28]. What debts, brothers? Since debts here cannot be understood except as sins; do we pray that he forgive again the debts which he forgave in Baptism? Surely every Egyptian who was following died. If nothing of the following enemies was made a remnant, why do we pray that it be forgiven, except on account of the hands failing against Amalek? *Forgive us, as we also forgive*. He appointed a medicine, he established a pact. Here he dictates the prayers, there he answers the one praying: he knows by what right the matter is conducted in heaven, how the things desired can be obtained. Do you wish to be forgiven? Forgive, he says. For what have you that you may bestow on God, from whom you wish it to be bestowed on you? Is Christ the Savior now walking on earth? Does Zacchaeus now receive him into his house rejoicing[^29]? Does Martha prepare lodging and feasts for him[^30]? He needs none of these things, he sits at the right hand of the Father. But *when you did it for one of these least ones of mine, you did it for me*[^31]. This is the stretching out of the hands, under which Amalek failed. And you indeed give out to the poor man, when you give something to the hungry: perhaps you will have less of what you gave, but in your house, not in heaven. But here too on earth, by whose command you provided it, he himself supplies what you gave. Speaking of this matter, the Apostle says: *He who supplies seed to the sower, will also supply bread for food*[^32]. For you are a worker of God, when you give to the needy: you sow in winter what you may reap in summer. Why, then, do you fear, unbeliever, lest in this great house so great a head of the household not feed his worker? It will be there too, but what suffices you. God will give to need the whole, not to greed. Work, then, undaunted, stretch out your hands, let Amalek fail. But from here, as I said, when you have given something in your house, you either see less of what you saw there: you do not see it there when you have given, until God gives again. Tell me, when you forgive from the heart, what do you lose? When you forgive him who sins against you, what less will you have in your heart? For from there you forgive, but you lose nothing. Rather, indeed, a certain wave of love was going in your heart, and was flowing as from an inner vein: you hold hatred against your brother, you have stopped up the fountain. Not only, then, do you lose nothing, when you forgive; but you are more abundantly watered. Love is not narrowed. You place there a stone of stumbling, and you make for yourself narrows. I will avenge myself, I will be revenged, I will show him, I will do it: you seethe, you labor, you who are allowed by forgiving to be secure, to live secure, to pray secure. For behold, what are you about to do? You are about to pray. What shall I say, when? Today you are about to pray. Or are you not about to pray? Full of anger and hatred, you threaten vengeance: you do not forgive from the heart. Behold, you pray, behold, the hour of praying comes, you begin either to hear or to say those words. With the earlier ones said and heard, you are about to come to this verse. Or if you are not about to come, where are you about to go? That you may not forgive your enemy, will you deviate from Christ? Plainly, if you will deviate in the prayer, so as to be unwilling to say, *Forgive us our debts*; because you cannot say, *as we also forgive our debtors*; lest it be quickly answered you: So I forgive as you too forgive: therefore, because you cannot say this, and you are unwilling to forgive, you will deviate from this verse, and pass it over, and say what follows: *Do not suffer us to be led into temptation*[^33]; there your creditor will seize you, whose face as it were you were avoiding. Just as anyone in a town, when he meets one to whom he owes something, if a side-street is at hand, leaves where he was going; and goes by another part, lest he see the face of the creditor. This you thought you did in this verse: you avoided to say, Forgive, as I forgive; lest he so forgive, that is, not forgive, because you do not forgive; and you were unwilling to say it, avoiding the face of the creditor. Whom do you avoid? whom do you avoid? Where will you go, where you can be and he not be? You will be about to say: *Where shall I go from your spirit? and from your face where shall I flee? If I ascend into heaven, you are there; if I descend into hell, you are present*[^34]. How far can the debtor flee from Christ, than to go into hell? This creditor is present even there. What will you do, except what follows? *If I take up my wings straight, and fly to the ends of the sea*[^35]; that is, by my hope I will meditate on the end of the age, I will live in your precepts, I will be lifted up by the two wings of love. Fulfill, then, the two wings of love. Love your neighbor as yourself[^36]; and do not hold hatred, from which you may flee the creditor.

**3. 8.** There remains the third kind of penance, of which I will say something briefly, that, with the Lord helping, I may pay what was proposed and promised. There is a penance more grave and more sorrowful, in which those are properly called in the Church penitents, removed even from participating in the Sacrament of the altar, lest by receiving unworthily, they eat and drink judgment to themselves. That penance, then, is sorrowful. The wound is grave: adultery perhaps has been committed, perhaps murder, perhaps some sacrilege, a grave matter, a grave wound, lethal, deadly: but the physician is almighty. Now, after the suggestion of the deed and the delight and the consent and the perpetration, he stinks as it were four days dead: but not even him did the Lord abandon, but cried out: *Lazarus, come forth*. The mass of the burial yielded to the voice of mercy: death yielded to life, hell yielded to heaven. Lazarus was lifted up, he came forth from the tomb: and he was bound, as men are who, in the confession of sin, do penance. Already they have come forth from death: for they would not confess, unless they came forth. The very confessing is to come forth from the hidden and dark place. But what does the Lord say to his Church? *Whatever you loose*, he says, *on earth, will be loosed in heaven also*[^37]. Accordingly, as Lazarus came forth, because the Lord fulfilled the good of his mercy, to bring to confession the hidden, stinking dead man; now the ministry of the Church fulfills the rest: *Loose him, and let him go*[^38]. But, dearest ones, let no one propose this kind of penance to himself, let no one prepare himself for this kind: yet if it perhaps happens, let no one despair. Judas the betrayer was made to perish utterly not so much by the crime he committed as by his despair of pardon. He was not worthy of mercy, therefore no light shone in his heart, that he should run to the pardon of him whom he had betrayed, like those who crucified him; but by despairing he killed himself, and hanged himself with a noose, suffocated himself. What he did in his body, this was done in his soul. For this wind of this air too is called spirit. As, then, those who tie their own neck, thereby kill themselves, because the spirit of this air does not enter into them: so those who despair of the pardon of God, by that very despair suffocate themselves within, so that the Holy Spirit cannot visit them.

**3. 9.** Pagans are wont thence to insult Christians about the penance which is instituted in the Church: and against certain heresies the catholic Church has held this truth about doing penance. For there were those who said that to certain sins penance ought not to be given; and they were shut out of the Church, and made heretics. In whatever sins, the pious mother Church does not lose her bowels of compassion. Therefore even pagans are wont thence as it were to insult us, not knowing what they say; because they have not yet come to the word of God, which makes the tongues of infants eloquent. You, they say, make men sin, when you promise them pardon, if they do penance. This is dissolution, not admonition. To this opinion they exaggerate words, as much as each one can; with tongue either sounding or stammering, they do not keep silent: yet even when we speak to them, although they are conquered, they do not consent. Yet how they are conquered, let your love receive briefly, because the Lord's mercy has established everything most excellently in his Church. They say we give license to sins, because we promise the harbor of penance. If the access to penance were closed, would not that sinner so much the more add sins to sins, the more he despaired of being forgiven? For he would say to himself: Behold I have sinned, behold I have committed crime, now there is no place for me of pardon; penance is fruitless, I am to be condemned: why do I not now live as I wish? Because I do not find love there, here at least let me feed greed. For why should I abstain? There every place is closed to me, here whatever I do not do, I lose; because the life which is to be after this will not be given me. Why, then, do I not serve my lusts, to fulfill and satisfy them, and do whatever is not lawful, but is pleasing? It would perhaps be said to him: But, wretch, you will be caught, accused, tortured, punished. Evil men know these things are said by men, and kept among men; they attend to many living evilly and wickedly who have their sins unpunished: they can hide them, redeem those they cannot hide; redeem unto old age a life lascivious, blasphemous, sacrilegious, lost. They enumerate to themselves: What? did not that man who did such great things die an old man? Do you not consider that he therefore died a sinner and a wicked old man, so that God might show in him his patience, awaiting penance? Whence the Apostle says: *Are you ignorant that the patience of God leads you to penance? But you, according to the hardness of your heart and the impenitent heart, have treasured up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works*[^39]. There is need, then, that this fear possess the mind, there is need that he who does not wish to sin think God present to himself, not only in public, but also in his house; not only in his house, but also in his bedchamber, at night, on his bed, in his heart. Therefore, if you take away the harbor of penance, by despair they will increase their sins. Behold, those say nothing who think sins are therefore increased, because the harbor of penance is set forth in the Christian faith. What, then? Ought not God thereafter to provide, lest through that hope of pardon sins should again be increased? For just as he provided lest they be increased by despairing, so he ought to provide lest they be increased by hoping. For just as in truth he who has despaired increases his sins, so he too can increase his sins who has hoped for pardon: so that he says to himself: I will do what I wish, God is good, when I convert, he will forgive me. So plainly say to yourself: When I convert, he will forgive me; if tomorrow is certain to you. Does not Scripture admonish you to this, saying: *Do not delay to convert to the Lord, nor put it off from day to day; for suddenly his wrath will come, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you*[^40]? Behold, to both the providence of God has kept watch for us. Lest by despairing we increase our sins, the harbor of penance is set forth: again, lest by hoping we increase them, an uncertain day of death is given.
