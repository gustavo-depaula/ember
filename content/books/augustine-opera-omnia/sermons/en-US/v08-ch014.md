# Sermon 284: On the Heavenly Birthday of the Martyrs Marianus and James

**1.** Today, by God’s favor, the time has dawned for us to pay our debt. Since the debtors are willing, then, why are the creditors making a commotion? If everyone keeps a quiet mind, what we pay can reach everyone. We owe you a sermon on the suffering and glory of the holy martyrs. Since they suffered most gloriously, they call upon us to be patient. They endured raging crowds; let us have an attentive people, since we have seen a believing people. The martyrs’ steadfastness must be praised, but what eloquence is sufficient to praise it? When will I accomplish by speaking what has already been accomplished in your hearts by believing? But where does so great a gift of patience come from? Where but from the source of every excellent gift? Where does an excellent gift come from but from the source of the perfect gift? For there too it is written, *Patience has a perfect work. Every*, it says, *excellent gift and every perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no change or shadow of a moment’s turning*. From the unchanging fountain patience descends into changeable human minds to make them too unchanging. How can a human being please God except through God? Where does a human being obtain a good life except from the fountain of life? Where does a human being obtain illumination except from the eternal light? *For with you,* it says, *is the fountain of life. With you,* it says. I could say, “From me”; but if I say, “From me,” I withdraw from you. Therefore, *with you* is *the fountain of life. In your light*—not in ours—*we shall see light in your light*. Therefore, *Draw near to him and be enlightened*. He is the fountain of life: draw near, drink, and live. He is light: draw near, receive, and see. Unless he flows into you, you will be dry.

**2.** From here, then, our martyrs drank; from here they became intoxicated and no longer recognized their own people. How many holy martyrs do we suppose were tempted, as their suffering approached, by the coaxing of their loved ones, who tried to call them back to the temporary, empty, fleeting sweetness of this life? But those who, thirsty, had drunk from the fountain that is with God and had become intoxicated poured forth their confession of Christ. They paid no attention to their earthly relatives, drunk with the wine of error, loving them wrongly and persuading them to turn away from life; they did not recognize them. Marianus’s mother was not among those people; she was not among those who offered harmful persuasion, coaxed with fleshly affection, and deceived by loving. The mother of holy Marianus was not among them. Her name was not an empty one; she was not called Mary in vain. A married woman, indeed, not a virgin, not one who conceived untouched through the Holy Spirit, yet chaste in her marriage, she had borne a child whom she guided toward a most glorious martyrdom by her encouragement, rather than calling him back from it by harmful coaxing. O Mary, you too are holy—unequal in merit, indeed, but equal in desire! You too are blessed! She bore the Prince of martyrs; you bore a martyr of the Prince. She bore the Judge of witnesses; you bore a witness of the Judge. Blessed was your childbearing, more blessed your affection. When you gave birth, you groaned; when you lost him, you exulted. What does this mean: you groaned when you gave birth, and exulted when you lost him? It was not without reason, except that you had not lost him. Where there was no grief, there was faith. Spiritual faith had driven fleshly grief out of your heart. You saw that you were not losing your son but sending him ahead; all your joy lay in wanting to follow him.

**3.** We marvel at these things, praise these things, love these things. O blessed martyrs, where did these things come to you from? I know your hearts are human; where did these divine things come to you from? I say, “From God.” Who says, “From yourselves”? Who begrudges you your good by praising you wrongly? Does someone or other say that these things come from yourselves? Answer him, *My soul will be praised in the Lord.* Does someone say that these things come from yourselves? Answer him, if you are meek; answer, *My soul will be praised in the Lord.* Answer this too among God’s people: *Let the meek hear and rejoice*. Does someone say that these things come from yourselves? Answer him, *A person cannot receive anything unless it has been given to him from above*. For the Lord Jesus says to us and to you, *Without me you can do nothing*. *Without me,* he says, *you can do nothing.* This was said to you too. Recognize the shepherd’s words; beware the deceiver’s flattery. That impious, unjust, ungrateful pride—I know it displeases you. Holy martyrs, you suffered for Christ; but your suffering benefited you, not Christ. What would you lack if it had not been given to you? Drive this venom of the enemy serpent away from your ears. It is the tongue that said, *You will be like gods*. Ungrateful free choice cast man down; let choice now set free say to the Lord, *Lord, the patience of Israel*. Why are you proud, unbeliever? Do you praise the martyrs’ patience as though they could be patient of themselves? Listen instead to the Apostle, the teacher of the Gentiles, not to a deceiver of unbelievers. Surely you praise the martyrs’ patience for Christ, and do you attribute it to them? Listen instead to the Apostle addressing the martyrs and quieting human hearts. Listen, I say, to his words: *For it has been granted to you for Christ.* Listen to devotion encouraging, not flattery deceiving: *To you,* he says, *it has been granted.* Granted—listen: *It has been granted to you for Christ, not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for him*. *It has been granted to you:* what can be added to this statement? *It has been granted to you:* acknowledge it as a gift, lest you lose it by claiming it as your own. *To you,* he says, *it has been granted for Christ.* What for Christ, except suffering? But do not guess; hear what follows: *not only to believe in him*—for this too has been granted. But it is not only this that has been granted: *but also to suffer for him.* This too has been granted. Let the martyr turn his back on the unbelieving and ungrateful flatterer; let him turn his face toward the most generous giver, and attribute his very suffering to God, rather than supposing he has offered it to God out of his own resources. Let him say instead, *My soul will be praised in the Lord; let the meek hear and rejoice.* And when you ask him, “What does this mean, *My soul will be praised in the Lord?* Is it not praised in you, then?” he answers, *Will my soul not submit to God? For my patience comes from him*. Why, then, *my* patience? I opened my arms and gladly received it: *mine, from him.* Both *from him* and *mine;* and because it is *from him,* it is all the more securely *mine.* It is mine, but it does not come to me from myself. To possess my gift, I acknowledge God the giver. For if I do not acknowledge God the giver, God takes away his good, and my evil remains, through my own choice.

**4.** Trustworthy Scripture says, *God made man upright, but they themselves have sought out many thoughts*. *God made,* it says, *man upright, but they themselves.* How *they themselves,* except through free choice? *But they themselves have sought out many thoughts.* It had said that man was made upright; yet it did not say, “They themselves have sought out crooked thoughts,” though it had said “upright,” or “unjust thoughts,” but *many.* Because of this multiplicity, *the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling burdens the mind that thinks about many things*. May God free us from this multiplicity of human thoughts, and may he free us from the one [so the supplied Latin], so that from being many we may be one in him. May he fuse us in the fire of love, so that with one heart we may follow the one, and not fall away from the one into many things and, abandoning the one, be scattered among them. For the Apostle was speaking of this one thing when he said, *Brothers and sisters, I do not consider myself to have attained it.* What? *But one thing.* What one thing? *Forgetting what lies behind and stretching forward to what lies ahead, I press on*. I pursue one thing; one thing, he says, I pursue. But I do not consider myself to have attained it, because the corruptible body burdens the mind that thinks about many things. See where the martyrs were going. When their zeal burned, they paid no attention to the great clamor, because they loved the one. Look at the martyrs’ desire: *One thing,* he says, *I have asked of the Lord. One thing I have asked.* “I bid farewell,” he says, “to the world’s multiplicity. *One thing I have asked:* one blessedness, one happiness, one true happiness, not many false ones.” *One thing,* he says, *I have asked of the Lord; this I will seek.* What is this one thing? *To dwell in the Lord’s house all the days of my life.* Why this? *To contemplate the Lord’s delights*. When the holy martyrs thought of that delight, all evils, bitterness, and harshness became worthless to them. There was delight against delight; there was delight against pain. That delight fought against both, against the raging world and against the flattering world. It answered the world, “Why do you flatter me? What I love is sweeter than what you promise. I hear God saying to me—or rather, holy Scripture—*How great is the abundance of your sweetness, Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you*!” Here again is a good multiplicity, because it is not divided against itself but united in the one.

**5.** It is no wonder, then, my brothers and sisters. Do you know at what point the martyrs’ names are read? The Church does not pray for them. The Church rightly prays for other departed people who have fallen asleep; it does not pray for the martyrs, but rather commends itself to their prayers. For they struggled against sin to the point of shedding blood. They fulfilled what is written: *Fight for the truth even to death*. They despised the world’s promises, but that is little; it is little to despise death, little to endure hardships. Where the struggle reaches the shedding of blood, there is the most glorious and complete victory. For the first temptations set before our Lord, the Prince of martyrs, were allurements: *Tell these stones to become bread. I will give you all these kingdoms. Let us see whether the angels will catch you, for it is written, Lest you strike your foot against a stone.* These are the world’s pleasant things: in the bread, the desire of the flesh; in the promise of kingdoms, worldly ambition; in the curiosity of putting God to the test, the desire of the eyes. All these are of the world, but they flatter rather than rage. Consider the Leader of the martyrs, setting before them struggles as examples and mercifully helping those who struggle. Why did he allow himself to be tempted, except to teach us to resist the tempter? The world promises fleshly pleasure: answer it, “God is more delightful.” The world promises worldly honors and high positions: answer it, “God’s kingdom is higher than them all.” The world promises needless or damnable curiosities: answer it, “God’s truth alone does not err.” When the Lord had been tempted by this threefold temptation—for all this world’s allurements fall under three things: pleasure, curiosity, or pride—what does the Evangelist say? *After the devil had completed every temptation*: every temptation, that is, belonging to allurements. Another temptation remained, amid harsh and difficult, savage, atrocious, and merciless things. Another temptation remained. Knowing what had been completed and what remained, the Evangelist says, *After the devil had completed every temptation, he withdrew from him for a time*. He withdrew from him, that is, as the lurking serpent; he will come back as the roaring lion. But the one who will trample the lion and the dragon will conquer him. He will return; he will enter Judas and make him the betrayer of his Teacher. He will bring the Jews, no longer flattering but raging. Taking possession of his vessels, he will cry out through all their tongues, *Crucify him, crucify him*! Why are we surprised that Christ conquered there? He was almighty God.

**6.** Christ chose to suffer for our sake. The Apostle Peter says, *He suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you might follow in his footsteps*. He taught you to suffer, and taught you by suffering. Words were not enough unless an example was added. And how did he teach, brothers and sisters? He was hanging on the cross, and the Jews were raging. He hung from cruel nails, but did not lose his gentleness. They raged, barked all around him, and mocked him as he hung there, like people in a frenzy raging on every side of the supreme physician placed in their midst. He hung there and healed. *Father,* he said, *forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing*. He prayed, yet still hung there. He did not come down, because he was making medicine for those frenzied people out of his own blood. And since the words of the Lord as he prayed, and his own mercy as he answered—for he prayed to the Father and, together with the Father, answered—since those words could not have been poured out in vain, after his resurrection he healed those whose utter frenzy he had endured while hanging there. He ascended into heaven and sent the Holy Spirit. After his resurrection he did not show himself to them, but only to his faithful disciples, lest he appear to have wanted to taunt those who killed him. For teaching his friends humility was more important than reproaching his enemies with the truth. He rose again: he did more than they demanded when, not believing but taunting, they said, *If he is the Son of God, let him come down from the cross*. And he who would not come down from the wood rose from the tomb. He ascended into heaven and sent the Holy Spirit from there; he filled his disciples, corrected their fear, and made them confident. Peter’s trembling was suddenly changed into a preacher’s courage. Where did this come from in a human being? Look for Peter relying on himself, and you find Peter denying; look for God helping, and you find Peter preaching. Weakness trembled for a time so that presumption might be overcome, not so that devotion might be destroyed. He fills with his Spirit and makes into the boldest preacher the man to whom, when he was presumptuous, he had foretold, *You will deny me three times.* For Peter had relied on his own strength, on free choice rather than God’s gift. He had said, *I will be with you even to death*. In his abundance he had said, *I shall never be moved.* But he who by his will had given strength to Peter’s splendor turned away his face, and Peter was troubled. *The Lord,* it says, *turned away his face:* he showed Peter to Peter. But afterward he looked upon him and established Peter on the rock. Let us therefore, my brothers and sisters, imitate as far as we can the Lord’s example of suffering. We will be able to fulfill it if we ask him for help, not running ahead like presumptuous Peter, but following and praying like Peter as he made progress. For when Peter denied him three times, notice what the Evangelist says: *And the Lord looked upon him, and Peter remembered*. What does *looked upon him* mean? The Lord did not look at him with his bodily face as though reminding him. That is not how it happened: read the Gospel. The Lord was being judged inside the house; Peter was being tempted in the courtyard. Thus *the Lord looked upon him,* not with his body but with his majesty, not with the gaze of fleshly eyes but with his most exalted mercy. He who had turned away his face looked upon Peter, and Peter was set free. Thus the presumptuous man would have perished if the Redeemer had not looked upon him. And now, washed by his tears, corrected and rescued, Peter preaches. The one who denied preaches; those who strayed believe. That medicine of the Lord’s blood takes effect in the frenzied. As believers they drink what in their rage they shed. “But imitating the Lord is too much for me,” someone says. By the Lord’s grace, imitate a fellow servant: imitate Stephen; imitate Marianus and James. They were human beings, fellow servants, born as you were, but crowned by him who was not born in that way.
