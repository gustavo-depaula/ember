# Sermon 149

**1. 1.** Before last Sunday, I recall that I became a debtor to your Holiness for certain questions proposed from the Scriptures. Now, as far as the Lord deigns to grant, it is time to pay, lest we remain longer in debt for anything except love alone, which is always paid and always owed. We had said that we must inquire about the vision of Peter, what that *vessel* means—*like a sheet let down from heaven by four corners, in which were all the four-footed creatures of the earth, and creeping things, and birds of the air*[^1]; and what was said to Peter by the divine voice, *Kill and eat*[^2]; and that this was done three times and taken up.

**2. 2.** Against those, indeed, who suppose that gluttony was commanded to Peter by the Lord God, it is easy to argue. First, because even if we wish to take literally what was said, *Kill and eat*, to kill and eat is not a sin; rather, the sin is to use immoderately the gifts of God, which he bestowed for human use.

**2. 3.** For the Jews had received certain animals that they might eat, and certain ones from which they were to abstain; and the apostle Paul shows that they received this as a sign of things to come, saying, *Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in matters of food and drink, or with regard to a feast day or a new moon or a sabbath, which is a shadow of things to come*[^3]. And so in the times of the Church he now says in another place, *All things are clean to the clean, but it is evil for the man who eats with offense*[^4]. For there were those, at the time the Apostle was writing this, who ate meats to the offense of certain weaker persons. For the sacrificial flesh of those beasts which the soothsayers offered was then sold in the market, and many of the brothers abstained from eating meats, lest even unknowingly they should run into that flesh of which a sacrifice had been made to idols. Hence in another place the same Apostle, that the conscience might not tremble with fear, says, *Eat whatever is sold in the meat market, asking no question on the ground of conscience. For the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof*[^5]. And again, *If one of the unbelievers invites you, and you wish to go, eat whatever is set before you, raising no question on the ground of conscience. But if anyone says to you, "This has been offered in sacrifice," then do not eat it, for the sake of the one who informed you, and for conscience' sake*[^6]. Therefore, in these matters everything clean or unclean is established not in the contact of the flesh, but in the purity or the stain of the conscience.

**3. 4.** Hence a liberty was given to Christians that was not given to the Jews. For all the animals that the Jews were forbidden to eat are signs of things, and, as has been said, shadows of things to come. Just as that circumcision signifies the circumcision of the heart, which they bore in the flesh and rejected in the heart, so too those foods are precepts of mysteries and signs of things to come. For instance, it was written for them that they should eat those animals that chew the cud and have a divided hoof, but should not eat those that lack either both or one of these[^7]: by this are signified certain people who do not belong to the fellowship of the saints. For the divided hoof pertains to conduct, the chewing of the cud to wisdom. Why does the divided hoof pertain to conduct? Because it slips with difficulty. For a slip is a sign of sin. And how does chewing the cud pertain to the teaching of wisdom? Because Scripture says, *A desirable treasure rests in the mouth of the wise, but a foolish man swallows it down*[^8]. So whoever hears and through negligence becomes forgetful, as it were swallows down what he has heard, so that it no longer has any savor in his mouth, burying the very hearing of it in forgetfulness. But whoever meditates on the law of the Lord day and night[^9] chews the cud, as it were, and on a kind of palate of the heart delights in the savor of the word. So this which was commanded to the Jews signifies that those who are either negligent hearers, or have evil conduct, or are blameworthy in both faults, do not belong to the Church, that is, to the body of Christ, to the grace and the fellowship of the saints.

**4. 5.** So too the other precepts of this kind given to the Jews are shadowy significations of things to come. After our Lord Jesus Christ, the light of the world, came, they are read only so that they may be understood, not also so that they may be observed. A liberty was therefore given to Christians not to act according to this empty custom, but to eat what they will, with moderation, with blessing, with thanksgiving. So perhaps it was also said to Peter, *Kill and eat*, so that he might no longer hold to the observances of the Jews; yet it was not the gluttony of the belly and foul voracity that was commanded him.

**5. 6.** But that you may understand this to have been shown in a figure, there were creeping things in that vessel. Could Peter possibly eat serpents? What, then, does this signification mean? That vessel signifies the Church; the four corners by which it hung, the four parts of the world, throughout which the catholic Church is stretched, spread everywhere. Whoever, therefore, wishes to go off into a part and be cut off from the whole does not belong to the sacrament of the four corners. But if he does not belong to the vision of Peter, neither does he belong to the keys that were given to Peter. For from the four winds, God says, his saints will be gathered at the end[^10], because now the gospel faith is spread abroad through all those four quarters. Those animals, then, are the nations. For all the nations that were unclean, in their errors and superstitions and lusts, before Christ came, were made clean at his coming, their sins forgiven. Hence, after the forgiveness of sins, why should they not be received into the body of Christ, which is the Church of God, whose person Peter bore?

**6. 7.** For Peter appears in many places in the Scriptures to bear the person of the Church, most of all in that place where it was said, *To you I hand over the keys of the kingdom of heaven. Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven*[^11]. Did Peter receive these keys, and Paul not receive them? Did Peter receive them, and John and James not receive them, and the rest of the apostles? Or are these keys not in the Church, where sins are forgiven every day? But because in this signification Peter bore the person of the Church, what was given to him alone was given to the Church. Therefore Peter bore the figure of the Church; the Church is the body of Christ. Let it therefore now receive the nations, already made clean, to whom sins have been forgiven; whence Cornelius, a gentile man, and those who were gentiles with him, sent to him. The almsgiving that was accepted had cleansed him in a certain measure; it remained that, like clean food, he should be incorporated into the Church, that is, into the body of the Lord. But Peter was hesitant to hand over the gospel to the gentiles, because those who had believed from the circumcision were forbidding the apostles to hand over the Christian faith to the uncircumcised, and were saying that they ought not to come to a share in the gospel unless they had received circumcision, which had been handed down to their fathers.

**7. 8.** That vessel, then, removed this hesitation; and so after that vision he was admonished by the Holy Spirit to go down and go with those who had come from Cornelius, and he went. For Cornelius and those who were with him were reckoned among those animals that had been shown in the vessel; yet God had already cleansed them, since he had accepted their alms. They were therefore to be killed and eaten, that is, the past life in which they had not known Christ was to be slain in them, and they were to pass over into his body, as into the new life of the fellowship of the Church. For Peter himself, when he had come to them, briefly recalled what had been shown him in that vision. For he said, *You yourselves know how unlawful it is for a Jewish man to associate with or visit a foreigner; but God has shown me that I should call no man common or unclean*[^12]. And this God indeed showed when that voice sounded, *What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean*[^13]. And afterward, coming to the brothers in Jerusalem, when some were in an uproar because the gospel was being handed over to the gentiles, to restrain their agitation he recalled this same vision[^14]; and unless it pertained to the same understanding, it would not have needed to be recalled.

**8. 9.** Perhaps it may also be asked why it was a linen sheet in which those animals were. Surely not without cause. For we know that the moth does not consume linen, which corrupts other garments. Let each one shut out from his heart the corruptions of evil desires, and so be established incorruptibly in faith, that he may not be penetrated by depraved thoughts as by moths, if he wishes to belong to the sacrament of that sheet, by which the Church is figured.

**9. 10.** Why was it let down from heaven three times? Because all those very nations, which belong to the four parts of the world through which the Church is scattered—the Church that the four corners by which that vessel was bound together signified—are baptized in the name of the Trinity. Believing in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, they are made new, that they may belong to the society and communion of the saints. The four corners, then, and the threefold lowering also show the number twelve of the apostles, as if three were assigned to each of the four. For four times three makes twelve. Enough, I think, has been treated concerning this vision.

**10. 11.** Another question had been deferred by us: why the Lord, in that very sermon which he delivered on the mountain, said to his disciples, *Let your works shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father who is in heaven*[^15]. And a little later in that same sermon he says, *Beware of practicing your righteousness before men, in order to be seen by them*[^16]; and, *Let your alms be in secret, and your Father who sees in secret will reward you*[^17]. The doer often wavers between these two precepts and does not know which to obey, when surely he wishes to obey the Lord, who commanded both. How will our works shine before men, that they may see our good deeds, and how again will our almsgiving be in secret? If I wish to observe the one, I offend against the other; if I observe the other, I sin against this. So both passages of Scripture must be so harmonized that the divine precepts may be shown to be unable to be contrary to one another. For this conflict that seems to be in the words seeks the peace of the one who understands. Let each have concord with the word of God in his heart, and there is no discord in Scripture.

**11. 12.** Consider, then, a man giving alms in such a way that no one whatever knows of it, if it can be done—not even the one to whom it is given—so that, avoiding even his eyes, he rather puts down what the other may find than holds out what he may take. What more can he do to hide his almsgiving? Surely this man runs into that other saying and does not do what the Lord says, *Let your works shine before men, that they may see your good deeds*. No one sees his good deeds; he invites no one to imitate them. The rest will be barren, so far as it lies in him, while they suppose that what God commanded is done by no one, if people act in such a way that their good works are not seen—when there is greater mercy shown toward the one to whom an example of good imitation is set before him than toward the one to whom food for refreshing the body is held out. Consider another flaunting and parading his alms before the people, wanting nothing else in them than to be praised; let his works shine before men. You see that he does not offend against that precept, but he offends against the other, where the Lord says, *Let your alms be in secret*. Whoever is such a man even grows slack if some ungodly persons arise who perhaps reproach what he does. He hangs upon the tongue of those who praise; he is like the virgins who do not carry oil with them. For you know of the five foolish virgins who did not carry oil with them, and the others, the wise, who did carry oil with them. The lamps of all were burning, but some had not with them the means to feed that light, and were so distinguished from those who had it that the ones were called foolish, the others wise[^18]. What, then, is it to carry oil with oneself, but to have the conscience of pleasing God by good works, and not to place there the limit of one's joy, in the praise of people who cannot see the conscience? For a person can see what he does, but God sees with what intention he does it.

**12. 13.** Let us therefore set before us someone keeping both precepts, obedient to both. He holds out bread to the hungry, and he holds it out before those whom he wishes to make his imitators, imitating also the Apostle who says, *Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ*[^19]. So he holds out bread to the poor man, manifest in the deed, devout in heart. Whether he seeks his own praise there, or the glory of God, no human being sees, no human being judges; yet those who with kindly zeal are ready to imitate believe that the good they see being done is also done with a devout spirit, and they praise God, by whose precept and gift they see such things being done. His work therefore appears, that people may see and glorify the Father who is in heaven; but its effect is in the heart, so that his almsgiving may be in secret, and the Father who sees in secret may reward him. This man kept the measure he owed, despising no precept, but fulfilling both. For he took care that his righteousness should not be done before men—that is, that it should not have its limit there, in being praised by men—since he wished not himself, but God, to be praised in his good work. And because that intention is within, in the conscience itself, his almsgiving was done in secret, that the One from whom nothing is hidden might repay him. For who can show his heart to people when he acts, so as to display with what intention of mind he acts?

**13. 14.** For the very words too, brothers, were carefully chosen by the Lord. Notice how he speaks: *Beware of practicing your righteousness before men, in order*, he says, *to be seen by them*. If he placed the limit there, where he said, *in order to be seen by them*, that limit is blameworthy and culpable—to wish to do good only up to human praise, seeking nothing further from it. Whoever, therefore, acts only in order to be seen by people is rebuked by the Lord in this saying. But there, where he commands our good deeds to be seen, he did not place the limit so that people should merely see a person and praise a person; rather, he passes on to the glory of God, so that the doer's intention may be carried all the way to that. *Let your works shine*, he says, *before men, that they may see your good deeds*; but you must not seek this. What, then? He adds and says, *and let them glorify*, he says, *your Father who is in heaven*. If you seek this, that God may be glorified, do not fear to be seen by people. Even so your almsgiving is within, in secret, where he alone whose glory you seek sees that you seek this. Hence the apostle Paul, after he was thrown down as a persecutor of the gospel and raised up as a preacher, says, *I was unknown by face to the churches of Judea that were in Christ. They only heard it said, "He who once persecuted us is now preaching the faith he once ravaged"; and in me*, he says, *they magnified God*[^20]. He did not rejoice that the man who had received was recognized, but that God who had given was praised. For he himself said, *If I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ*[^21]. And yet in another place he says, *Just as I too please all men in all things*. And this is a similar question. But what does he add? *Not seeking*, he says, *what is profitable to me, but to many, that they may be saved*[^22]. This is what he says in that other place, *And in me they magnified God*; what the Lord too says, *That they may glorify your Father who is in heaven*. For then they are saved, when in the works they see done through people they glorify the One from whom people received these things.

**14. 15.** Two questions remain; but I fear that I am a burden to those already weary, and again I fear to defraud those still hungry. Yet I remember what I have paid and what I owe. For it remains to see what this means, *Let not your left hand know what your right hand is doing*[^23]; and concerning the love of an enemy, why it seemed to the ancients that license was given to hate their enemies, whose love is commanded to us. But what am I doing? If I treat these matters briefly, perhaps I shall not be understood as I ought; if at greater length, I fear that I may weigh you down more with the burden of the sermon than I relieve you with the fruit of the exposition. But surely, if you understand less than enough, I still hold myself a debtor, that at another time these things may be more fully treated. Yet now they ought not to be so left that nothing at all is said about them. The left hand is the carnal desire of the soul, the right hand the spiritual love of the soul. If, then, when anyone gives alms, he mingles in the desire for temporal advantages, so that he seeks something of that sort in the work, he mingles the conscience of the left hand with the works of the right. But if with simple love and a pure conscience before God he helps a person, looking to nothing else than to please the One who commands these things, his left hand does not know what his right hand does.

**15. 16.** But concerning the love of an enemy the question is harder, nor can it be settled with such brevity. But when you hear it, pray for us; and perhaps the Lord God will quickly grant what we think to be difficult. For we live from one storehouse, since we are in one family. So what we think to be deep within the hidden place, perhaps he who promises sets at the threshold, that it may most easily be given to those who ask. The Lord Christ himself loved his enemies; for hanging on the cross he said, *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^24]. Stephen followed his example, when stones were being thrown at him, and said, *Lord, do not hold this sin against them*[^25]. The servant imitated the Lord, that no servant might be slothful and suppose this to be a deed that could be done by the Lord alone. If, then, it is too much for us to imitate the Lord, let us imitate the fellow servant. For we are all called to the same grace. Why, then, was it said to the ancients, *You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy*[^26]? Because perhaps even to them what was said was true; but to us it is said more plainly, according to the dispensation of the times, through the presence of him who saw what was to be hidden from some and revealed to others. For if we have an enemy whom we are never commanded to love—and that is the devil—then *You shall love your neighbor*, the man; *and hate your enemy*, the devil. But because among human beings themselves enmities often arise, in the souls of those who through unbelief give place to the devil and become his vessels, that he may work in the sons of distrust[^27]—and yet it can happen that a person leaves off his malice and turns to the Lord; and while he still rages, while he still persecutes, he is to be loved, and one must pray for him and do him good—thus you will both fulfill the first precept, to love your neighbor, the man, and hate your enemy, the devil; and the second, to love your enemies, the men, and pray for those who persecute you.

**16. 17.** Unless perhaps you suppose that Christians at that time did not pray for Saul, the persecutor of Christians. Perhaps that voice of Stephen the martyr was heard for his conversion. For he was in that number of his persecutors, and guarded the garments of those who stoned him[^28]. The same Paul, writing to Timothy, also says, *I urge first of all that supplications, exhortations, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and all who are in high places, that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life*[^29]. He commanded, then, that prayer be made for kings; and at that time kings were persecuting the churches. But those whom they persecuted as they prayed for them, they now defend, their prayers having been heard for them.

**17. 18.** Do you wish, then, to keep that precept of the ancients as well? Love your neighbor, that is, every human being. For all of us, born of the first two parents, are surely neighbors. Indeed, the Lord Jesus Christ himself, who commands us to love our enemies, testified that on those two precepts the whole Law and the Prophets depend: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind*; and, *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^30]. He commanded nothing there about the love of an enemy. Do these two precepts, then, not contain the whole? Far from it. For when he says, *You shall love your neighbor*, there are all human beings, even if they are enemies; because, even according to spiritual kinship, you do not know what, in God's foreknowledge, a person is to you who seems for the time to be your enemy. For since the patience of God leads him to repentance, perhaps he will come to know and will follow the One who leads. For if God himself, who knows who are going to persevere in sins, who are going to abandon righteousness and slip irrevocably into iniquity, nevertheless makes his sun rise on the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust[^31]—inviting them surely to repentance through his patience, so that those who neglect his goodness may experience his severity in the end—with how much solicitude ought a man to be ready to be reconciled, lest perchance, not knowing what someone will be, when he looks upon his present enmities, he hate the one with whom he will reign in eternal happiness? Fulfill, then, the first precept: love your neighbor, every human being; and hate your enemy, the devil. Fulfill the second too. Love your enemies, but the men; pray for those who persecute you, but for the men; do good to those who hate you, but to the men.

**18. 19.** *If your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he thirsts, give him drink; for in doing this you will heap coals of fire upon his head*[^32]. Here too there is a question. For how does anyone love a person whom he wants to burn with coals? But if it is understood rightly, there is no contention. For it is said of those laying-waste coals that are given to a man against a deceitful tongue[^33]. For when anyone does good to an enemy, and, not overcome by his evil, overcomes evil with good[^34], very often the other will repent of his enmities and be angry with himself for having injured so good a man. And that burning itself is repentance, which, like coals of fire, consumes his enmities and his malice.
