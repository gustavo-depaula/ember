# Sermon 296: On the Birthday of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul

**(*On the Gospel Passage: Simon, Son of John, Do You Love Me?*)**

**1.** Today's Gospel reading, fitting for today's solemnity, which just sounded in our ears, if it descended from our ears into our heart and found a resting place there—for God's word rests in us when we yield to God's word—admonished all of us who minister the Lord's word and sacrament to you to feed his sheep. Blessed Peter, first of the Apostles, as much a lover as a denier of the Lord Jesus Christ, as the Gospel shows, followed the Lord about to suffer. But he could not then follow by suffering himself. He followed with his feet, not yet fit to follow in conduct. He promised to die for him and could not even die with him, for he had dared more than his capacity could sustain. He promised more than he could do, because it was also unfitting for him to do what he promised. *I will lay down,* he said, *my life for you.* The Lord was going to do this for the slave, not the slave for the Lord. Therefore, because he dared too much, his love was out of order; therefore, he feared and denied. But afterward, once risen, the Lord teaches Peter to love. Loving without order, he failed under the weight of suffering; to one loving with order, he promises suffering.

**2.** We remember Peter's weakness as he grieved that the Lord would die. I recall it now. See, I recall it: those who remember should say it with me in their hearts; those who had forgotten should remember at my reminder. The Lord Jesus Christ himself foretold his approaching Passion to his disciples. Then Peter, loving him but still in a fleshly way, fearing that the killer of death would die, said: *Far be it from you,* *Lord, far be it from you; spare yourself*. He would not have said, *Spare yourself,* unless he recognized God. Therefore, Peter, if you recognize God, why fear lest God die? You are human; he is God. For a human being God became human, taking on what he was not without losing what he was. Therefore, the Lord was going to die in that in which he would also rise. Peter therefore dreaded human death and did not want it to touch the Lord; unknowingly he wanted to close the purse from which our price would flow. He then heard from the Lord: *Get behind me, Satan, for you savor not God's things but human things. To him* a little earlier he had said—when Peter said, *You are the Christ, the living God's Son—Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah, because flesh and blood did not reveal it to you, but my Father in heaven.* A little earlier blessed, afterward Satan. But why blessed? Not from himself: *Flesh and blood did not reveal it to you, but my Father.* Why Satan? From the human being and in the human being: *For you savor not God's things but human things.* Such, then, was Peter when he followed, loving the Lord and wanting to die for the Lord. It happened as the physician foretold, not as the sick man presumed. Questioned by a maidservant, he denies once, twice, a third time. The Lord looks at him; he weeps bitterly and wipes away the stains of denial with tears of devotion.

**3.** The Lord rises and appears to the disciples. Peter now sees alive the one for whom he had been afraid as he died; he sees not the Lord slain, but death slain in the Lord. Now strengthened by the example of the Lord's own flesh, so that death need not be feared so greatly, he is taught to love. Now he needs to love; now that he has seen the Lord alive after death, let him love, let him love confidently—confidently, because he will follow. So the Lord says, *Peter,* *do you love me?* And he says, *I love you, Lord.* And the Lord says: Because you love me, I do not want you to die for me; I have already done that for you. What, then? *Do you love me?* What will you give me in return, because you love me? *Do you love me? I love you. Feed my sheep.* Again he says this, and a third time, so that love may confess three times, because fear had denied three times. See, understand, learn. The only question is, *Do you love?* The only answer is, *I love.* The one who answers is told, *Feed my sheep.* Having entrusted his sheep to Peter, and Peter along with his sheep to himself, he now foretells his suffering and says: *When you were younger, you used to gird yourself and go wherever you wished; but when you grow old, someone else will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go. He said this,* says the Evangelist, *to indicate by what death he would glorify God*. You see that feeding the Lord's sheep involves not refusing death for the Lord's sheep.

**4.** *Feed my sheep.* Does he entrust his sheep to someone able to answer for them, or someone less able? First, what sheep does he entrust? Sheep purchased not with money, not with gold or silver, but with blood. If a human master entrusted his sheep to his slave, he would certainly consider whether that slave's personal funds were sufficient to cover the price of his sheep. He would say: If he loses them, scatters them, or uses them up, he has the means to repay me. He would therefore entrust his sheep to a slave able to answer for them, and would look for financial resources in the slave to cover the sheep he had bought with money. But now the Lord Christ, because he entrusts to a slave the sheep he bought with blood, looks for the slave's capacity to answer for them in the shedding of his blood in suffering, as though saying: *Feed my sheep;* I entrust my sheep to you. What sheep? Those I bought with my blood. I died for them. *Do you love* *me?* Die for them. That human slave of a human master would indeed repay money for sheep destroyed; Peter paid his blood for sheep preserved.

**5.** Come, brothers, I want to say something suited to the occasion. Peter was not alone in hearing what was entrusted to Peter, what was commanded to Peter. The other Apostles also heard it, held to it, and observed it, especially the apostle Paul himself, who shared his suffering and his day. They heard these things and passed them on for us to hear. We feed you, and we are fed together with you. May the Lord give us strength to love you so that we can even die for you, whether in deed or in disposition. For although suffering did not come to the apostle John, that does not mean that a spirit prepared for suffering could have been lacking. He did not suffer, but he could have suffered; God knew his readiness. Likewise, the three young men were sent into the furnace to burn, not to live. Shall we deny that they were martyrs because the flame could not burn them? Ask the fires: they did not suffer. Ask their will: they were crowned. *God is able,* they said, *to rescue us from your hands; but even if he does not—*there are steadfast hearts, there is stable faith, there is unshaken courage, there is confident victory—*but* *even if he does not, let it be known to you, king, that we do not worship the statue you have set up.* God was pleased to do otherwise: they did not burn, but they extinguished the fire of idolatry in the king's heart.

**6.** You see, then, dearly beloved, what lies before God's servants in this age, for the sake of the future glory that will be revealed in us. No temporal tribulation, whatever its kind or magnitude, weighs against that glory. *The sufferings of this present time are unworthy,* the Apostle says, *of the future glory that will be revealed in us*. If this is so, let no one now think in a fleshly way; this is no time for it. The world is shaken; the old self is shaken off; the flesh is pressed—let the spirit flow forth. Peter's body lies in Rome, people say; Paul's body lies in Rome; Lawrence's body lies in Rome; the bodies of other holy martyrs lie in Rome. Yet Rome is wretched, Rome is devastated; it is afflicted, crushed, burned. So many deadly slaughters occur, through famine, plague, and the sword. Where are the memorials of the Apostles? What are you saying? This is what I said: Rome is suffering such great evils; where are the memorials of the Apostles? They are there, they are there, but they are not in you. If only they were in you, whoever you are who say these things, whoever you are who talk such folly, whoever you are who have been called in the spirit yet think according to the flesh, whoever you are who are like this! If only the memorials of the Apostles were in you, if only you thought about the Apostles! You would see whether earthly happiness was promised to them, or eternal happiness.

**7.** Listen to the Apostle, if the memory of him lives in you: *For the passing lightness of our tribulation produces in us, to an incredible degree and beyond an incredible degree, an eternal weight of glory, as we attend not to what is seen but to what is unseen. For what is seen is temporal, but what is unseen is eternal*. In Peter himself the flesh was temporal, and you do not want the stone in Rome to be temporal? The apostle Peter reigns with the Lord; the body of the apostle Peter lies in a particular place. It is a memorial that awakens love for eternal things, so that you may think of heaven with the Apostle, rather than cling to the earth. Tell me: if you are faithful, recall the memorials of the Apostles, and the memory of the Lord your God as well, who certainly is already seated in heaven. Hear where the Apostle sends you: *If you have risen with Christ, set your minds on what is above, where Christ is, seated at God's right hand. Seek what is above, not what is on earth. For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ, your life, appears, then you too will appear with him in glory*. You hear this in a single phrase: Lift up your heart. Are you grieved, then, and weeping because wood and stones have fallen, and because people destined to die have died? Can we produce anyone who has died who would otherwise have lived forever? Are you grieved because wood and stones have fallen, and because people destined to die have died? If your heart is lifted up, where is your heart? What has died there? What has fallen there? If your heart is lifted up, *where your treasure is, there your heart is*. Your flesh is below; even if your flesh is terrified, let your heart remain unmoved. But still, you say, I did not want it. What did you not want? I did not want Rome to suffer so much. We forgive you for not wanting it; do not be angry with God for wanting it. You are human; he is God. Do you say, I do not want it, where he says, I want it? He does not condemn you for your “I do not want it,” and do you blaspheme his “I want it”? But why did God want this? Why did God want this? For now, serve the will of the Lord your God; once made his friend, you will know the counsel of the Lord your God. What slave would be so proud that, when his master ordered something to be done, he would say, Why? The master keeps his counsel to himself; let obedience comply. If the slave carries it out, if he acts well, if he becomes a friend instead of a slave, as the Lord himself said, *I will no longer call you slaves, but friends*, perhaps he will also learn his master's counsel. Meanwhile, before he knows the counsel, let him willingly bear the decision.

**8.** Indeed, I am still teaching patience, not yet wisdom. Be patient: the Lord wills it. Do you ask why he wills it? Put off your eagerness to know, put off your zeal; prepare to obey. He wants you to bear what he wills. Bear what he wills, and he will give you what you want. And yet, my brothers, I dare to say that you will gladly listen if you already possess the first elements of obedience, if the gentle and meek patience of bearing the Lord's will dwells in you—not only his gentle will. For we do not bear what is gentle; we love it. We endure what is harsh; we rejoice in what is gentle. Look at your Lord, look at your head, look at the example for your life; attend to your redeemer, your shepherd. *Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me*. How he displayed the human will, and immediately turned its resistance toward obedience! *Yet* *not what I will, but what you will, Father*. See, he said this to Peter too: *When you grow old, someone else will gird you and take you where you do not wish to go*. In him too he showed the human will trembling before death. Because he died unwillingly, was he crowned unwillingly? So what was it that you did not want? Perhaps to lose your little property, which you were going to leave here? Take care that you do not remain with what must be left behind. Perhaps you did not want your son to die before you; you did not want your wife to die before you. What then? Even if Rome had not been captured, was not one of you going to die first? You did not want your wife to die before you; your wife did not want her husband to die before her. Was God going to comply with both? Let the ordering rest with him who knows how to order what he has created. You comply with his will.

**9.** I can already see what else you are saying in your heart: Look, in Christian times Rome is being afflicted, or has been afflicted, and has been burned. Why in Christian times? Who are you who say this? A Christian. Then answer yourself, if you are a Christian: Because God willed it. But what do I say to the pagan? He mocks me. What does he say to you? On what grounds does he mock you? Look, when we used to offer sacrifices to our gods, Rome stood firm. Now, because the sacrifice of your God has prevailed and spread, and the sacrifices of our gods have been restrained and forbidden, look what Rome is suffering. Give him a brief answer for now, to get rid of him. But let your own meditation be different. You have not been called to embrace the earth, but to gain heaven; not to earthly happiness, but to heavenly happiness; not to temporal success and fleeting, passing prosperity, but to eternal life with the angels. Still, give a quick answer to this lover of fleshly happiness, this murmurer against the living God, who wants to serve demons and wood and stones. According to their history, this is the third burning of the city of Rome. According to their history, according to their own writings, the burning of the city of Rome that has just occurred is the third. The city that has now burned once amid the sacrifices of Christians had already burned twice amid the sacrifices of pagans. Once it was burned by the Gauls so thoroughly that only the Capitoline Hill remained. The second time, at Nero's hands—I do not know whether to call it his cruelty or his dissoluteness—Rome blazed in a second fire. Nero, emperor of that very Rome, a slave of idols, a murderer of the Apostles, gave the order; he gave the order, and Rome was burned. Why, do you think? For what reason? An arrogant, proud, dissolute man took delight in Rome's burning. I want to see, he said, how Troy burned. So it burned once in this way, then a second time, and now a third. Why does it please you to shriek against God over a city accustomed to burning?

**10.** But, they say, so many Christians suffered such great evils there. Have you forgotten that Christians are to suffer temporal evils and hope for everlasting goods? Whoever you are who are a pagan, you have something to mourn: you have lost temporal things and have not yet found eternal things. The Christian has something to consider: *Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various trials*. When were such things read to you in a temple? “The gods who protect Rome have not saved it now because they are not here.” You should say: They should have saved it when they were here. We show that our God is truthful. He foretold all these things; you have read them, you have heard them. But I do not know whether you remember them, since words like these trouble you. Have you not heard the prophets, have you not heard the Apostles, have you not heard the Lord Jesus Christ himself proclaim the evils to come? When old age overtakes the world, when the end draws near—you have heard it, brothers, we have heard it together—*there will be wars, there will be disturbances, there will be afflictions, there will be famines*. Why do we contradict ourselves, believing these things when they are read, and murmuring when they are fulfilled?

**11.** But more, they say; the human race is being devastated more severely now. For the moment, considering past history and leaving the question open, I do not know whether it is more severely. But suppose it is more severely; I grant that it is. The Lord himself resolves the question. The world is being devastated more severely now, he says, more severely. Why is it being devastated more severely now, when the Gospel is preached everywhere? You notice how widely the Gospel is proclaimed, and fail to notice how impiously it is despised. Now, brothers, let us leave the pagans outside for a little while and turn our eyes toward ourselves. The Gospel is preached; the whole world is full of it. Before the Gospel was preached, God's will was hidden; through the preaching of the Gospel, God's will was made plain. Through the preaching of the Gospel we have been told what we should love, what we should despise, what we should do, what we should avoid, what we should hope for. We have heard it all; God's will is not hidden throughout the world. Take the world as a slave, and attend to the Gospel. Hear the Lord's voice; this slave is the world: *The slave who does not know his master's will and does not do what is fitting will receive few blows*. The slave is the world; it is a slave, because *the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*. *The slave who does not know his master's will:* see what the world was before. *The slave who does not know his master's will and does not do* what is fitting *will be beaten with few blows*. *But the slave who knows* *his master's will:* see what the world is like now. Tell yourselves what follows—or rather, let us tell ourselves: *The slave who knows his master's will and does not do what is fitting will be beaten with many blows*. If only it may be beaten with many blows, and not condemned once for all! Why do you refuse to be beaten with many blows, slave who know your Lord's will and do what deserves blows? You are told—here is one expression of your Lord's will—*Store up treasure for yourselves in heaven, where neither moth nor rust nor anything that eats away destroys, and where thieves neither break through nor steal* 22. You are on earth; he is in heaven, saying to you: Give it to me; keep your treasure where I am its guardian; send it ahead of you. Why are you keeping it? Can a Goth take away what Christ guards? Yet you, supposedly more prudent and wiser than your Lord, insist on storing up treasure only on earth. But you have learned your Lord's will; he wanted you to store it above. Therefore, if you store it on earth, be ready for many blows. See, you know the will of your Lord, who wants you to keep it in heaven; you keep it on earth and do what deserves blows. And when you are beaten, you blaspheme, you murmur, and you say that what your Lord does to you should not have been done. Should what you do, wicked slave, have been done?

**12.** At least hold to this position: do not murmur, do not blaspheme. Praise your God instead, because he corrects you; praise him because he reforms you so that he may console you. *For the Lord corrects the one he loves, and scourges every son he receives*. You, a pampered son of the Lord, want to be received and do not want to be scourged, so that you may live dissolutely and he may prove a liar. Should the memorial of the Apostles, through which heaven is prepared for you, therefore preserve the theaters of madmen for you on earth forever? Did Peter die and receive burial so that a stone would not fall from a theater*?* God shakes the pleasures out of the hands of undisciplined children. Brothers, let us diminish our sins and our murmuring. Let us be enemies of our own iniquities and our own murmuring; let us be angry with ourselves, not with God. *Be angry*—by all means be angry, but to what purpose? *And do not sin*. Be angry for this reason: so that you do not sin. For everyone who repents is angry with himself; the penitent directs anger against himself. Do you want God to spare you? Do not spare yourself. For if you spare yourself, he will not spare you; if he too spares you, you perish. You do not know what you are asking for, wretched man; you perish. For just as it is written, *He scourges every son he receives,* so fear this also: *The sinner has provoked the Lord.* How do you know, one might ask*,* how do you know that *the sinner has provoked the Lord*? I saw a prosperous sinner; I saw him doing evil every day, suffering no evil, and blaspheming against the Holy Spirit. I shuddered, I grieved*. The sinner has provoked the Lord.* This sinner, who has done such great evils and suffers no evil, has irritated the Lord, has provoked the Lord: *In the greatness of his anger he will not investigate.* This is what follows: *The sinner has provoked the Lord; in the greatness of his anger he will not investigate*. He will not investigate precisely because he is very angry; when he removes correction, he prepares condemnation. *He will not investigate:* for if he investigated, he would scourge; if he scourged, he would reform*.* But now he is very angry; he is very angry with the prosperous wicked*.* Do not envy them; do not wish to be like them. It is better to be scourged than condemned*.*

**13.** Thus the Lord entrusted his sheep to us, because he entrusted them to Peter—if indeed we are worthy in even the smallest degree to tread the dust of Peter's footprints, if indeed we can. The Lord entrusted his sheep to us. You are his sheep; together with you we are sheep, because we are Christians. As I have already said, we are fed and we feed. Love God, so that God may love you; you cannot show how much you love God except by how much you show that you love God's gains. What can you provide for God, sensible person? What can you provide for God? What Peter too provided; it all comes to this: *Feed my sheep.* What can you provide for God? That he may be greater? That he may be better? That he may be richer? That he may be more honored? Whatever you may be, he will be what he was. So look beside you, to see whether you owe your neighbor a service that will reach God. *When you did it for one of my least ones, you did it for me*. If, then, you are commanded to break bread for the hungry, should you close the church to someone knocking?

**14.** Why have I said this? We were saddened by what we heard happened while we were absent: someone from the Donatists came to the Church and confessed the sin of rebaptism; when the bishop encouraged him to do penance, some brothers protested, and he was turned away. I tell Your Charity, this twisted our very insides. We admit to you that this sort of vigilance did not please us. I know they acted out of zeal for God; I know*,* I do not doubt that they acted out of zeal for God. But they should also attend to those words of the apostle Paul, how he laments even those who *have zeal for God, but not according to knowledge*. See, he was not admitted today; tomorrow he will die. At whose hands will his life be required? You will say: But he is pretending. I answer: But he is asking. I show that he is asking; you show that he is pretending. Christian, now I want you to teach me too: how do you know he is pretending? Because he fears for his property. We know many who feared for their property and became Catholics for that reason. When they received freedom, some returned to the Donatists, but some remained. As long as they had not entered, they feared for their property; once they entered, they learned and remained. How, then, do you know that this man who fears for his property will be among those found to be insincere, especially now that so much light has been shed on the truth, so much proof brought against falsehood? Why do you want to judge the heart, human being? Did we sweat for this, labor for this, was truth shown to be unconquered for this—that it should be hostile to those who seek it? We labored so that truth might be displayed and falsehood refuted. The Lord helped, and it was done. Perhaps the person for whose sake it was done has been changed by some reflection. Why do you want to judge the heart? I see someone asking, and you accuse someone of pretending? Christian, admit what you see; leave what you do not see to God. I tell Your Charity briefly: we have heard from the Lord himself that his sheep must be fed, and we know what he says about the sheep through Ezekiel: one sheep must not butt another, one sheep must not push another, the strong must not oppress the weak. Attend to what the Apostle says: *Correct the disorderly, console the fainthearted, support the weak*. *Correct the disorderly:* let this be done. *Console the fainthearted:* let this be done. *Support the weak:* let this be done. *Be patient with everyone:* let this be done. *Let no one repay anyone evil for evil:* let this be done. He says so much; we pay attention there only to *Correct the disorderly*. Pay attention: *Correct the disorderly.* Count the rest: *Console the fainthearted, support the weak, be patient with everyone, see that no one repays anyone evil for evil*. You pay attention only to *Correct the disorderly.* Take care that you yourself are not disorderly—indeed, worse still, that you do not want both to be disorderly and to escape correction. We ask you through Christ, we implore you: do not pour away our labor. Or do you think we ought to be pleased with ourselves because we have conquered falsehood? Victory always belongs to truth. What are we? Falsehood was conquered long ago; but thanks be to God, it has been openly conquered and exposed to human beings. The cultivation has been abundant; why is the fruit being obstructed?

**15.** From now on, brothers, let these things not happen. Let no one love the Church in such a way that he begrudges the Church its gains. What I am speaking of happened three or four days ago, and the report spread widely among everyone that Donatists are not admitted when they come to the church. Do you think no harm has been done because this report reached everyone? I ask that this voice may therefore sound out today, so that the good news may drown out the bad report. Make the effort; this is what we have said, this is what we proclaim. Let those who have never yet been Catholics come and be admitted in the customary way. As for those who have already been Catholics and have proved slippery, have proved unstable and weak, have proved faithless—am I sparing them? Absolutely faithless—perhaps those who were faithless will become faithful. Let them too come, to be admitted to penance. Nor should they flatter themselves that, when they returned to Donatus's party, they did penance there. That was penance for a good deed; let there be true penance for an evil deed. When they did penance in Donatus's party, they repented of what they had done well; now let them do penance so that they repent of what they have done badly. Because they have proved faithless, are you afraid they may trample what is holy? See, provision is made even for this fear of yours: they are left in penance. They will remain in penance when they wish to be reconciled, with no one any longer compelling them, no one frightening them. For a Catholic penitent no longer suffers the threats of the laws. He has begun to desire reconciliation while no one was frightening him; at least then, trust his will. Suppose he was compelled to become a Catholic: he will be a penitent. Who compels him to ask for admission to reconciliation except his own will? So let us admit weakness now, so that afterward we may test willingness.
