# (Erfurt 1 = Sermon 282, expanded): Sermon of Saint Augustine, Bishop

On Saints Perpetua and Felicity.

**1. 1.** {The holy martyrs whose feast we celebrate today not only distinguished themselves by outstanding virtues in their suffering, but also, through their own names, signified the reward for so great a labor of devotion, both their own and that of their other companions. Perpetua and Felicity are indeed the names of two, but perpetual felicity is the reward of all. For all the martyrs did not bravely undergo the temporary toil of their struggle in confession and suffering except to rejoice in perpetual felicity. Under the guidance of divine providence, therefore, these women were destined to be not only martyrs but also the closest of companions, as in fact they were, so that they might mark a single day of their glory and hand down to posterity a common feast to celebrate. For just as the example of their most glorious struggle urges us to imitate them, so their names bear witness that we will receive an inseparable gift. Let each hold the other; let each be bound to the other. We do not hope for either without the other. Perpetuity is of no use if there is no Felicity, and Felicity abandons us if there is no Perpetuity. Let these few words, suited to the time available, suffice concerning the names of the martyrs through whom this day has been consecrated for us.

**2. 2.** As for the women who bore these names, we heard when the account of their martyrdom was read, and know from the record handed down to us, that these women of such great virtues and merits were not only female but also married women. One of them was also a mother, so that an affection less able to endure suffering was added to the weakness of her sex.} You who are unmarried, take an example of faith, courage, patience, and devotion from a mother; you young men, from women; you virgins, from married women! There was also the fact that, in keeping with their social standing at birth, they had been delicately brought up by their parents’ habits and affection, {so that the enemy, assailing them in every way as though they were unable to bear the harsh and cruel burdens of persecution, thought they would immediately yield to him and soon become his. But through the most watchful and steadfast strength of their inner selves, they frustrated all his plots and broke his attacks.

**3. 3.** In the army of Christ the King,} they stood ready for battle, yielding to no adversity: {not held back by the weakness of their sex, not enfeebled by womanly thoughts, not softened by the world’s flatteries or terrified by its threats. Though female, they fought ardently; though women, manfully; though delicately raised, with endurance; though weak, with strength. They overcame the flesh by the spirit, fear by hope, the devil by faith, and the world by love. With these weapons our King’s army is invincible; equipped with these weapons, Christ’s soldiers triumphed by having their bodily limbs slaughtered rather than preserved, and by dying rather than killing.} For they preferred the command of the eternal King to the command of an earthly king and surrendered their bodies, so that they would neither serve nor worship any god except their own God. They did not fear those who kill the body, but him who has power to kill both body and soul in the hell of fire. In this way they cared not only for their spirits, which they established immovably in their victorious resolve, but also for the very bodies they seemed to despise, so that the injustice of persecution might sow them in dishonor and the truth of judgment raise them in glory.

**4. 4.** In this contest Perpetua, transformed into a man as had been revealed to her in a vision, defeated the devil. Stripped of the world and clothed in Christ, she attained the unity of faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, reaching perfect manhood and becoming an outstanding member of his body, for whose sake she had cast away her whole body, not just one member.

**5. 5.** In this contest Felicity was not prevented from the confession of martyrdom even by the burden of her womb. For she was pregnant both in body and in heart. Her heart had conceived through fruitful fellowship with God, her body through human marriage. The law of nature delayed the bodily birth; the force of persecution pressed for the other. The one lacked the maturity of its time; for the other, the opportunity of interrogation was at hand. The one would be miscarried if hastened; the other would be killed if denied. Thus this deeply devout woman remembered that she was both to give birth under the woman’s punishment and to confess the Virgin’s child. For the one she had made room in her bodily womb, for the other in her spiritual heart. Burdened in a human way with the former, she grew weary; honored by God with the latter, she rejoiced. And so, having rightly heard and firmly believed Christ himself saying, *Whoever does the will of my Father is my brother and sister and mother*, she did not become a mother of Adam before becoming a mother of Christ: by confessing she brought forth the heavenly man before the judge, while with her groaning she preserved the earthly one for the prison. There too, what an answer she gave when, crying out with Eve’s voice in the pains of childbirth, she was asked what she would do among the beasts if she was so unable to endure the necessity of giving birth! She said, *Here* *it is I who suffer what I suffer; but there another will suffer for me, for whose faith I am going to suffer*. Fittingly, it came about that, when she encountered the cow notorious for its savagery, she felt it so little that she did not even retain a memory of it. In the burden of her womb she was shown what she was herself; in the suffering of martyrdom, what had been given to her.

**6. 6.** (3) {In this company of outstanding glory there were also male martyrs; on that very day, men too conquered through a most courageous martyrdom. Yet they did not lend their names to the day. This did not happen because the women surpassed the men in dignity, but because feminine weakness defeated the fiercest enemy with a greater miracle, and manly courage fought for perpetual felicity.}
