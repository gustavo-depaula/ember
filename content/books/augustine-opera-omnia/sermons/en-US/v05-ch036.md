# Sermon 173

**1.** When we celebrate the days of our dead brothers, we ought to have in mind both what is to be hoped and what is to be feared. For according to this it is to be hoped, since *precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his holy ones*[^1]; but according to this it is to be feared, because *the death of sinners is most evil*[^2]. And therefore, on account of hope: *The just will be in eternal memory*; on account of fear: *He will not fear an evil hearing*[^3]. For there will be a hearing than which none is worse, when it will be said to those on the left, *Go into the eternal fire*[^4]. From this evil hearing the just man will not fear. For he will be at the right hand among those to whom it will be said, *Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the kingdom*[^5]. But in this life, which is led in the middle, before the highest goods and before the highest evils, in the midst of middling goods and evils—that is, on neither side of the highest; because both whatever goods there are here for man are, in comparison with the eternal goods, nothing; and whatever evils a man experiences in this life are, in comparison with the eternal fire, not even to be compared—in this middle state of life, then, we ought to hold what we have now heard from the Gospel: *He who believes*, he says, *in me, though he die, lives*. He both proclaims life, and does not deny death. *He who believes in me, though he die, lives*. What is, *though he die, lives*? Though he die in body, he lives in spirit. Then he adds: *And whoever lives and believes in me will not die forever*[^6]. Surely *though he die*—how, if he *will not die*? But *though he die* for a time, *he will not die forever*. So this question is resolved, so that the words of truth are not contrary to one another, and can instruct the affection of devotion. Therefore, though we are going to die in body, we live if we believe.

**2.** But our faith is most especially distinguished from every faith of the gentiles in the resurrection of the dead. For this they wholly do not receive: because they have not wherein to receive it. For the will of man is prepared by the Lord, that it may be a receptacle of faith[^7]. The Lord says to the Jews, *My word does not find room in you*[^8]. Therefore it finds room in those in whom it finds what to take in. For in those the word that takes in finds what to take in, whom God by promising does not deceive. For he who seeks the lost sheep[^9] both knows the one he seeks, and where to seek it, and how to gather its scattered members, and to restore it to one health, and so to restore it that he loses it no more. Let us console one another, then, in these our sayings too. The human heart can avoid grieving over a dear one who has died; yet the human heart is better healed when it grieves than made inhuman by not grieving. Mary cleaved to the Lord, and grieved over her dead brother. But why do you wonder that Mary grieved then, when the Lord himself wept? But it may move anyone, how he wept over the dead man whom he himself commanded immediately to live[^10]. He did not weep over the dead man, whom he himself raised; but over the death which man procured for himself by sinning. For if sin had not gone before, without doubt death would not have followed. Therefore the death even of the body followed, which the death of the soul preceded. The death of the soul preceded by forsaking God, and the death of the body followed by the soul's forsaking. By the former it forsook willing, by the latter it was forced to forsake unwilling. As though it were said to it: You withdrew from him whom you ought to have loved, withdraw from what you loved. For who wishes to die? Surely no one: and so wholly no one that it was said to blessed Peter, *Another will gird you, and carry you where you do not wish*[^11]. If, then, there were no bitterness of death, there would not be the great fortitude of the martyrs.

**3.** Therefore the Apostle too says, *Concerning those who sleep, I do not wish you to be ignorant, brothers, that you not be saddened, like the gentiles too, who have no hope*. He did not only say, *that you not be saddened*; but, *that you not be saddened thus, as the gentiles, who have no hope*. For it is necessary that you be saddened: but where you are saddened, let hope console you. For how are you not saddened, where a body which lives from the soul becomes lifeless, the soul departing? He who walked lies still, he who spoke is silent, the closed eyes take in no light, the ears open to no voice: all the offices of the members have come to rest; there is no one to move the steps for walking, the hands for working, the senses for perceiving. Is this not the house which some invisible dweller was adorning? He who was not seen has departed, what is seen with grief has remained. This is the cause of sadness. If this is the cause of sadness, let there be the consolation of this sadness. What consolation? *That the Lord himself, at a command and at the voice of an archangel, and at the last trumpet, will descend from heaven, and the dead in Christ will rise first: then we who are alive, who remain, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet Christ in the air*. Is this too for a time? No; but what is it? *And so we will always be with the Lord*[^12]. Let sadness perish, where there is so great a consolation; let mourning be wiped from the mind, let faith drive out grief. In so great a hope it is not fitting that the temple of God be sad. There dwells the good Consoler, there the Promiser who does not deceive. Why should we lament a dead person long? Because death is bitter? Through it the Lord too passed. Let these few things suffice for your Charity: may he console you more abundantly who does not migrate from your heart; but may he so deign to dwell, that he may also deign to change us in the end. Let us turn to the Lord, etc.
