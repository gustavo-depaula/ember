# Sermon 218/C: On the Passion of the Lord

**1.** The passion of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ is the confidence of glory and the teaching of patience. For what would the hearts of the faithful not promise themselves from the grace of God, for whom it was too little that the only Son of God, coeternal with the Father, should be born as man from man, unless he should even die at the hands of the men whom he created? Great is what is promised to us by the Lord as future: but much greater is what we recall has already been done for us. Where were they, or what were they, when Christ died for the ungodly[^1]? Who could doubt that he will give his life to the saints, who gave to those same ones, while still ungodly, his death? Why does human frailty hesitate to believe it will be that men will at some time live with God? Much more incredible has already been done, that God died for men. For who is Christ, except that which *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^2]? This Word of God *was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^3]. For he did not have in himself whereby to die for us, unless he had taken mortal flesh from us. So the immortal could die, so he willed to give life to mortals; about to make us afterward partakers of himself, of whom he had first been made a partaker. For we had neither whence to live of our own, nor he whence to die of his own. So he carried on with us a wondrous transaction of mutual sharing; ours was that whence he died; his will be that whence we live. Yet the flesh, which he took from us whence to die, even that, since he is the Creator, he gave; but the life, by which in him and with him we are to live, he did not receive from us. And through this, as far as concerns our nature, by which we are men, he died not of his own, but of ours; since his nature, by which he is God, cannot die at all. But as far as concerns his own creature, which as God he made, he died even of his own; since the flesh too, in which he died, he himself made.

**2.** Not only, then, ought we not to blush at the death of the Lord our God, but rather most of all to trust in it and most of all to glory in it; for, taking up from us the death which he found in us, he most faithfully pledged that he would give us in himself the life which we cannot have of ourselves. For he who so loved us that what we deserved by sin he, without sin, suffered for sinners, how will he not give us what righteousness gives, he who justifies? how will he not render, who promises in truth, the rewards of the saints, he who without iniquity bore the punishment of the unrighteous? Let us therefore confess, brothers, undismayed, or even profess, that Christ was crucified for us; not fearing, but rejoicing: not ashamed, but glorying let us say it. The apostle Paul saw this, and commended the title of glory. He who, when he had many great and divine things which he might recall of Christ, did not say that he gloried in the wonders of Christ, that, being God with the Father, he created the world, that, being also man as we are, he commanded the world; but *but far be it from me to glory*, he says, *except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ*[^4]. He saw for whom, who, where he had hung; and from so great a humility of God and divine loftiness the Apostle drew presumption.

**3.** But these men who insult us, that we worship a crucified Lord, the more they seem to themselves to be wise, the more incurably and hopelessly they are foolish, and do not understand at all what we believe or say; for we do not say that that died in Christ which was God, but that which was man. For if, when any man himself dies, that which is most of all man — that is, that by which he differs from a beast, that he has understanding, that he discerns the human and the divine, the temporal and the eternal, the false and the true, that is, the rational soul — does not suffer death with its body, but, that body dying, departs alive, and yet it is said: The man is dead; why should it not likewise be said: God is dead, so that it be understood that not that which is God could die, but that which God had taken up, mortal, for mortals? For as, when a man dies, his soul does not die in the flesh, so too when Christ died, his divinity did not die in the man. But God, they say, could not be mingled with man, and become with him one Christ. According to that fleshly and empty opinion and human notions, we would believe much more difficultly that spirit can be mingled with flesh than that God can be mingled with man; and yet no man would be a man, unless the spirit of man were mingled with a human body. Since, then, of spirit and body there is a more difficult and more wonderful mixture than of spirit and Spirit; if, then, the spirit of man, since it is not a body, and the body of man, since it is not a spirit, yet, that he might be a man, both were commingled; how much more, that out of both there might be one Christ, could God, who is a Spirit[^5], be mingled, not with a body apart from a spirit, but with a man having a spirit, by spiritual participation.

**4.** Let us too, then, glory in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world is crucified to us, and let us be crucified to the world[^6]; that we might not blush at this cross, we placed it on the very forehead, that is, in the dwelling of modesty. But now what teaching of patience is in this cross, and how wholesome it is, if we should try to unfold, what words would suffice for the matter, what time for the words? For what man, who most truly and most intently believes in Christ, would dare to be proud, when God teaches humility not only by word, but also by his own example? But how useful this teaching is, that sentence of holy Scripture briefly admonishes: *Before ruin the heart is exalted, and before glory it is humbled*[^7]; with which this too accords: *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^8]; and this: *He who exalts himself will be humbled; and he who humbles himself will be exalted*[^9]. Accordingly, since the Apostle admonishes us not to be high-minded, but to consent to humble things[^10], let him consider, if he can, into how great a precipice of pride a man is borne, if he does not consent to the humble God; and how pernicious it is for a man impatiently to bear what the just God willed, if patiently the God bore what the unjust enemy willed.
