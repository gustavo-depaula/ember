# Sermon 221: On the Holy Night

**1.** Since our Lord Jesus Christ made the day which he had made mournful by dying glorious by rising, recalling both times to a solemn memory, let us watch by remembering his death, and let us rejoice by taking up his resurrection. This is our yearly feast, and our Passover; not, as for the old people, figured by the slaying of an animal, but, as for the new people, fulfilled by the victim of the Savior; because *our Passover, Christ, was sacrificed*[^1], and the old things have passed away, and behold, they have been made new[^2]. For we mourn only as burdened by our sins, or we rejoice only as justified by his grace; since *he was handed over for our trespasses, and rose for our justification*[^3]. The one we are glad in by mourning, the other by rejoicing; and what was sadly committed and gladly sent ahead on our account and for us, we do not pass over by ungrateful forgetfulness, but celebrate with grateful memory. Let us watch, then, dearest ones, because the burial of Christ was prolonged up to this night; that in this very night might come the resurrection of the flesh, which then was mocked on the wood, now is adored in heaven and on earth. For this night is understood to pertain to the following day, which we hold as the Lord's Day. And surely he ought to rise by night, because by his resurrection he illuminated even our darkness; nor was it sung to him in vain a little before: *You will light my lamp, Lord; my God, you will lighten my darkness*[^4]. Accordingly, our devotion too commends so great a mystery; that, as by his resurrection our faith, strengthened, already watches, so too by our watching even this night, illuminated, may shine before; that we may worthily be able, with the Church spread throughout the world, to consider today, lest we be found in the night. To so many and so great peoples, whom so distinguished a solemnity everywhere gathered in the name of Christ, the sun has departed, the day has not withdrawn, since to the shining sky a shining earth has succeeded.

**2.** Yet if anyone inquire the causes of this our so great vigil, he can both skillfully find them and faithfully answer. For he who gave us the glory of his name, he himself illuminated this night; and to him whom we say: *You will lighten my darkness*[^5], he offers light to our hearts; that, as with rejoicing eyes we behold this splendor of the lamps, so with an illuminated mind we may see the reason of this so bright night. What is it, then, that on a yearly feast the Christians watch today? For now most of all we watch, nor is any other solemnity wont to be thought such; in this desire, while we seek and say: When do we watch? we watch to so many days; as though, in comparison with this, other vigils were not to be reckoned. The Apostle indeed commended to the Church the assiduity of vigils as of fasts, recalling of himself and saying: *In fastings often, in watchings often*[^6]; but the vigil of this night is so great that it alone claims for itself, as if its own, the common name of the rest. So first of the general vigil, afterward of the special and present one, as the Lord shall grant, we will say a few things.

**3.** In that life, for whose rest to be obtained we labor, which Truth promises us after the death of this body, or even after the end of this age, in the resurrection, we shall never sleep, as surely we shall never die. For what else is sleep but a daily death, which neither takes a man entirely from here, nor detains him too long? And what else is death but a long and deepest sleep, from which God rouses a man? Where, then, no death comes, neither does its image, sleep, intervene. Finally, there is no sleep but of mortals. This is not the rest of the angels: they, because they always live, never refresh their health with sleep. As life itself, so there is a vigil without end; nor is it anything else there to live than to watch, nor anything else to watch than to live. But we, in this body, which is corrupted and weighs down the soul[^7], since we live only if we repair our strength by sleeping, interrupt life by a likeness of death, that we may at least be able to live by intervals. And through this, whoever chastely and innocently uses vigils assiduously, without doubt meditates the life of the angels — for as much as the weakness of this flesh is a burden to the earthly, the heavenly desires are repressed — exercising against the deadly mass by a longer vigil, that he may earn for himself merit in perpetual life. For he is not consistent with himself, who desires always to live, and does not love to watch more at length; he wills that death not be at all, and does not will to lessen its image. This is the cause, this the reason, why in vigils the mind is more often to be exercised by the Christian.

**4.** Now, brothers, while we recall a few other things, attend concerning the special vigil of this night. For it has been said why we ought more often to take away sleep and add vigils: but now it must be said why with so great celebrity we watch especially on this night. That on the third day the Lord Christ rose from the dead, no Christian doubts. But that this was done on this night, the holy Gospel witnesses. For it is not in doubt that the whole day is reckoned from the preceding night; not according to the order of the days which are recalled in Genesis — although there too the darkness went before; for *darkness was upon the deep*, when *God said: let there be light, and light was made*[^8] — but because that darkness was not yet night, was not yet day. For *God divided between the light and the darkness*[^9]: and first he called the light day, then the darkness night, and from the light made until the next morning one day was recalled[^10]. It is manifest that those days began from light, and, the night passed, were each ended at morning. But after the created man turned away from the light of righteousness into the darkness of sin, from which the grace of Christ freed him, it came about that now we reckon days from nights; because we strive to come not from light to darkness, but from darkness to light, and, with the Lord helping, we hope to. So too the Apostle says: *The night is far gone, the day is at hand; let us therefore cast off the works of darkness, and put on the armor of light*[^11]. The day of the Lord's passion, then, on which he was crucified, followed its own night already passed; and therefore it was closed and ended up to the Day of Preparation, which the Jews also call the "pure supper," the observance of the sabbath beginning from the start of that night. Then the day of the sabbath, beginning from its own night, was ended at the evening of the beginning night, which pertains to the beginning of the Lord's Day; since the Lord consecrated it by the glory of his resurrection. Of that night, then, pertaining to the beginning of the Lord's Day, we now in this solemnity celebrate the memory. We keep by watching that night on which the Lord rose, and that life of which we were speaking a little before, where there is neither any death nor sleep, he began for us in his own flesh; which he so roused from the dead that now it dies no more, nor will death any longer have dominion over him[^12]. For since those who came to his tomb, by whom, loving him, he was sought, did not find his body at dawn, and received an answer from the angels that he had already risen, it is manifest that he rose on that night whose extremity that dawn was. Accordingly, he to whom, rising, we sing together by watching a little longer, will grant that we may reign by living with him without end. But even if perhaps in these hours, in which we keep this vigil, his body was still in the tomb, and he had not yet risen, not even so are we unfitting in watching; because he slept that we might watch, who died that we might live. Amen.
