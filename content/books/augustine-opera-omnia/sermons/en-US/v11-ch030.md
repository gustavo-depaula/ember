# Sermon 348/A: Augm. (Dolbeau 30)

**1.** The cause of the coming and incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ is that, when he came, he found all men sinners. The Apostle says most openly that this was the cause of his coming, in this way: *It is a human saying,* he says, *and worthy of all acceptance, that Jesus Christ came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first*[^1]. No cause, then, brought the Son of God, God and eternal God and coeternal with and equal to the Father, from heaven to earth to take on flesh and to die for us, except that there was no life in us. The physician would not descend, except to the sick; Life would not descend, except to the dead. Today, when the Apostle was read, you who were attentive heard: *God commends his love in us, in that, when we were yet sinners, Christ died for us; much more, now justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him*[^2]. This is the *grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord,* which first the prophets, then he himself by his own mouth, then the apostles after the presence of his flesh, then the whole Church holds, confesses, preaches and commends and worships. This is the *grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord.*

**2.** Whence your love ought first to know, indeed to recall what you know and what you have always heard, that no man can be saved by his own merits and strength. For man was easy at wounding himself, just as of the very life of our flesh too it is easy for anyone to kill himself: is he able to raise himself again? That we might fall, then, we needed no help; rather, because we dismissed the help of God, therefore we fell. But that we may rise from our ruin, let us seek his help, lest we remain in our sins. *Christ died for us*—you heard the Apostle—not for himself, but *for us.* Why not for himself, but *for us?* Because he had no cause of death, in whom was no sin. Death is the penalty of sin. For even Adam, unless he had sinned, would surely not have died, nor would we, mortal, be born of his propagation. But that one came without sin, who might loose all sins: for one bound could not loose the bound, nor one guilty free the condemned. He took on flesh from a virgin without the lust of a man; the flesh taken on was not a wound, but the medicine of the wound. *Christ died for us.*

**3.** What else shall we seek from him? What is Christ? You have heard: when he asked his disciples, it was said by Peter: *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^3]. He is the Son by nature, we by grace; he the only one, we many, because he was born, we adopted. Since, then, God had one and only Son, his very only and proper *Son,* as the Apostle says, *he did not spare, but handed him over for us all*[^4]. What greater medicine could the human race ask or hope for, than that the only Son be sent not to live with us, but to die? But that he might die for us, he took on flesh in which he might die, because, when he was the Word God with God the Father, he had nothing whereby to die. *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^5]. Where is visibility and tangibility in the Word, where is passion or death in God? By the mind alone can be seen what cannot be seen. But even the mind itself, covered with darkness, blinded by sins—the whole man weak, the whole languid, the whole wounded, I dare to say the whole dead and extinct—whence would it see him present everywhere, not having a sound inner eye, whereby the invisible things are seen?

**4.** Therefore the whole in us was not sound: there descended the curer of body and soul, because the Savior of body and soul. For if these physicians can cure what they did not create from these medicines and herbs which they did not create, if, then, a human physician cures from what is God's, how much more does God care for what is his own? But the physician cares for one about to die, God for one about to live forever. And this very thing, that he willed to die for us, was our medicine. Great is that mercy, brethren, of our physician, that he willed to cure us not from his medicine-chest, but from his blood. *Much more,* he says, *now justified.* Whence? *In his blood,* not in our strength, not in our merits, but *in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him,* not through ourselves, but *through him.* He bound us to the cross: surely, if we wish to live, let us not cling to death. He who clung to himself, clung to death. For life is not in the dead. What does the dead man presume of himself? He could die of himself, but he cannot revive of himself. To sin through ourselves we both could and can, yet to rise again through ourselves we shall at no time be able. Let our hope be in nothing but God. Let us groan to him, presume in him; what pertains to us, let us strive by will, that we may merit by prayer.

**5.** Since these things are so, brethren, I will tell you more openly, for it is not something we should hide: a certain new heresy, lying hidden and secretly and widely creeping, as far as we could, until it should burst out, we tolerated in silence; the error itself we always refuted; the names we kept silent, lest perhaps, when we refuted the error, the men should be corrected; we kept the names silent: for nothing is better, nothing more desirable, than that they, hearing what was preached by us according to the most ancient foundation of the Church, should fear to preach their errors and in silence be healed, turned to him who heals all who call upon his name. This we did for a long time. For about impiety of this kind we even wrote some things, and they were already in the hands of readers, and yet the names of those about whom we had written had not yet been brought to your notice. There were here some of them, and some have been corrected, of whose salvation, in the name and mercy of the Lord, we rejoice. For of these, who have been corrected from that error, very many asked us to compose writings about that very error.

**6.** But now we have heard: that very one who is the prince and author of this pernicious dogma, they were saying that in the eastern parts, by episcopal proceedings, he had been absolved and pronounced catholic. Therefore the things objected to him he denied to be his, and what others seemed to scatter through his teaching, he denied that he held, but even anathematized. The proceedings indeed have not yet reached us. But yet, because we are wont to write to him familiarly as to a servant of God, as he to us, last year, when my son the presbyter Orosius, who is with us, a servant of God from Spain, went to the east with my letters, I wrote through him to the same Pelagius, not noting him in my letters, but exhorting him to hear from the presbyter what I charged. But the presbyter found that very place where he was already most disturbed by his preachings and the dissensions of the brethren; thence he brought back to me a letter of the holy presbyter Jerome, much to be venerated by us for the merit of his age and holiness and erudition, known to all. But this presbyter Jerome had already written against him also a . . . book . . . on free choice, which was brought to us too. But he, as I said, by ecclesiastical proceedings was absolved, confessing the grace of God which he seemed to deny and assail by his disputations.

**7.** But afterward, a few days ago, there came thence to us our fellow citizen, the deacon Palatinus, son of Gattus, of Hippo—many recognize him, and more know his name; he is present to his father; he is here among the deacons, he stands, he hears me, this is he. He brought me a certain brief little book of Pelagius himself against the things objected to him, not as a part of the proceedings, but a defense made and composed by him, just as perhaps he defended himself even in the episcopal proceedings, which, as I said, could not yet reach our hands. And he charged the deacon to give me that same defense of his to read; yet he did not send me his letter. Whence I am anxious lest afterward he deny that he sent even what he sent. To dispute anything from it I was unwilling, until we read the proceedings, where ecclesiastical and episcopal authority seems to be contained. But why did I wish to intimate this to your faith? Because I know not what great disturbance was made at Jerusalem and announced to us full of sadness, so that it is even said that by a popular tumult two monasteries at Bethlehem were burned. Which it was not needful to tell you, except that I recognized it had now reached some of you too. Better, then, that you heard the whole from me, than that you be wounded by hidden rumors.

**8.** What evil, then, this heresy has, briefly receive, that you may beware and, whomever you hear either secretly whispering such things or with open disputations clamoring them, do not hide from us. For we fear lest the cancer creep, when it is spared, and we suddenly find many such, as many as we can scarcely or never heal. What evil, then, this heresy has, hear. What I was saying a little before and commending to you, the *grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord,* this grace that heresy assails by its pestiferous disputations. How, you say? They say that human nature can do so much, that free choice of the will can do so much, that, just as through ourselves we were made sinners, so also through ourselves we can be justified. And since a just man is better than a man—for the name "man" is of nature; the name "justice," of happiness and beatitude—since, then, a just man is better than any man whatever, they say that God made man, but that man himself made himself just, so that man seems to give himself more than God had given him.

**9.** Let your love attend, then: through the malignant disputations of these men our prayers too are assailed. For so they act, so they dispute, that we seem to pray without cause. For the Lord taught us how to pray, lest perhaps in our prayers we ask carnal and temporal things, as it is to ask that your head not ache, that you not die, that you not bury a son, that you not suffer loss, that, oppressed by someone, you not be sent to prison, and whatever such temporal and worldly things there are here. These things they grant us to pray; that which the Lord taught they take away, not because they dare to deny it, but because they dispute such things whereby it is taken away. For when it says to you: "You suffice yourself for doing justice; if you will, you do it; if you will, you do not do it; you need no help of God for fulfilling what he commanded, because the grace of God is not that which helps you that you not sin, but that grace which made you with a free will." When, therefore, they say these things, they call the grace of God that by which we were made, which grace we have in common even with the pagans. For it is not that we were created and they were not created, or that we came forth from the workshop of another artificer than they: one God we and they have as author, one founder, one creator, *who makes his sun to rise upon the good and the bad, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^6]. This they call the grace of God; another they are unwilling to call, not that by which we are men even with the pagans, but that by which we are Christians. You know what grace they deny, hear more evidently.

**10.** You know that the apostle Paul set before our eyes our conflict with the flesh, that we may live piously and justly, the very struggle in which we labor, saying: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man. But I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin—and of death—which is in my members*[^7]. In this difficulty he cried out and said: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?* and it was as it were answered: *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^8]. This grace they do not deny, but they hear that you struggle with the flesh and with the evil habit of your sins: "You suffice yourself to conquer. Why do you ask for help? By your own virtue you can fulfill this." But the Apostle gave way. He confessed weakness that he might obtain health: *I see,* he says, *another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin.* What does it profit me that in the inner man I delight by the mind in the law of God? See, I am fought, see, I am dragged, see, I am oppressed, see, I am led captive. See whether, as it were looking up from a great pressure, he did not cry out to God. If he said: *Who will free me from the body of this death,* except my own virtue?, he would seem to have spoken proudly, but perhaps we would understand even this, that he could not say it except of God, to whom the psalm says: *I will love you, Lord, my strength*[^9]. Did he, then, say: *Who will free me,* except my nature, except my will, except the strength of my choice and power? He did not say it. He humbled himself that he might be exalted: *The grace of God,* he said, *through Jesus Christ our Lord.*

**11.** On account of this grace the Lord commended to us what we should pray for: *Hallowed be*—and what?—*your name*[^10]. Is the name of God not holy? What is *hallowed be,* but in us? Now you, if by free will and by the proper strength of nature you can hallow in yourself the name of God, why do you pray, why do you ask of the highest majesty what you have in your power? What more? {(1.) Those two: *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors,* and: *Lead us not into temptation*[^11], when these are objected to them, what do you think they answer? I shuddered, my brethren, when I heard it. I indeed did not hear it with my ears, but the holy brother and my fellow bishop, our Urbanus, who was a presbyter here and is now bishop of Sicca, when he had returned from the city of Rome and there had a conflict with a certain one holding such views, or narrated that he had had a conflict, told me that he said this, when he was pressed by the weight of the Lord's prayer. For he was pressing him and saying: "If it is in our power not to sin and it is in our power to overcome all the temptations of sins by our wills alone, why do we say to God: Lead us not into temptation?" What do you think he answered? "We ask," he says, "God not to lead us into temptation, lest we suffer some evil which we do not have in our power: that I not fall from a horse and break my foot, that a robber not kill me, and the like. For these things," he says, "I do not have in my power. For to conquer the temptations of my sins, if I will, I both can, nor can I by the help of God."

**12.** (2.) You see, brethren, how malignant a heresy! You see how you all shudder: beware lest you be caught! For I know the cunning and shiftings of impious men averse from the truth and, because they have now fallen into their own opinions, unwilling to be conquered. See, I beseech you. For see, he found something to say, that we therefore say: *Lead us not into temptation,* lest something befall us which we do not have in our power according to bodily temptation. Was it from this, then, that the Lord said: *Watch and pray, that you enter not into temptation?*[^12] Did he say this: *Watch and pray,* that you not break a foot, or that your head not ache, or that you not run into loss? He did not say this, but what did he say? What he said to Peter: *I have prayed for you, that your faith fail not*[^13]. *I have prayed,* he says, for you. God says to man, the Lord to a servant, the master to a disciple, the physician to a sick man: *I have prayed for you.* What? *That it fail not.* What? Your hand? your foot? your eye? your tongue, by some paralysis, that is, dissolution of the members? No, but *that your faith fail not.* According to these men, we have it in our power that our faith fail not.

**13.** (3.) Why is God asked for us? That he grant us what these men say we ought not to ask of the everlasting majesty, but have in our power. The blessings, my brethren, our blessings which we make over you, they empty out, void, dash to pieces. You have heard me, I believe, my brethren, when I say: "Turned to the Lord, let us bless his name. May he grant us to persevere in his commandments, to walk in the straight way of his instruction, to please him in every good work," and the like. "Clearly," they say, "all this is set in our power." Therefore we wish such things for you in vain. Let us defend ourselves and you, lest we both bless without cause, and you without cause subscribe "Amen." My brethren, your "Amen" is your subscription, your "Amen" is your stipulation, your consent. Lest perhaps some of these condemn both us and you, let us defend ourselves from the apostle Paul: let us see whether he wished such things for his people as we pray over you. Hear what he said in a certain place. I say a brief thing. What do you say, O new heretic, whoever hears me, if you are present? what do you say? "That we have it in our power not to sin in such a way that we can fulfill this without the help of divine grace." Do you say this? "This," he says. Therefore we have it in our power not to sin without the help of God? "Clearly," he says, "our free choice suffices us for this." What is it, then, that the Apostle says, writing to the Corinthians: *But we pray to God that you do no evil?*[^14] You have attended, you have heard, you have received; and because it is most manifest, without doubt you have understood what the Apostle prayed. *We pray,* he says, *to the Lord, that you do no evil.* He could say: We admonish you, *that you do no evil*; we teach you, *that you do no evil*; we command you, we charge you. Which indeed if he said, he would say rightly, because our will too does something; for our will does not do nothing, but alone it does not suffice. Yet he preferred to say: *We pray,* that he might commend that very grace, that they might understand, when they do no evil, that they avoid evil not by their own will alone, but fulfill what is commanded by the help of God.

**14.** (4.) Therefore, brethren, when it is commanded, acknowledge the choice of the will; when what is commanded is prayed for, acknowledge the benefit of grace. For you have both in the Scriptures: both it is commanded and it is prayed for; what is commanded, this is prayed for. See what I say. It is commanded that we understand. How is it commanded that we understand? *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^15]. You have heard that it is commanded: ask, that you may be able to fulfill what is commanded. "How," you say, "do I ask?" Hear the Scripture. What is commanded you? *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding.* Because it is commanded, you have recognized the will. Hear that it is prayed for, that you may recognize grace. *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments*[^16]. It is commanded that we have wisdom. Because it is commanded, I read it. "Where do you read it?" he says. Hear: *You who are unwise among the people, and foolish, at length be wise*[^17]. Now what does he say? "You see how God commanded us to be wise." Therefore wisdom is in our power? Now I have said it, I have heard the precept, I have known the will: hear the prayer, that you too may be able to acknowledge grace. It is about wisdom, which is commanded us. Let us hear what the apostle James says: *But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all abundantly*[^18]. Continence is commanded us. Where is it commanded? The Apostle to Timothy: *Keep yourself continent*[^19]. It is a command, it is a precept: it must be heard, it must be done. But unless God helps, we remain. We try indeed to do it by will, and the will strives at something. Let not power presume, unless weakness be helped. For surely it is commanded: *Keep yourself continent.* Hear another place of Scripture: *And when I knew,* he says, *that no one can be continent unless God grant it, this very thing too was wisdom, to know whose gift this was*[^20]. "And what," he says, "did I do?" *I went to the Lord and entreated him*[^21]. What need is there to run through many things, my brethren? Whatever is commanded us, must be prayed for that it be fulfilled. But not in such a way that we forsake ourselves and, like sluggards, lie supine and say: "May God rain foods upon our faces, that we may at all not wish to do anything"; and when the food has been rained upon our mouth, may we even say: "May God gulp it down for us." We too ought to do something, we ought to be eager, we ought to try, and in that wherein we have been able, give thanks, in that wherein we have not been able, pray. When you give thanks, you take care that you not be condemned as ungrateful; but when you ask for what you do not yet have, you take care that you not remain empty, because you are hindered.

**15.** (5.) Consider, then, these things, my brethren: whoever approaches you and says to you: "What, then, do we do? We have nothing in our power, unless God give all. Therefore God will not crown us, but will crown himself," now you see that he comes from that vein. It is a vein, but it has poison. For it has been struck by a serpent, it is not sound. For this Satan does now daily, how through the poisons of heretics he may cast out of the Church, as then through the poison of the serpent he cast out of paradise. Let no one say that that man was absolved by the bishops. The confession as it were a correction was absolved, because the things he said before the bishops seemed catholic; but the things he wrote in his books the bishops who absolved him did not know. And perhaps he was corrected: for we ought not to despair of a man, who perhaps preferred to join himself to the catholic faith and fled to its grace and help. Perhaps this has been done: yet not the heresy absolved, but a man denying the heresy.} But when we have read the proceedings, when they reach our hands, whatever more openly we know of this evil or perhaps of his correction, we shall, the Lord helping, have to announce to your love.
