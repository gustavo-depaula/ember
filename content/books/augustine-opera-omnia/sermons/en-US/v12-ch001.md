# Sermon 2A

**1.** Brothers, we should marvel at and love God's words and benefits, which he himself commends to us, not so that by loving him we may bestow anything on him, but so that we ourselves may not perish by failing to love him 〈…〉 that health may be given to the paralytic and weakness may abound in the Apostle Paul: health is given to the one so that his cure may not be \*despaired of, and weakness abounds in the Apostle so that he may not be exalted. For who could explain in fitting words how father Abraham is, in a sense, \*commanded to perform a pious act of impiety, and obeys God who commands him to sacrifice his beloved son? Great faith, great obedience! What did I mean by saying, “A pious act of impiety is commanded”? It is impious to be commanded to kill one's son, but it is more impious not to listen to God. This, then, is that impious cruelty where piety is commanded, and love must not be reckoned cruelty. For when Abraham had already despaired of offspring from his wife, whom barrenness and age had made infertile, God had promised it through the power of his mercy and had given what he promised. Would he then cruelly take away what he had mercifully given? The father loved his son, longed for and beloved, promised and received when hope was gone. He is commanded to kill the very son he had received in this way. Can you imagine what thoughts passed through his heart? But blessed Abraham undoubtedly said this to himself: “What the one who gave him has commanded cannot be bad for my son.” For this is faith: it trusts that it will receive and does not hesitate to return, because God can restore again what he has received back. For *if Abraham was justified by works*, as the Apostle says—by works, that is, considered his own—*he has glory, but not before God*. †Uti autem† [corrupt reading], that he may have glory before the Lord. For when the voice rang out—God was speaking to him through an angel—“Sacrifice your beloved son to me!” who would not say, who would not say in his heart, “Can God command this? And should we believe that it is God who says, ‘Kill your son!’? If I killed any stranger, it would displease God; am I to kill my son and \*please God?” This was the first act of faith, this was clear faith: not only to believe God and obey God, but to understand that God had commanded this. [^1]

**2.** For the one commanding this was bringing forth a great mystery and foretelling his own Son in Abraham's son. Whose image did the father bear then? Which father? Abraham. Whose image did he bear? Surely that of God the Father, of whom the Apostle says: *He did not spare his own Son*. What, dearest brothers, could be clearer than this: Christ, who is God, carried his own Cross, as we read in the Gospel, and Isaac carried the wood for his own sacrifice. [^2]

**3.** Abraham therefore \*believed, because through deep understanding, †vestro abdito† [corrupt reading], and God's great gift, he knew that God had commanded this. For he knew that God had promised him offspring 〈in〉 Isaac. Why should he hesitate to kill \*him whom God could raise again? This was great faith: to obey God when he commanded him to kill his son and to think nothing evil of God. So he went on without fear. [^3]

**4.** The son saw no sheep; he did not know that he himself was the sheep. He asked where the sheep for the sacrifice was, because he saw everything ready, namely, fire and wood. His father answered him: *The God whom we worship will provide for himself*. He said this, indeed, as human affection 〈moved him〉, so as not to distress the son who was to be sacrificed and have a sorrowful victim approach the place of sacrifice. Yet what he seems simply to have said is understood to have been a prophecy. The same voice that had commanded him to cut his son's throat forbade him to kill him, and at the command of the same one he restrained the hand that he had already begun to raise at his command. Why, then, did God not want him to kill Isaac, whom he could have raised again, unless because the figure of that sacrifice was being enacted in Isaac, but the resurrection was reserved for Christ the Lord? For if Isaac had been killed and had risen, he would not have signified Christ, \*but would have been Christ. It was not written without having happened; rather, it happened, and what happened signified what was to come. [^4]

**5.** Yet so that they would not leave without a sacrifice, a ram appeared, caught by its horns in the brambles. Recognize in this ram too a figure of the Lord and Savior; recognize Christ crowned with the Jews' thorns. Thus Isaac is Christ and the ram is Christ, yet neither Isaac nor the ram is Christ, because there is one Christ. But the true Christ is signified by many figures. For Christ is both a lamb and a lion. And what could be more opposite than a lion and a lamb? A lamb because of his innocence, a lion because of his strength, yet one Christ. Christ is also the rock from which water flowed, and Christ is the cornerstone from which water did not flow. The one signifies one thing, the other another; all are in the one who is the true Christ. [^5]

**6.** In all the deeds and prefigurations of the prophets, past events were bringing forth what we now see fulfilled; and if we believe, we recognize the times and are blessed. From how far off God sees us, and how what he had already done in his \*foreknowledge \*comes to pass in us! Let us, then, be patient before every act of God's will. How patient Isaac himself was! Certainly his father did not want him distressed when he said: *Son, the God whom we worship prepares a victim for himself*. Yet the very hour arrived, and what was he to do but carry out what had been commanded? He bound his son's feet and laid him on the altar. The son did not cry out, did not object, did not weep. Great was the father's obedience, but greater the son's patience! He dared utter no evil word, no contrary word, no sorrowful word against his father, who was binding him, laying him on the altar, and about to strike him. Whom did this figure serve except him who *was led like a sheep to slaughter and, like a lamb silent before its shearer, did not open his mouth*? [^6]

**7.** Finally, through this deed the nations are promised to Abraham. Let us rejoice, dearest brothers, because we are the ones who were promised. *By myself*, he says, *I swear, says the Lord; by myself I swear*. The Almighty's oath is a confirmation of the truth. Just as a man, when he swears, wishes to confirm what he promises, so when God is heard to swear, it confirms the promise. But someone says, “Does God swear too?” I say that, rather, no one should swear except the one who cannot swear falsely. Swearing is not evil; swearing falsely is evil. But a human being is forbidden to swear precisely because swearing falsely is evil and habit must be feared, lest he begin to swear falsely 〈…〉 [^7]
