# Sermon on the Providence of God (Dolbeau 29)

**1.** On the occasion of this apostolic reading, brethren, which you have just heard as it was read, where the most blessed apostle Paul says: *But do you reckon this, O man, who judge those who do such things and do them, that you will escape the judgment of God?*[^1] and the rest that follows pertaining to this, the Lord admonishes us to dispute something, as much as he himself grants, about his providence by which he deigns to expend care on human affairs. For very many men deny this, while they behold certain many and great things in this life and converse of mortals coming about as it were by unconsidered chances, and, because they themselves cannot attain the causes and order of them, they do not think them to pertain to the counsel of God governing, but to come about by a certain fortuitous rashness. Of these many, that is one thing, against which the holy Apostle in this reading meets the thoughts of men, namely of those who say: "If God cared for the life of men, he would in no way let the criminal and impious live." So, when it displeases them that the impious live, they themselves speak impiety against God. To whom the apostolic teaching answering says: *Do you reckon this, O man, who judge those who do such things and do them, that you will escape the judgment of God? Or do you despise the riches of his goodness and patience and long-suffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance? But according to the hardness of your heart and your impenitent heart, you treasure up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works*[^2]. How, then, has God no care for human affairs, *who will render to each according to his works?*

**2.** But those who do not believe that God cares for human affairs without doubt believe neither the apostles nor any divine words and writings, which especially and greatly commend this. It must be asked of them, then, whence they suppose the providence of God to be foreign to human affairs. "Because everything," they say, "that is governed by providence ought to be ordered and arranged. But what," they say, "is more disordered and disturbed than human affairs, where very often the bad prevail with such happiness that they even besides have dominion over the good, but the good are ground down by misery and are compelled to be subject to the bad?" If, then, on account of this they deny the providence of God, they ought to confess his providence, when we see the good excel in riches, honors, powers, but the bad held in check by the right and dominion of those: for here surely the order of human affairs appears whole and beautiful. "No," they say; "for if this were equally and always so, then we would confess human affairs most ordered. And likewise if we discerned all the bad to be happy, but all the good to be servants of the bad, beset with pains, destitute of resources, here too some constancy of a certain order would move and admonish us to believe that the bad flourish in this visible happiness, because they are deprived of the invisible, which is in the mind, much better and firmer, but that the good therefore do not enjoy this exterior happiness, because much more excellently they are blessed within, and their joy, which is now kindled by the hope of immortality, is, the harshnesses of hardships ended, to be fulfilled. But now," they say, "since in this life in a disturbed and mingled way certain best men excel the worst, and certain worst men the best, this very inconstancy spreads out no order, shows no care of God for human affairs."

**3.** To whom we answer and admonish them—if at least they hear anything patiently against their presumption—that they first nourish the weakness of their sense with piety, by which they might be made fit, through his Spirit, by their unskilled strength, for searching out the counsels of God, and let them reckon that it pertained to the care of God that they should be men, and thence, a certain step as it were made, let them ascend to higher things, where, as far as they are able, they may understand that it pertains to God what kind we ought to be, to whom it pertained that we should be. For surely, if this moves them, that they deny human affairs to be governed by divine providence, because they discern them disordered and in a manner turbid, let them not first pass over man himself nor pass him by, cast down and trodden upon, nor, themselves passed over, hasten to judge other things rashly, but let them tarry a little upon themselves and not disdain to inspect themselves somewhat more diligently, and let them see, even in so great a ruin of vices, how great an order of nature is in them.

**4.** First, that man consists of soul and body and that the invisible and more powerful substance moves the subject visible one—surely natural rule, which is the soul endowed with it, and natural servitude, which is the flesh subjected—show the beauty of a noble order. Further, in the soul, that reason by excellence of nature avails most and surpasses the other parts of it, what else but order is made clear? For no one is so given over to lusts that he would doubt what to answer, if he were asked whether it is better that which is borne by rash desire, or that which is governed by reason and counsel; and through this even whoever lives imprudently, not rationally, yet answers which of these is better, even if not corrected in action, surely admonished by the question; and so not even in a man who bears perverse conduct does the voice of order perish, when nature refutes the vice.

**5.** The body too, with what wonderful ordering it holds its members arranged throughout its whole mass, who could consider with sufficient thought? who could proclaim with worthy praises the head set between the shoulders and, as more eminent than the other members and to be honored, set upon the rest of the body as upon a vehicle, in which those conspicuous senses, distinguished by their proper places and seats, keep watch as it were on a higher tower for the guarding of safety, and what is done outside or brought in from without, whether glad or sad, they announce to the inner mind, judging of all things in a certain secret chamber of its own, like eager messengers? For the eyes serve for announcing shapes and colors, the ears sounds and voices, the nostrils odors, the throat flavors; but the touch, as it were a general sense, is diffused through the whole body, but takes its beginning also from the head; the hands follow, set under the head, apt for working necessary things, bringing in useful things, repelling contrary things; then the chest and belly, like certain chests, are fixed to the opposed back, in which the vital organs, since they are dangerously touched, are carried enclosed. Last the feet are set under for bearing all things and are moved for journeying.

**6.** But now who would not delight to attend, so as in his own work to marvel more and more at the artificer, how provision has been made not only for safety and usefulness, but also for dignity and beauty? Equal members correspond to equal in pairs, like the eyes, ears, cheeks, shoulders, hands, sides, feet, and finally the fingers themselves on hands and feet. On this side and that, the single members agree with the single and all with all by a debt of parity rendered, and, that it may be more expressly recognized that account was had not only of soundness but also of beauty, the male chest, even though it will not nurse, is adorned by the congruence of the twin breasts. But those things which were created single are set in middle places, lest, placed in one part, they should defraud another part of its due seemliness, like the head and the neck, and in the head itself the nose and the mouth, the navel in the belly, and the rest below, which, just as God by providing determined them to be created, so man would have to speak of in praise, had he not by sinning made them shameful. Now that—when it comes to mind—who would wish to pass over in silence, that the inner parts of the body, although they are ordered by wonderful designs, yet, because they are thought of more honorably than they are seen, the Creator covered with a covering of skin? For since the intellect indeed marvels at them when known, but the sight shudders at them when bared, it was foreseen, both for their usefulness and their dignity, that they should lie hidden, and that in considering them the attention of minds should be so exercised that the offense of the eyes be avoided.

**7.** This institution of a rational living being, this ordering of the soul ruling and the flesh serving, of mind and spirit, of head and body and invisible nature, of cognition and work, of intellect, sense, and motion, the receptacle of memory, the document of knowledge, the choice of will, the use and adornment of the members, and the whole by which a man is a man—whom could it have as author but God? Or, because, apart from the rational mind, apart too from the body's upright stature by which men are admonished to lift up their hearts to heaven, an ordered disposition is found also in the soul and body of a beast, is God therefore to be denied to be the creator of man? Rather even on this account is one and the same God the more to be acknowledged as the creator both of man and of beast. For there would not be any life of any kind made anywhere, unless the uncreated Life had made it. Nor in the begetting of the little bodies of any living beings whatever, however minute and lowest, or even in the seeds, roots, trunks, branches, leaves, flowers, fruits of innumerable trees and herbs, would so manifest an order arise from the hidden bosoms of nature, unless by the creating of him whose magnificent and, if it may be said, all-effecting wisdom, containing in itself, as it were set in art, the unchangeable and invisible reasons of changeable and visible things, *reaches,* as it is written, *reaches from end to end mightily and disposes all things sweetly*[^3]. Since these things are so, and since in all earthly things we do not doubt that human affairs, like men themselves, excel, by what folly, at last, do we deny the providence of God in great things, which we marvel at in small, unless perhaps we understand that he leaves the lives of men without any judgment, who with such great skill makes and orders the definite numerousness of contemptible hairs! Therefore let us, even without any doubt, believe this, that what seems disturbed and disordered in human affairs is of no one's counsel at all, but rather of a higher counsel, of a divine and greater order than can be comprehended by our smallness.

**8.** {For from this especially must we believe what piety preaches, that there is to be a manifest judgment, because now we see human happinesses and calamities to be indiscriminately common to the good and the bad as it were without any judgment, since the justice of God, whose providence so stands out in small things, in no way leaves the greater things to float about everywhere without any judgment. But what is greater, not only in this human, but also in the angelic creature, than that the bad be punished by a just misery, but the good fully enjoy beatitude? What, then, now is as it were well for the bad man is a hidden punishment, a false happiness. But what is ill for the good man is not a denial of reward to piety, but by the patience of piety it is increased unto greater rewards. Likewise that sometimes even in this life it is ill for the bad man is either an amendment or an affliction of sins. But when it is well for the good man, it is not the most certain joy of that heavenly fatherland, but some solace of this perilous pilgrimage. If unfaithful impiety considered these and such things, it would not deny divine providence in governing and ordering human affairs, nor persist in its dark and deadly powers against the light and life of wisdom.}

**9.** But you, dearly beloved brethren, who have now believed in Christ, do not be drawing the yoke with unbelievers, by reckoning that God takes no care how men live, who takes care that not only men, but even beasts, fish, birds have whereby to live. For what the Apostle says about oxen, that there is no care to God, is not so to be taken that we reckon it not to pertain to God's providence that animals be born and fed, since the Lord Jesus most openly by examples of this kind rebukes or rouses the faith of men, that they may see how he feeds the birds of heaven and how he clothes the hay of the field, and thence believe that he does not neglect the food and clothing of his servants. But for this reason the Apostle said: *For there is no care to God about oxen*[^4], lest we should think that what is written: *You shall not muzzle the ox that treads out the grain*[^5], was said for the sake of the oxen, not for the sake of men. There was, then, no care to God in the holy Scripture to give precepts to men how they should treat their beasts of burden; but it is not that there is no care to God in the nature of things, that all animals be born and be nourished by foods congenial to them.

**10.** But the foolish and unfaithful, who contend that God does not care for human affairs, because in the goods and evils of men they can find no order, let us send to consider the miracles of nature. For how does he believe the divine words to be true, who does not believe them divine? {Let the unfaithful man, then, attend to that which he cannot say was instituted by men, lest he deny that God gives order to human affairs, who taught the little bee to order the cells of the honeycomb so wonderfully: who certainly gave to man himself to be offended by disordered things and to be delighted by the order of things? Does he not find this in the nature of his own soul, which he did not make for himself? For why, an order not found in human affairs, does he not believe that God governs human affairs, except because he naturally knows how to prefer ordered things to disordered? Does, then, man judge that ordering rather than disturbance befits divine works, and does God not have an ordered judgment about all men, who created the sense of order in individuals? He has it clearly, he has it; let not piety doubt, even though weakness does not comprehend. We praise, namely, those works of craftsmen which we are able to inspect, but at those of engineers we are astonished, and, unless they have been laid open and shown to us, we marvel that they could do the impossible. Why, then, do we judge so rashly of the judgments of God and hasten to deny the order of the divine work, where we are not able to see it, praising the providence of the Creator in the leaves of trees and thinking it not to be in human affairs, and do not rather believe that the order of human affairs runs unsearchably and hiddenly, which we are not able either to comprehend, being immense, or to inspect, being hidden? But the manifest orders of things divinely instituted, from which the hidden may be conjectured, strike even the eyes of the impious.}

**11.** But we, besides those conspicuous things which are discerned in heaven and earth, have in faith a most certain sign by which we may know human affairs to pertain to the care of God, so that it is not lawful for us not only to deny but even to doubt it: namely Christ Jesus our Lord himself *who, though he was in the form of God, did not count it robbery to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men and found in form as a man; he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even the death of the cross*[^6]. How, then, does man not pertain to the care of God, for whose sake the Son of God became man? How does God not care for the life of men, for whom the Son of God endured death? How does he not order, although he does not make, even the conduct of the bad, when through those very bad men Christ suffered what would avail to establish the conduct of the good? How does he not providently use even the sins of the impious, when through them pious blood was poured out unto the remission of sins? How will he not repay punishments to the unfaithful, who therefore frees the faithful that he may not repay? How will he not render to the faithful the reward of faith, for whom he endured even the reproach of the cross? How will the goods and evils not be eternal which he will give as judge, who taught that temporal goods and evils are to be despised, being judged? Therefore not only that God cares for human affairs, but how much he cares, there is no greater and surer document than the evidence of the man Christ being born, his patience in dying, his power in rising.

**12.** For in the founding and governing of other things too his providence without doubt appears, without which no leaf falls, no seed is put forth; but nowhere does it so appear how much he loves man as where he made the man through whom he made man, and willed Life to die that he might live who had lost life, and made him through whom he will give the reward to be the example of the reward itself, this *great sacrament of piety which was manifested in the flesh, justified in the spirit, appeared to the angels, was preached among the nations, was believed in the world, was taken up in glory*[^7]. From the very beginning of the human race, until it should come about, the future was foretold through the Spirit of God, and still the unfaithful answer—they speak iniquity—that human affairs do not pertain to the care of God. But let us, attributing to the gift of God, not to our own merits, that, although we were by nature children of wrath like the rest, not indeed by nature, as it was afterward vitiated by its own iniquity, we were made children of mercy, distinguished from the rest, not by nature, not by law, but by grace, not gaze at the unfaithful as to what false things they say of God's providence, but labor that they may hear true things, pray that they may believe.
