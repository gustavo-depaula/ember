# Sermon 23/B: (Dolbeau 6)

**1.** To what hope the Lord our God has called us, what we now bear, what we endure, and what we await, I do not doubt is known to your love. We bear mortality, we endure weakness, we await divinity. For God wishes not only to give us life but also to deify us. When would human weakness dare to hope this, unless divine truth promised it? But divine truth not only promised it, as we said, and that we are to be gods—it not only promised this—and because it promised it, it is surely true, for neither does so faithful a promiser deceive, nor is so omnipotent a giver hindered from fulfilling what he promised. Yet it was too little for our God to promise us divinity in himself, unless he also took on our weakness, as if saying: "Do you wish to know how much I love you, how certain you ought to be that I will give you my divine nature? I took on your mortal nature." Let it not seem to us incredible, brethren, that men become gods, that is, that those who were men become gods. More incredible is what has already been granted us, that he who was God became man. And that indeed we believe to have already been done; the other we await as to come. The Son of God became the Son of man, that he might make sons of men sons of God. Hold firmly to this, which I believe you remember we have already spoken of to your love: that he is mortal not from what is his own, nor are we immortal from what is our own—not from his own, not from his nature, not from the substance by which he himself is God; but in another way from his own, because from his creation, because from what he founded, from what he created: for the maker of man became man, that man might become a partaker of God. And now we have this in faith, and it is kept for us in hope; at a fixed time it will appear; they will rejoice who now, while it does not appear, have believed. But those who, while it does not appear, were unwilling to believe, will be confounded when it appears.

**2.** Let not the Christian mind, then, shudder and as it were be terrified—the mind that is told to mock the gods of the gentiles and to worship and know that the one God is to be worshiped—when it hears in the psalm, which we just now sang: *For God stood in the synagogue of gods*[^1]. What is a *synagogue?* Since it is a Greek word, I think many of you know and many do not. "Synagogue" in Latin is called an assembly; this, then, we sang: *For God stood in the* assembly *of gods.* To what end this? *In the midst to distinguish the gods*[^2]. Our God, the true God, the one God, *stood in the synagogue of gods,* surely of many, gods not by nature but by adoption, by grace. There is much difference between God who exists, God always God, the true God, not only God but also a God who deifies—that is, so to speak, a deifying God, a God not made who makes gods—and the gods who are made, but not by a craftsman.

**3.** And because everyone who makes is surely better than what he makes, now see what gods the pagans adore, and what God you adore. You adore the God who makes you gods; but they adore gods whom, by making and adoring, they lose, so that they themselves may become gods; by making false ones they fall from the true. And to those they make they do not grant that they be gods, but that they be called what they are not. They themselves lose what they could be, and they do not give to those the being they cannot be. He who makes a false god offends the true one, and by making what he cannot, he himself does not become what he can. For he himself, if he wills, becomes a god—not such as the one he worships, but such as he who makes the one he worships. What, then, do men wish: to become gods, or to make gods? It seems more powerful to them if they make gods than if they become gods. But even if they could make them, would it be a god because it is so called? He on whom you impose the divine name will be called a god; but it will be either wood or stone or gold, or whatever else it is. Indeed you, O impious man, wish to make a god whom you do not make, but you can form an image and impose a name on it. It will not be what you call it, but it will be what he made whom you do not call upon. For God made wood, God made stone, gold, silver; you, out of that stone which God made, wish to make a god. You neither give it what you made, nor take from it what he made.

**4.** You, then, did not make what you made: for if I ask you what you made, you will answer "a god"; the very one you made answers better than you. For we can in a manner question even these things, which have neither soul nor sense, so that when questioned they do not perceive, but yet bear before them an appearance, by which appearance they declare, so to speak, to our senses what they are. You made a god, of wood for example. Surely if it is a god, it is not wood; if it is wood, it is not a god. And yet you answer that you made a god; but I, setting you the wooden one aside, question the wood itself. But lest you think me wooden too, because I question the wood—what if I were to question it?—see: the voice does not question the soul, but the eyes the form. My sight questions the appearance and matter of that wood. And lest perhaps mortal sight be deceived, my touch also questions it. And if you think this too little, even an axe can question your god, that my God made it wood. In all these questionings it answers me that it is wood, which you call a god, without its own voice, yet more trustworthy than your voice.

**5.** You say God lies, but you are refuted by the very thing you made. Nor, because you are refuted by it, will it be better than you; even if you lie and it does not lie, you call it a god and it declares itself wood, it will not be better than you. There is no occasion for you to wish to adore it as a better thing. You perceive, it does not perceive; you hear and it does not hear; you see and it does not see; you walk and it does not walk; you live, and I cannot say "it is dead," because it never lived. You are better than what you made; adore the better one who made you. It is an injury to you to be made like what you made. What kind of being do you seek to adore? You are angry at one who curses you, if he says to you: "May you be such," and yet you adore what you would detest to be, and by adoring you are in a certain manner made like it—not that you become wood and cease to be a man, but that you in a manner make your inner man such as you made the outer. For God made your mind as it were eyes, and you are unwilling to see the truth. God made your understanding as it were hearing, and you are unwilling to understand justice. But if our inner man had no sense of smell, there would be no reason for the apostle to say: *We are the good fragrance of Christ in every place*[^3]. If that inner man had no mouth, the Lord would not say: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice*[^4]. The inner man, then, has all things—God gave them—but he is unwilling to use them, and wishes to become like the image he himself formed, of which the prophet says: *They have eyes and do not see, they have ears and do not hear, they have nostrils and do not smell, they have a mouth and do not speak, they have hands and do not work*, and the rest. And see how it concludes: *Let all who make them become like them, and all who trust in them*[^5]. How can men become *like* mute images? But according to this likeness which we are commending: if the inner man become in a manner without sense, he becomes in a certain way like the image and, the image of him by whom he was made being lost in himself, he wishes to take on the image of the one he made. For why did the Lord say: *He who has ears to hear, let him hear*[^6], unless because there are some who have ears and do not hear?

**6.** Let not your heart, then, be terrified, because *God stood in the synagogue of gods, and in the midst to distinguish the gods.* For distinguishing in the midst, he says certain precepts; and those who despise these precepts are unwilling to be what he said we should be. And some will charge it to themselves: "Why does he distinguish, if all are gods?" For why does he distinguish, if all are gods? Except because there are those who hear, there are those who despise. For there are those who give thanks, there are those who are ungrateful to grace and are distinguished, but by him who knows how to distinguish. Let no one who is made wish to distinguish; let him distinguish who made; let the maker judge of his own works, who cannot err when he judges. But by giving his Spirit he makes men too to judge, not through themselves, not from themselves, not by their own nature, not by their own merit, but by his grace and his gift. *We,* he says, *have received not the spirit of this world, but the Spirit which is from God, that we may know the things given to us by God. But the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God: for it is folly to him, and he cannot know it, because it is spiritually discerned. But the spiritual man discerns all things, yet he himself is discerned by no one*[^7]. But if we have the Spirit of God, let us distinguish not only ourselves, but also ourselves from images.

**7.** For truly, brethren, men who do not distinguish themselves from them are to be grieved over, not praised for distinguishing—unless perhaps a man is to be praised who knows that there is much difference between himself and a stone. But what kind of man would he be, if he thought himself to be what a stone is! And would that he might advance even this far! He makes himself better than a stone, to which, if he were like it or made himself like it—for he never will be: for however far he follows his own work, he will not kill in himself the work of God—if, then, he made himself like it, by thinking himself like it, I do not say: he would do himself an injury—for perhaps he makes little of his own injury, and rightly does such a man make little of his injury—this I say which should move him: he does God an injury. By comparing himself badly, he becomes sacrilegious against the one by whom he was made: for man was made to the image of God. If, then, you were, or rather would be called, sacrilegious by the public laws for doing injury to the image of the emperor, what will you be by doing injury to the image of God? Which is worse: to throw a stone at the image of a man, or to make a stone the image of God? Let us, then, leave those as if too dead, because, even though they can be roused, they cannot be roused by us. Yet we ought not therefore to despair of them, because we cannot rouse them: for *God is able to raise up sons of Abraham from these stones*[^8].

**8.** But we ought to distinguish ourselves also from the divinities of those stones, woods, gold, and silver, because there are those who think they can be defended by a kind of reasoning, when they say: "We too know that the images are empty things, but we do not worship them." And when you ask: "What, then, do you worship?", they answer: "The divinities of the images. We adore indeed what we see, but we worship what we do not see." What are these divinities? Let us hear our God saying through the prophet: *For all the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens*[^9], where it is not fitting for demons to dwell. In one way the prophet mocked the demons, in another way he mocked the images. The images, how? *The images of the nations, silver and gold.* He did not wish to say stone and wood, but what they hold for great, what they think precious, their choicest things he sets forth to be mocked: *silver indeed and gold,* but yet *the works of men's hands*[^10]. But what did man make there? That it should be gold? That it should be silver? This God made. What, then, did man make? That *they have eyes and do not see.* This, then, man made in the god he made, which he would not wish to be done in himself by the one who made him. He made a blind god, and he would not wish to be made blind by God. What, then? Because he named gold and silver, precious metals, and chose this for mockery which they hold for great, does it therefore make any difference as to what he mocked? Gold differs somewhat from wood—gold is more precious than wood—but as to having eyes and not seeing, they are equal. The usefulness or splendor is unequal, but yet the blindness is equal.

**9.** In one way, then, these images were mocked—without soul, without sense, without life; but in another way those things which they worship there for great, that is, the demons, when he says: *All the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens.* And the apostle in one way mocked the idol: *We know,* he says, *that an idol is nothing*[^11]; but in another way he charged that the demons must be guarded against, saying: *What the nations sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons and not to God. I do not wish you to become partners of demons*[^12]. He did not say: "I do not wish you to become partners of idols." For you would perhaps fear to be what you could never be, to be a partner of a wooden idol, lest you be cast together into the fire; fear to be a partner of demons, lest you be cast together into the eternal fire. For attend, brethren, to what I say. To be a partner of an idol, even if you wished, you cannot; but a partner of demons, if you wish, you will be, if you do not wish, you will not be. For to all the partners of the devil and his angels it will be said at the end: *Go into the eternal fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels*[^13]. I seem to myself, brethren, in a manner to distinguish the gods in the midst, but not I: the Word of God, whether it be expounded or sung or read, itself has the force and power of distinguishing.

**10.** But someone from the crowd will say to me: "Far be it that I should seek out demons, and not rather detest them. I utterly detest and flee and execrate them." That indeed is a good word, a good profession. But what if your head aches and you seek a soothsayer? What if you have a perilous lawsuit and desire a diviner? These are vessels of demons. Why do you seek the vessels of those you detest? If you speak the truth, I shall recognize it by your deed. Your profession seems certain, before there is a temptation. Recognize the one who speaks to you: for Satan will never speak to you through an image, but through an evil man whose heart he possesses: for *he works,* as the apostle says, *in the sons of disobedience*[^14]. When, then, you begin to hear: "Provide for yourself, take counsel for yourself; there is one whom you may consult: he will tell you all things truly; there is one who will commend your cause, who will commend your business: he will choose you a day for beginning the affair," then see the devil speaking out of the form of a man, whom he has already seduced into his fellowship. And if you do not wish to be a partner of demons, shun the partner of demons. For you will be a partner of Christ, not to equal majesty, but to one inheritance, as the apostle says: *Heirs indeed of God, but coheirs of Christ*[^15].

**11.** But whence do men seek the fellowship of demons? By losing endurance: *For woe to those who have lost endurance*[^16]. Who does not know that you are in straits, pressed by affliction, tossed by weakness, wasted by decay, harried by the snares of the enemy? So be it. These things are true, they are troublesome, they trouble, they press, they grind. What of it? Were you called by Christ to delights? I think God would have spoken justly to you, if he said: "Suffer: for you are a man, made mortal by your own will, by my law." For our very first nature sinned, and from there we draw the fact that we are born. Let us bear our condition. The creator says: "I will recreate you; those whom I created mortal, I will recreate immortal. Bear your condition, that you may receive your possession." God speaking to man, I think, would speak these things justly: "Bear, endure; it is decay, it is corruption; bear the physician's cutting: let all the rot be penetrated, let all that was badly conceived burst forth." How much do men suffer under human physicians! They are bound, cut, burned, as long as it pleases him who promises an uncertain health, as long as it pleases him who did not make you, as long as a man pleases against a man, and he endures all things. It is too little that he endures the one cutting; he asks him to do it. Do you not, then, think that you are being purified, when you suffer tribulation? Do you not, then, believe him who said: *For as gold and silver are tried in the fire, so acceptable men in the furnace of humiliation*[^17]? Bear, then, what the physician applies to the sick man, what the goldsmith applies to the gold to be purified.

**12.** For this world is like the craftsman's furnace. In the goldsmith's furnace there is both chaff and gold and fire; so too in this world, there is both the unbeliever and the believer and temptation. The unbeliever is the chaff, the believer the gold, temptation the fire. These three are in a narrow place; yet this place is so narrow that all three have their own properties: the fire to burn, the chaff to be consumed, the gold to be purified. Do not wonder, then, that you see the world full of scandals, iniquities, corruptions, afflictions, men blaspheming, finding fault with the Christian times, because these things rush in grievously. Let not these blasphemies and this fault-finding be feared by you: the chaff burns; they say these things indeed with eloquent and as it were flaming words. Do not wonder at the chaff glittering when it burns: a little after it will be ash. It blazes, it crackles, it sends forth smoke. O gold, be silent and be purified. The chaff burns in its blasphemies; be you purified of your filth.

**13.** "But many evils there are, and many grow more frequent, since the times began to be Christian." This indeed is not easily to be conceded to the unlearned. Let them read the evils of former ages in their own writings; let them read of greater wars of their elders; let them read of the devastations of regions; let them read of the captivities of nations, as success alternated, now on this side, now on that, contending to snatch the kingdom to themselves. There were among the ancients too famines and plagues: let them read, if they have leisure for it; but if they have no leisure to read, why have they leisure to talk much? Yet we confess that certain things happen more frequently, that through the failure of resources and a worse state of affairs, the things that were built before with great pomp now fall and slip into ruins. The pagan wonders that what was made by the hand of men falls, and he himself, made by the hand of God, wishes to fall. Consider, my brethren—I will speak more freely from this place, where the Lord gives you confidence to preach his truth: for the person of no one is to be regarded—I do not say of any man, but of the age itself, especially because the psalm just now reproved thus: *How long will you judge iniquity and accept the persons of sinners?*[^18], distinguishing between gods and gods; terrified, then, we terrify, and bidden we speak—consider, recall what extravagance of things built the theaters and amphitheaters. Were the times better, because trifles were more licentious, because the reins of baseness were looser, because to each it was easily permitted what badly pleased him? Surely those are the dens of baseness. Attend to what is done there, and see when the times are better: when those were being built, or when they fall?

**14.** We ask them that, if they are angry with us, they read their own authors. Let them see whether their own philosophers approved these basenesses for them, did not mock them, did not forbid them, did not accuse them. Let them choose any of the better among their own, and in them first recognize their own vices, before they come to the grace of our Christ. How much was said by those authors of theirs against the dissolute, how much said against the prodigal, how much said against those who pour out their goods to merit statues, and, that they may be of stone, wish to be in rags! Let them, then, read these things among their own: there is no need for them to wish to learn even their own literature from us, because we, if we love them, teach them unbecomingly, and perhaps more becomingly forget them. Yet, as far as we can remember, they found fault with many such things, which these men zealously do. And because the means, the wealth, the license, the prosperity for these trifles do not avail them, they accuse Christ, ungrateful to the master who came over them like boys playing badly, and by the severity of his vigor struck from the hands of the weeping boys, as it were, balls of clay and little cells of glass, with which even in playing they were vexing themselves, but who, if they were willing, would be healed. But let those things go as they go, let them go as it was foretold: so is the promise of God fulfilled.

**15.** Flee evils, lay hold of goods: the time of the winepress has come. As on freer branches the olive was shaken by various blasts, in the first times running riot in the license of trifles: in the hanging olive the oil and the dregs grew together. That these two may be separated from one another by a due division, pressure is needed. The psalm is inscribed *For the winepresses*[^19], and its text speaks nothing of the vat, nothing of the press, nothing of the baskets; whatever it speaks pertains to the human race. You hear the name of the winepress: attend to the winepress of what thing. The human race too must be brought from a kind of freer coalescing to certain tribulations, to certain pressures; it must be set to the threshing, weights must be laid upon it. Amid the threshings and pressures you see more abundant luxuries, you see more rapacious avarice, you see more unbridled lusts: the dregs run through the streets. You find fault with these things and say: "See, in Christian times even robberies grow greater, and harsher importunities are exercised against men." The dregs are black, foul, useless, running through the public square. O if you could have eyes, by which you might also see the oil flowing into the storage jars! You attend to the multitude of adulterers: why do you not attend to the multitude of holy virgins? You attend to men fornicating: why do you not attend also to those who, by equal consent with their wives, are continent? You attend to men receiving others' goods with great avarice, without any shame: why do you not attend to those who give away their own with great mercy, without any madness? So many badly rich men displease you: let so many well-poor men please you, because dire iniquity made those rich and pious will made these poor. Why your eye on the dregs alone, that you may accuse the pressures and be unwilling to be in the pressure? Be the oil that is separated inwardly from the dregs, not that is sent out from it. Say with a certain oil: *I found tribulation and sorrow, and I called upon the name of the Lord*[^20]; say with a certain oil: *It is good for me that you humbled me, that I might learn your judgments*[^21]. Whence is it that you see some blaspheming in the pressures, others giving thanks in the pressures, those black, those shining? whence this, but because what is sung *for the winepresses* is being fulfilled? Do not, then, find fault with him who comes to press, because he comes to distinguish; rather recognize the time of distinguishing, and you will not have a tongue of supplanting.
