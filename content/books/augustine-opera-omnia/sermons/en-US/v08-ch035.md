# Sermon 299/A: Discourse on the Birthday of the Apostles Peter and Paul

**1.** Today the birthday of the most blessed apostles Peter and Paul has dawned: the birthday that did not entangle them in the world, but freed them from the world. For through human weakness a person is born to hardship; through Christian love martyrs are born to a crown. Because of their merit this day has been set before us for solemn celebration and imitation of their holiness, so that, recalling the martyrs' glory, we may love in them what their killers hated, and, loving their virtue, honor their suffering. Through virtue merit was gained; in suffering the reward was repaid. One day belongs to two martyrs and two Apostles. As far as we have received from the Church's tradition, they did not suffer on one day, and they did suffer on one day. Peter suffered first on this day; Paul suffered later on this day. Merit made their suffering equal; love met on the same day. He accomplished this in them who was in them, who suffered in them, who suffered with them, who helped them as they fought, who crowned them as they conquered. Let us therefore hear Peter's merit from the Gospel; let us hear Paul's merit from the apostolic letter. The Gospel has just been read; we have just heard: *The Lord said to Peter: Simon Peter, do you love me? And he said: I love you, Lord. And again the Lord: Feed my sheep.* A third time he asks nothing different from what he asked twice. It was for him to ask a third time; Peter was already weary of answering a third time. For *Peter* *was saddened,* the Gospel says, *because the Lord questioned him a third time, and he said: Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you. And the Lord: Feed my sheep*. He who asks what he knows wants to teach something. What, then, did the Lord want to teach Peter by asking a third time what he already knew? What do we think, brothers, except that love should remove weakness, and that Peter should know that he must confess three times through love, since he had denied three times through fear? Peter's merit was that he fed God's sheep. Let us hear Paul's merit in turn. He said to his disciple, announcing his coming suffering and removing fear from him by his example: *I bear witness before God and Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom.* He bound him by this solemn testimony and added, *Preach the word; press on in season and out of season.*

**2.** Hearing this, we too, according to our measure, are welcome to the willing and unwelcome to the unwilling. One who offers bread is welcome to a hungry person; one who compels eating is unwelcome to a sick person. Food is offered to the one, pressed upon the other; nourishment is pleasant to one and bitter to the other, but love abandons neither. Let us therefore take the Apostles' merits as examples; as for sufferings, let us not only have no fear of them, but even undergo them if necessary. Hear what the same Apostle says: *For I am already being offered in sacrifice*. Being offered belongs to sacrifice: he knew his suffering was a sacrifice to God. It was not those who killed him who offered such a sacrifice to the Father, but that priest who had said, *Do not fear those who kill the body*. *The time,* he says, *of my release is at hand.* What, then, Paul, do you hope for in that release, weary worker, by way of rest? *The time,* he says, *of my release is at hand.* What have you done? What do you hope for? *I have fought the good fight, completed the course, kept the faith.* How did he keep the faith except by not being frightened by persecutors? *Preach the word in season and out of season.* How wicked it is, then, for us to fail to keep faith when we are afraid—especially with him who teaches us both to love better things and to fear greater things! Whatever sweetness this life may have, it is not paradise, not heaven, not God's kingdom, not the fellowship of angels, not companionship with those citizens of the Jerusalem above. Let the heart be carried upward; let the earth be trodden by the flesh. The Lord has taught us to despise passing things and love eternal things; he has taught us, healed us, and continues healing us, because he deigns to do so. For he did not find us healthy, but came as a physician to the sick. The cup of suffering is bitter, but it thoroughly heals every disease; the cup of suffering is bitter, but the physician drank it first, so that the sick person would not hesitate to drink. Therefore let this cup be drunk if he gives it who knows what to give and to whom. But if he does not want it drunk, let him heal in another way, provided he heals. Let us confidently submit to the hands of so great a physician, utterly certain that he will apply nothing that is not good for us. For Paul demanded the debt as something due to merit. And what merit? *I have completed* *the course, fought the good fight, kept the faith.* You have done these things: what do you hope for? *Henceforth there remains for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day*. He does not say “give,” but “repay.” If he will repay, he owed it. Had he received a loan, then, that he should owe? He owes the crown, repays the crown, having become a debtor not through our loan but through his promise; for when he crowned Paul's merits, he was indeed crowning his own gifts.

Sermon 299/A, expanded version  
Sermon of Saint Augustine delivered to the people  
on the birthday of the holy apostles Peter and Paul

**1. 1.** We commemorate the festival of this holy day that gathers us today, so well known to your ears, your minds, and your deeds, sharing your thanksgiving and rejoicing with you. The birthday of the apostles Peter and Paul has dawned: the birthday that did not entangle them in the world, but freed them from the world. For through human weakness a person is born to hardship; through Christian love martyrs are born to a crown. Because of their merit this festive day has been set before us for solemn celebration and imitation of their holiness, so that, recalling the martyrs' glory, we may love in them what their killers hated, and, loving their virtue, honor their suffering. Through virtue merit was gained; in suffering the reward was repaid [in their crowning]. One day belongs to two martyrs and two apostles. As far as we have received from the Church's tradition, they did not both suffer on one day, and they did both suffer on one day. Peter suffered first on this day; Paul suffered later on this day. Merit made their suffering equal; love met on the same day. He accomplished this in them who was in them, who suffered in them, who suffered with them, who helped them as they fought, who crowned them as they conquered. But since we have said that a day of solemnity has been set before us, not pointlessly or to celebrate fleshly joy, but to imitate a spiritual crown—and everyone wants to be crowned, while few want to fight—let us hear Peter's merit from the Gospel and Paul's merit from the apostolic letter, following the order of their sufferings, not the order of the readings.

**2. 2.** The Gospel has just been read; we have just heard: *The Lord said to Peter: “Simon Peter, do you love me?” He answered: “I love you,” and the Lord: “Feed my sheep.” And again the Lord: “Simon Peter, do you love me?”* *And he said: “I love you, Lord,” and again the Lord: “Feed my sheep”*. A third time he asks nothing different from what he asked twice. It was for him to ask a third time; Peter was already weary of answering a third time. For *Peter* *was saddened,* the Gospel says, *because the Lord questioned him a third time, and he said: “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” And the Lord: “Feed my sheep”.* He who asks what he knows wants to teach something. What, then, did the Lord want to teach Peter by asking a third time what he already knew? What do we think, brothers, except that love should remove weakness, and that Peter should know that he must confess three times through love, since he had denied three times through fear? Peter's merit, that he fed God's sheep, would never have been crowned with true martyrdom if he had fed his own sheep. The Lord did not add *my sheep* three times without reason, unless there would be people who wanted to boast of martyrdom while feeding their own sheep. An apostolic soul, a Catholic soul, simple, humble, subject to God, seeking not its own glory but his, so that *whoever glories may glory in the Lord,* feeds for the shepherd and is a shepherd in the shepherd. Heretics feed their own sheep, but impose the Lord's mark on their sheep not for truth's sake, but for protection. For there are many—as we know, since examples abound everywhere—who fear losing their possessions and affix the titles of certain powerful people, so that by this act one person possesses while another intimidates. Therefore, because they do not see their own name glorified throughout the world, they have placed Christ's name on their sheep—if only these had been purchased and not plundered! One bought them; others stole them. He bought them who redeemed them from the devil, who gave his blood as the price: a worthy price indeed to purchase the whole world. Perhaps more was given than we were worth, but the buyer was a lover. So lost slaves possess sheep—I will no longer say their own, but sheep they want to be their own; they have imposed the Lord's mark on their stolen goods. But the true Lord is not idle: through his other servants he utters true words to the sheep, so that they may recognize the shepherd's voice and return. Let them return to the flock, return confidently: we receive the sheep, but do not damage the mark.

**3. 3.** Perhaps some of the brothers wondered why, although we are greatly concerned to gain our brothers and receive them back from the destruction of error, we said nothing about heretics in our earlier sermons. It has reached us that those wretched and pitiable people also said that terror of the Circumcellions had imposed silence on us. It is indeed true that they do not cease trying to frighten us away from preaching the word of peace; but if wolves frighten us, what will we answer the one who says, *Feed my sheep*? They bare their teeth to tear; we put forth our tongue to heal. We certainly speak and do not keep silent; we say the same things, say the same things often. Let them hear what they do not want to hear; let them do what they ought to do. To the unwilling we are indeed unwelcome. But if we are loved among the willing so that we may face danger among the unwilling, we will have confidence in Christ's name, persevering in preaching God's word, with your prayers helping us. For we believe that when you hear of our dangers, how we live amid the fury of marauders, you pray for us. Mutual love tells us this. For we have not entered your hearts, but he who is in you tells us, because he is in us too. Yet I give this warning: when you pray for us, pray that God may protect above all our well-being that accords with him, eternal salvation. As for this temporal well-being, let him do what he knows is good for us and his Church. For we have heard from the same teacher, shepherd, prince, and head of shepherds that we should *not* fear *those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul*. We have also heard from the Psalm's voice a most saving prayer: *Do not hand me over, Lord, to the sinner because of my own desire.* For it is evil for anyone to be handed over to a sinner because of his own desire. The martyrs were [not] handed over to sinners; the apostles whose birthdays we celebrate today were handed over to sinners; but before them the Lord of martyrs and apostles was handed over to sinners. All these were handed over to sinners, but not because of their own desire. Who, then, are handed over to sinners because of their own desire, except those who agree with persecutors for the sake of some worldly desire? For example, to stay with the example in which we find ourselves: if, while they threaten and rage with clubs, swords, and fires, we remain silent out of desire for this life, we will have been handed over to sinners because of our desire, and we live as dead people, possessing bodily health while losing the soul of love. But to live well, let us love both you, so that you may not be led astray, and them, so that we may gain them. They threaten: let us reprove. They rage: let us pray. They repel: let us teach.

**4. 4.** For we have also heard Paul's merit, so that I may now speak of his merits too, in the order I promised. He said to his disciple, announcing his coming suffering and removing fear from him by his example: *I bear witness before God and Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead,* *and [by] his appearing and his kingdom.* He bound him by this solemn testimony and added, *Preach the word; press on in season and out of season.* Hearing this, we too, according to our measure, are welcome to you and unwelcome to them. Yet in Christ's name we do not cease to proclaim and repeatedly offer God's word, the preaching of peace, *in season and out of season.* One who offers bread is welcome to a hungry person; one who compels eating is unwelcome to a sick person. Food is offered to the one, pressed upon the other; nourishment is pleasant to one and bitter to the other, but love abandons neither. Let us therefore take the apostles' merits as examples; as for sufferings, let us not only have no fear of them, but even undergo them if necessary. Hear what the same apostle says: *For I am already being offered in sacrifice,* or *poured out as an offering—*some manuscripts have *poured out as an offering,* others *offered in sacrifice;* pouring out an offering and offering in sacrifice both belong to sacrifice. He knew his suffering was a sacrifice to God. It was not those who killed him who offered such a sacrifice to the Father, but that priest who had said, *Do not fear those who kill the body. The time,* he says, *of my release is at hand.* What, then, Paul, do you hope for in that release? O weary worker, for what rest? *The time,* he says, *of my release is at hand.* What have you done? What do you hope for? *I have fought the good fight, completed the course, kept the faith.* How did he keep the faith except by not being frightened by persecutors away from preaching God's word *in season and out of season*? How wicked it is, then, for us to fail to keep faith when we are afraid—especially with him who teaches us both to love better things and to fear greater things!

**5. 5.** Whatever sweetness this life may have, it is not paradise, not heaven, not God's kingdom, not the fellowship of angels, not companionship with those citizens of the Jerusalem above. Let the heart be carried upward; let the earth be trodden by the flesh. The Lord has taught us to despise passing things and love eternal things; he has taught us, healed us, and continues healing us, because he deigns to do so. For he did not find us healthy, but came as a physician to the sick. The cup of suffering is bitter, but it thoroughly heals every disease; the cup of suffering is bitter, but the physician drank it first, so that the sick person would not hesitate to drink. Let it be drunk, if he wills it; for his will toward us is better than our own. He is more prudent than we are, knowing better than we ourselves what is good for us, and knowing better than we do what is happening within us. So it is with a sick person and a physician: the one suffers and does not know what is wrong with him; the other examines another's suffering and declares the truth. A person asks another what is happening within himself and desires testimony about his own interior from someone outside. If the skill of a human physician can do this, how much more can the Lord's power! Indeed, this very day reminds me to offer an example of this. Before the Lord's suffering, when that suffering was approaching, Peter himself, whose day we celebrate today, was sick and did not know what was happening within him. He was entirely unaware of the interior workings of his weakness. He had presumed that he would die with the Lord; he dared more than he could accomplish. The sick man presumed upon his future suffering; the physician declared his future denial. What wonder, then, if in that weakness the physician's judgment proved truer than the sick man's? It came to something like an attack of higher fever, and he could not follow into suffering. Therefore let this cup be drunk if he gives it who knows what to give and to whom. But if he does not want it drunk, let him heal in another way, provided he heals. Let us confidently submit to the physician's hands, utterly certain that he will apply nothing that is not good for us.

**6. 6.** For Paul demanded the debt as something due to merit. And what merit? *I have completed the course, fought the good fight, kept the faith.* You have done these things: what do you hope for? *Henceforth there remains for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day.* *The just judge will repay.* But the merciful Father made the person whom he would repay. For what sort of man did Christ find in Saul, who later became Paul? Was he not sick and in danger, frantic with a kind of mad disease among the Jews? Is he not that Saul who was present when Stephen was stoned, who guarded the clothing of those throwing stones so that he might stone him through everyone's hands? Is he not the man who, after receiving letters from the chief priests, went wherever he could to bind Christians and lead them to punishment? Was he not, while traveling and breathing threats and murder, as we read, called and thrown down by a heavenly voice, recalled by a word to the Word? What merits of his, then, preceded the Lord's calling him in this way? I do not ask what had preceded that deserved a crown, but what did not deserve condemnation. He made a persecutor of the Church into a preacher of peace; he loosed all his sins; he placed him in a position to loose the sins of others through him. These were gifts of mercy, not debts owed to merits. Hear Paul himself, certainly not ungrateful for God's grace; hear him recalling and proclaiming these things: *I who formerly,* he says, *was a blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent aggressor, but I obtained mercy*. Does he say there, “It was repaid to me”? For if he said, “*I who formerly was a blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent aggressor,* *but* it was repaid to me,” what should have been repaid for these deeds except condemnation? But he says, “*I obtained mercy.* Punishment was not repaid to me, so that afterward a crown might be repaid.” See, brothers: a crown is owed to the one to whom punishment was owed. *Formerly I was,* he says, *a blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent aggressor.* You see the merit: punishment was owed. Therefore punishment is not repaid; he obtains mercy instead of punishment. Having received mercy, he is not ungrateful: he fights the good fight, completes the course, keeps the faith. He has made the forgiver of sins his debtor. *There remains for me,* he says, *the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay me on that day.* He does not say “give,” but *repay;* if he will repay, he owed it. I dare to say it outright: if he will repay, he owed it. Had he received a loan, then, that he should owe? He owes the crown, repays the crown, having become a debtor not through our loan but through his promise. For when he crowned Paul's merits, he was indeed crowning his own gifts.

**7. 7.** Therefore, dearly beloved brothers, God is a debtor because he is a promiser. Thus, if someone promises us something, when we approach him to give it, we use this expression: “Repay what you promised me.” We press a debtor when we say “Repay,” yet acknowledge his kindness when we say “Repay what you promised,” not “what you received.” He has therefore promised certain things both to all of us and to the whole world, and those very things are great. To avoid saying too much, he promised Christ, Christ's suffering, Christ's blood for us; he promised through his prophets, promised through his books; he promised the Church spread throughout the world, promised victories to the martyrs, promised the Church the breaking of idols, promised judgment at the end and eternal life. To avoid recalling too much—for it is difficult to enumerate all his promises—let us consider for now these things I have mentioned. He promised Christ: *See, a virgin will conceive and bear a son, and you will call his name Emmanuel, which means God with us,* and the other things you know, which would take long to recall. He promised his suffering, resurrection, and glorification: these have happened. He promised that martyrs for his name would be brave in suffering and victorious in perseverance. The world rages; its raging was promised, not so that the seed would be trampled, but so that the crop would be sown. The martyrs' blood was poured out everywhere; the Church's harvest filled the world. These things have happened. The Church itself was promised in the Scriptures as destined to reign, but had not yet been displayed in reality. The apostles proclaimed it, sowed it everywhere; what had been said had not yet come: *And all the kings of the earth will worship him; all kings will serve him.* It had not yet come, but was held in a written guarantee. For God wished human weakness to be so secure about his promise that he not only spoke but also wrote it down. He spoke for those who believed; he gave a written guarantee for those who doubted. Everything was held in a kind of bond, in holy Scripture, not yet in the experience of repayment. Kings too believed, for it was in God's bond: *All the kings of the earth will worship him; all nations will serve him.* The Church has now spread so that all nations too may serve him. You have this also in the bond: *And there will be a visitation upon the idols of the nations*. You have in the bond: *Lord God, my refuge, nations will come to you from the end of the earth and say: Truly our fathers worshiped lies, images that brought them no benefit.* Not the images themselves, indeed, but demons and human beings raged on behalf of the images, killed martyrs, and caused a triumph over themselves. Babylon is being repaid for what it did.

**8. 8.** For a certain impious city is described throughout all lands as a kind of agreement in human impiety, and this is mystically called Babylon in the Scriptures. Again, there is a certain city journeying as a stranger on this earth among all nations in agreement in devotion, and this is called Jerusalem. Now both cities are mingled; at the end they will be separated. Divine Scripture addresses both in many places, and in one place says to Jerusalem: *Repay her double for what she has done; repay her,* so that Jerusalem should repay Babylon double. What is this double repayment? How do we understand double being repaid to the city of Babylon? For the sake of its idols it killed Christians, but it could not kill Christ, could not kill our God. It ravaged Christians' flesh, did not harm their spirit, did not touch our God. It is repaid double, in its people and in its gods. For they killed people; they could not kill our God. But now people are received into Jerusalem after their unbelief has been killed; images are broken. They look for their people and do not find them, for pagans become Christians. Someone who is not what he was has been killed. Just so Paul, formerly Saul: although the preacher lives, the persecutor has been killed. Formerly Christians sought somewhere to hide themselves while pagans raged; now pagans seek somewhere to hide their gods. And when these are broken, their defenders still do not want to be silent, yet they mutter in corners—as though, if they ever dare, they do anything except what he who possesses us has promised! Or when they acted, did they act by their own power? See, Christians were arrested, confessed Christ, and were killed. Let someone confess Mercury, swear by Mercury: when he sees even a single constable in a cloak, “I did not do it, I was not there, I did not sacrifice; where did you see me?” But those holy servants of God: “Were you at the Christians' gathering?” “I was.” We read the martyrs' confessions; we exult in the joy of their examples. These things have happened, repaid by the Lord because they were promised. Previously they were held in Scripture; now they have also been displayed. What I have said about idols has been displayed and is being displayed. And the Church, spread throughout the world, has already embraced almost all nations, and those it does not embrace it will embrace. It grows daily; everywhere the Christian people increase in Christ's name.

**9. 9.** Among Christians, few live well and many live badly. But these few are few in comparison with their chaff; in comparison, I say, with their chaff they are few. This threshing floor will be winnowed; there will be an enormous heap of chaff, but a shining mass of saints will also appear. The chaff will go into the fire, the wheat into the granary; yet now both are everywhere. How has this come about? Those who sowed, brothers, whose memory we celebrate today—through these God displayed [to him] what he promised them, and through them what he promised us. What did he promise them? *Henceforth there remains for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will repay on that day.* What did he promise us? *In your offspring all nations will be blessed.* How was it accomplished through these men? *Their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.* What will heretics read out against these things? I think that they too celebrate the apostles' birthday today; they indeed aspire to celebrate this day, but they do not dare sing this Psalm.
