# Sermon 26

**1** The psalm we have sung to God, and in which we have mutually exhorted one another to adore him and prostrate ourselves before him and weep before the Lord who made us, warns us to inquire somewhat more diligently what it means by saying, *who made us*. For that man was created by God no man doubts, except one who is ungrateful. For we know—since so we have read and so we have believed—that God made man, among the many things he made, to his own image. This is man's first condition, this is the first human creation. Yet I do not think the Holy Spirit wanted to warn us of this, as something great, in this psalm, when it says, *Let us weep before the Lord, who made us*[^1]. For in another place it says: *He made us, and not we ourselves*[^2]. Of which, as I said, no Christian doubts. For not only did God create the first man, from whom all men come, but God even today creates individual men, he who said to a certain holy one of his: *Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^3]. First, then, he created man without a man; now he creates man from a man. Yet whether man without a man, or man from a man, *he made us, and not we ourselves*[^4]. According, then, to this first and easy sense of these words—yet a true one—*let us adore him*, brothers, *and prostrate ourselves before him, and weep before the Lord who made us*[^5]. For he did not make us and then desert us. He did not take care to make us and not take care to keep us. *Let us weep before the Lord who made us*, because we did not weep when he made us, and yet he made us. He, then, who made us before he was asked, does he desert us when he is asked? As though a man doubted whether he would be heard when he prayed, Scripture warned him, saying: *Let us weep before the Lord, who made us*. Surely he hears those whom he made; surely he cannot fail to care for those whom he made.

**2** Yet with a deeper meaning, and, as I judge, a more useful one, the Holy Spirit saw some men saying or about to say that God made them men, but that they make themselves just; and foreseeing them, he warned them and called them back from this self-exaltation, saying: *He made us, and not we ourselves*[^6]. For why did he add, *and not we ourselves*, when it would have sufficed to say, *He made us*? Except that he wanted to warn against that making in which men say, "We made ourselves"—that is, "to be just, we made ourselves just by free will." When we were created, we received free choice. So that we may be just, then, we do it by free choice. Why do we still call upon God to make us just, which we have in our power, that we make ourselves just? Hear, hear: He made us just too, *and not we ourselves*. The first man was created in a nature without fault, in a nature without flaw. He was created upright, he did not make himself upright. But what he himself made of himself is known: falling from the hand of the potter, he was broken. For the very one who had made him was guiding him. He wished to desert the one by whom he had been made. God permitted it, as though saying: "Let him desert me and find himself, and let his misery prove that he can do nothing without me."

**3** In this way, then, God wanted to show man what free choice avails without God. O evil free choice without God! We have experienced what it avails without God. We were made wretched for this reason, that we experienced what free choice avails without God. Having experienced it, then, at long last, let us know, and *Come, let us adore* him, *and prostrate ourselves before him. Come, let us adore and prostrate ourselves before him, and weep before the Lord who made us*[^7]. That, lost through ourselves, he who made us may remake us. See, man was made good, and through free choice man was made evil. When will an evil man, through free choice deserting God, make a good man? He could not, when good, keep himself good, and shall he, evil, make himself good? When he was good, he did not keep himself good, and now that he is evil, does he say, "I make myself good"? What do you do, evil man, who perished while good, unless he who remains good remake you?

**4** *He, then, made us, and not we ourselves. But we are his people and the sheep of his pasture*[^8]. See, he who made us made us, men, his people. For when created as men, we were not yet his people. See, my brothers, and from the very words of the psalm attend to why it said, *He made us, and not we ourselves*. For this reason it said, *He made us, and not we ourselves*—that we might be his people and the sheep of his pasture. *He made us*. For the pagans too are born, and all the impious, all the adversaries of his Church. That they might be born, he himself made them. For no other god created them. Those who are born of pagans were made by him, created by him. And they are not his people, nor the sheep of his pasture. Nature is common to all, not grace. Let nature not be thought grace—and yet even if it be thought grace, since it too was granted freely. For a man who did not exist did not earn that he should exist. If he earned it, he already existed; but he did not yet exist. Therefore the one who might earn it did not exist, and yet he was made. He was not made like the cattle, nor made like the tree, nor made like the rock. But he was made to the image of the Creator. Who gave this benefit? God, who existed and existed from eternity. To whom did he give it? To man, who did not yet exist. He gave who existed, he received who did not exist. And who could do this except the one who *calls the things that are not as though they were*[^9]? Of whom the Apostle says: *Who chose us before the foundation of the world*[^10]. He chose us before the foundation of the world: we were made in this world, nor did the world exist when we were chosen. Ineffable wonders, my brothers. Who would be able to unfold this? Who could even think of what he might unfold? Those who do not exist are chosen. Neither does he who chooses err, nor does he choose in vain. Yet he chooses, and has chosen ones, whom he is about to create to be chosen. But he has them with himself, not in their nature, but in his foreknowledge.

**5** Therefore do not be exalted. We are men. *He made us*. We are faithful—if indeed we are, when we dispute these things against grace—but see, we are faithful. Even faithful, even just—because *the just man lives by faith*[^11]—*he made us, and not we ourselves*[^12]. I ask what he made us. You will say, "Men." The psalm was not speaking of that. That we know, that is known, that is plain. Nor do we need great learning to know this, that he himself made us men. But see what it was speaking of: *He made us, and not we ourselves*. What did he make us, except what we are? But what are we? *We*—see what we are. What? *His people, and the sheep of his pasture*. He himself made us his people, he himself made us the sheep of his pasture. He who sent the innocent sheep to be slaughtered made sheep out of wolves. This is grace. Apart from that common grace of nature by which we were made men, who were not and were not worthy because we were not—apart from that grace, this is the greater grace by which we were made *his people and the sheep of his pasture, through Jesus Christ our Lord*.

**6** But someone will say: "Were we made through Jesus Christ even to be men too?" Indeed so; through Jesus Christ the pagans too were made. Not as pagans—not that they should be pagans, but that they should be men—they were made through Jesus Christ. For who is Jesus Christ, except *in the beginning the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God? He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him*[^13]? To him, then, the pagans too owe it that they were created men, and they are to be punished all the more, because they forsook the one by whom they were made and worshipped the things they themselves made.

**7** Apart, then, from that grace by which human nature was founded—for this is common to Christians and pagans—this is the greater grace, not that we were created men through the Word, but that through the Word made flesh we were made faithful. *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^14]; *in the beginning was the Word*[^15]. The man Christ Jesus did not yet exist, *and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^16]. The world itself did not exist, when God the Word existed. *All things were made through him*[^17], and the world was made through him. Therefore, at the time when he made us to be men, he was not yet a man. The Apostle commends this grace the more to Christians where he says: *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men*. He did not say "Christ Jesus," lest you should think it said according to the Word, but he added *man*: *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^18]. What is a mediator? One through whom we might be joined, through whom we might be reconciled, because, separated by our own sins, we lay in death, we had utterly perished. Christ was not man when man was created. That man might not perish, he was made man.

**8** These things I say to you against a new heresy that tries to rise up, so that we are often compelled to dispute, because we want you to be firm in good but untouched by evil. For this is their argument, when they first began to arise and to dispute against grace, attributing much not to man's liberty but to his weakness, and exalting the wretched man lying prostrate in such a way that he cannot rise by a hand stretched out to him from above. Disputing, then, against grace on behalf of free choice, they gave offense to pious and catholic ears. They began to be regarded with horror, they began to be avoided as a sure ruin. It began to be said of them that they disputed against grace. And to relieve this odium they invented such a device. "I do not," he says, "dispute against grace." How do you prove it? "By this very thing," he says, "that I defend free choice—that I do not dispute against the grace of God." See the sharpness, but it is glassy. It shines, as it were, with vanity, but is shattered by truth. Attend to how sharply, as it were, was thought out what they wished to say. "By this very thing," he says, "that I defend the free choice of man, and say that free choice is sufficient that I should be just, I do not say it without the grace of God." The ears of the devout are roused. Now whoever hears this begins to rejoice: "Thanks be to God! He does not defend free choice without the grace of God. For there is free choice, but it avails nothing without the grace of God. If, then, they defend free choice not without the grace of God, what evil do they say?" Explain to us, then, O teacher, what grace you mean. "When I say," he says, "man's free choice, see that I say man's." What then? "Who created man? God. Who gave him free choice? God. If, then, God created man, and God granted man free choice, whatever man can do by free choice, to whose grace is it owed, except to him who founded him with free choice?" And this is said, as it were, sharply by them.

**9** See, however, my brothers, how they preach that general grace by which man was created, by which we are men. And surely we are men even along with the impious, but we are not Christians along with the impious. This grace, then, by which we are Christians—this is what we want them to preach, this is what we want them to acknowledge, this is the grace of which the Apostle says: *I do not make void the grace of God. For if justice is through the law, then Christ died for nothing*[^19]. See whence the Apostle said it. He said it of the law: *If justice is through the law, then Christ died for nothing*. Because, then, justice was not from the law, therefore Christ died, that by faith those might be justified who would not be justified by the law. *For if a law had been given that could make alive*, he says, *justice would altogether be from the law*—which we recalled even yesterday—*but Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise*—promise, not prediction; the one who promised himself does it—*that the promise*, he says, *by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to believers*[^20]. See what sort of men the grace of the Savior found us, whom not even the law could make healthy. But why was the law given, if nature was sufficient? Why was the law given, if nature was sufficient? And yet not even the law could suffice, so weak was nature itself. The law was given, but not one that could make alive. Why, then, was it given? *The law*, says the Apostle, *was given for the sake of transgression*[^21]. It was set down for the sake of transgression, to make you a transgressor. "Why, to make me a transgressor?" Because God knew your pride. He knew that you were saying: "Oh, that there were someone to teach me, oh, that there were someone to show me!" See, the law says to you: *You shall not covet*[^22]. You have come to know the law saying, *You shall not covet*. Concupiscence, which you did not know, rose up. For it was within, but it was not known. You began to strive to overcome what was within, and what was hidden appeared. Proud man, through the law you have been made a transgressor. Acknowledge grace and be a praiser.

**10** "But who gave the law?" you say. For there are vain men, and, worse, impious men, who say that the law was given by another, but grace through our Lord Jesus Christ; as though the law were evil, the law perverse, but grace upright. And they want to distinguish the two Testaments in such a way that they say the old Testament is from some prince of darkness or other, but the new Testament from the Lord God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Hear Paul himself. If you think the law was given by another, not by God, because through it you were made a transgressor, hear the Apostle himself, the praiser of the law. *And so*, he says, *the law is holy, and the commandment holy.* Add: *and just.* Add further: and *good. Did then what is good*, he says, *become death to me? Far be it! But sin, that it might appear as sin*[^23]. For there was sin, but the sin lay hidden. When did the sin lie hidden? When it did not yet endure you as its adversary. You began to strive, and the one who held you appeared. While you followed, you did not feel the chain. You sought a refuge, and the bond appeared. You wanted to flee, and you began to be dragged. Because, then, you began to be dragged, let the one who is not bound come to your aid. Who is not bound, except the one who said: *If you have found sin in me, tell me*[^24]? Who is not bound, except the one who said: *Behold, the prince of the world comes, and in me he will find nothing*[^25]? Why should he kill me—he will find nothing, because death is justly owed to sin. Why, then, do you die? *That all may know*, he says, *that I do the will of my Father*[^26]. He himself looses, who is not bound. He himself frees from the dead, who is *free among the dead*[^27].

**11** But he himself also sent the law. Through his servant the law, through himself grace. Attend to Elisha, in a great and lofty mystery, acting as a prophet who foretells, not only by speaking. The son of his hostess had died. What did the dead boy signify, except Adam? It was announced to the holy prophet, who bore in his prophecy a type of the Lord Jesus Christ. He sent his staff through his servant, and said to him: *Go, go, lay it upon the dead boy*[^28]. The servant went, like an obedient servant. The prophet knew what he had done. He laid the staff upon the dead, and he did not rise. *For if a law had been given that could make alive, justice would altogether be from the law*[^29]. The law, then, could not make alive. He himself came, the great to the small, the savior to save, the living to the dead. He himself came. And what did he do? He drew in his limbs to a youthful size, as it were emptying himself to take the form of a servant. He drew in his limbs to a youthful size, fitted himself small to the small, that he might make the body of our lowliness conformed to the body of his glory. And so in this type of Christ prophetically expressed the dead boy was raised, as the impious is justified.

**12** Let this grace be preached. This is the grace of Christians, through the man the Mediator, through the one who suffered and was raised, who ascended into heaven and led captivity captive and gave gifts to men. This grace, I say, let it be preached. Against this grace let it not be disputed by the ungrateful. The prophetic staff did not suffice for the dead. Would dead nature itself suffice? And let us confess to be grace even that by which we were founded—though we have hardly read it called by this name—yet, because it was given freely, let us confess it grace. But let us show you that this grace is greater, by which we are Christians. Attend. Before we were founded, we merited nothing good, and therefore it is grace by which we were founded, since we merited nothing good. If, then, the grace is great when we merited nothing good, how great is the grace when we merited only evil? He who did not yet exist did not merit good; a sinner, he merited evil too. He who was made did not yet exist. He did not yet exist, but neither had he offended. He did not yet exist, and was made; he offended, and is saved. He who did not yet exist hoped for nothing, and was made. But fallen, he awaited damnation, and was freed. This *is grace through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^30]. *He himself made us*, and before we existed at all *he himself made us*, and made and fallen he himself made us just, *and not we ourselves*[^31]. *If, then, there is any new creature in Christ*[^32], the old has fallen, the new has been made.

**13** There was one mass of perdition from Adam, to which nothing but punishment was owed. From it vessels were made unto honor, from the same mass. *For the potter has power* over the clay, from the same mass. From what mass? Surely it had already perished, surely a just damnation was already owed to that mass. Be glad that you have escaped. For you have escaped the death that was owed, and found the life that was not owed. *The potter has power over the clay to make from the same mass one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor*[^33]. But you say: "Why did he make me unto honor, and another unto dishonor?" What shall I answer? Are you going to hear Augustine, you who do not hear the Apostle saying: *O man, who are you that you should answer back to God?*[^34] Two little ones are born. If you ask what is owed, the mass of perdition holds them both. But why does a mother carry one to grace, and a sleeping mother smother the other? You will say to me: what did the one merit who was carried to grace, what did the one merit whom the mother smothers by sleeping? Both merited nothing good. But *the potter has power over the clay to make from the same mass one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor*[^35]. Do you want to dispute with me? Rather, marvel with me, and cry out with me: *O the depth of the riches!*[^36] Let us both be terrified, let us both cry out: *O the depth of the riches!* Let us both agree in dread, lest we perish in error. *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out!*[^37] Search out the unsearchable, do the impossible, corrupt the incorruptible, see the invisible.

**14** *His judgments are unsearchable*[^38]. You have heard, let it suffice you. *And his ways past tracing out. For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that it should be repaid to him?*[^39] Who has first given, when he received the whole freely? *Who has first given to him, that it should be repaid to him?* If the Lord wished to repay, he would repay nothing but the owed punishment. They gave nothing, that it should be repaid to them. *For nothing you will save them*[^40]. *Who has first given to him*, as it were on account of his own merits? *Who has first given to him*, who has gone before the grace that is given freely? If any merits go before grace, it is no longer given freely, but rendered as a debt. But if it is not given freely, why is it called grace? *Who, then, has first given to him, that it should be repaid to him? For from him and through him and in him are all things*[^41]. What, surely, except all the good things we received from him, and received that we might be good? *For every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no change*[^42]. For you were changed for the worse. *With whom there is no change*, he himself comes to your aid. *With whom there is not even a shadow of a moment*, for you lie in the darkness of your own night. From him, then, are all things. No one has first given him anything, let no one demand a debt. *By grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves, it is the gift of God*[^43].

**15** "But it troubles me," you say, "that one perishes, another is baptized. It troubles me, it troubles me as a man." If you want to hear the truth, it troubles me too, because I am a man. But if you too are a man, and I am a man, let us both hear the one who says: *O man!* Surely, if we are troubled because we are men, the Apostle addresses human nature itself, weak and feeble, saying: *O man, who are you that you should answer back to God? Does the thing molded say to the one who molded it: Why have you made me* so?[^44] If a beast could speak, and said to God, "Why did you make this one a man, and me a beast?" you would not justly be angered and say, "O beast, who are you that you should answer back to God?" You too are a man, but to God you are a beast. And would that you were his beast and the sheep of his pasture. Acknowledge the benefits of the shepherd, and you will not follow the wolves of error. We were wolves. *We too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^45]. But a sheep died, and made us sheep. *Behold the Lamb of God, behold the one who takes away the sin*—not of this one or that one, but *of the world*[^46]. Therefore, my brothers, from the fact that we are something—if indeed we are something in his faith—however much we are, let us claim nothing for ourselves, lest we lose even what we received. But in what we received, let us give glory to him, let us honor him, let him water his own seeds. What would our earth have, unless he had sown it? But he also gives rain. He does not desert what he sowed. *The Lord will give sweetness, and our earth will give its fruit*[^47]. Let us turn to the Lord, and so on.
