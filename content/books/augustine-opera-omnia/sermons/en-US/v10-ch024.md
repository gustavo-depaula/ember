# Sermon 351: On the Usefulness of Doing Penance

**1. 1.** How useful and necessary the medicine of penance is, men most easily understand who remember that they are men. For it is written: *God resists the proud, but to the humble he gives grace*[^1]. And the Lord says in the Gospel: *For he who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted*[^2]; and that publican went down from the temple more justified, anxious in the confession of his sins, than the Pharisee, secure in the enumeration of his merits. For although that man too gave thanks to God, saying, *I give you thanks, God, that I am not as the rest of men, unjust, adulterers, robbers; as also this publican is. I fast twice on the Sabbath, I give tithes of all that I possess*; yet preferred to him was the one who *stood at a distance, and did not dare to lift his eyes to heaven, but struck his breast, saying: God, be merciful to me a sinner*[^3]. For that Pharisee rejoiced not so much in his own health as in the comparison of others' diseases. But it was more useful for him, since he had come to the physician, to show by confessing the things from which he was sick, than to dissemble about his own wounds and dare to glory in others' scars. No wonder, then, if the publican departed more cured, who was not ashamed to show what pained him. For in visible things, that anyone may reach lofty places, he is raised up on high: but God, since he is most excellent of all, is reached not by elation but by humility. Whence the prophet says: *The Lord is near to those who have crushed their heart*[^4]. And again: *The Lord is lofty, and looks upon the lowly, and the lofty he knows from afar*[^5]. He put "the lofty" for the proud. The lowly, then, he looks upon, to lift them up; the lofty he knows, to cast them down. For when he says that *he knows the lofty from afar*, he shows well enough that he attends to the lowly from close by: yet he proclaimed the Lord himself to be lofty. For God alone is not arrogant, however much he praises himself with proclamation. Let pride not think, then, that it is hidden from the eyes of God: for God knows the lofty. Nor again let it think itself joined to God: for the lofty he knows from afar. Whoever, then, refuses the humility of penance does not intend to draw near to God. For it is one thing to lift oneself up to God, another to lift oneself up against God. He who casts himself down before him is raised up by him: he who raises himself against him is cast down by him. For there is one solidity of greatness, another emptiness of inflation. He who swells outwardly wastes away within. He who *chooses to be cast down in the house of God, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners*[^6]—him God chooses, that he may dwell in his courts; and the one who takes nothing upon himself he takes up into the seat of blessedness. Whence in the Psalm it is most sweetly and most truly sung: *Blessed is the man whose help is from you, O Lord*. Do not think that one who humbles himself always lies prostrate; since it was said: *he will be exalted*. And do not suppose his exaltation to take place in the eyes of men through bodily heights; for when he had said, *Blessed is the man whose help is from you, O Lord*, he consequently joined and showed the spiritual loftiness of that same help: *Ascents*, he says, *he has set up in his heart, in the valley of weeping, to the place which he has appointed*[^7]. Where, then, did he set up ascents? In the heart, namely in the valley of weeping. This is: *He who humbles himself will be exalted*. For just as the ascent indicates exaltation, so the valley indicates humility, and the valley of weeping, weeping. For just as grief is the companion of penance, so tears are the witnesses of grief. And it follows most fittingly, and he says: *For he who gave the law will give a blessing*[^8]. For the law was given for this, that it might show the wounds of sins, which the blessing of grace might heal. The law was given for this, that it might make his weakness known to the proud, and persuade the weak to penance. The law was given for this, that we might say in the valley of weeping: *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members*: and with that very weeping might cry out: *Unhappy man that I am! who will free me from the body of this death?* and there might come to our aid, with him hearing who lifts up the broken, looses the fettered, enlightens the blind[^9], *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^10].

**2. 2.** There are, moreover, three actions of penance, which your learning recognizes along with me. For they are customary in the Church of God, and known to those who attend diligently. One is that which brings the new man to birth, until through the saving Baptism there is made the washing-away of all past sins: so that, as in a newborn child, the pains pass away by which the inward parts were urged to birth, and gladness follows sadness. For everyone who is now established as the arbiter of his own will, when he approaches the Sacraments of the faithful, unless he repents of his old life, cannot begin a new one. From this penance, when they are baptized, only little ones are immune: for they cannot yet use free choice. To them, however, for the consecration and remission of original sin, the faith of those by whom they are offered profits; so that whatever stains of sins they have contracted through others, from whom they were born, they may be cleansed also by the questioning and answering of others. For most truly it is lamented in the Psalms: *Behold, in iniquity I was conceived, and in sins my mother nourished me in the womb*[^11]. Likewise it is written that not even the infant, whose life is one day upon the earth, is clean in the sight of God[^12]. Excepting such, then—about whose rank and merit in that future lot of the saints which is promised, to wish to inquire further exceeds the measure of men; yet it is piously believed to profit them toward spiritual salvation, which is guarded by the firm strength of ecclesiastical authority throughout the whole world—of other men none passes over to Christ, so as to begin to be what he was not, unless he repents of having been what he was. This first penance is enjoined on the Jews, the apostle Peter saying: *Do penance, and let each one of you be baptized in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ*[^13]. Such was commanded by the Lord himself, when he said: *Do penance, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near*[^14]. Of this John the Baptist too, full of the Holy Spirit, the forerunner and preparer of the way of the Lord, says thus: *Brood of vipers, who showed you to flee from the wrath to come? Bring forth, then, fruit worthy of penance*[^15].

**3. 3.** But the second penance is that whose action must be undergone, through this whole life in which we pass in mortal flesh, with the perpetual humility of supplication. First, because no one desires the eternal, incorruptible, and immortal life unless he repents of this temporal, corruptible, and mortal life. For no one is so born into the new life through the sanctification of Baptism that, just as he there lays down all past sins, he also at once lays down mortality itself of the flesh and corruption. And if it is not so, it remains that what is written, which everyone also feels in himself while he is in this life, that *the body which is corrupted* weighs down *the soul, and* the earthly habitation *presses down the sense thinking many things*[^16]. And because this will not be in that blessedness, when death will be swallowed up in victory[^17], who can doubt that, in whatever temporal happiness we are engaged, we ought nevertheless to repent of this life, so that with all eagerness we may run toward that incorruption? For from this it is that the Apostle too says: *As long as we are in the body, we are on pilgrimage away from the Lord: for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^18]. Who, then, hastens and desires to return to the homeland, and to contemplate that sight which is face to face, except one who repents of his pilgrimage? From which grief of the penitent that pitiable cry too bursts forth and sounds: *Woe is me! that my pilgrimage has been made distant*. And lest you think one not yet faithful says this, see what follows: *I dwelt in the tents of Kedar: with those who hated peace I was peaceable; when I spoke to them, they fought against me vehemently*[^19]. These are the words not only of a faithful man, but even of a most steadfast evangelist and a most brave martyr. For from this is that of the Apostle too: *For we know that, if our earthly house of this habitation is dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made by hands, eternal in the heavens. For in this we groan, desiring to be clothed over with our habitation which is from heaven: if indeed, being clothed, we be not found naked. For we who are in this habitation groan, being weighed down, in that we do not wish to be unclothed, but to be clothed over, that the mortal may be swallowed up by life*[^20]. What, then, do we desire, except not to be as we now are? And what do we groan over, except, by repenting, that we are thus? But when shall we not be thus, except, the earthly house dissolved, so that we obtain the heavenly habitation by the changing of the whole man both in soul and in body? Wherefore holy Job too did not say that there is temptation in this life, but said that this very life is temptation, speaking thus: *Is not human life upon the earth a temptation?* In which passage he also wonderfully touched the mystery of fallen man, saying: *Like a servant fleeing his master, and reaching a shadow*[^21]. For this is not to be called life, rather than the shadow of life. And not without reason did the fugitive Adam, after the offense of sin, hide himself from the face of the Lord, covered with the leaves of trees, by which shady recesses are darkened, *like a servant fleeing his master*, as it was said, *and reaching a shadow*.

**3. 4.** All these things have been said for this, lest anyone, although through Baptism he has been justified from prior sins, should yet dare to be proud, if he commits nothing for which he should be separated from the communion of the altar, as though now boasting of full security; but let him rather keep humility, which is almost the one Christian discipline: nor let earth and ashes[^22] be proud, until this whole night passes, *in which all the beasts of the forest pass through, the roaring whelps of lions, seeking from God their food*[^23]. For this food Job himself was sought, who said: *Human life upon the earth is a temptation*. The Lord too: *On this night*, he said, *Satan has asked to sift you like wheat*[^24]. Who, then, of sound mind would not groan? To whom would it not, through penance, displease to be thus? Who would not, supplicating with all humility, show himself capable of being heard by the divine help, until all this material and earthly shadow of temptations passes away, and he who never fails may shine for us too, the everlasting day, and illumine the hidden things of darkness, and make manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then there will be praise to each one from God[^25]? Then, however much anyone may glory that he has his body so subdued that, crucified to the world, he chastises his members reduced to servitude away from every evil work, lest sin now reign in his mortal body, to obey its desires; that he worships the one only true God, given to no rite of idols, ensnared by no rites of demons, not taking in vain the name of the Lord his God, certain in awaiting everlasting rest, rendering due honor to his parents, neither bloody with murder, nor disgraced by fornication, nor fraudulent by theft, nor double by lying, nor sordid with the coveting of another's property or wife; nor, even in his own affairs, either dissolved in luxury or shriveled in avarice; not contentious, not abusive, not slanderous; finally selling all that he has, and giving to the poor, and following Christ, and fixing the root of his heart in the heavenly treasure: what can seem able to be added to so full a justice? Yet I do not want him to glory. Let him understand that all these things have been bestowed on him, not that they exist from himself. For what does he have that he has not received? But if he received it, why does he glory, as though he had not received it[^26]? Let him indeed give out the Lord's money: let him take counsel for his neighbor, as he feels counsel has been taken for himself. Nor let him think it enough to keep whole what he received, lest it be said to him: *Wicked and slothful servant, you should have given my money, and I, coming, would have exacted it with interest*[^27]: lest what he received be taken from him, lest he be cast into the outer darkness. If those who can keep whole what they received ought to fear so most vehement a punishment, what is the hope of those who impiously and wickedly scatter it? This man, then, will be engaged in human affairs, bound by the office not of carnal but of spiritual acquisition; not indeed obligated by worldly business, but yet, because he serves God as a soldier, not torpid and abject in the idleness of sloth. Let him give, then, if he can, all his alms with cheerfulness, whether he gives out something for the carnal needs of the poor, or, as a dispenser of the heavenly bread, builds in the hearts of believers a camp unconquered against the devil. *For God loves a cheerful giver*[^28]. Therefore let him not be broken by weariness in the difficulties of affairs, which must necessarily arise, that it may be shown to man that he is man. Let not anger creep upon him against one who either bursts in hatefully, or, inopportunely compelled by want, asks; or who, while you are occupied with something greater, indifferently demands to be helped in his own business; or who, by blind greed or pitiable slowness, resists in a matter of manifest justice. Let him give nothing more or less than is fitting: let him not speak more than is needful, or even when it is not needful. *For beautiful are the feet of those who evangelize peace, who evangelize good things*[^29]. But yet from the dry earth they draw dust, which is indeed shaken off unto the judgment of those who by a perverse will despise this offering to them. Not only, then, on account of the very mortality and ignorance of this life, and on account of the malice of the day—which would that it sufficed, as it was said of it: *Sufficient for the day is its own malice*[^30]—which we are commanded to bear and carry until it passes, and to endure God by acting manfully, that we may bring forth fruit with patience; but also on account of the very dust of this world, which clings to the feet of those who counsel along the roads of counseling, and the losses which befall in that most busy action of dispensation, which the Lord may grant be compensated with greater gains, we ought to have a daily penance.

**3. 5.** But if this is required of the dispensers of the word of God and the ministers of his Sacraments, the soldiers of Christ, how much more the rest of the stipendiary multitude, and a certain province of the great king? Which, lest perhaps that most faithful and most brave soldier the apostle Paul should offend it even by a false suspicion of avarice, he soldiered at his own expense: and where perhaps the necessary provision was lacking: *Other churches*, he says, *I despoiled, taking from them a stipend for your ministry*[^31]. How much more, then, ought the provincials of the Church, bound by worldly affairs, to do daily penance? Who, although they ought to be free, pure, and whole from thefts, robberies, frauds, adulteries and fornications and every luxury, from the cruelty of hatreds and the obstinacy of enmities, finally from all the foulness of idolatry, the triviality of spectacles, the impious vanity of heresies and schisms, and from all disgraces and crimes of this kind: yet, on account of the administration of household affairs and the closest bonds of marriages, sin so much that they seem not so much sprinkled with the dust of this world as smeared with mud. This is what the Apostle says to them: *Now indeed there is altogether a fault in you, that you have lawsuits among yourselves. Why do you not rather suffer injustice? Why are you not rather defrauded?* For that is execrable which he adds on account of certain ones, saying: *But you do injustice and defraud, and this to your brothers*[^32]. Yet, apart from injustices and frauds, the very having of lawsuits and disputes among themselves about worldly matters he calls a fault: which nevertheless he admonishes must be borne, if disputes of this kind are at least settled by ecclesiastical judgment. From this too is that: *He who is without a wife thinks of the things that are of God, how he may please God: but he who is joined in matrimony thinks of the things that are of the world, how he may please his wife*[^33]. Which he likewise notes of the woman. Or that, when he says: *And again be together, lest Satan tempt you on account of your incontinence*. Which, that he might show to be a sin, but conceded to weakness, he at once subjoined: *But this I say by way of pardon, not by way of command*[^34]. For only for the sake of begetting is the blameless mingling of the two sexes. How many are the other sins, whether in speaking of the affairs and business of others, which do not pertain to you; or in empty cacklings, since it is written: *The fool in laughter lifts up his voice, but the wise man will scarcely laugh quietly*[^35]; or in the very foods, which are prepared for the necessity of sustaining this life, a too greedy and immoderate appetite, often attesting by the next day's indigestion the limit it exceeded: or in selling and buying things, perverse wishes for dearness and cheapness. It wearies me to gather all of them, which everyone more certainly comprehends and reprehends in himself, if he attends not negligently to the mirror of the divine Scriptures. Which things, although they are not felt to strike with a lethal wound, like murder and adultery, or the rest of this kind: yet all gathered together, like a scab, the more they are, the more they kill, or so spread out our beauty that they separate us, away from the form of that comely Bridegroom beyond the sons of men, from his most chaste embraces, unless they are dried up by the medicine of daily penance.

**3. 6.** But if this is false, why do we daily beat our breasts? Which we too, the prelates standing at the altar, do with all. Whence also in praying we say what we ought to say in all this life: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^36]. For we do not pray that those be forgiven which, unless we believe them already forgiven in Baptism, we doubt of the faith itself: but surely we say this about daily sins, for which each one also does not cease to offer, according to his strength, the sacrifices of alms, of fastings, and of prayers and supplications themselves. Whoever, then, attending diligently to himself, seduces himself by no flattery, understands well enough with how great a danger of eternal death, and with how great a poverty of perfect justice, he is on pilgrimage away from the Lord; although already in Christ, that is, established on the way, he strives to return. For if we have no sins, and beating our breasts say, *Forgive us our debts*, then surely from this very thing we sin gravely, no one doubting, since amid the very Sacraments we lie. Wherefore, in so far as we are joined to our God by faith, hope, and love, and imitate him in so far as we can, we do not sin, but are sons of God: but in so far as, from the occasion of carnal weakness—because it is not yet dissolved by death, not yet changed by resurrection—reprehensible and wicked impulses creep in, we sin. Which surely it befits us to confess, lest, stiff-necked, we deserve not the health of our languor, but the condemnation of pride. Whence both are most truly written: both, *He who is born of God does not sin*[^37]; and what we read in the same Letter of John: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^38]. For that was said from the firstfruits of the new man, this from the remnants of the old: for we do both in this life. But gradually the newness comes on, and gradually, with the oldness yielding, it succeeds. But when both are being done, we are in the stadium; nor do we only strike the adversary with good works, but are also struck by incautiously avoiding sins. Nor is it now considered which of us has conquered, but who strikes more often, who fights more bravely; until he drags others with him into everlasting death, who envied man standing for having fallen, and at the end it is said by the others triumphing: *Where is, O death, your strife? where is, O death, your sting?*[^39] But neither are we more easily cast down by the enemy than when we imitate him by being proud; nor do we more vehemently lay him low than when with humility we follow the Lord; nor do we inflict on him sharper griefs than when we heal the blows of our sins by confessing and repenting.

**4. 7.** The third action of penance is that which must be undergone for those sins which the decalogue of the Law contains; and of which the Apostle says: *That those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^40]. In this penance, then, each one ought to exercise greater severity upon himself; that, judged by himself, he may not be judged by the Lord, as the same Apostle says: *For if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged by the Lord*[^41]. Let a man, therefore, ascend against himself the tribunal of his own mind; if he fears that which *we must be presented before the tribunal of Christ, that there each one may receive what he has done through the body, whether good or evil*[^42]. Let him set himself before his own face, lest this be done to him afterward. For God threatens this to the sinner, saying: *I will rebuke you, and set you before your own face*[^43]. And so, with judgment established in the heart, let an accusing thought be present, conscience the witness, fear the executioner. Thence let a certain blood of the confessing soul flow forth through tears. Finally, from the mind itself let such a sentence be brought forth, that the man judges himself unworthy of the participation of the body and blood of the Lord: so that he who fears to be separated from the kingdom of heaven by the final sentence of the supreme Judge may, through ecclesiastical discipline, be for a time separated from the Sacrament of the heavenly bread. Let the image of the future judgment be turned before his eyes: so that when others approach the altar of God, to which he himself does not approach, he may consider how greatly to be trembled at is that punishment by which, while others receive eternal life, others are cast headlong into eternal death. For to this altar, which is now placed in the Church on earth, exposed to earthly eyes, for celebrating the signs of the divine mysteries, even many wicked men can approach: since God commends in this time his patience, that in the future he may exert his severity. For they approach, not knowing that the patience of God leads them to penance. But they, according to the hardness of their heart and the impenitent heart, treasure up for themselves wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works[^44]. But to that altar to which Jesus our forerunner entered for us, to which the head of the Church went before, the other members to follow, none of those will be able to approach of whom, as I already mentioned, the Apostle said: *That those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*. For the priest alone, but there plainly the whole, will stand, the body namely being joined, who is the head, which has already ascended into heaven. He it is to whom the apostle Peter said: *A holy people, a royal priesthood*[^45]. How, then, will he dare or be able to enter into the inner parts of the veil, and into those invisible Holy of Holies, who, despising the medicine of heavenly discipline, was unwilling to be separated for a little while from visible things? For he who was unwilling to be humbled, that he might be exalted, when he wishes to be exalted, will be cast down; and forever he will be sundered from the eternal saints, whoever in this time, through the merits of obedience and through the satisfaction of penance, has not provided for himself a place in the body of the priest. For with what brow of shamelessness will he then wish the face of God to be turned away from his sins, who now does not say with his whole heart: *For I acknowledge my misdeed, and my sin is ever before me*[^46]? In what way, I ask, does God deign to pardon what man himself disdains to acknowledge in himself?

**4. 8.** Or what sort of thing is that in which they flatter themselves, who seduce themselves by their own vanity? For persevering in their malices and luxuries, when they hear the Apostle saying, *That those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^47], they dare to promise themselves, apart from the kingdom of God, the salvation which they desire; and so they speak among themselves, while they refuse to do penance for their sins, and at some time to change their lost morals for the better: I do not wish to reign, it is enough for me to be saved. In which they are first deceived, because there is no salvation at all for those whose iniquity perseveres. For what the Lord says: *Because iniquity has abounded, the love of many will grow cold; but he who perseveres to the end, he will be saved*[^48]; he surely promised salvation to those persevering in love, not in iniquity. But where there is love, those evil works which separate from the kingdom of God cannot be. *For the whole law is fulfilled in one saying, in that which is written: You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^49]. Then, if there is some difference between those who reign and those who do not reign; yet it is needful that all be in one kingdom, lest they be reckoned in the number of enemies or strangers. For all Romans possess the Roman kingdom; although not all reign in it, but obey the rest who reign. But the Apostle did not say: *Those who do such things* will not reign with God; but *will not possess the kingdom of God*. Which was said also of flesh and blood: *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God*; because *this corruptible will put on incorruption, and this mortal will put on immortality*[^50]; so that now, not flesh and blood, but from an animal body it may merit the state and nature of a spiritual body. Or let that final sentence of our Judge terrify them, which he therefore wished now to be disclosed, that by his faithful it may be guarded against; giving *to those who fear him a sign, that they may flee from the face of the bow*[^51]. For excepting those who will even judge with him, to whom also he promised, saying: *You shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel*[^52]. In which number of those who judge are understood all who, for the sake of the Gospel, left all that was theirs, and followed the Lord. For the number twelve refers to a certain universality. For the apostle Paul will not be absent there, because he was not among those twelve. Excepting those, then, whom he also signified by the name of angels, when he said: *When the Son of man comes to judge with his angels*. For angels are surely messengers. And we most rightly take as messengers all who announce the heavenly salvation to men. Whence the Evangelists too can be interpreted as good messengers: and of John the Baptist it was said: *Behold, I send my angel before your face*[^53]. These, then, as I had begun to say, excepted, all the rest of the multitude of men, as is manifest in the words of the Lord himself, will be divided into two parts. For he is going to place the sheep at the right hand, the goats at the left: and it will be said to the sheep, that is, the just: *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which was prepared for you from the foundation of the world*. Of this kingdom surely the Apostle spoke, when he enumerated the evil works: *That those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*. Hear what those who will be at the left hand hear: *Go*, he says, *into the eternal fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels*[^54]. Wherefore, will anyone dare to presume on the Christian name, and not with all obedience and fear hear the Apostle saying: *For know this, recognizing that no fornicator, or unclean person, or greedy man, which is the service of idols, has an inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God? Let no one seduce you with empty words: for on account of these things the wrath of God comes upon the sons of unbelief. Do not, then, become partakers with them*[^55]. But more amply to the Corinthians he says these things: *Do not err; neither fornicators, nor those serving idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor men who lie with men, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor the abusive, nor the rapacious, will possess the kingdom of God*. But see how he took away fear and despair of salvation from those who had committed these things in the old life. *And these things indeed*, he says, *you were, but you have been washed, but you have been sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and in the Spirit of our God*[^56].

**4. 9.** Whoever, then, after Baptism is held bound by the work of some of his former evils, is he so much an enemy to himself that he still doubts to change his life, while there is time, when he so sins and lives? For surely, in that he so perseveringly sins, he treasures up for himself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God. But in that he still lives, the patience of God leads him to penance. Entangled, then, in the bonds of such deadly sins, does he decline, or defer, or doubt to flee to the very keys of the Church, by which he may be loosed on earth, that he may be loosed in heaven; and dare he promise himself, after this life, some salvation, because he is merely called a Christian; nor tremble at that truthful thunder of the Lord's voice: *Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, will enter the kingdom of heaven; but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven, he will enter the kingdom of heaven*[^57]? What, does not the same apostle, to the Galatians, enumerating such things, conclude with the same ending? *Manifest are*, he says, *the works of the flesh, which are fornications, uncleannesses, luxuries, the service of idols, sorceries, enmities, contentions, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envyings, drunkennesses, carousings, and things like these; which I foretell to you, as I have foretold, that those who do such things will not possess the kingdom of God*[^58]. Let a man, therefore, judge himself in these things by his own will, while he can, and change his morals for the better: lest, when he can no longer, he be judged by the Lord even against his will. And when he himself has brought forth against himself the sentence of a most severe, but yet a medicinal medicine, let him come to the prelates, through whom the keys are ministered to him in the Church: and, as a son already beginning to be good, keeping the order of the maternal members, let him receive from those set over the Sacraments the measure of his satisfaction; so that, in offering the sacrifice of a contrite heart, devout and suppliant, he may yet do that which not only profits himself toward receiving salvation, but also others toward an example. So that, if his sin is not only in his own grave evil, but also in so great a scandal of others, and this seems expedient to the prelate for the benefit of the Church, let him not refuse to do penance in the knowledge of many, or even of the whole people, let him not resist, let him not add swelling, through shame, to a lethal and deadly blow. Let him always remember that *God resists the proud, but to the humble he gives grace*[^59]. For what is more unhappy, what more perverse, than not to blush over the very wound, which cannot be hidden, and to blush over its bandaging?

**4. 10.** Let no one suppose, brothers, that for this reason he ought to despise the counsel of this saving penance, because he perhaps notices and knows many to approach the Sacraments of the altar whose such crimes he is not ignorant of. For many are corrected, like Peter: many are tolerated, like Judas: many are unknown, until the Lord comes, who will enlighten the hidden things of darkness, and make manifest the thoughts of the heart[^60]. For very many are unwilling to accuse others, while through them they desire to excuse themselves. But very many good Christians for this reason keep silent, and suffer the sins of others which they know, because they are often deserted by proofs, and what they themselves know they cannot prove to the ecclesiastical judges. For although certain things are true; yet they are not easily to be believed by a judge, unless they are demonstrated by sure indications. But we cannot prohibit anyone from communion (although this prohibition is not yet deadly, but medicinal), unless either he has confessed of his own accord, or has been named and convicted in some judgment, whether secular or ecclesiastical. For who would dare to assume both to himself, that he himself be to anyone both accuser and judge? A rule of this kind the apostle Paul too is understood to have briefly conveyed in the same Letter to the Corinthians, when, mentioning certain such crimes, he gave the form of ecclesiastical judgment and all similar things. For he says: *I wrote to you in a letter not to associate with fornicators: not at all with the fornicators of this world, or the greedy, or the rapacious, or those serving idols; otherwise you would have had to go out of this world*. For men living in this world cannot but live with such people: nor can they win them for Christ, if they avoid their conversation and company. Whence the Lord too, eating with publicans and sinners: *The healthy*, he said, *have no need of a physician, but those who are ill. For I did not come to call the just, but sinners*[^61]. And therefore the Apostle continues, and adds: *But now I have written to you not to associate, if anyone called a brother is either a fornicator or serving idols, or greedy, or abusive, or a drunkard, or rapacious; with such a one not even to take food. For what is it to me to judge those who are outside? Do you not judge those who are within? But those who are outside, God will judge. Take away the evil from among yourselves*[^62]. By which words he shows well enough that the evil are to be taken away from the communion of the Church not rashly or in any way whatever, but through judgment: so that if through judgment they cannot be taken away, they should rather be tolerated, lest, by perversely avoiding the evil, anyone, himself departing from the Church, bind to Gehenna those whom he seems to flee. Because for this too examples are set before us in the holy Scriptures, as in the harvest, that the chaff be borne until the final winnowing[^63]; as within those nets, where the good fish are tolerated with an even mind along with the bad until the separation which will be on the shore, that is, at the end of the age[^64]. For not contrary to this passage is that which the Apostle says in another place: *Who are you who judge another's servant? To his own Lord he stands or falls*[^65]. For he did not wish a man to be judged by a man on the judgment of suspicion, or even by an extraordinary usurped judgment, but rather according to the law of God, according to the order of the Church, whether one confessed of his own accord, or was accused and convicted. Otherwise why did he say: *If anyone called a brother is either a fornicator, or serving idols*, and the rest; unless because he wished that naming to be understood which is made against anyone, when a sentence is brought forth in judicial order and with integrity? For if a naming suffices, many innocent persons must be condemned, because they are often falsely named in some crime.

**4. 11.** Let those, then, whom we admonish to do penance, not seek for themselves companions in punishment; nor let them rejoice that they find more. For they will not therefore burn the less, because they will burn with many. For this is not the sure counsel of health, but the empty solace of malevolence. Or do they perhaps attend to many even in the ecclesiastical honors of prelates and ministers who do not live in accordance with the sermons and Sacraments which through them are ministered to the peoples? O wretched men, who, by looking at these, forget Christ! who both foretold so long beforehand that the law of God should rather be obeyed, than that those should seem worthy of imitation who do not do what they say[^66]; and, tolerating his betrayer to the end, sent him even to evangelize with the others. But these are as absurd and preposterous and wretched, who choose to imitate the evil morals of their superiors rather than to keep the precepts of the Lord preached through them; as if some traveler should think he must remain on the road, when he sees the stone milestones full of letters teaching the way, and not walking. For why does he not rather, if he desires to arrive, look at and follow such companions as both demonstrate the way well, and walk in it perseveringly and eagerly? But if these are lacking, or rather appear less—for they cannot be lacking: for men do not so seek with eager love what they may preach for imitation, as they seek with suspicious iniquity what they may murmur at for deception, partly by not finding the good, while they themselves are evil; partly by fearing to find them, while they always wish to be evil. But yet let us grant that men worthy of imitation do not appear now. Whoever you are who think this, behold the Lord with your mind, who was made man, to teach man to live. If Christ dwells in the inner man through faith in your heart, and you recall that which John says: *He who says he abides in Christ, ought himself also to walk as he walked*[^67]; so neither will there be lacking to you one to follow, and when another sees you, he will cease to complain of a lack of good men. For if you do not know what it is to live rightly, learn the divine precepts. For perhaps many live rightly; but for this reason no one seems to you to live rightly, because you are ignorant of what it is to live rightly. But if you know, do what you know; that you both may have what you seek, and may show to others what they may imitate. Attend with your mind to Christ, attend to the Apostles, the last of whom is the one who says: *Be imitators of me, as I too am of Christ*[^68]. Attend with your mind to so many thousands of martyrs. For why does it delight you to celebrate their birthdays with base feasts, and not delight you to follow their life with honorable morals? There you will see not only men, but also women, finally boys and girls, neither deceived by imprudence, nor perverted by iniquity, nor broken by fear of danger, nor corrupted by love of the world. So, when you find nothing to excuse you, not only the inevitable rightness of the precepts, but also the innumerable multitude of examples will surround you.

**5. 12.** But concerning the usefulness and wholesomeness of penance, that we may at length accomplish what we set out to do: if, now despairing of health, you add sins to sins, as it is written, *The sinner, when he has come into the depth of evils, despises*[^69]: do not despise, do not despair; cry out even from the depth to the Lord and say to him: *From the depths I have cried to you, O Lord; Lord, hear my voice. Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my supplication. If you observe iniquities, O Lord; Lord, who will endure? For with you is propitiation*[^70]. From such a depth the Ninevites cried out, and found this propitiation; and the prophet's threat was more easily emptied out than the humiliation of penance[^71]. Here perhaps you say: But I have already been baptized in Christ, by whom all my past sins were forgiven me: I have become too cheap, repeating my ways, and a dog horrible to the eyes of God, returned to my own vomit. Where shall I go from his spirit? and from his face where shall I flee? Where, brother, except to his mercy by repenting, whose power you had despised by sinning? For no one rightly flees from him, except to him, from his severity to his goodness. For what place will receive you fleeing, where his presence will not find you? If you ascend into heaven, there he is: if you descend into hell, he is present. Gather, then, your wings straight, and dwell in hope in the farthest parts of this world: for even there his hand will lead you, and his right hand will bring you through[^72]. For whatever you have done, whatever you have sinned; you are still in this life, from which God would altogether take you away, if he did not wish to heal you. Why, then, are you ignorant that the patience of God leads you to penance[^73]? For he who by crying out did not persuade you not to depart, by sparing cries out for you to return. Behold King David: he too had already, surely, received the Sacraments of that time, he was already, surely, circumcised, which our fathers held in place of Baptism. For to this end the Apostle says that holy Abraham received the seal of the justice of faith[^74]. He was also already anointed with that venerable anointing, by which was prefigured the royal priesthood of the Church. But suddenly made guilty of both adultery and murder, yet not in vain, repenting of so monstrous and abrupt a depth of crime, he cried out to the Lord, saying: *Turn your face away from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities*[^75]. By what merit at last, except because he likewise says: *I acknowledge my iniquity, and my sin is ever before me*[^76]? But what did he offer to the Lord by which to make him propitious to himself? *For if you had wished*, he says, *a sacrifice, I would surely have given it; in holocausts you will not delight. A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God does not despise*[^77]. Not only, then, did he devoutly offer, but also, by saying these things, he showed what ought to be offered. For it does not suffice to change one's morals for the better, and to depart from evil deeds; unless also for those things which have been done, satisfaction is made to God through the grief of penance, through the groan of humility, through the sacrifice of a contrite heart, with alms cooperating. *For blessed are the merciful, for God will have mercy on them*[^78]. For it was not said only that we should abstain from sins: *But also for past things*, he says, *beseech the Lord, that they may be forgiven you*[^79]. And Peter was already faithful, already in Christ and had baptized others. Behold, then, Peter presuming and accused, fearing and wounded, weeping and healed. Already too, after the coming of the Holy Spirit from heaven, a certain Simon wished to buy that same Holy Spirit with money, contriving a most wicked and impious traffic, already baptized in Christ: and yet, rebuked by Peter himself, he received the counsel of penance[^80]. The apostle Paul too says, who surely sent Letters to the faithful: *Lest again, when I come to you, my God humble me, and I mourn over many of those who sinned before, and did not do penance over the uncleanness and luxury and fornication which they committed*[^81]. There surround us, then, both the precepts of acting rightly, and the examples not only of those acting rightly, but also of those repenting in order to receive the salvation which had been lost by sinning. But suppose it uncertain whether God forgives. What does he lose, in supplicating God, who did not doubt to lose salvation, in offending God? For who is certain that even an emperor forgives? And yet money is poured out, seas are crossed, the uncertainties of storms are undergone; and almost, that death may be avoided, death itself is undertaken. Then supplication is made by men to a man: these things are done without doubting, when it is doubtful to what end they come out. And yet the keys of the Church are more certain than the hearts of kings: by which keys whatever is loosed on earth, is promised to be loosed in heaven also[^82]. And far more honorable is the humility by which each one humbles himself to the Church of God: both a lesser labor is imposed, and with no danger of temporal death is eternal death avoided.
