# Sermon 309: On the Feast of Cyprian the Martyr

**1. 1.** So gracious and religious a solemnity, by which we celebrate the passion of the blessed Martyr, demands of your ears and hearts the sermon we owe. Without doubt the Church was then sad, not at the loss of one falling, but at the longing for one departing; ever desiring to see present so good a ruler and teacher. But those whom the anxiety of the contest had afflicted, the crown of the victor consoled. And now, not only without any sadness, but even with immense gladness, we recall by reading and loving all that was then done; and on this day it is now granted to rejoice, not to fear. For we do not dread him coming terribly, but await him returning cheerfully. It is fitting, then, with exultation to recall as past that whole passion of the most faithful and most strong and most glorious Martyr, which the brethren then sustained with anxiety as still to come.

**1. 2.** First, then, that for the faith of confessing Christ he was sent into exile at Curubis—it was not holy Cyprian who was harmed, but much was bestowed on that city. For where would he be sent, where that one would not be, on account of whose testimony he was being sent? Christ, therefore, who says: *Behold, I am with you all days, even to the consummation of the age*[^1], received his own member in every place, wherever the fury of the enemy drove it. O foolish unbelief of the persecutor! If you seek the exile to which a Christian may be ordered to go; first, if you can, find from where Christ may be forced to go out. You think to shut the man of God out from his homeland into a foreign one—the man who in Christ is nowhere an exile, in the flesh everywhere a pilgrim. But now it delights us to consider and recall, after that which Cyprian had not felt, but the enemy thought, was exile, what followed in the order of his passion. For when the holy martyr Cyprian, chosen by God, had returned from the city of Curubis, to which he had been sent in exile by the order of the proconsul Aspasius Paternus, he remained in his gardens: and from there he daily expected men to come to him, as had been shown to him.

**2. 3.** What further could the persecutor's onslaught rage against a heart ever prepared, strengthened too by the coming revelation of the Lord? For when would he desert one who suffers patiently, whom he did not allow to be taken unawares not knowing? Now, then, as to the fact that two men were sent to present him for the passion, who even lifted him with themselves into the chariot and set him in the middle; this too was of divine admonition, that, rejoicing, he might recall that he belonged to the body of him who was reckoned among the wicked. For Christ, hung on the wood between two thieves, was offered as an example of patience. But Cyprian, between two officers, carried by chariot to the passion, was following the footsteps of Christ.

**2. 4.** And that, when he had been deferred to another day and was among his guards, and when the multitude of brothers and sisters, gathering there, kept vigil before the doors, he ordered the girls to be guarded—with how great attention is this to be considered? with how great praise to be proclaimed? with how great heralding to be commended? With the death of his body near, the pastoral vigilance in the pastor's soul was not dying; and the care of guarding the Lord's flock was held with a sober mind even to the last day of this life: nor did the hand of the bloody executioner, now very near, shake off from his soul the diligence of a most faithful steward. So he thought of being a future martyr, that he did not forget that he was a bishop: caring more how he might render to the prince of the shepherds an account of the sheep committed to him, than what he might answer the unbelieving proconsul concerning his own faith. For he loved the one who had said to Peter: *Do you love me? Feed my sheep*[^2]. And he was feeding his sheep, for whom, imitating him, he was preparing to shed his blood. He ordered the girls to be guarded, knowing that he had not only a simple Lord, but also a wily adversary. And so against the lion openly roaring in confession he armed his manly breast, against the wolf lying in wait for the flock he fortified the female sex.

**3. 5.** So truly does he take counsel for himself who thinks of God as judge, before whom each will plead the cause of the life he has led and of the office laid upon him by God: where every man receives, as the Apostle testifies, *the things he has done through the body, whether good or evil*[^3]. So does he take counsel for himself who, living by faith, and taking care lest he be taken unawares by the last day, counts every day his last, and so leads conduct pleasing to God even to the last day. So did blessed Cyprian, both a most merciful bishop and a most faithful martyr, take counsel for himself, not as the deceitful tongue of the devil, through the mouth of the impious judge possessed by him, seemed to admonish him, saying: "Take counsel for yourself." For when he saw his immovable mind, when he said to him: "The princes have ordered you to perform the rites"; and Cyprian answered: "I will not do it": he added and said: "Take counsel for yourself." This is the deceitful tongue of the devil: even if not of this man who did not know what he was saying, yet of the one who was speaking through him. For the proconsul was speaking, not so much according to the ruling men, whose commands laid upon him he was boasting of, as according to the prince of the power of the air, of whom the Apostle says: *Who works in the children of disobedience*[^4]: whom Cyprian knew to be working through this man's tongue too, which the man himself did not know. Cyprian knew, I say, when he heard from the proconsul: "Take counsel for yourself," that what flesh and blood said stupidly, this the devil said deceitfully: and he beheld in one work two: the one with his eyes, the other by faith. This one did not want him to die, that one did not want him to be crowned: accordingly he was placid toward this one, cautious toward that one; to this one he answered openly, that one he conquered secretly.

**4. 6.** "Do," he said, "what has been commanded you: in so just a matter there is no deliberation." For that one had said: "Take counsel for yourself." To this it was answered: "In so just a matter there is no deliberation." For he takes counsel who either imparts or seeks advice. But the proconsul did not want to receive counsel from Cyprian, but rather was admonishing him to receive it from him. But Cyprian: "In so just a matter," he says, "there is no deliberation." I do not yet take counsel, because I do not yet doubt: for justice itself has taken away doubt from me. But the righteous man, that he may die secure in the flesh, lives certain in the faith. Many martyrs had preceded Cyprian, whom he had kindled by his most ardent exhortations to conquer the devil; and it was surely just that those whom, speaking truly, he had sent ahead, he should, suffering, intrepidly follow: therefore "in so just a matter there is no deliberation." What shall we say to these things? at what shall we exult here? With so great a conception of joys, into what does our heart and our mouth break forth, except into that very last word of the venerable Martyr? For when Galerius Maximus had read the decree from the document: "It is my pleasure that Tascius Cyprian be punished by the sword," he answered: "Thanks be to God." Having, then, the memory of the present place concerning so great a matter, the festivity of a most solemn day, the setting forth of a most healthful example, let us too say with all our marrow: Thanks be to God.

[^2]: John 21:17
