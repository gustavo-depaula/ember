# Sermon 288: On the Birthday of John the Baptist

**(*On Voice and Word*)**

**1.** Today’s feast, returning each year, renews the memory that the Lord’s forerunner was born wondrously before the wondrous one. It is especially fitting today for us to consider and praise his birth. For an annual day has been dedicated to this miracle precisely so that forgetfulness may not erase God’s benefits and the mighty deeds of the Most High from our hearts. John, then, was the Lord’s herald, sent before him but made through him. For *all things* *were made through him, and without him nothing was made.* A man was sent before God made man, acknowledging his Lord, announcing his Creator, discerning with his mind the one already present on earth and pointing him out with his finger. For these are his words as he points out the Lord and bears witness: *Behold the Lamb of God; behold him who takes away the sin of the world*. Fittingly, then, a barren woman bore the herald, and a virgin the judge. In John’s mother, barrenness received fruitfulness; in Christ’s mother, fruitfulness did not impair virginity. If your patience, your quiet eagerness, and your attentive silence give me the opportunity, with the Lord’s help, to say what he grants me to say, your attention will undoubtedly bear fruit and our effort be worthwhile, as I bring to your ears and hearts something belonging to a great mystery.

**2.** There were prophets before John—many, great, and holy, worthy of God, filled with God, foretelling the Savior and bearing witness to the truth. Yet of none of them could it be said what was said of John: *Among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist*. What, then, is the meaning of this greatness sent before the great one? It bears witness to great humility. For John was so great that he could be thought the Christ. John could have exploited people’s mistake without having to labor to persuade them that he was the Christ, because those who heard and saw him had already thought this without his saying it. He did not need to sow the error, only to confirm it. But as the humble friend of the bridegroom, jealous for the bridegroom, not adulterously putting himself in the bridegroom’s place, he bears witness to his friend and commends to the bride the one who truly was the bridegroom. So that he may be loved in him, he hates being loved instead of him. *The one who has,* he says, *the bride is the bridegroom.* And as though you asked, “What about you?” he says, *But the friend* *of the bridegroom stands and hears him, and rejoices greatly at the bridegroom’s voice*. *He stands and hears:* the disciple hears the teacher; because he hears, he stands, for if he does not hear, he falls. John’s greatness is especially commended by this: although he could have been thought the Christ, he preferred to bear witness to Christ, commend him, and humble himself, rather than be taken for him and deceive himself. Rightly he was called more than a prophet. For the Lord himself speaks this way of the prophets who preceded his coming: *Many prophets and righteous people wished to see what you see, and did not see it*. Those who were filled with God’s Spirit to announce the coming Christ longed, if it could be so, to see him present on earth. That is why Simeon was kept from departing this world until he saw, newly born, the one through whom the world was established. He indeed saw the Word of God as an infant in the flesh, but the one who was already the angels’ teacher with the Father was not yet teaching or openly assuming the teacher’s role. Simeon saw him, then, but as an infant; John saw him already preaching and choosing disciples. Where? At the River Jordan. For Christ’s teaching began there. There Christ’s future baptism was commended, because he received the baptism that went before it, prepared the way, and said, *Prepare the way for the Lord; make his paths straight*. For the Lord wished to be baptized by his servant, so that those baptized by the Lord might see what they receive. Thus he began from the place which prophecy had fittingly indicated beforehand: *He will rule from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*. At that very river, from which Christ began to rule, John saw Christ, recognized him, and bore witness. He humbled himself before the great one, so that in his humility he might be exalted by the great one. And he called himself the bridegroom’s friend. What kind of friend? Perhaps an equal? Certainly not: far beneath him. How far? *I am not worthy,* he says, *to untie the strap of his sandal*. This prophet—indeed, more than a prophet—deserved to be foretold by a prophet. For Isaiah said of him what was read to us today: *The voice of one crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord; make his paths straight. Every valley will be filled, and every mountain and hill brought low; crooked places will become straight and rough places level roads, and all flesh will see God’s salvation. Cry out! What shall I cry out? All flesh is grass, and all its glory like the flower of grass. The grass has withered, the flower has fallen; but the word of the Lord remains forever*. Let Your Charity pay attention. When John was asked who he was, whether he was the Christ, Elijah, or the prophet, he said, *I am not* *the Christ, nor Elijah, nor the prophet.* And they said, *Who, then, are you? I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness.* He called himself a voice. You have John as the voice. What do you have Christ as, except the Word? The voice is sent ahead so that the Word may afterward be understood. And what kind of Word? Hear the one who shows him to you clearly: *In the beginning,* he says, *was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him nothing was made*. If all things, then John too. Why should we marvel if the Word made a voice for himself? See, see them both at the river, both voice and Word: John the voice, Christ the Word.

**3.** Let us ask what the difference is between a voice and a word. Let us ask attentively: it is no small matter and requires no small attention. The Lord will grant that I do not grow weary in explaining, nor you in listening. Here are two things, voice and word. What is a voice? What is a word? What are they? Hear something you can verify in yourselves, and, questioning yourselves, answer from your own experience. A word is not called a word unless it conveys a meaningful thought. A voice, however, even if it merely sounds and makes a meaningless noise, like the sound of someone crying out rather than speaking, can be called a voice but cannot be called a word. Someone groaned: it is a voice. He wailed: it is a voice. It is an unformed sound, carrying or conveying noise to the ears without any intelligible meaning. A word, however, is not called a word unless it signifies something, unless it carries one thing to the ears and conveys another to the mind. Thus, as I was saying, if you cry out, that is a voice; if you say “human being,” that is a word, and likewise if you say “animal,” “God,” “world,” or something else. For all these utterances I have spoken signify something; they are not empty, making a sound but teaching nothing. If, then, you have now distinguished between a voice and a word, hear something to marvel at in these two, John and Christ. A word has very great value even without a voice; a voice is empty without a word. Let us give an account and, if we can, explain what we have proposed. Suppose you wanted to say something. The very thing you want to say has already been conceived in your heart: it is held in memory, made ready by the will, alive in the understanding. And the very thing you want to say does not belong to any language. The thing itself which you want to say, which has been conceived in your heart, does not belong to any language—not Greek, Latin, Punic, Hebrew, or the language of any nation. It is simply a thing conceived in the heart, ready to come forth. Thus, as I said, there is a certain thing, a certain thought, an intelligible meaning conceived in the heart, ready to come forth and be conveyed to the listener. As it is known to the person in whose heart it is, it is a word, already known to the speaker but not yet to the hearer. Here, then, is the word, already formed, already complete, remaining in the heart. It seeks to come forth to be spoken to the hearer. The person who has conceived the word he is to say, and knows that word within his own heart, considers whom he is going to address. I will speak in Christ’s name to ears instructed in the Church, and I dare now to convey something a little more subtle to people who are not beginners. Let Your Charity therefore pay attention. See the word conceived in the heart: it seeks to come forth to be spoken, and considers whom it is to be spoken to. Does it find a Greek? It seeks a Greek voice through which to come forth to the Greek. Does it find a Latin speaker? It seeks a Latin voice through which to come forth to the Latin speaker. Does it find a Punic speaker? It seeks a Punic voice through which to come forth to the Punic speaker. Remove the diversity of hearers, and that word conceived in the heart is neither Greek nor Latin nor Punic, nor does it belong to any language. It seeks a voice suited to the listener who is present, through which it may come forth. Now, brothers and sisters, to put before you something you can understand, I have conceived in my heart that I will say “God.” What I have conceived in my heart is something great. For God is not the two syllables of “Deus”; God is not that short sound. I want to say “God,” and consider whom I am addressing. Is he a Latin speaker? I say “Deum.” Is he a Greek? I say “Θεόν.” To the Latin speaker I say “Deum”; to the Greek I say “Θεόν.” The sound of “Deum” differs from “Θεόν”: the letters here are different from the letters there. But in my heart, in what I want to say, in what I am thinking, there is no difference of letters, no variation in the sound of syllables: it is what it is. One voice was employed to announce it to a Latin speaker, another to a Greek. If I wished to announce it to a Punic speaker, I would use another; to a Hebrew, another; to an Egyptian, another; to an Indian, another. How many voices would the word of the heart produce as the people changed, without any change or variation in itself? It goes to a Latin speaker in a Latin voice, to a Greek in Greek, to a Hebrew in Hebrew. It reaches the hearer without leaving the speaker. For do I lose what I produce in another by speaking? That sound, employed as an intermediary, has brought something into being in you without its departing from me. I was already thinking of God; you had not yet heard my voice. On hearing it, you too began to possess what I was thinking, but I did not lose what I possessed. Thus within me, as in the innermost chamber of my heart, as in the sanctuary of my mind, the word preceded my voice. The voice had not yet sounded in my mouth, and the word was already in my heart. But to bring to you what I have conceived in my heart, it seeks the service of the voice.

**4.** If, with the help of your attention and prayers, I can say what I wish, I think whoever understands will rejoice. Whoever does not understand should pardon a man who is laboring and pray to God who shows mercy. For what I say also comes from him. From the same source as my speaking, what I am to say is present in my heart; but the services of the voice struggle to reach your ears. What, then, brothers and sisters? What, then? Surely you have paid attention; surely you now understand that the word was in my heart before it employed a voice through which to come forth to your ears. I think everyone can understand this, because what happens to me happens to everyone who speaks. Look: I already know what I want to say; I hold it in my heart and seek the service of a voice. Before the voice sounds in my mouth, the word is already held in my heart. Thus the word preceded my voice; in me the word comes first and the voice afterward. But to enable you to understand, the voice comes to your ear first, so that the word may be conveyed to your mind. For you could not know what was in me before the voice unless it came to be in you after the voice. Therefore, if John is the voice and Christ the Word, Christ is before John, but with God; Christ is after John, but among us. A great mystery, brothers and sisters! Pay attention; take in the greatness of the matter again and again. For your understanding delights me and makes me bolder with you, with the help of him whom I preach—I, so little, preaching one so great; I, a human being of whatever kind, preaching God the Word. With his help, then, I grow bolder with you; and having first given this explanation of the distinction between voice and word, I set forth what follows. John represented the voice in this mystery, for he was not the only voice. Every human being who announces the Word is a voice of the Word. For what the sound of our mouth is to the word we carry in our heart, every devout soul that preaches is to that Word of whom it was said, *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God*. How many words—or rather, how many voices—a word conceived in the heart produces! How many preachers the Word, remaining with the Father, produced! He sent patriarchs, he sent prophets, he sent so many and such great heralds of himself. The abiding Word sent voices; and after the many voices sent ahead, the one Word himself came, as if in his own vehicle, in his own voice, in his own flesh. Gather, then, as into one, all the voices that preceded the Word, and place them all in John’s person. He bore the mystery of them all; he alone was the sacred and mystical representation of them all. Therefore he was especially called the voice, as the sign and mystery of all the voices.

**5.** Now consider, then, the meaning of *He must increase, but I must decrease.* Pay attention, if I can express it—if I am capable, I will not say of conveying it, but at least of thinking it through: in what way, for what reason, with what intention and purpose, in accordance with the distinction I have made between voice and word, the voice himself, John himself, said, *He must increase, but I must decrease*. O great and wonderful mystery! Consider the one representing the voice, in whose person the mysteries of all voices were contained, saying of the person of the Word, *He must increase, but I must decrease.* Why? Pay attention. The Apostle says, *We know in part and we prophesy in part; but when what is perfect has come, what is partial will be done away with*. What is perfect? *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.* This is perfect. What is perfect? Let the Apostle Paul also say it: *Who, being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal to God*. This one equal to God the Father, this Word of God with God, through whom all things were made, we will see as he is—but at the end. For now, as the Evangelist John says, *Beloved, we are God’s children, and what we shall be has not yet appeared. Beloved, we know that when he appears, we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is*. This vision is promised to us; for this vision we are instructed; for this vision we cleanse our hearts. For he says, *Blessed* *are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*. He showed his flesh, showed it to servants, but showed the form of a servant. As his own voice among the many voices he had sent ahead, he also showed his own flesh. The Father was being sought as though the Son himself were already seen as he is, when the Son, equal to the Father, was speaking to servants in the form of a servant. *Lord,* Philip said to him, *show us the Father, and it is enough for us.* He was seeking the goal of his entire striving, the endpoint of his progress, where, once he arrived, he would seek nothing further. *Show us,* he said, *the Father, and it is enough for us.* Good, Philip, good! You understand very well that the Father is enough for you. What does *enough* mean? You will seek nothing beyond him; he will fill you, satisfy you, perfect you. But consider whether this one whom you hear may also be enough for you. Is he enough alone, or together with the Father? But how can he be alone, when he never departs from the Father? Let him therefore answer Philip, who wishes to see: *Have I been with you so long, and have you not known me? Philip, whoever has seen me has also seen the Father*. What does *Philip, whoever has seen me has also seen the Father* mean, except “You have not seen me, and that is why you seek the Father”? *Philip, whoever has seen me has also seen the Father.* Yet you see me and do not see me. For you do not see me as the one who made you; you see what I became for your sake. *Whoever has seen me,* he says, *has also seen the Father.* How, except because *in the form of God he did not consider it robbery to be equal to God*? What, then, was Philip seeing? That *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men and found in appearance as a man*. Philip was seeing this, the form of a servant, destined to be set free to behold the form of God. Thus John represents all voices; Christ represents the Word. All voices must decrease as we advance toward seeing Christ. For the more you advance toward seeing wisdom, the less you need a voice. There is a voice in the prophets, a voice in the apostles, a voice in the Psalms, a voice in the Gospel. Let that reality come: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.* When we see him as he is, will the Gospel be read there? Will we hear prophecies? Will we read the apostles’ letters? Why not? Because voices fall away as the word increases, because *he must increase, but I must decrease.* Indeed, the Word in himself neither increases nor decreases. But he is said to increase in us when, by making progress, we grow toward him—just as light increases in our eyes when it is seen more fully as our sight grows stronger, though it was certainly seen less fully when our sight was weak. It was less to sick eyes and is greater to healthy eyes, although in itself it neither diminished before nor increased afterward. Thus the service of the voice decreases as the mind progresses toward the Word. In this way Christ must increase and John decrease. Their sufferings indicate this: John was diminished by being beheaded; Christ was exalted, increasing, as it were, on the cross. Their birthdays indicate this too: from John’s birthday the days begin to shorten, while from Christ’s birthday they begin to lengthen again.
