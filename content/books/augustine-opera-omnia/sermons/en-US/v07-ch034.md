# Sermon 259: On the Lord's Day of the Octave of Easter

**1.** Today's day is for us a great sacred sign of perpetual happiness. For as this day is not going to pass, so the life which this day signifies is not going to pass. And so, brothers, we exhort and beseech you in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom our sins have been forgiven, who willed his own blood to be our price, who condescended to make us, unworthy even to be called his servants, brothers, that all your purpose—why you are Christians, and bear his name on your forehead and in your heart—be directed to nothing but that life which we will have with the angels: where there is perpetual rest, everlasting gladness, unfailing blessedness, no disturbance, no sadness, no death. Which life none can know, except those who experience it: but they cannot experience it, except those who believe. For if you demand that we show you what God promises you, we cannot. But you heard how the Gospel of John concludes: *Blessed are those who do not see, and believe*[^1]. Both you wish to see, and I. Let us alike believe, and together we will see. Let us not be hard against the word of God. For is it fitting, brothers, that Christ should now come down from heaven, and show us his scars? Therefore he condescended to show them to that unbeliever, that he might rebuke the doubting, and instruct those about to believe.

**2.** This eighth day, then, signifies the new life at the end of the age: the seventh, the future rest of the saints on this earth. For the Lord will reign on earth with his saints, as the Scriptures say, and will have here a Church which no bad one will enter, separated and purged from all contagion of wickedness; which the one hundred fifty-three fish signify, about which we have already discoursed at some time, as far as I remember. For the Church will here first appear in great brightness and dignity and righteousness. There it will not be allowed to deceive, to lie, to hide a wolf under a sheep's skin. *For the Lord will come*, as it is written, *and will illumine the hidden things of darkness and will make manifest the thoughts of hearts: and then there will be praise for each from God*[^2]. The wicked, then, will not be there: for they will already be separated. Then, as it were a purged mass, the multitude of saints will appear, as on a threshing floor, and so will be sent into the heavenly barn of immortality. For as grain is first cleansed where it is threshed; and the place where the grains endured the threshing, that they might be cleansed of chaff, is adorned with the dignity of the cleansed mass. For we see on the threshing floor, after the winnowing, a heap of chaff on one side, and a heap of grain on the other. But to what the chaff is destined, we know; and how the grains make gladness for the farmers. As, then, the grain first appears on the threshing floor, separated from the chaff, and when, after such great labors, that heap which lay hidden in the chaff, which was not seen when it was threshed, has made joy, it is then sent into the barn, and kept in secret: so in this age, you see how this threshing floor is threshed, but the chaff is so mingled with the grain that it is hard to distinguish: because it has not yet been winnowed. So, then, after the winnowing of the day of judgment, the mass of saints will appear, shining with dignity, mighty in merits, and bearing before it the mercy of its liberator. And this will be the seventh day. As though the first day in the whole age be the time from Adam to Noah; the second, from Noah to Abraham, and as the Gospel of Matthew now divides, the third from Abraham to David; the fourth, from David to the migration to Babylon; the fifth, from the migration to the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. From the Lord's coming, then, the sixth is being passed; we are in the sixth day. And therefore, as man was formed in Genesis on the sixth day in the image of God, so in this time too, as it were the sixth day of the whole age, we are renewed in Baptism, that we may receive the image of our Maker. But when this sixth day has passed, rest will come after that winnowing, and the saints and just of God will keep Sabbath. But after the seventh, when in the threshing floor the dignity, splendor, and merit of the harvest of the saints has appeared, we will go into that life and into that rest, of which it was said: *That eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him*[^3]. Then, as it were, there is a return to the head. For as, when these seven days have been completed, the eighth is the same as the first: so after the ages of the passing age have been ended and completed, seven of them, we will return to that immortality and blessedness from which man fell. And therefore the octaves complete the sacred signs of the infants. Hence too the number seven itself, multiplied seven times, makes forty-nine; and with one added, as it were there is a return to the head, and they become fifty: which number is celebrated by us in a mystery up to Pentecost. Which also, according to that division of the number forty, to which is added as a reward the denarius, by a different reasoning appears the same again. For both reasonings come back to the same number fifty. Which, multiplied by three because of the mystery of the Trinity, makes one hundred fifty. With the number three itself added, as a witness and indicator of the tripling and of the Trinity, we understand the Church in those one hundred fifty-three fish.

**3.** But meanwhile now, until we come to that rest, in this time in which we labor, and are in the night, as long as we do not see what we hope for, and make our way in the desert, until we come to the heavenly Jerusalem, as to a land of promise flowing with milk and honey: now, then, since temptations do not cease, let us do good works. Let the medicine always be at hand, to be applied as it were to almost daily wounds. But the medicine is in the good works of mercy. For if you wish to obtain the mercy of God, be merciful. If you deny to a man, when you are a man, humanity; God will deny to you divinity too, that is, the incorruption of immortality, by which he makes us gods. For God needs nothing from you: but you need from God. He asks nothing from you, that he may be blessed: but you, unless you receive from him, cannot be blessed. What do you receive from him? I do not know whether you would dare to complain if you received from him, who established all things, something that he established most excellent. But he gives you, not something from the things he established; but himself to you to enjoy, himself the Creator of all things. For what among the things made by him can be more beautiful and better than the one who made them? And how will he give himself to you? as it were by your merits? If you ask what you have deserved, attend to your sins; hear the verdict of God passed on the transgressing man: *You are earth, and into the earth you will go*[^4]. Since the threat went before, when the precept was given: *On the day that you touch it, you will die the death*[^5]. If you seek the merit of sins, what occurs except punishment? Forget, then, your merits, lest they cause terror in your heart: or rather, do not forget them, lest through pride you repel mercy. We commend ourselves, brothers, to God by works of mercy. *Confess to the Lord, for he is good, for his mercy endures forever*[^6]. Confess, because God has mercy, and wishes to forgive the sins of those who confess. But offer him a sacrifice. Have mercy on a man, O man, and God will have mercy on you. You a man, and another a man, two wretched ones. But God is not wretched, but merciful. But if a wretched man does not have mercy on a wretched man, how does he demand mercy from him who will never be wretched? See what I say, brothers. Whoever is cruel toward a shipwrecked man, for example, is cruel only so long as shipwreck does not befall him. But if it befalls him, recalling his former life, when he sees a shipwrecked man, a similar misery once strikes him; and whom the fellowship of humanity could not bend to mercy, the fellowship of calamity bends. How quickly he has mercy on a slave who once was a slave. How quickly he who was a hired man grieves for a hired man cheated of his wage. How deeply he sympathizes with a man bitterly mourning his son, who once mourned something of the kind. Therefore however great a hardness of the human heart the likeness of misery dissolves. If, then, you, who either have been wretched, or fear lest you be wretched (for as long as you live here, you ought both to fear what you were not, and to remember what you were, and to consider what you are): set, then, both in the memory of past miseries, and in the fear of future ones, and in the affliction of present ones, you do not have mercy on a man calamitous and needing your help, and you wait for him to have mercy on you, whom misery never touches? And you do not give from what you have received from God, and you want God to give to you from what he did not receive from you?

**4.** All of you, my brothers, who are going to your homes, and after this will scarcely see us, except for some solemnity, work mercy, because sins abound. There is no other rest, no other way, by which we come to God, by which we are restored, by which we are reconciled to him whom we most dangerously offended. We are going to come into his sight: there let our works speak for us; and let them so speak that they overcome our offenses. For whatever is the greater, this will prevail, either to punishment, if the sins have deserved it; or to rest, if good works. But mercy is twofold in the Church: one under that heading by which no one spends money, no one even labor; the other which demands of us either the service of work, or the expenditure of money. That which demands of us no outlay or labor is established in the soul, that you forgive him who has sinned against you. For paying out this alms your treasury is in your heart: there you unfold yourself before God. It is not said to you: Bring out your purse, open your chest, unseal your barn; nor is this said to you: Come, walk, run, hasten, intercede, speak, visit, labor. Standing in one place you have cast from your breast the resentment you held against your brother, you have done mercy, with no expense, with no labor, by goodness alone, by the thought of mercy alone. For if we should say: Pay out your goods to the poor; we would seem hard. Surely we are now at least gentle and easy, when we say: Spend what you will lose nothing of, forgive that it may be forgiven you. Yet let us say this too, Give and it will be given to you. The Lord joined these in the precept, and expressed these two kinds of mercy. *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you*: this is the mercy of the one forgiving. *Give, and it will be given to you*[^7]: this is the mercy of the one giving. See whether God does not give us more. You forgive a man, in that a man, you a man, has injured you: God forgives you, in that you, a man, have offended God. For is this—to injure a man—the same as to offend God? Therefore he gave you more: because you forgave that in which a man was injured; he forgives that in which God was offended. Attend to the other mercy of distribution. You give bread, he gives salvation; you give to the thirsty a cup of some liquid, he gives you the cup of his wisdom. Surely these are not even to be compared, what you give, and what you receive? See how one should lend at interest. If anyone wishes to be a lender, we by no means forbid it: but let him lend to the one who will not be poor by paying back more and greater things, and to whom belongs even this very thing, of whatever kind, that you give him, so that you may receive more and better.

**5.** This too I advise your Holiness, that you may know that he does a double mercy who gives something to the poor in such a way that he himself pays it out. For there ought to be not only the kindness of the giver, but also the humility of the one ministering. Somehow, my brothers, the soul of him who holds out to the poor man as it were sympathizes with the common humanity and weakness, when the hand of the one who has is placed into the hand of the one in need. Although the one gives, the other receives, the minister and the one ministered to are joined. For it is not calamity that joins us, but humility. Your abundance will be for you, if it please the Lord, and for your sons. But of this earthly abundance there is no mention, which you see liable to so many mischances. A treasure lies quiet in the house, it does not allow its owner to be quiet. The robber is feared, the burglar is feared, the unfaithful servant is feared, the bad and powerful neighbor is feared. The more is held, the more is feared. But if you pay out to God in the poor, you do not lose it, and you are made secure, because God himself keeps it for you in heaven, who also gives you what is necessary on earth. Or do you perhaps fear lest Christ lose what you have entrusted to him? Does not each one choose a faithful steward from his household, to whom he commits his money? Who, even if he has it in his power not to take it away, yet does not have it in his power not to lose it. What is more ample than the faith of Christ? what more divine than omnipotence? He can neither take anything away from you, because he himself gave it to you in hope of what you would give him: nor lose anything, because the Almighty holds all things. You refresh the bowels of the poor, when you make love-feasts. Because we seem to minister, and our things are given, and given through us; and yet those things are given which God gave us. It is good, brothers, that you distribute even with your own hand: it is very pleasing to God. He himself receives, and will give to you, who gave you, before he owed it, what you would give. The service of ministering ought to be joined to the office of paying out. Since you are allowed to have two rewards, why do you lose one? But whoever is less able to give to all, let him give to the poor according to his strength, with cheerfulness. *For God loves a cheerful giver*[^8]. The kingdom of heaven is set forth to be bought at any price. There is no reason for anyone who has two pennies to say that he is not able to buy it. At so much that evangelical widow bought it.

**6.** The festal days have been completed, now will come those of meetings, exactions, lawsuits: see how you live in these, my brothers. From the leisure of these days you ought to conceive gentleness, not meditate plans of quarrels. For there are men who took leisure during these days for this reason, that they might plot the malices which they would carry out after these days. We ask you to live in such a way as those who know they will render an account to God of your whole life, not of these fifteen days alone. Then the questions of the Scriptures, which I proposed yesterday, and, the shortness of time hindering, did not solve, I confess I owe. But surely, because the days that follow now permit even the exactions of money by forensic and public law, demand this rather from me by Christian law. For now all come for the sake of the solemnity: after these days let the love of the law bring it about that what I promised be demanded of me. For he who gives, gives to you through me: he himself surely gives to all of us. For I know the Apostle saying: *Render to all their dues: to whom tribute, tribute; to whom toll, toll; to whom honor, honor; to whom fear, fear: owe no one anything, except to love one another*[^9]. Love alone is always to be rendered; no one is a stranger to such a debt. For what I owe, brothers, in the name of the Lord I will render. But I confess to you, I render it not to the slack in service, but to those who demand it.
