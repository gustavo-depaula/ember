# Sermon 83: On the Words of the Gospel, Matthew 18:21-22: "How Often Shall My Brother Sin Against Me," and the Rest

**1. 1.** Yesterday the holy Gospel admonished us not to neglect the sins of our brothers: *But if your brother sins against you,* it says, *rebuke him between you and him alone. If he listens to you, you have gained your brother. But if he disregards you, take with you two or three, that at the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may stand. But if he disregards them also, tell the Church. But if he disregards the Church, let him be to you as a heathen and a tax collector*[^1]. Today, too, the chapter that follows pertains to this same matter, which we heard just now as it was being read. For when the Lord Jesus had said this to Peter, Peter went on and asked the Master how often he should forgive a brother who had sinned against him; and he asked whether seven times sufficed. The Lord answered him: *Not only seven times, but even seventy times seven*[^2]. Then he told a parable that is very terrifying: that the kingdom of heaven is like a man, a head of a household, who settled accounts with his servants; among whom he found a debtor of ten thousand talents. And when he had ordered that all he had, and his whole household, and he himself be sold, and the debt paid, the servant, falling at his master's knees, begged him for a delay, and earned remission. For his master took pity on him, as we heard, and forgave him the whole debt. But that man, free of the debt yet a servant of iniquity, after he had gone out from his master's presence, himself found a debtor of his own, who owed not ten thousand talents, as much as his own debt had been, but a hundred denarii; and he began to drag him about by the throat and to say: *Pay what you owe*[^3]. But the man begged his fellow servant, as he himself had begged the master; but this man did not find such a fellow servant as the other had found a master. Not only did he refuse to remit the debt to him, but he did not even grant a delay. He hauled the man off, twisted about, to make payment, though he himself was now free of his master's debt. The fellow servants were displeased, and they reported to their master what had been done; and the master had the servant brought before him and said to him: *Wicked servant,* when you owed me so much, I took pity on you and *forgave you all; ought you not therefore also to have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I also had mercy on you*[^4]? And he ordered that all he had remitted to him be exacted.

**2. 2.** So he set forth this parable for our instruction, and by admonishing us he refused to let us perish. *So,* he says, *will my heavenly Father also do to you, if each of you does not forgive his brother from your hearts*[^5]. Behold, brothers, the matter is in the open, the admonition is useful; and very wholesome obedience is owed, that what is commanded may be fulfilled. For every man is both a debtor to God and has his brother as his debtor. For who is there who is not a debtor to God, except one in whom no sin can be found? And who is there who does not have his brother as a debtor, except one against whom no one has sinned? Do you suppose anyone can be found in the human race who is not himself also bound by some sin to his brother? Every man, then, is a debtor, while himself also having a debtor. Therefore the just God set up a rule for you in your debtor, which he himself will also follow with his own. For there are two works of mercy that set us free, which the Lord himself placed briefly in the Gospel: *Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you; give, and it shall be given you*[^6]. *Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you,* pertains to pardoning. *Give, and it shall be given you,* pertains to bestowing a benefit. As to what he says about pardoning—you too want to be pardoned for what you sin, and you have another whom you can pardon. Again, as to what pertains to bestowing a benefit—a beggar asks of you, and you are a beggar of God. For all of us, when we pray, are beggars of God: we stand before the door of the great head of the household, indeed we even prostrate ourselves, as suppliants we groan, wanting to receive something; and that very something is God himself. What does a beggar ask of you? Bread. And what do you ask of God, except Christ, who says: *I am the living bread, who came down from heaven*[^7]? Do you want to be forgiven? Forgive: *Remit, and it shall be remitted you.* Do you want to receive? *Give, and it shall be given you.*

**3. 3.** But hear what may trouble you in this open precept. In the remission where pardon is sought, and is owed by the one who forgives, this can trouble you, what troubled Peter too. *How often,* he says, *must I forgive? Does up to seven times suffice?* It does not suffice, says the Lord: *I do not say to you, Seven times, but, Seventy times seven*[^8]. Now count how often your brother has sinned against you. If you can reach the seventy-eighth fault, so as to pass beyond seventy times seven, then contrive your vengeance. Is it then true, as he says, and is the matter really so, that if he has sinned seventy times seven, you forgive; but if he has sinned seventy-eight times, you are now permitted not to forgive? I dare, I dare to say that even if he has sinned seventy-eight times, you should forgive. But if he has sinned, as I said, seventy-eight times, forgive. And if he has sinned a hundred times, forgive. And why should I keep saying so many and so many times? However often he sins, forgive. So have I dared to go beyond the measure of my Lord? He fixed the limit of forgiving at the number seventy-seven; shall I presume to leap over this limit? It is not so; I have not dared anything more. I heard my Lord himself speaking in his Apostle, where no measure and number is fixed beforehand. For he says: *Forgiving one another, if anyone has a complaint against another, just as God in Christ forgave you*[^9]. You have heard the rule. If Christ forgave you sins seventy times seven, if up to here he pardoned and beyond that refused, then you too set a limit, and forgive no further. But if Christ found thousands of sins of sinners and yet forgave them all, do not withdraw your mercy, but require the payment of that number. For not without reason did the Lord say *seventy times seven,* when there is no fault at all that you ought not to forgive. Behold, that very servant, whose debtor was found while he himself was a debtor, owed ten thousand talents. For I think that ten thousand talents are, to put it modestly, ten thousand sins. For I will not say one talent, which would contain all sins. But how much did that servant owe him? He owed a hundred denarii. Now is that not more than seventy times seven? And yet the master was angry, because he did not remit it to him. For not only are a hundred more than seventy-seven; but a hundred denarii are perhaps a thousand asses. But what is that to ten thousand talents?

**4. 4.** And by this, let us be ready to forgive all the faults committed against us, if we desire to be forgiven. For if we consider our sins, and count what we have done by deed, what by eye, what by ear, what by thought, what by countless impulses, I do not know whether we go to sleep without a talent. Therefore we ask daily, daily we knock at the divine ears in prayer, daily we prostrate ourselves and say: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^10]. What debts of yours? All, or some part? You will answer, All. So also you, then, to your debtor. This rule, then, you set down; this condition you state: on this pact and agreement, when you pray, you call it to mind, so that you say: *Forgive us, as we also forgive our debtors.*

**4. 5.** What, then, does *seventy times seven* mean? Hear, brothers, a great mystery, an admirable sacrament. When the Lord was baptized, the holy evangelist Luke there recounted his generations—in what order, in what series, by what lineage one came down to that generation by which Christ was born. Matthew began from Abraham and came, descending, all the way to Joseph. But Luke began to count by ascending. Why does the one descend, the other ascend? Because Matthew was commending the generation of Christ by which he descended to us; therefore, when Christ was born, he began to count by descending. But Luke, because he began to count at the time when Christ was baptized—there is the beginning of the ascension—this one began to count by ascending, and in counting he completed seventy-seven generations. From whom did he count? Attend, from whom. He set out to count from Christ all the way to Adam himself, who first sinned and begot us under the bond of sin. He came all the way to Adam, and there are counted seventy-seven generations: that is, from Christ to Adam, which we said are seventy-seven; and from Adam to Christ, seventy-seven. If, then, no generation was passed over, no fault is passed over where it ought not to be forgiven. For he therefore enumerated the seventy-seven generations of him whose number the Lord commended in the remission of sins; since he began to count from the baptism, where all sins are loosed.

**5. 6.** And in this too, brothers, receive a still greater sacrament. In the number seventy-seven there is the mystery of the remission of sins. So many generations are found from Christ to Adam. Then, a little more carefully, question the secret of that very number, and search out its hiding places; knock more diligently, that it may be opened to you. Righteousness consists in the law of God: that is true. For the law is commended in the ten precepts. Therefore that servant owed ten thousand talents. This is that memorable Decalogue, written with the finger of God, handed to the people through Moses the servant of God. So that servant owed ten thousand talents: it signifies all sins, on account of the number of the law. The other man too owed a hundred denarii: no less from the same number. For a hundred times a hundred makes ten thousand; and ten times ten, a hundred. And the one owed ten thousand talents, and the other ten times ten. For there is no departure from the lawful number, in both of which you will find both kinds of sin. Each is a debtor, and each a pleader for pardon and one who obtained it; but that servant, a bad servant, an ungrateful, unjust servant, refused to repay what he had received, refused to bestow what had been bestowed on him though unworthy.

**6. 7.** See then, brothers: each one begins from baptism, goes out free, the ten thousand talents are remitted to him; and when he has gone out, he is to find his fellow servant a debtor. Let him therefore observe that very sin: for the number eleven is the transgression of the law. For the law is ten, sin eleven. For the law is by ten, sin by eleven. Why is sin by eleven? Because it is the transgression of ten, so that you go to eleven. In the law a measure is fixed; but transgression is sin. Now where you transgress ten, you come to eleven. So great a mystery was figured when it was commanded that the tabernacle be built. Many numbered things were spoken of there, in a great sacrament. Among other things, curtains of haircloth were commanded to be made, not ten, but eleven[^11]: because by the haircloth is shown the confession of sins. But what more do you seek? Do you wish to know that all sins are contained in this number seventy-seven? Sevenfold is usually reckoned for the whole, since time turns in seven days, and when the seven are finished, one returns again to the head, that the same pattern may turn round. Through revolutions of this kind of pattern the ages pass; yet there is no departure from the number seven. For he meant all sins when he said *seventy times seven:* because if you multiply that eleven seven times, it makes seventy times seven. Therefore he willed all sins to be forgiven, who marked them out beforehand by the number seventy-seven. Let no one hold himself bound by not forgiving, lest he be held bound against himself when he prays. For God says, Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you. But I forgave first; do you forgive at least afterward. For if you do not forgive, I will call you back; and whatever I had forgiven you, I will charge again against you. For the Truth does not lie; for Christ does not deceive or is deceived, who added, saying: *So will your Father, who is in heaven, also do to you*[^12]. You find a Father, imitate the Father. For if you are unwilling to imitate him, you arrange to be disinherited: *So,* then, he says, *will your heavenly Father do to you, if each of you does not forgive your brothers from your hearts*[^13]. Do not say with the tongue, I forgive, and put it off in the heart. For God shows you the punishment, threatening vengeance. God knows where you speak. A man heard your voice; God inspects your conscience. If you say, I forgive, then forgive. It is better that, while you cry out with the mouth, you forgive in the heart, than that you be soothing with the mouth and cruel in the heart.

**7. 8.** So now undisciplined boys plead, and do not want a beating, who prescribe to us in this way when we want to administer discipline: I have sinned, forgive me. Behold, I forgave, and he sins again. Forgive: I forgave. He sins a third time. Forgive: a third time I forgave. Now let him get a beating the fourth time. And he: Have I not worn you out seventy times seven? If by this prescription the severity of discipline goes to sleep, with discipline repressed unpunished wickedness rages. What, then, must be done? Let us rebuke with words, and if need be, even with blows; but let us forgive the offense, let us cast the fault out of the heart. For this is why the Lord added: *from your hearts*[^14]—so that if discipline is imposed through love, gentleness may not depart from the heart. For what is so loving as a physician carrying his instrument? The one to be cut weeps, and he is cut; the one to be cauterized weeps, and he is cauterized. That is not cruelty; far be it that it be called the savagery of the physician. He rages against the wound, that the man may be healed; for if the wound is stroked, the man is lost. So, then, would I admonish these things, my brothers, that we love in every way our brothers who sin, that we not dismiss love for them from our heart, and that we administer discipline when there is need; lest, through the relaxing of discipline, wickedness grow, and we begin to be accused before God; for it was recited to us: *Those who sin, rebuke before all, that the rest may have fear*[^15]. Surely if anyone—what alone is true—distinguishes the times and resolves the question, it is true. If the sin is in secret, rebuke in secret. If the sin is public and open, rebuke publicly, that the one may be amended and the rest may fear.
