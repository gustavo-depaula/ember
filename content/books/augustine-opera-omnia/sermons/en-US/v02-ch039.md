# Sermon 50

**1** The Manicheans bring a false charge against the prophet Haggai, accusing him invidiously because he said, in the person of God speaking: *Mine is the gold, and mine is the silver*[^1]; and because they study to set the Gospel pugnaciously against the old Law, so that both Scriptures may seem to them, as it were, opposed and contrary, they pose the question thus: "In Haggai," they say, "the prophet, it is written: *Mine is the gold, and mine is the silver*; but in the Gospel our Savior called this sort of thing the mammon of *iniquity*, of the use of which the blessed Apostle, writing to Timothy, says: *The root of all evils is avarice, which some craving have turned away from the faith and entangled themselves in many sorrows*[^2]." This is their posing of the question, or rather an accusation of the old Scriptures, through which the Gospel was foretold, from that very Gospel which was foretold through them. For if they posed the question, they would perhaps inquire; and if they inquired, they would perhaps find.

**2** But why do the wretches not understand that the Lord, speaking in Haggai, said for this reason, *Mine is the gold, and mine is the silver*, that both the one who is unwilling to share with the needy what he has, when he hears the precepts of doing mercy, may understand that God commands it to be given not from the property of the one he commands, but from his own; and the one who holds out something to a poor man may not think he does it from his own, lest perhaps he be not so much confirmed by the name of mercy as puffed up by the vanity of pride? *Mine is*, he says, *the gold, and mine is the silver*, not yours, O rich of the earth. Why, then, do you hesitate to give the poor man from what is mine, or why are you lifted up when you give from what is mine?

**3** And do you want to see what a matter of the just judge gold and silver are? The greedy man is tormented by them, the merciful man is helped by them. The divine justice distributing his own property, both rightly-done deeds are made manifest by them, and sins are punished by them. For gold and silver and every earthly possession is both the exercise of humanity and the punishment of desire. When God grants such things to us men, he shows in them how much the soul despises, whose riches are the very one who grants them. For no one can appear a despiser, except of a thing of which he has been made the possessor. For even those who do not have them can despise these things. But whether they pretend or truly despise, God sees, who is the inspector of the heart. But to men, that they may be able to imitate, the thought of the despiser is seen only in the hands of one giving out. But when God grants these things to evil men, he shows in them how, even in the very goods that God bestows, the soul is tormented, to which the one who bestows such great things has become cheap. For to the good he supplies occasions of benefits; the evil he torments with the fear of losses. And therefore, if both lose gold and silver, the former will retain the heavenly riches with glad heart, but to the latter there will remain both a house empty of temporal goods and a conscience emptier of eternal goods.

**4** His, then, is the gold and silver, who knows how to use gold and silver. For even among men themselves, anyone is then to be said to have something, when he uses it well. For what he does not handle justly, he does not hold by right. But what he does not hold by right, if he says it is his own, it will not be the voice of a just possessor, but the shamelessness of an impudent squatter. Wherefore, if a man not unfittingly says something is his own—not what he has seized by unjust and foolish desire, but what he handles by most prudent power and most just moderation—how much more does God truly and properly say that the gold and silver are his own, which he both founded by most generous goodness, and administers by most just command, so that without his nod and dominion neither can the evil have gold and silver for the punishment of avarice, nor the good for the use of mercy! Which, however, they can neither establish that it should exist, nor distribute and order that it be present to one and lacking to another.

**5** But if gold and silver were given into the power of the evil alone, it would rightly be thought evil. If of the good alone, it would rightly be thought some great good. Again, if it were lacking to the evil alone, poverty would seem a great punishment. But if it were lacking to the good alone, poverty would seem the highest blessedness. Now, if you want to know that gold can be well had, the good too have it. If you want to know that they are not good through gold, the evil too have it. Likewise, if you want to know how poverty is not misery, there are certain poor men who are blessed. If you want to know how poverty is not blessedness, there are certain poor men who are wretched. So, then, the founder and administrator of things, God, has so distributed gold and silver to men, that it is in itself, by its nature and kind, good—though not the highest and great good—and according to the rank of its order shows the praiseworthy founder of the universe; but its abundance does not exalt the good nor want crush them, while the evil it both blinds when it is offered and torments when it is taken away.

**6** A thing, then, founded both for the praise of the Founder and for the proving of the good and the punishment of the greater evil, can in no way be rightly blamed. And God most truly says it is his own, which he not only founded by most abundant goodness, but also dispenses by most provident moderation. But when the Lord in the Gospel calls this kind of thing the *mammon of iniquity*, he signifies that there is another mammon—that is, other riches, which none but the just and good can possess—so that it is called the *mammon of iniquity* for this reason, because iniquity calls these riches. But justice knows there are other riches, by which the inner man is adorned, as the blessed Peter says: *Who is rich before God*[^3]. Those are called just riches, because they are granted to good and just merits. Those are called the true riches, because whoever has them will not be in need. But these are unjust riches, not because gold and silver are unjust, but because it is unjust to think them riches, which do not take away want. For the more anyone loving them has greater riches, the more will he burn with want. How, then, are they riches, with whose increase want increases, which, the greater they are to their lovers, bring not satiety but inflame desire? Do you think him rich, who would be in less want if he had less? For we see that some, when they had a little money, rejoiced in little gains. But after the true substance of gold and silver—yet false riches—began to abound to them, when you offer a little, they now refuse it. You believe them now satisfied, but it is false. For greater money does not close the throat of avarice, but stretches it; does not water it, but kindles it. They spurn the cup, because they thirst for a river. Is he, then, to be called richer or more needy, who, when he wanted to have something so as not to be in need, therefore has more so as not to be in less need?

**7** But this is not the fault of gold and silver. For suppose some merciful man has found a treasure. Is not, by the working of mercy, hospitality afforded to pilgrims, the hungry fed, the naked clothed, the needy helped, captives redeemed, churches built, the weary refreshed, the litigious reconciled, the shipwrecked restored, the sick cared for, bodily wealth distributed on earth, spiritual wealth stored up in heaven? Who does these things? The merciful and good man. Whence does he do them? From gold and silver. Serving whom does he do them? Him who says: *Mine is the gold and mine is the silver*. You see now, as I judge, brothers, with how great an error and how great a madness the crime of those who use things badly is transferred to the very things they use badly. For if gold and silver are blamed for this reason, that men depraved by avarice, neglecting the precepts of the most almighty Creator, are seized with detestable desire toward the things he founded, let every creature of God be blamed too, because, as the Apostle says, certain perverse men *worshipped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever*[^4]. Let this very sun be blamed too, which surely the same Manicheans, since they do not understand it to be a creature, do not cease to worship and adore as if the Creator himself or as if some part of him. Why, then, do they not blame it, when men often stir up most unjust quarrels over the use of the sun and light in their buildings, and, that the rays may flow somewhat more freely and more amply into their windows, often try to pull down others' houses, and pursue with most bitter enmities those who oppose them, even if they oppose by most certain right? If, then, some more powerful man unjustly and wickedly oppress one of the weak on account of the use of the sun, plunder him, or even drive him into exile or death, is it the crime of the sun, which he desires to use more abundantly, or rather the iniquity of one who uses it badly, who, while he craves to acquire the temporal light more amply for the eyes of the body, does not open the bedroom of his heart to the light of equity?

**8** From which let these men, if they can, understand either that they ought not to accuse gold and silver, although about gold and silver most greedy men often fight, or that they ought to transfer their accusations from the earth to heaven and from the gleaming metals to the stars and the sun itself, when wicked men often clash in inexpiable discord over possessing the light of the sun. At the same time let them learn too what difference there is between this visible light and the light of justice. Since it can come about that, the greater the avidity with which anyone wants to enjoy this light, the greater the blindness by which he falls away from the light of justice. For no creature can justify a man, but that he may be able to use all creatures justly, he is to be justified by the Creator. And so the Lord himself, although as a just judge he condemns avarice everywhere, yet as a true master shows the use of earthly abundance, in that very place which these men wished to cast against the prophet as contrary. For he says: *Make for yourselves friends of the mammon of iniquity*[^5]. This is to say, that which is the mammon of iniquity ought not to be your mammon. For then you will be able justly to use the abundance of earthly things, and to make friends from it who may *receive you into the eternal dwellings*, if this mammon is not yours—that is, if you do not think yourselves made rich by it. Since your riches, since the true riches are those which will free you from all want, are not to be compared with earthly means. But that you may deservedly enjoy those, you must first use well these, which are not true riches, nor yours, because they are unjustly called riches. For they do not take away want, and the unjust think them riches. For by these they think themselves freed from want. But you ought to desire other riches—that is, the true and your own. But *if in the unjust mammon you have not been faithful, who will give you the true? And if in another's you have not been faithful, who will give you your own?*[^6]

**9** Although it is manifest that the Manicheans, in their own manner, bring a false charge against the prophetic sayings. For whoever examines even moderately the context of that same Scripture will find that the prophet spoke not of this silver or gold, with which avarice imprudently rages, but rather of that of which the Apostle too makes mention, saying: *But if anyone builds on the foundation gold and silver, precious stones*[^7]. With this gold and silver that treasure is rich, which the Lord himself testifies was found in a field and bought, by someone wonderfully and praiseworthily greedy, when he had sold all his goods. For, foretelling the Lord himself, and figuratively designating, as is his custom, the times of the new age—that is, of the Church—the prophet says thus: *Yet one little while, and I will shake heaven and earth and sea and the dry land, and I will move all the nations. And the desired of all the nations will come, and I will fill this house with glory, says the Lord of hosts. Mine is the gold, and mine is the silver, says the Lord of hosts. Great will be the glory of this last house more than of the first, says the Lord of hosts. And in this place I will give peace, says the Lord of hosts*[^8].

**10** If these men were willing not to be dogs and swine, to whom we are forbidden to give what is holy and to cast pearls, but desired to receive by asking and to find by seeking and to have it opened to them by knocking, how they might be able perhaps, even without any interpreter, with the Holy Spirit himself as guide, to perceive that the things said pertain, without any gloom, to the new people—that is, to the Christian people, whose great priest is Jesus the Son of God—at least in that place where it was said: *Yet one little while, and I will shake heaven and earth and sea and the dry land, and I will move all the nations. And the desired of all the nations will come*. For of the last—that is, the second coming of the Lord, in which he is to come in splendor—this verse was uttered, when the prophet says: *And the desired of all the nations will come*. For when he first came in mortal flesh through the Virgin Mary, he was not yet desired by all the nations, because they had not yet believed. But the Gospel having been disseminated through all the nations, in all the nations the desire of him is kindled. For through all the nations there are and will be his elect, who with the whole heart say in prayer: *Your kingdom come*. But the first coming sowed mercy beforehand for judgment, in which judgment the splendor of the second coming will be eminent. First, then, heaven had to be moved, when the angel announced him to the Virgin who would conceive, when the star led the Magi to adore him, when again the angels indicated his birth to the shepherds; the earth to be moved, when it was disturbed by his miracles; the sea to be moved, when this world raged with persecutions; the dry land to be moved, when those believing in him hungered and thirsted for justice; finally all the nations to be moved, when his Gospel ran everywhere. Then afterward the *desired of all the nations* would come, as, the prophet declaring, he is to come. And this house will be filled with glory—that is, the Church.

**11** Accordingly he subjoined: *Mine is the silver and the gold*. For all wisdom, which is figuratively signified by the name of gold, and *the words of the Lord, chaste words, silver tried by fire of the earth, purified seven times*[^9]—all such silver and gold, then, is not of men but of the Lord, so that, since the house will be filled with glory, he who glories may glory in the Lord. For because that great priest, the inhabitant of this house, our Lord Jesus Christ, on account of the return of man who through pride had gone out of paradise, deigned to offer himself as an example of humility, which he attests in the Gospel, crying: *Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble of heart*[^10]: lest perhaps anyone in his house—that is, in the Church—if he can perceive or say anything wisely, should, wanting it to seem his own, be exalted, see how great a medicine is said to him by the Lord God: *Mine is the gold and mine is the silver*. For so what follows will come about, that *the glory of this last house may be great, more than of the first*. For the first house—that is, the citizens of the earthly Jerusalem—as the Apostle says, *not knowing the justice of God and seeking to establish their own justice, were not subject to the justice of God*[^11]. See whether these men, while they say the gold and silver are their own, were not able to come to the eternal glory of the last house. Yet when the prophet says: *Great will be the glory of this last house more than of the first*, he shows that the first itself too was not without some glory. For of it the Apostle too was speaking, when he said: *For if that which is done away was through glory, much more that which abides will be in glory*[^12].

**12** But the last verse, with which this discourse of the prophet is concluded: *And in this place*, he says, *I will give peace, says the Lord of hosts*[^13]. What is "in this place," except that he perhaps points out something earthly, as with a finger? For what can be contained in a place, except a body? Not absurdly, then, do we understand the last resurrection of the body, by which the most perfect blessedness is completed, when now *the flesh does not lust against the spirit, nor the spirit against the flesh*[^14]. *For this corruptible will put on incorruption, and this mortal will put on immortality*[^15]. There will be no other law in the members fighting against the law of the mind, because *in this place I will give peace, says the Lord of hosts*.

**13** For what the prophets say about the contempt of earthly gold and silver, who is so deaf against the divine voices as not to know it? For so those men, to deceive men, produce from the Apostle what he says: *The root of all evils is avarice, which some pursuing have strayed from the faith, and entangled themselves in many sorrows*[^16]—as though you could easily find some book of the old Scriptures where avarice is not blamed and condemned with worthy execration. But since the question now is about gold and silver, why do they not hear the prophet saying: *But neither their silver nor their gold will be able to free them in the day of the wrath of the Lord*[^17]? If anyone thirsting hears only this and pours it into the marrow of his soul, will he not, alienating his whole self from the blandishments of false happiness, into the embrace of God, stripped of the old man that he may cover himself with immortality, call himself? But why should we treat any longer of this question? I believe it is manifest to your Charity that the sect of the Manicheans deals with the unskilled not by truth but by fraud, so that they prefer the Scriptures not whole to whole, the new to the old, but by excerpting sentences, which it tries to show as if opposed to itself, that they may deceive the unskilled. But there is no epistle of the Apostle, or even book of the Gospel, of the new Testament itself, of which these things could not be done, that by certain sentences that one book might seem to be contrary to itself, unless its whole context be treated with the most diligent attention of the reader.
