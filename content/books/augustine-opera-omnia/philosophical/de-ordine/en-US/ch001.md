# Book I

**1. 1. 1.** To grasp and to hold to the order proper to each thing, Zenobius, and then the order of the universe by which this world is held together and ruled—whether to see it or to set it forth—is for human beings most difficult and most rare. And to this is added that, even if someone could do this, he cannot also bring it about that he find a hearer worthy of matters so divine and obscure, whether by merit of life or by some habit of learning. And yet there is nothing that the best minds more eagerly seek after, and that those more eagerly desire to hear and learn who gaze upon the rocks and storms of this life with head raised as far as may be, than how it comes about both that God cares for human affairs and that so great a perversity is everywhere spread through human affairs that it seems it ought to be assigned not to a divine governance, nor even to any servant's, if so great a power were given to him. Therefore this is left, as if of necessity, to be believed by those who have such matters at heart: either that divine providence does not extend to these last and lowest things, or that all evils are surely committed by the will of God. Both are impious, but more so the latter. For although to believe that anything is deserted by God is, while most ignorant, also most dangerous to the soul, yet among human beings themselves no one has charged anyone with not having been able to do something; but blame for negligence is far more pardonable than blame for malice and cruelty. And so reason is, as it were, compelled—not unmindful of piety—to hold either that these earthly things cannot be administered by the divine, or that they are neglected and despised rather than so governed that every complaint about God should be mild and free of blame.

**1. 1. 2.** But who is so blind in mind as to hesitate to assign to the divine power and governance whatever there is of reason in the movements of bodies that lies beyond human arrangement and will? Unless perhaps either the limbs of even the tiniest animals are shaped by chance with so fixed and fine a measure; or what one denies to be by chance, he could admit was done by anything but reason; or indeed, throughout all nature, we should dare, by any trifles of empty opinion, to alienate from the most secret judgment of majesty that which we marvel at as ordered in each and every thing without any human art busying itself. But this very thing is full of questions: that the limbs of a flea are wonderfully arranged and distinct, while meanwhile human life turns and tosses in the inconstancy of innumerable disturbances. But on this reckoning, if someone could see so minutely that on a mosaic pavement his sight could take in nothing beyond the span of a single tile, he would blame the craftsman as ignorant of order and composition, because he would think the variety of the little stones confused—he by whom those inlaid figures, coming together into the appearance of a single beauty, could not be seen and surveyed at once. For nothing else happens to less learned people, who, unable with a feeble mind to embrace and consider the whole fitting-together and harmony of things, think, if something offends them, that—because it is great to their own thought—a great ugliness clings to things.

**1. 1. 3.** The greatest cause of this error is that a person is unknown to himself. Yet that he may know himself, he has need of great practice in withdrawing from the senses and gathering his mind into itself and keeping it within itself. This is attained only by those who either, in solitude, sear over certain blows of opinion which the course of daily life inflicts, or heal them by the liberal disciplines.

**1. 2. 3.** For the mind thus restored to itself understands what the beauty of the universe is—which beauty surely takes its name from the One (*unum*). And so it is not granted to see that beauty to the soul which goes out into the many and pursues poverty with greed, a poverty it does not know can be avoided only by separating itself from the multitude. But by the multitude I mean not of human beings, but of all the things that the senses touch. And do not wonder that the soul suffers want the more, the more it seeks to embrace more things. For just as in a circle, however large, there is one center to which all things converge, which the geometers call the center, and although the parts of the whole circumference can be cut into innumerable pieces, yet there is nothing except that one point by which all the rest are equally measured, and which rules over all by a kind of law of equality; whereas if you wish to go out from there toward any part whatever, all things are lost the more one proceeds into the many—so the mind, poured out from itself, is battered by a kind of immensity, and is worn away by a true beggary, since its own nature compels it to seek the one everywhere, and the multitude does not let it find it.

**1. 2. 4.** But both what these things I have spoken of are like, and what cause there is for the error of souls, and in what way all things both come together into one and are perfect, and yet sins are to be fled—all this you will surely grasp, my Zenobius. For I know your character well, and your mind, a lover of beauty of every kind, without the immoderation and squalor of lust. By a divine right this sign of future wisdom marks you out against ruinous desires, that you not desert your own cause, enticed by false pleasures; than which prevarication nothing more shameful and dangerous can be found. You will grasp these things, then—believe me—when you have given attention to learning, by which the mind is purified and cultivated, in no way fit before this for the divine seeds to be entrusted to it. What all this is like, and what order it demands, and what reason promises to the studious and the good, and what kind of life we lead, your dearest friends, and what fruit we gather from our liberal leisure—these books will, I think, teach you well enough; books sweeter to us for your name than for our own labor, especially if you are willing—choosing the better things—to insert and fit yourself into that very order about which I am writing to you.

**1. 2. 5.** For when a pain in the stomach had forced me to leave the school—I who already, as you know, was contriving to flee into philosophy even without any such necessity—I betook myself at once to the country house of our most intimate friend Verecundus. What shall I say—he being glad of it? You know very well the man's singular benevolence, both toward all and toward us in particular. There we discussed among ourselves whatever seemed useful, with a pen at hand, of course, by which all might be taken down, which I saw was conducive to my health. For since I was held back by some care for speaking, no immoderate contention crept in during the discussion. And likewise, so that if we wished to commit anything of ours to writing, there should be neither the necessity of saying it otherwise nor the labor of recollection. Now these matters were carried on with me by Alypius and my brother Navigius, and Licentius, who had suddenly and wonderfully given himself to poetry. Military service had likewise returned Trygetius to us, who, as a veteran, fell in love with history. And we already had something of these matters in our books.

**1. 3. 6.** But one night, when I had awakened as usual and was silently turning over within myself things that came into my mind from I know not where—for the love of finding the truth had now turned this into a habit for me, so that, if such cares were present, either the first half or at least the last, yet about half the night, I lay awake thinking of whatever it might be; nor did I let myself be called away from myself by the young men's late studies, for they too worked so much through the whole day that it seemed too much to me if they should also take up some part of the nights for the labor of their studies; and they had this very rule from me, that they should occupy themselves with something besides their books and accustom the mind to dwell within itself—so, as I said, I was awake, when behold, the sound of the water that flowed past behind the baths drew me to give ear, and was noticed more attentively than usual. It seemed quite strange to me that the same water made its noise now more clearly, now more faintly, as it rushed against the stones. I began to ask myself what the cause might be. I confess, nothing occurred to me; when Licentius, striking a piece of wood beside his bed, frightened off the troublesome shrew-mice, and in this way showed himself awake. To him I said, "Did you notice, Licentius—for I see your Muse has lit a lamp for you to study by—how this channel sounds inconstantly?" "This is nothing new to me now," he said. "For once, when I had been awakened by longing for fair weather and had put my ear to it, lest a storm come on, this water did what it does now." Trygetius agreed. For he too was lying awake on his bed in the same room, without our knowing it; for it was dark—which in Italy is almost a necessity even for the moneyed.

**1. 3. 7.** So when I saw that our school, such as was present (for both Alypius and Navigius had gone to the city), was not asleep even at those hours, and that course of the waters was warning me to say something about it, I said, "What do you think is the cause that this sound thus alternates? For we do not suppose that anyone at these hours is interrupting that flow so often, either by passing through or by washing something." "What do you think," said Licentius, "but that somewhere leaves of whatever kind—which in autumn fall continually and copiously—being thrust into the narrows of the channel, are at times overcome and give way; but when the wave that pressed has passed through, are again gathered and packed; or that something else happens by the varying chance of the floating leaves, which avails in like manner now to check, now to release that flow?" It seemed probable to me, having nothing else; and I confessed, praising his talent, that I had found nothing, though I had long sought why it was so.

**1. 3. 8.** Then, after a little silence, I said, "Deservedly did you wonder at nothing, keeping yourself within with Calliope." "Deservedly," said he, "but just now you plainly gave me something great to wonder at." "What is this?" I said. "That you," said he, "wondered at those things." "From where," I said, "is wonder wont to arise, or what is the mother of this fault, except a thing unusual, beyond the manifest order of causes?" And he said, "Beyond the manifest, I accept; for beyond order nothing seems to me to happen." Here I, raised up with a livelier hope than I usually have when I require something of these young men, because the young man's mind had conceived so great a thing and so suddenly—he having turned to such matters only yesterday, with no question about these things ever having been stirred up among us before—said, "Well, well; but indeed you have perceived much, you have dared much: believe me in this; by a long stretch you transcend Helicon, to whose peak you are trying to come as to heaven. But I should much like you to defend this opinion, for I shall try to shake it." "Leave me to myself just now, I beg you," he said; "for I have my mind very much bent on something else." Here I, fearing not a little lest, plunged utterly into the study of poetry, he be carried far from philosophy, said, "I am provoked to attack those verses of yours, chanting and howling in every kind of meter, since they try to raise between you and the truth a wall more monstrous than between your lovers; for those at least could breathe through even a small chink." For he had just then begun to sing of Pyramus.

**1. 3. 9.** When I had said this in a sterner voice than he expected, he fell silent for a while. And I had now abandoned my undertaking and returned to myself, lest I should wish in vain and ineptly to seize one already preoccupied. Then he said,  
*I myself, poor wretch, am like the shrew, betrayed by my own outcry*  
—a thing said no more fittingly by Terence than it can now be said by me about myself. But perhaps that last part will be turned to the contrary: for what he says,  
*Today I have perished*  
I perhaps today shall be found. For if you do not despise what the superstitious are wont to do, taking auguries even from mice: if I, by my noise, warned that mouse or shrew which brought you word of my being awake, that—if it has any sense—it should return to its den and rest with itself, why should not I myself be warned by that noise of your voice to philosophize rather than to sing? For that, as I have already begun to believe, since you prove it to me daily, is our true and unshaken dwelling. Therefore, if it is not troublesome to you and you think it ought to be done, ask what you will: I will defend, as far as I can, the order of things, and I will assert that nothing can happen apart from order. For I have so drunk in and absorbed it in my mind that, even if anyone should overcome me in this discussion, I will assign even this not to any rashness but to the order of things. For it will not be the matter itself, but Licentius, that is overcome."

**1. 4. 10.** I, rejoicing again, restored myself to them. Then I said to Trygetius, "What seems good to you?" "I do indeed favor order most highly," he said, "but I am still uncertain, and I desire that so great a matter be discussed most carefully." "Then let that part have your favor," I said; "for as to your being uncertain, I think you have that in common with Licentius and with me too." "Surely," said Licentius, "I am certain of this opinion. For why should I doubt to demolish the wall you mentioned, before it has fully raised itself up? For truly poetry cannot turn me away from philosophy so much as can distrust of finding the truth." Then Trygetius, with joyful words, said, "We now have—what is more—a Licentius who is not an Academic; for he used to defend them most eagerly." "Leave off these things now, I beg you," said Licentius, "lest this crafty, ensnaring point of yours twist me away from and break me off from a certain divine thing, which has begun to show itself to me, and toward which I, gaping, hang suspended." Here I, seeing my joys abound far more richly than I had ever once dared even to wish, eagerly poured out that verse:  
*So may that Father of the gods grant it, so may lofty Apollo*  
for he himself will lead us through, if we follow where he bids us go and where to set our seat—he who now gives the augury and glides into our souls. For lofty Apollo is not the one who, in caves, on mountains, in groves, stirred by the smoke of incense and the slaughter of cattle, fills the mad; but another, surely—another is that lofty truth-teller, and that very Truth (for why should I beat about with words?), whose prophets are all who can be wise. Therefore we shall set to, Licentius, worshippers relying on piety; let us crush beneath our footsteps the ruinous fire of smoky desires.

**1. 4. 11.** "Now," said he, "ask, I pray you, if I can explain this great something-or-other in my own words too." "Answer me this very thing," I said, "first: from where does it seem to you that this water flows in, not at random but in order? For that it slides over little wooden channels and is led all the way to our uses can pertain to order. For it was brought about by human beings using reason that by its one course they might at once both drink and wash, and it followed, according to the convenience of the places, that it should be so. But that those leaves, as you say, fell so as to bring about what we wondered at—by what order of things, after all, and not rather by chance, shall we think it was done?" "As if, indeed," said he, "it could seem to anyone—gazing most serenely on the fact that nothing can happen without a cause—that they ought or could have fallen otherwise than they did. What? Do you now want me to go through the positions of the trees and branches, and the very weight that nature has imposed on the leaves? What of the mobility of the air by which they fly, or the softness by which they descend, and their various falls according to the state of the sky, according to their burden, according to their shapes, and the countless other and more obscure causes—what concern is it of mine to inquire into? These things are hidden from our senses, deeply hidden; yet that which is enough for the question taken up—somehow it is not hidden from the mind: that nothing happens without a cause. For an annoying questioner can go on asking: what was the cause that trees were placed there? I will answer that men followed the richness of the soil. What if the trees are not fruit-bearing, and grew at random? Here too I will answer that we see too little; for the nature that produced them is by no means random. What more? Either let me be taught that something happens without a cause, or believe that nothing happens except by a fixed order of causes."

**1. 5. 12.** To him I said, "Though you call me an annoying questioner—for I can scarcely help being one, who fought to keep you from conversing with Pyramus and Thisbe—I will nonetheless go on asking you. This nature, which you want to seem ordered, for what good (to say nothing of countless other things) did it bring forth those very trees that bear no fruit?" While he was thinking what to say, Trygetius said, "Is the use of trees furnished to human beings in their fruits alone? How many other uses there are, which come from their shade, from their wood—though that is last—from their very branches or leaves?" "Do not, I beg you," said Licentius, "give these answers to his questions. For countless are the things that can be brought forward from which there is no use to human beings, or at least it is so hidden or so feeble that it cannot be drawn out or defended by human beings, especially by us. Let him rather teach us how anything can happen that a cause has not preceded." "We shall look at these things later," I said. "For it is not now necessary that I be the teacher, since you, who have professed yourself certain of so great a matter, have so far taught nothing to me, who desire exceedingly to learn and for this alone lie awake day and night."

**1. 5. 13.** "Where are you sending me?" he said. "Is it because I follow you more lightly than those leaves follow the winds, by which they are thrown into the flowing water, so that for them to fall is too little unless they are also dragged along? For what else will it be when Licentius teaches both Augustine and the things that lie in the midst of philosophy?" "Do not, I beg you," I said, "either cast yourself down so low or exalt me so high. For I too am a boy in philosophy, and I do not much care, when I ask, through whom he answers me who receives my daily complaints—whose prophet I believe you will one day be; and perhaps that 'one day' is not far off. But still, others too, much removed from studies of this kind, can teach something, when they are constrained, as by the bonds of questions, in a fellowship of disputants. And the same person 'something' is not 'nothing.' Or do you not see (for I will gladly use your own simile) that those very leaves carried by the winds, that swim on the waves, resist somewhat the river that hurls itself headlong, and remind human beings of the order of things—if, that is, what you defend is true?"

**1. 5. 14.** Here he, leaping even from his bed for joy, said, "Who would deny, great God, that you administer all things by order? How all things hold together! With what fixed successions they are pressed into their own knots! How great and how many things have been done that we might speak these words! How great things come about that we might find you! For from where does this very thing flow and come, except from the order of things—that we awoke, that you noticed that sound, that you sought with yourself the cause, that you did not find the cause of so small a matter? The shrew too comes forth, that I, awake, might be betrayed. Finally, your very own speech—you perhaps not intending it (for it is not in anyone's power what comes into the mind)—is somehow so turned about that it itself teaches me what I ought to answer you. For, I pray you, if these things that have been said by us, committed to writing as you have arranged, should spread somewhat more widely to the report of human beings, would not the matter seem so great that some great seer or Chaldean, consulted about it, ought to have answered long before it happened? And if he had so answered, he would be called so divine, he would be so extolled with the praises of all, that yet no one would dare to ask of him why a leaf fell from the tree, or whether a wandering mouse was troublesome to a man lying down. For did any of them ever say of his own accord, or was forced by a consulter to say, that such things would be? And yet, if he were to say that a certain not-ignoble book would come to be, and saw that it would necessarily come to pass (for otherwise he could not divine it), surely whatever the tumbling of leaves in the field does, whatever the lowliest little beast does in the house, is as necessary in the order of things as those writings. For by these words they come about, words that, without those preceding lowliest things, could neither come into the mind, nor proceed from the mouth, nor be handed down to posterity. Therefore now, I ask, let no one ask of me why anything happens. It is enough that nothing happens, nothing is begotten, that some cause has not begotten and set in motion."

**1. 6. 15.** "It is plain," I said, "young man, that you do not know how many things, and by what sort of men, have been said against divination. But answer now—not whether anything happens without a cause (for I now see you are unwilling to answer that), but whether this order of yours that you have taken up seems to you to be anything good or evil." And he, muttering somewhat, said, "You did not ask in such a way that I can answer one of the two. For I see here a kind of middle. For order seems to me neither good nor evil." "What, at least, do you judge to be contrary to order?" I said. "Nothing," said he. "For how can anything be contrary to that which has occupied the whole, has taken hold of the whole? For what will be contrary to order must necessarily be apart from order. But I see that nothing is apart from order. We must therefore think that nothing is contrary to order." "Then," said Trygetius, "is error not contrary to order?" "In no way," said he. "For I see no one err without a cause. But the series of causes is included in order. And error itself is not only begotten by a cause, but also begets something for which it becomes a cause. Therefore, in that it is not outside order, it cannot be contrary to order."

**1. 6. 16.** And when Trygetius had fallen silent, and I could not contain myself for joy—because I saw that the young man, the son of my dearest friend, was becoming my son too, and not only that, but was already rising and growing into a friend for me as well; and he whose zeal I had despaired of even for ordinary letters was, as if having regard for his own possession, coming with full force into the midst of philosophy—while I silently marveled at this and boiled over in congratulation, suddenly he, as if seized by a kind of inspiration, cried out, "O if I could say what I want! I ask, wherever you are, words, come to my aid. Both good things and evil things are in order. [7. 17.] Believe it, if you will; for how to explain it I do not know."

**1. 7. 17.** I marveled and was silent. But Trygetius, when he saw the man become a little affable, as if the drunkenness had been digested, and given back to conversation, said, "It seems absurd to me, Licentius, and plainly foreign to the truth, what you say; but I beg you to bear with me a little, and not to drive me off by shouting." "Say what you will," said he; "for I do not fear that you will carry me off from that which I see and almost hold." "Would," said Trygetius, "that you were not wandering away from that order which you defend, that you were not borne (to put it more mildly) into so great a carelessness toward God. For what could be said more impious than that even evils are contained in order? For surely God loves order." "Truly he loves it," said he; "from him it flows, and with him it is. And if anything can be said more fittingly about a matter so deep, think it over yourself, I beg you. For I am not fit to teach you these things now." "What should I think?" said Trygetius. "I accept entirely what you say, and it is enough for me in what I understand. For surely you said both that evils are contained in order, and that order itself flows from the highest God and is loved by him. From which it follows that evils too are from the highest God, and that God loves evils."

**1. 7. 18.** In this conclusion I feared for Licentius. But he, groaning at the difficulty of words, and not at all seeking what to answer, but how to bring out what had to be answered, said, "God does not love evils; and for no other reason than that it is not of order that God should love evils. And therefore he greatly loves order, because through it he does not love evils. But how can evils themselves not be in order, since God does not love them? For this very thing is the order of evils, that they not be loved by God. Or does it seem to you a small order of things, that God both love good things and not love evil ones? Thus evils, which God does not love, are not apart from order; and yet he loves order itself: for this very thing he loves—to love good things and not to love evil ones, which is of the great order and the divine arrangement. And this order and arrangement, because by its very distinction it guards the harmony of the universe, brings it about that even evils must necessarily exist. Thus, as it were from antitheses—which is pleasing to us even in speech—from contraries, the beauty of all things together is figured forth."

**1. 7. 19.** After this he paused moderately. And suddenly raising himself toward where Trygetius had his bed, he said, "For I ask of you, I pray: is God just?" Trygetius was silent—too much, as he afterward reported, marveling and shuddering at his fellow-pupil's and friend's speech, breathed out by a new inspiration. And he being silent, the other went on thus. "For if you answer that God is not just, you see to it what you are doing, you who a while ago were charging me with impiety. But if, as is handed down to us, and as we ourselves feel by the very necessity of order, God is just, then surely he is just by distributing to each his own. But what distribution can be spoken of where there is no distinction? Or what distinction, if all things are good? Or what can be found apart from order, if by God's justice their own things are rendered to each according to the merits of the good and the evil? But all of us confess God to be just. Therefore the whole is included in order." When he had said this, he sprang back from his couch, and now in a gentler voice, since no one made answer to him, he said, "Do you answer me nothing—not even you, who pressed me to these things?"

**1. 7. 20.** To him I said, "A new religion has now come upon you in this; I yield, I say. But what seems good, I will answer by daylight, which now seems to me to be returning—unless that brightness clothing the windows is the moon's. At the same time, I must keep silent, lest forgetfulness swallow up your great goods, Licentius. For when would our writings not demand that these things be committed to them? I will plainly tell you what I think: I will argue against you as much as I can; for no greater triumph can be given me than if you overcome me. But if your weakness—which, less fed by the learning of the disciplines, will perhaps not be able to sustain so great a god—should yield either to the cleverness or to some sharp error of the men whose part I shall try to take, the matter itself will warn you how great strength you must prepare, that you may return more firmly to him; and at the same time because I want this discussion of ours to come out more polished; for I ought not to give it to coarse ears. For our Zenobius often discussed many things with me about the order of things, and I could never satisfy his deep questioning, whether because of the obscurity of the matters or because of the narrowness of the times. And he was so impatient of frequent postponements that, to force me to answer more carefully and more fully, he even challenged me with a poem—and a good poem, for which you should love him the more. But it could neither then be read to you, most remote from the study of these things, nor can it now. For his departure was so sudden and disturbed by that tumult that none of these things could come into our mind; for he had decided to leave it for me to answer, and many things concur to make this discourse be sent to him. The first is that it is owed; then, that it is fitting that even thus the kind of life we now lead should be made known to one so benevolent toward us; finally, that in rejoicing over hope for you he yields to no one. For even when he was present, out of friendship for your father, or rather for all of us, he was much anxious lest certain sparks of your talent, which he diligently noticed, should not so much be kindled into flame by my care as be extinguished by your carelessness. And when he learns that you are zealous for poetry too, he will rejoice so that I now seem already to see him exulting."

**1. 8. 21.** "You will do nothing more pleasing to me," he said; "but whether you laugh at my fickleness and boyish lightness, or whether it comes about in us by some truly divine prompting and order, I would not hesitate to tell you: I have suddenly become more sluggish toward those meters; another thing, far other, I know not what, has now shone again upon me with a kind of light. More beautiful is philosophy, I confess, than Thisbe, than Pyramus, than that Venus and Cupid and loves of every such kind." And with a sigh he gave thanks to Christ. I received these things—what shall I say?—gladly; or what shall I not say? Let each take it as he will, I care nothing, except that perhaps I rejoiced immoderately.

**1. 8. 22.** Meanwhile, after a little, the day opened itself up. They rose, and I, weeping, prayed much, when I hear Licentius chanting, joyfully and chattily, that verse of the prophet: *O God of hosts, turn us again, and show your face, and we shall be saved* [^1]. The day before, when he had gone outside after supper for the needs of nature, he had sung this a little more loudly than our mother could bear—that such things should be sung again and again in that place. For he said nothing else, since he had lately taken in the very tune of the chant and loved, as happens, an unaccustomed melody. That most religious woman, as you know, rebuked him for this very thing, that the place was unsuited to a song. Then he had said, jesting, "As if, indeed, if some enemy were to shut me up here, God would not hear my voice."

**1. 8. 23.** So in the morning, when he had come back alone (for each had gone out for the same reason), he came to my little bed. "Tell me truly," he said—"so may what you wish come about for us—what you think of me." And I, taking the young man's right hand, said, "What I think you perceive, you believe, you understand. For I do not suppose you sang for so long yesterday in vain, that the God of hosts might show himself to you, once you are turned to him." But he, recalling it with wonder, said, "You say something great, and true. For it moves me not a little that just now I was so reluctantly called away from those trifles of my poem, and now it irks and shames me to return to them, so wholly am I borne up into certain great and wondrous things. Is this not truly to be turned toward God? At the same time I rejoice also at this, that the scruple of superstition was cast upon me in vain—that I kept singing such things in such a place." "This does not displease me either," I said, "and I think it pertains to that order that we should say something even on this point. For I see that both the place itself, at which she was offended, and the night, fit that song. For from what things do you suppose we pray to be turned toward God and to see his face, except from a kind of filth and squalor of the body, and likewise from the darkness in which error wraps us? Or what else is it to be turned, except to be raised up into oneself out of the immoderation of vices, by virtue and temperance? And what else is the face of God than that very Truth for which we sigh, and to which, beloved by us, we render ourselves clean and beautiful?" "It could not be better said," he said, crying out. Then, more softly, as if into my ear: "See, I beg you, how many things have met together, that I may believe something is now happening for us by a more prosperous order."

**1. 8. 24.** "If you care for order," I said, "you must return to those verses. For the learning of the liberal disciplines—moderate, of course, and concise—makes lovers more lively and more persevering and more refined for embracing the truth, so that they more ardently desire it, more constantly pursue it, and at last cling more sweetly to what is called, Licentius, the happy life. At which name all raise themselves up and, as it were, attend to your hands, to see whether you have something you can give to the needy, hindered by various diseases. To them, when wisdom has begun to prescribe that they bear the physician and let themselves be cured with some patience, they fall back into their own rags. Wasted away by the heat of these, they scratch the itch of their wretched pleasures more gladly than, by enduring and undergoing the physician's warnings—a little hard and burdensome to their diseases—they would be restored to the health and the light of the sound. And so, content with that name and sense of the highest God as with an alms, they live wretched—yet they live. But other men, or, to speak more truly, other souls, while they wear this body, that best and most beautiful spouse seeks out as now worthy of his chamber, for whom not to live, but to live happily, is enough. Go, then, meanwhile, to those Muses. Nevertheless, do you know what I want you to do?" "Command what pleases you," he said. "When," I said, "Pyramus and that beloved of his have, one upon the other, slain themselves, as you are about to sing, in that very grief by which it is fitting that your song be more vehemently inflamed, you have a most convenient opportunity. Seize the execration of that foul lust and those poisonous fires, by which those pitiable things come about; then raise yourself wholly into the praise of pure and sincere love, by which souls, taught by the disciplines and made beautiful by virtue, are joined to the understanding through philosophy, and not only flee death but also enjoy a most happy life." Here he, silent and long nodding in thought, with a turn of his head went away.

**1. 8. 25.** Then I too rose, and having paid our daily prayers to God, we had begun to go to the baths (for that place was suited and familiar to us for discussion, when, with a gloomy sky, we could not be in the field), when behold, before the doors we noticed cocks joining in a too fierce battle. It pleased us to attend. For what do the eyes of lovers not range over, where do they not roam, lest from anywhere the beauty of reason—which by knowledge and unknowing measures and governs all things—give some hint, beauty that draws its gaping followers wherever and to whatever place it bids itself be sought? For from where, or where, can it not give a sign? As in those very cocks it was to be seen: the heads thrust forward intent, the feathers ruffled, the vehement blows, the most cautious avoidances, and in every movement of the irrational animals nothing that was not graceful—since, of course, another reason from above governed all things; and finally the very law of the victor: a proud crowing and the limbs gathered, as it were, into one orb, as if in the haughtiness of dominion; but the sign of the vanquished: the little feathers raised from the neck, and in voice and movement everything misshapen, and by that very fact somehow neat and beautiful by the laws of nature.

**1. 8. 26.** We asked many things: why all act thus; why for the sake of dominion over the females subject to them; why, then, the very appearance of the fight led us somewhat, even apart from that higher consideration, into the pleasure of the spectacle; what there was in us that sought many things removed from the senses; and what, again, that was captured by the invitation of the senses themselves. We said to ourselves: Where is there no law? Where is there not authority owed to the better? Where is there not a shadow of constancy? Where is there not an imitation of that truest beauty? Where is there no measure? And thence, warned that there should be a measure to watching, we went on where we had purposed. And there, as we could—diligently indeed, for the events were recent, and when could things so notable escape the memory of three eager men?—we gathered all the little works of our night-study into this part of the book; and nothing else was done by me that day, that I might spare my health, except that before supper I was accustomed daily to hear half a volume of Virgil with them, we everywhere considering nothing other than the measure of things. Which no one can fail to approve; but to perceive it, when anyone is doing something eagerly, is most difficult and most rare.

**1. 9. 27.** Then the next day, early in the morning, we came eagerly to the usual place and sat down in it. And I, both attentive upon me, said, "Be here, Licentius, as much as you can, and you too, Trygetius; for no small matter is at hand: we are inquiring about order. Why should I now, set as if in that school from which I rejoice to have somehow escaped, praise order to you copiously and in order? Receive, if you will—nay, make it that you are willing—that than which nothing can be said about its praise more briefly or, as it seems to me, more truly. Order is that which, if we hold to it in life, will lead us to God; and which, if we do not hold to it in life, we shall not come to God. But that we are going to come, unless my mind deceives me about you, we presume and hope. Therefore this question ought to be turned over among us most diligently and resolved. I could wish the others were present who are wont to take part with us in these affairs. I could wish, if it could be done, to have with me now—as intent as you are—not these only, but at least all our friends, whose talent I always admire; or at least Zenobius alone, whom I never received at leisure—as his greatness deserved—when he was working at so great a matter. But because that does not happen, they will read our writings, since we have now resolved not to lose words about these matters, and to bind the things themselves, fleeting from memory, with the bond, as it were, of writings, by which they may be brought back. And perhaps order itself demanded this, which procured their absence. For surely you too rise up with a more upright mind to so great a matter, because it is laid on us alone to carry it through; and when those read it who are our greatest care, if anything moves them to contradict, this discussion will beget for us other discussions, and the very succession of discourses will insert itself into the order of the discipline. But now, as I had promised, I shall oppose Licentius, as far as the matter allows—he who has now almost finished the whole cause, if he can solidly and firmly fortify it with a wall of defense."

**1. 10. 28.** Here, when I saw that they were, by their silence, their face, their eyes, the suspense and immobility of their limbs, and by the greatness of the matter, sufficiently moved and inflamed with desire of hearing, I said, "Then, Licentius, if it seems good to you, gather into yourself whatever of strength you can, file down whatever of sharpness you have, and embrace in a definition what this order is." Then he, when he heard himself forced to define, shuddered as if sprinkled with cold water, and looking at me with a more troubled face and, as happens, smiling at his very trepidation, said, "What is this? Why do I seem to you, as it were, to nod assent? Do you believe me filled with some adventitious spirit?" And at once rousing himself: "Or perhaps," he said, "there is something with me." And he was silent a little, that whatever notion he had about order might be drawn into a definition. Then, more upright, he said, "Order is that through which are done all the things that God has established."

**1. 10. 29.** "What of God himself?" I said. "Does he not seem to you to be done by order?" "Surely," said he, "he does seem so." "Then God is done," said Trygetius. And the other said, "Why? Do you deny that Christ is God, who both came to us by order, and says that he was sent by God the Father? If, then, God sent Christ to us by order, and we do not deny that Christ is God, then not only does God do all things, but God too is done by order." Here Trygetius, in doubt, said, "I do not know how I should take that. For when we name God, it is not Christ himself who, as it were, comes to mind, but the Father. But he comes to mind then, when we name the Son of God." "You are making a fine thing of it," said Licentius. "Shall we then deny that the Son of God is God?" Here Trygetius, since it seemed dangerous to answer him, yet forced himself and said, "He too is indeed God, but still we properly call the Father God." To him I said, "Restrain yourself rather; for the Son is not improperly called God." But he, moved by religious scruple, since he did not even want his words to be written down, while Licentius pressed that they remain—after the manner, that is, of boys, or rather of almost all people, alas!—as if, indeed, it were being done among us for the sake of boasting. When I rebuked this motion of his mind with rather grave words, he blushed; and at his disturbance I noticed Trygetius laughing and rejoicing. And to them both I said, "Is it thus you act? Does it not move you, by what masses of vices and darkness of ignorance we are pressed down and covered over? Is this that attention of yours, of a little while ago, over which—fool that I was—I rejoiced, and that rising-up toward God and the truth? O if you could see, even with eyes as bleared as mine, in what dangers we lie, of what disease that laughter shows the madness! O if you could see! How quickly, how at once, and how much more lastingly you would turn it into weeping. Wretches, do you not know where we are? That the souls of all the foolish and unlearned are sunk down is common; but to those sunk not in one and the same way does wisdom hold out aid and a hand. There are others, believe me, there are others who are called upward, others who are loosened into the depths. Do not, I beg you, double my miseries. Let my own wounds be enough for me, which—asking God, by almost daily weeping, that they be healed—I yet often convince myself that I am too unworthy to be healed as quickly as I wish. Do not, I beg you—if you owe me any love, any tie of kinship, if you understand how much I love you, how much I esteem you, how much care for your characters torments me, if I am worthy that you not neglect me, if, finally—God being witness—I do not lie that I wish nothing more for myself than for you—repay me my kindness; and if you gladly call me master, render me my wages: be good."

**1. 10. 30.** Here, when tears imposed a limit on me, lest I say more, Licentius, bearing it most ill that everything was being written down, said, "Why, what have we done, I pray you?" "Do you," I said, "still not even confess your sin? Do you not know that in that school I was wont to be gravely vexed because boys were led, not by the usefulness and beauty of the disciplines, but by love of the emptiest praise, so far that some were not even ashamed to recite the words of others and received applause (a thing to be groaned over!) from the very men whose were the things they recited? Thus you—although you have never, I think, done anything of the sort—are nevertheless trying to introduce and sow into philosophy, and into that life which I rejoice at last to have taken up, the last but, of all the rest, the more harmful plague of wasting rivalry and empty boasting; and perhaps, because I frighten you away from that vanity and disease, you will be more sluggish toward the studies of learning, and, driven back from the ardor of windy fame, you will freeze into the torpor of idleness. Wretched me, if it shall be necessary even now to endure such men, from whom vices cannot depart without the succession of other vices." "You will prove," said Licentius, "how much more purified we shall be. Only this we beg, by all that you love, that you be willing to forgive us, and order all those things to be deleted; at the same time, that you spare even the tablets which we no longer have. For nothing of the many things discussed by us has been transferred into the books." "By all means," said Trygetius, "let our penalty remain, that the very fame which entices us may, by its own scourge, frighten us away from love of it. For to make these writings known only to our friends and intimates, we shall sweat not a little." The other assented.

**1. 11. 31.** And meanwhile my mother came in and asked us what progress we had made; for the question was known to her too. When I had ordered both her entrance and her question to be written down, after our manner, she said, "What are you doing? Have I ever heard, even in those books you read, that women were brought into this kind of discussion?" To her I said, "I do not much care for the judgments of the proud and ignorant, who rush into the reading of books as into the greeting of people. For they do not consider what sort the people themselves are, but in what sort of clothes they are dressed and with how great a pomp of wealth and fortune they shine. For such people, in writings, do not much attend either to where the question comes from, or to what the disputants are striving to arrive at, or to what has been explained and accomplished by them. Yet because among them some are found whose minds are not to be despised (for they are sprinkled with certain seasonings of culture, and are easily led through the golden and painted doors to the most holy inner shrines of philosophy), our forebears have done enough for them too, whose books I see are known to you while we read them to you. And in these times—to omit the rest—a man most outstanding in talent and eloquence and in the very insignia and gifts of fortune, and, what is before all, in mind, Theodorus, whom you yourself know well, is bringing it about that neither now nor among posterity may any kind of human being rightly complain about the literature of our times. But as for my books, if the hands of any chance to touch them, and, having read my name, they do not say, 'Who is this fellow?' and throw down the volume, but, whether out of curiosity or excessive zeal, despising the lowliness of the threshold, proceed to enter, they will not take it amiss that I am philosophizing with you, nor perhaps will they despise any of those whose conversation is mingled in my writings. For they are not only free—which is enough for any liberal discipline, let alone philosophy—but born in the highest place among their own. But the writings of most learned men contain even cobblers turned philosophers, and far baser kinds of fortune; who nevertheless shone with so great a light of talent and virtue that they would in no way wish—even if they could, on any condition—to exchange their goods for any nobility of this kind. Nor will there be lacking, believe me, that kind of people to whom this very thing—that you philosophize with me—will be more pleasing than if they found here anything else of either delight or weight. For there were among the ancients women too who philosophized, and your philosophy pleases me very much."

**1. 11. 32.** "For, lest you be ignorant of anything, mother, this Greek word by which philosophy is named is in Latin called 'love of wisdom.' Whence even the divine Scriptures, which you vehemently embrace, teach that not philosophers altogether, but the philosophers of this world, are to be avoided and mocked. But that there is another world, most remote from these eyes, which the understanding of a few sound persons beholds, Christ himself sufficiently signifies, who does not say, *My kingdom is not of the world*, but, *My kingdom is not of this world* [^2]. For whoever thinks that all philosophy is to be fled wants nothing else of us than that we not love wisdom. I would therefore despise you in these writings of mine if you did not love wisdom; but I would not despise you if you loved it moderately; much less if you loved wisdom as much as I do. But now, since you love it much more than you love me, and I know how much you love me, and since you have so profited in it that you are now terrified by the horror neither of any chance misfortune nor of death itself—which is most difficult for the most learned men, and which all confess to be the highest citadel of philosophy—shall I not gladly give myself to you even as a pupil?"

**1. 11. 33.** Here, when she had said sweetly and reverently that I had never lied so much, and I saw that we had poured out so many words that they both had to be written down and there was now a limit to the book and no tablets remained, it pleased us to defer the question, and at the same time that I might spare my stomach. For those things which it seemed necessary to me to move against those young men had stirred it more than I wished. But when we had begun to depart, Licentius said, "Remember how many and how necessary things are supplied to us, to be received from you, through that most hidden and divine order, even without your knowing." "I see it," I said, "and I am not ungrateful to God; and I presume that you yourselves, who notice these things, will for that very reason be better." This was the whole of my business that day.
