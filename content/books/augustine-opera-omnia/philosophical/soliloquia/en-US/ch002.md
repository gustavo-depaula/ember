# Book II

**2. 1. 1.** Augustine: Our work has been interrupted long enough, and love is impatient, nor is there any limit to tears unless what is loved be given to love; therefore let us take up the second book.

Reason: Let us take it up.

Augustine: Let us believe that God will be present.

Reason: Let us believe it indeed, if even this is in our power.

Augustine: He himself is our power.

Reason: So pray as briefly and as perfectly as you can.

Augustine: God, ever the same, may I know myself, may I know you. The prayer is made.

Reason: You who wish to know yourself, do you know that you exist?

Augustine: I know it.

Reason: How do you know it?

Augustine: I do not know.

Reason: Do you feel yourself to be simple or manifold?

Augustine: I do not know.

Reason: Do you know that you move?

Augustine: I do not know.

Reason: Do you know that you think?

Augustine: I know it.

Reason: Then it is true that you think.

Augustine: True.

Reason: Do you know that you are immortal?

Augustine: I do not know.

Reason: Of all these things you said you do not know, which do you prefer to know first?

Augustine: Whether I am immortal.

Reason: Then you love to live?

Augustine: I confess it.

Reason: What, when you have learned that you are immortal? Will that be enough?

Augustine: That will indeed be great, but to me too little.

Reason: Yet this which is too little—how much will you rejoice in it?

Augustine: Very much.

Reason: You will weep no more, then?

Augustine: Not at all.

Reason: What if life itself were found to be such that in it you were permitted to know nothing more than you know? Would you hold back from tears?

Augustine: On the contrary, I would weep so much that there would be no life at all.

Reason: Then you love to live not for the sake of living itself, but for the sake of knowing.

Augustine: I yield to the conclusion.

Reason: What if that very knowledge of things made you wretched?

Augustine: I do not believe that can happen in any way. But if it is so, no one can be blessed; for now I am wretched from no other source than ignorance of things. And if knowledge of things also makes one wretched, then misery is everlasting.

Reason: Now I see all that you desire. For since you believe that no one is made wretched by knowledge, from which it is probable that understanding makes one blessed; and since no one is blessed unless living, and no one lives who does not exist: you wish to exist, to live, and to understand—but to exist in order to live, and to live in order to understand. Therefore you know that you exist, you know that you live, you know that you understand. But whether these things will be forever, or none of them will be, or whether something remains forever and something perishes, or whether these can be diminished and increased while all remain—this you wish to know.

Augustine: It is so.

Reason: If, then, we prove that we shall always live, it will also follow that we shall always exist.

Augustine: It will follow.

Reason: It will remain to inquire about understanding.

**2. 2. 2.** Augustine: I see a most evident order, and a most brief one.

Reason: Be here now, then, so as to answer the questioner cautiously and firmly.

Augustine: I am here.

Reason: If this world will remain forever, is it true that the world will remain forever?

Augustine: Who would doubt it?

Reason: What if it will not remain? Is it not then true that the world will not remain?

Augustine: I do not resist.

Reason: What, when it has perished, if it is going to perish? Will it not then be true that the world has perished? For so long as it is not true that the world has fallen, it has not fallen; therefore it is contradictory that the world has fallen and that it is not true that the world has fallen.

Augustine: I concede this too.

Reason: What of this? Does it seem to you that anything can be true without truth being?

Augustine: In no way.

Reason: Then truth will be, even if the world perishes.

Augustine: I cannot deny it.

Reason: What if truth itself should fall? Will it not be true that truth has fallen?

Augustine: And who denies that?

Reason: But there cannot be anything true if truth is not.

Augustine: I already conceded this a little while ago.

Reason: In no way, then, will truth fall.

Augustine: Go on as you have begun, for nothing is truer than this inference.

**2. 3. 3.** Reason: Now I would have you answer me: does it seem to you that the soul senses, or the body?

Augustine: The soul seems to.

Reason: What? Does the understanding seem to you to pertain to the soul?

Augustine: It plainly does.

Reason: To the soul alone, or to something else?

Augustine: I see nothing besides the soul, except God, in which I would believe understanding to be.

Reason: Now let us look at this. If someone told you that this wall is not a wall but a tree, what would you think?

Augustine: Either that his sense, or mine, is deceived, or that by this name he calls a wall.

Reason: What if the appearance of a tree appears to him in it, and that of a wall to you? Could not both be true?

Augustine: In no way; because one and the same thing cannot be both a tree and a wall. For although different things may appear to each of us, it is necessary that one of us undergo a false imagining.

Reason: What if it is neither a wall nor a tree, and you are both deceived?

Augustine: That indeed can happen.

Reason: This one case, then, you had passed over above.

Augustine: I confess it.

Reason: What if you recognize that something appears to you other than it is? Are you then deceived?

Augustine: No.

Reason: It can happen, then, that what appears is false, and yet the one to whom it appears is not deceived.

Augustine: It can.

Reason: We must confess, then, that it is not the one who sees false things who is deceived, but the one who assents to false things.

Augustine: We must plainly confess it.

Reason: What about the false itself? Why is it false?

Augustine: Because it is otherwise than it appears.

Reason: If, then, there are none to whom it may appear, there is nothing false.

Augustine: It follows.

Reason: Then falsity is not in things, but in the sense; and the one who does not assent to false things is not deceived. It is concluded that we are one thing and the sense another; since, when it is deceived, we can not be deceived.

Augustine: I have nothing to gainsay.

Reason: But when the soul is deceived, do you dare to say there is nothing false?

Augustine: How could I dare that?

Reason: But there is no sense without soul, no falsity without sense. Either the soul, then, works falsity, or it cooperates with it.

Augustine: The preceding points drag me to consent.

**2. 3. 4.** Reason: Now answer this: does it seem possible that there could ever be a time when falsity does not exist?

Augustine: How can this seem so to me, when the difficulty of finding truth is so great that it would be more absurd to say falsity cannot exist than to say truth cannot?

Reason: Do you think that one who does not live can sense?

Augustine: It cannot be.

Reason: It is concluded that the soul lives forever.

Augustine: You drive me too quickly into joy; step by step, I beg.

Reason: And yet, if those things were rightly conceded, I see no room for doubt in this matter.

Augustine: It is too quick, I say. So I am more easily led to think that I conceded something rashly than that I should now grow secure about the immortality of the soul. Nevertheless, unfold this conclusion, and show how it has been brought about.

Reason: You said that falsity cannot be without sense, and that it cannot not be. Therefore there is always sense. But there is no sense without soul; therefore the soul is everlasting. Nor has it the power to sense unless it lives. Therefore the soul always lives.

**2. 4. 5.** Augustine: O dagger of lead! For you could conclude that man is immortal, if I had granted you that this world could never be without man, and that this world is everlasting.

Reason: You are watching well. But still it is no small thing that we have brought about—that the nature of things cannot be without soul—unless perhaps there will at some time be no falsity in the nature of things.

Augustine: I confess that this is indeed a consequence. But now I think we must deliberate further, whether the points conceded above do not wobble. For I see that no small step has been made toward the immortality of the soul.

Reason: Have you considered well enough that you have given nothing rashly?

Augustine: Well enough indeed, but I see nothing for which to charge myself with rashness.

Reason: Then it is concluded that the nature of things cannot be without a living soul.

Augustine: Concluded so far as this: that some souls can be born in turn, and others die.

Reason: What if falsity were taken away from the nature of things? Would it not come about that all things are true?

Augustine: I see it follows.

Reason: Answer: from what does it seem to you that this wall is true?

Augustine: Because I am not deceived by its appearance.

Reason: That is, because it is as it appears.

Augustine: Yes.

Reason: If, then, a thing is false from being otherwise than it appears, and true from being as it appears, then, the one to whom it appears being taken away, there is nothing either false or true. But if there is no falsity in the nature of things, all things are true. Nor can anything appear except to a living soul. Therefore the soul remains in the nature of things, if falsity cannot be taken away; it remains, if it can.

Augustine: I see that what had already been concluded has been made more robust; but by this addition we have advanced nothing. For there still remains that which moves me most: that souls are born and perish, and that, so that they are not lacking to the world, this comes about not by their immortality but by succession.

**2. 4. 6.** Reason: Do corporeal things—that is, sensible things—seem to you able to be grasped by the understanding?

Augustine: They do not.

Reason: What of this? Does it seem to you that God uses the senses to know things?

Augustine: I dare affirm nothing rashly on this matter; but so far as one may conjecture, God in no way uses the senses.

Reason: Then we conclude that only the soul can sense.

Augustine: Conclude it for now, as far as is probably permitted.

Reason: What of this? Do you grant that this wall, if it is not a true wall, is not a wall?

Augustine: I would grant nothing more readily.

Reason: And that nothing, if it is not a true body, is a body?

Augustine: This too is so.

Reason: Then if nothing is true unless it is as it appears, and nothing corporeal can appear except to the senses, and nothing can sense except the soul, and if a thing is not a body unless it is a true body, it follows that a body cannot exist unless soul has existed.

Augustine: You press too hard, and I have no way to resist.

**2. 5. 7.** Reason: Attend more carefully to this.

Augustine: See, I am here.

Reason: Surely this is a stone, and it is true if it is not otherwise than it appears; and it is not a stone if it is not true; and it can appear only to the senses.

Augustine: Yes.

Reason: Then there are no stones in the most hidden bosom of the earth, nor anywhere at all where there are none who sense; nor would this stone exist, unless we saw it; nor will it be a stone when we have departed and no one else is present to see it. And if you close your money-boxes well, however many things you have shut up in them, they will hold nothing. Nor indeed is wood, on the inside, wood. For whatever is in the depth of a body that scarcely lets light through escapes all the senses, and so is compelled not to exist at all. For if it existed, it would be true; and nothing is true except what is as it appears; but that does not appear; therefore it is not true—unless you have something to answer to this.

Augustine: I see that this is born from the things I conceded; but it is so absurd that I would more readily deny whichever of those you please than concede that this is true.

Reason: I do not object. So see which you wish to say: that corporeal things can appear only to the senses, or that only the soul can sense, or that a stone or anything else exists but is not true, or that the true itself must be defined otherwise.

Augustine: Let us look, I beg you, at this last one.

**2. 5. 8.** Reason: Define the true, then.

Augustine: The true is what is as it appears to a knower, if he is willing and able to know.

Reason: Then what no one can know will not be true? Moreover, if the false is what appears otherwise than it is—what if this stone appears to one as a stone, to another as wood? Will the same thing be both false and true?

Augustine: That earlier point moves me more—how, if something cannot be known, it thereby comes about that it is not true. For that one thing is at once both true and false, I do not much care about. For I see that one thing, compared with different things, is at once both greater and smaller. But that comes about from the fact that nothing is greater or smaller in itself. For these are names of comparison.

Reason: But if you say that nothing is true in itself, do you not fear it will follow that nothing is in itself? For from the same source that this is wood, it is also true wood. Nor can it come about that, by itself—that is, without a knower—it is wood, and not true wood.

Augustine: Then I say this, and so define it, nor do I fear that my definition will be rejected for being too brief: the true seems to me to be that which is.

Reason: Then nothing will be false, because whatever is, is true.

Augustine: You have cast me into great straits, and I find nothing at all to answer. So it comes about that, though I wish to be taught in no other way than by these questions, I now dread to be questioned.

**2. 6. 9.** Reason: God, to whom we have committed ourselves, will without doubt bring help and free us from these straits, if only we believe and beseech him most devoutly.

Augustine: Nothing would I do more gladly at this point; for nowhere have I borne such darkness. God, our Father, who exhort us to pray, and who grant what you are asked—since, when we pray to you, we live better and are better—hear me as I struggle in this darkness, and stretch out your right hand to me. Hold out your light before me, call me back from my errors; with you as my guide, let me return into myself and into you. Amen.

Reason: Be here as fully as you can, and attend most watchfully.

Augustine: Tell me, I beg, if anything has been suggested to you, lest we perish.

Reason: Be here.

Augustine: See, you have me doing nothing else.

**2. 6. 10.** Reason: Let us first sift, again and again, what the false is.

Augustine: I would be amazed if it were anything other than what is not as it appears.

Reason: Rather attend, and let us first question the senses themselves. For surely what the eyes see is not called false unless it has some likeness to the true. For example, a man we see in dreams is not, of course, a true man, but a false one, by the very fact that he has a likeness to the true. For who, seeing a dog, would rightly say he had dreamed of a man? Therefore that dog too is false, from the fact that it is like a true one.

Augustine: It is as you say.

Reason: What if someone awake, seeing a horse, thinks he sees a man? Is he not deceived because some likeness of a man appears to him? For if nothing appeared to him but the appearance of a horse, he could not suppose he saw a man.

Augustine: I yield entirely.

Reason: Likewise we call a painted tree false, and a face false that is returned from a mirror, and the motion of towers false to those sailing, and the bending of an oar false, for no other reason than that they are like the true.

Augustine: I confess it.

Reason: So too we are deceived in twins, in eggs, in several seals stamped by one ring, and in other such things.

Augustine: I follow entirely and concede.

Reason: The likeness of things, then, which pertains to the eyes, is the mother of falsity.

Augustine: I cannot deny it.

**2. 6. 11.** Reason: But all this forest, unless I am mistaken, can be divided into two kinds. For it lies partly in equal things, partly in inferior ones. Things are equal when we say that this is as like that as that is like this, as was said of twins or of the impressions of a ring. They are inferior when we say that what is inferior is like the better. For who looks into a mirror and rightly says that he is like that image, and not rather that it is like him? Now this kind lies partly in what the soul undergoes, partly in the things that are seen. But what the soul undergoes it undergoes either in the sense, as the motion of a tower, which is none; or within itself, from what it has received from the senses, such as are the visions of dreamers, and perhaps also of the deranged. Further, those things that appear in the very things we see are some produced by nature, others fashioned and feigned by living beings. Nature makes inferior likenesses by begetting or by reflecting. By begetting, when children are born like their parents; by reflecting, as with mirrors of every sort. For although men make most mirrors, they do not themselves fashion the images that are returned. Now the works of living beings are in pictures and figments of this sort; among which can also be included those things—if indeed they happen—which demons make. But shadows of bodies, since it is not far from the mark to call them like bodies and, as it were, false bodies, and since they cannot be denied to pertain to the judgment of the eyes, may fittingly be placed in that kind which nature makes by reflecting. For every body set against light reflects, and casts a shadow on the opposite side. Does anything seem to you to need contradicting?

Augustine: To me, nothing. But I eagerly await where these things are tending.

**2. 6. 12.** Reason: Well, we must bear it patiently until the other senses report to us that falsity dwells in the likeness of the true. For in hearing too there come about almost as many kinds of likeness: as when, hearing the voice of someone speaking whom we do not see, we think it some other person to whom he is like in voice; and among inferior cases there is the witness of the echo, or that ringing of the ears themselves, or in water-clocks a certain imitation of the blackbird or crow, or what dreamers or the deranged seem to themselves to hear. Now those little false notes, as the musicians call them, it is incredible how much they attest to the truth, as will appear later; yet even they—which is enough for now—are not far from a likeness of those they call true. Do you follow this?

Augustine: Most gladly. For I labor in no way to understand.

Reason: Then, not to delay—does it seem to you that one lily can be easily distinguished from another by smell, or thyme-honey from thyme-honey of different hives by taste, or the softness of a swan's feathers from a goose's by touch?

Augustine: It does not seem so.

Reason: What, when we dream that we smell, or taste, or touch such things? Are we not deceived by a likeness of images that is the more inferior the more empty it is?

Augustine: You speak the truth.

Reason: It appears, then, that in all the senses, whether in equal or in inferior things, we are either deceived by an enticing likeness, or, even if we are not deceived—suspending our assent or distinguishing the difference—we nevertheless call those things false which we detect to be like the true.

Augustine: I cannot doubt it.

**2. 7. 13.** Reason: Now attend, while we run over the same things again, so that what we are trying to show may become clearer.

Augustine: Here I am; say what you will. For I have resolved to bear this going-around once and for all, nor will I grow weary in it, with such great hope of reaching where I feel we are tending.

Reason: You do well. But attend: when we see like eggs, does it seem to you that we can rightly say any one of them is false?

Augustine: In no way does it seem so. For if they are all eggs, they are all true eggs.

Reason: What, when we see an image returned from a mirror? By what signs do we grasp that it is false?

Augustine: By the fact that it is not held, makes no sound, does not move of itself, does not live, and by countless other signs which it would be long to pursue.

Reason: I see you do not wish to linger, and your haste must be indulged. So, not to repeat each thing: if those men too whom we see in dreams could live, speak, be touched by the waking, and there were no difference between them and those whom, awake and sound, we address and see, would we then call them false?

Augustine: How could that rightly be said?

Reason: Then if they were true insofar as they appeared most like the true, and there were no difference at all between them and true men, while they were false insofar as they were proved unlike by those or other differences—must we not confess that likeness is the mother of truth, and unlikeness of falsity?

Augustine: I have nothing to say, and I am ashamed of my too-rash assent earlier.

**2. 7. 14.** Reason: It is ridiculous that you should be ashamed, as if it were not for this very purpose that we chose conversations of this kind. Since these, because we hold them when speaking only with ourselves, I wish to be called and titled "Soliloquies"—a new word indeed, and perhaps a harsh one, but apt enough for showing the matter. For since truth cannot be sought better than by questioning and answering, and since scarcely anyone is found who is not ashamed to be refuted in debate, and since for that reason it almost always happens that a matter well brought forward for discussion is shouted down by an unruly clamor of obstinacy, even with a laceration of minds, often disguised, sometimes open—it pleased me, most peaceably, I think, and most conveniently, to seek the truth, with God's help, by questioning myself and answering myself. So there is no reason for you to fear, if anywhere you have rashly bound yourself, to go back and untie it; for there is no escape from here otherwise.

**2. 8. 15.** Augustine: You speak rightly; but what I conceded wrongly I do not plainly see—unless perhaps that the false is rightly said to be what has some likeness to the true, since nothing else at all worthy of the name of the false occurs to me; and yet, again, I am forced to confess that things are called false from the fact that they differ from the true. From which it is concluded that this very unlikeness is the cause of falsity. And so I am confounded; for nothing easily comes to my mind that is begotten by contrary causes.

Reason: What if this is the one kind of its sort in the nature of things, the only one so constituted? Or do you not know that, though you run through innumerable kinds of animals, only the crocodile is found to move the upper part of its jaw in chewing—especially since scarcely anything can be found so like anything else that it is not in some respect also unlike?

Augustine: I see these things indeed; but when I consider that what we call false has both something like the true and something unlike, I cannot discern from which part rather it has deserved the name of the false. For if I say from the part that is unlike, there will be nothing that cannot be called false; for there is nothing that is not unlike something else which we grant to be true. Likewise, if I say it must be called false from the part that is like, not only will those eggs cry out against it, which are true by the very fact that they are most alike, but I shall not even so escape the one who would force me to confess that all things are false, since I cannot deny that all things are in some respect like one another. But suppose I do not fear to answer that likeness and unlikeness together bring it about that something is rightly called false—what way of escape will you give me? For I shall be pressed nonetheless to report that all things are false; since all things, as was said above, are found both like one another in some part and unlike. It would remain for me to say that nothing else is false except what is otherwise than it appears, were I not afraid of all those monsters which I thought I had long since sailed past. For I am driven back there by an unexpected dizziness, to say that the true is what is as it appears. From which it follows that nothing can be true without a knower; where I must dread a shipwreck on most hidden reefs, which are true even if they are not known. Or, if I say that the true is what is, it will be concluded, anyone gainsaying it, that the false is nowhere. And so those tides return, nor do I see that I have advanced anything for all the patience of your delays.

**2. 9. 16.** Reason: Rather attend; for I will by no means bring myself to think that we have implored the divine help in vain. For I see that, having tried everything as far as we could, nothing remains that may justly be called false except either what feigns itself to be what it is not, or what altogether strives to be and is not. But that former kind of the false is either deceptive or lying. For a thing is rightly called deceptive which has a certain appetite for deceiving; and this cannot be understood without a soul. But it comes about partly by reason, partly by nature: by reason, in rational animals, as in man; by nature, in beasts, as in the little fox. Now what I call lying is done by those who lie. They differ from the deceptive in this: that every deceptive thing seeks to deceive, but not everyone who lies wishes to deceive; for mimes and comedies and many poems are full of lies, with the will rather to please than to deceive, and nearly all who jest are lying. But he is rightly called deceptive or deceiving whose business it is that someone be deceived. Those, however, who do not act so as to mislead, but nevertheless feign something, no one doubts are to be called either liars merely, or, if not even that, at least lying. Unless you have something to say against this.

**2. 9. 17.** Augustine: Go on, I beg; for now perhaps you have begun to teach things not false about false things. But now I await what that kind is which you said strives to be and is not.

Reason: Why should you not await it? They are the very same things, many of which we recalled above. Or does it not seem to you that your image in the mirror wants, as it were, to be you yourself, but is false for this reason, that it is not?

Augustine: This very much seems so.

Reason: What of every painting, or likeness of any kind, and all such works of craftsmen? Do they not strive to be that to whose likeness each is made?

Augustine: I am altogether led to agree.

Reason: Now those things by which the sleeping or the deranged are deceived, you grant, I think, belong to this kind.

Augustine: None more so; for nothing more strives to be such as the waking or the sound discern; and yet they are false in this, that they cannot be what they strive to be.

Reason: Why should I now say more about the motion of towers, or the immersed oar, or the shadows of bodies? It is plain, I think, that they must be measured by this rule.

Augustine: Most plain.

Reason: I say nothing of the other senses; for no one who considers will fail to find that what is called false in the very things we sense is what strives to be something and is not.

**2. 10. 18.** Augustine: You speak rightly; but I wonder why those poems and jests, and the other deceptions, seemed to you to be set apart from this kind.

Reason: Because, of course, it is one thing to wish to be false, another to be unable to be true. And so we can join the very works of men—comedies, or tragedies, or mimes, and other such things—to the works of painters and sculptors. For a painted man can no more be a true man, though he strives toward the appearance of a man, than can those things written in the books of the comic poets. For they do not wish to be false, nor are they false by any appetite of their own, but by a certain necessity, so far as they could follow the will of their maker. But on the stage Roscius was by his will a false Hecuba, by nature a true man; but by that same will also a true tragic actor, in that, namely, he fulfilled what was set for him; yet a false Priam, in that he was made to resemble Priam, but was not Priam himself. From this now arises something marvelous, which nevertheless no one doubts to be so.

Augustine: What is it?

Reason: What do you think, except that all these things are true in some respect from the very source from which they are false in another, and that the only thing of advantage to their own truth is that they are false with respect to something else? From which they in no way reach what they either wish or ought to be, if they shun being false. For how could the one I mentioned be a true tragic actor, if he were unwilling to be a false Hector, a false Andromache, a false Hercules, and countless others? Or how would a painting be true, if the horse were not false? How would there be a true image of a man in a mirror, if not a false man? Therefore, if it is of advantage to certain things, in order that they be something true, that they be something false, why do we so greatly dread falsities, and seek truth as a great good?

Augustine: I do not know, and I wonder much—unless it be that in those examples I see nothing worthy of imitation. For we ought not to be true as actors are, or as the things reflected from mirrors, or as Myron's heifers cast in bronze, namely true in some manner of our own while sketched out and likened to another's manner, and false for that reason; but rather to seek that true which is not, as it were, two-faced, set against itself, so that in one part it is true and in another false.

Reason: You seek something great and divine. Yet if we find it, shall we not confess that by these very things truth is brought about and, as it were, forged—from which is named everything that is in any way called true?

Augustine: I assent not unwillingly.

**2. 11. 19.** Reason: What, then, seems to you? Is the discipline of disputation true or false?

Augustine: Who would doubt it is true? But grammar too is true.

Reason: As true as that other?

Augustine: I do not see what could be truer than the true.

Reason: That, surely, which has nothing false in it; and in gazing on it you stumbled a little while ago over those things which, I know not how, could not be true unless they were false. Or do you not know that all those fabulous and openly false things belong to grammar?

Augustine: I do not fail to know that; but, I think, they are not false through grammar, but rather, whatever they are, they are exhibited through it. For a fable is a falsehood composed for usefulness or delight. But grammar is the discipline that guards and governs articulate speech; by the necessity of its profession it is compelled to gather even all the figments of human language which have been committed to memory and letters—not making them false, but teaching and asserting a certain true account about them.

Reason: Rightly indeed; I care nothing now whether these things have been well defined and distinguished by you, but I ask this: whether grammar itself shows this to be so, or rather that discipline of disputation.

Augustine: I do not deny that the power and skill of defining, by which I have just tried to separate these things, is to be attributed to the art of disputation.

**2. 11. 20.** Reason: What of grammar itself? If it is true, is it not true insofar as it is a discipline? For "discipline" is named from "learning"; but no one can be said not to know what he has learned and holds; and no one knows false things. Therefore every discipline is true.

Augustine: I do not see what is rashly conceded in this little argument. Yet it moves me, lest by it grammar should seem to anyone to make even those fables true; for these too we learn and hold.

Reason: Did our teacher not want us to believe and to know what he taught?

Augustine: On the contrary, he urged vehemently that we should know.

Reason: Did he ever urge that we believe Daedalus flew?

Augustine: That, indeed, never. But certainly, unless we held the fable, he made it so that we could scarcely hold anything in hand.

Reason: Do you deny, then, that it is true that this is a fable, and that Daedalus was thus reported?

Augustine: I do not deny that this is true.

Reason: Then you do not deny that you learned something true when you learned these things. For if it were true that Daedalus flew, and boys took this as a feigned fable and repeated it, they would by that very fact retain false things, insofar as what they repeated was true. For from this arose what we wondered at above: that the fable about Daedalus's flight could not be a true fable unless it were false that Daedalus flew.

Augustine: Now I hold this; but what we gain from it I await.

Reason: What, except that the argument is not false by which we gathered that a discipline cannot be a discipline unless it teaches true things?

Augustine: And what does this have to do with the matter?

Reason: Because I want you to tell me from what grammar is a discipline; for from that source it is true, from which it is a discipline.

Augustine: I do not know what to answer you.

Reason: Does it not seem to you that, if nothing in it were defined, and nothing distributed and distinguished into genera and parts, it could in no way have been a discipline?

Augustine: Now I understand what you say; nor does any face of any discipline occur to me in which definitions and divisions and reasonings have not done this very thing by which it is called a discipline—while it is declared what each thing is, while to each is rendered its own without confusion of parts, while nothing proper is omitted, nothing foreign is reckoned in.

Reason: Then also that very thing by which it is called true.

Augustine: I see it follows.

**2. 11. 21.** Reason: Now answer: what discipline contains the principles of definitions, divisions, and partitions?

Augustine: It was already said above that these are contained in the rules of disputation.

Reason: Then grammar was created by the same art, so as to be a discipline, and so as to be true—which earlier you defended from falsity. And I may conclude this not of grammar alone, but plainly of all the disciplines. For you said, and said truly, that no discipline occurred to you in which the right of defining and distributing had not done this very thing, so that it might be a discipline. But if they are true insofar as they are disciplines, will anyone deny that truth itself is that through which all disciplines are true?

Augustine: I am nearly altogether ready to assent; but this moves me, that we also number the rules of disputation themselves among these same disciplines. Therefore I rather judge that to be truth by which this very argument too is true.

Reason: Excellent altogether, and most watchful; but you do not deny, I think, that it is true insofar as it is a discipline.

Augustine: On the contrary, that is the very thing that moves me. For I have noticed that it too is a discipline, and is for that reason called true.

Reason: What then? Do you think it could have been a discipline in any other way, unless all things in it were defined and distributed?

Augustine: I have nothing else to say.

Reason: But if this office belongs to it, then through itself it is a true discipline. Will anyone, then, think it strange if that by which all things are true is itself true from itself, if it is also truth?

Augustine: Nothing stands in the way of my proceeding straight to this opinion.

**2. 12. 22.** Reason: Then attend to the few things that remain.

Augustine: Bring out whatever you have, provided it be such that I may understand it and gladly concede it.

Reason: It does not escape us that "to be in something" is said in two ways. In one way, such that it can also be separated and be elsewhere, as this wood in this place, or the sun in the east; in the other, such that something is in a subject in such a way that it cannot be separated from it, as in this wood the form and appearance we see, as in the sun the light, as in fire heat, as in the mind a discipline, and any others there are like these. Or does it seem otherwise to you?

Augustine: These things are most familiar to us, and were most zealously taken in and learned from early youth; so that I cannot, when questioned about them, fail to concede them without any deliberation.

Reason: What of this? Do you not concede that what is in a subject inseparably cannot remain if the subject itself does not remain?

Augustine: This too I see is necessary; for whoever attends carefully to things understands that, the subject remaining, what is in the subject can fail to remain. Since the color of this body can be changed either by reason of health or by age, while the body itself has not yet perished. And this does not hold equally in all things, but in those in which the things that are in the subjects coexist not in order that the subjects themselves may exist. For it is not in order that this wall may exist that it comes to have the color we see in it; since, even if by some chance it grows black or white, or changes to some other color, the wall nonetheless remains and is called a wall. But fire, if it lacked heat, would not even be fire; nor can we call anything snow unless it is white.

**2. 13. 23.** As for what you asked, who would grant, or to whom could it seem possible, that what exists in a subject should remain when the subject itself perishes? For it is monstrous and utterly contrary to truth that something which would not exist unless it existed in that subject could exist even when the subject no longer existed.  
Reason: Then what we were seeking has been found.

Augustine: What are you saying?

Reason: What you hear.

Augustine: Is it now clearly settled, then, that the mind is immortal?

Reason: If the things you have conceded are true, most clearly—unless perhaps you say that the mind, even if it dies, is still a mind.

Augustine: I would never say that; but I say that by the very fact that it perishes, it comes about that it is not a mind. Nor does it call me back from this opinion that it was said by great philosophers that the thing which, wherever it comes, furnishes life, cannot admit death into itself. For although light, wherever it can enter, makes that thing shine, and cannot admit darkness into itself because of that memorable force of contraries, nevertheless it is extinguished, and that place, the light being extinguished, is darkened. So that which resisted the darkness, and in no way admitted darkness into itself, yet by perishing made room for it, just as it could also by departing. And so I fear that death may befall the body as darkness befalls a place—the soul sometimes departing like light, but sometimes being extinguished in the very place—so that there is not security against every death of the body, but some kind of death is to be desired, by which the soul is led out of the body unharmed and brought to a place, if there is any such place, where it cannot be extinguished. Or, if not even this is possible, and the soul is kindled in the body itself like a light, and cannot last elsewhere, and every death is a certain extinction of the soul in the body, or of life, then some kind must be chosen, so far as a man is permitted, by which the very life that is lived may be lived with security and tranquility—though I do not know how this can come about if the soul dies. O most blessed are they to whom it has been made certain, whether by themselves or by anyone else, that death is not to be feared, even if the soul perishes! But to me, wretched, no reasonings, no books, have yet been able to make this certain.

**2. 13. 24.** Reason: Do not groan; the human mind is immortal.

Augustine: From what do you prove this?

Reason: From those things which, with great caution, I think, you conceded above.

Augustine: I do not recall giving anything to you, who questioned me, with less than full watchfulness; but gather now the sum itself, I beg you. Let us see where we have arrived by such great windings, and do not, I pray, question me further. For if you are going to enumerate briefly the things I conceded, why is my answer needed again? Or is it to bring delays upon my joys in vain, if we have perhaps accomplished some good?

Reason: I will do what I see you wish, but attend most carefully.

Augustine: Speak now; here I am. Why do you slay me with delay?

Reason: Everything that is in a subject, if it always remains, the subject itself must necessarily also always remain. And every discipline is in the mind as in a subject. Therefore it is necessary that the mind always remain, if a discipline always remains. But a discipline is truth, and, as reason persuaded at the beginning of this book, truth always remains. Therefore the mind always remains, nor is the mind said to be dead. Therefore only he can, not absurdly, deny the mind to be immortal who proves that something of the foregoing was not rightly conceded.

**2. 14. 25.** Augustine: Now I want to give myself over to joy, but I am held back somewhat by two causes. For first it moves me that we have used so great a circuit, following I know not what chain of reasonings, when the whole of what was at issue could have been shown as briefly as it has now been shown. So it makes me anxious that for so long our discourse went about as if to lay an ambush. Then I do not see how a discipline—especially that of disputation—is always in the mind, since so few are versed in it, and whoever knows it was for so long, from infancy, untaught. For we cannot say either that the minds of the unskilled are not minds, or that there is in the mind a discipline they do not know. But if this is exceedingly absurd, it remains that either truth is not always in the mind, or that discipline is not truth.

**2. 14. 26.** Reason: You see that our reasoning has not made such great circuits in vain. For we were asking what truth is, which not even now, in this forest of things, with nearly all the paths wandered over, do I see we have been able to track down. But what are we to do? Shall we give up what we began, and wait to see whether any books of others fall into our hands that may satisfy this question? For I think many were written before our age which we have not read; and now, that we may not form opinions about what we do not know, we have it plain that on this matter there have been writings both in verse and in loosened prose—and by those men whose writings cannot lie hidden from us, and whose talents we know to be such that we cannot despair of finding in their letters what we wish; especially since here before our eyes is that man in whom we have come to know eloquence itself, which we grieved to be dead, revived perfect. Will he, who by his writings has taught us the manner of living, let us be ignorant of the nature of living?

Augustine: I do not think so, and I hope much from that source; but I grieve at one thing: that we are not able, as we wish, to open to him our zeal either toward himself or toward wisdom. For surely he would pity our thirst, and would pour himself out much sooner than now. For he is secure, since he has already persuaded himself fully of the immortality of the soul, and does not know that there are perhaps some who have come to know well enough the misery of this ignorance, and to whom, especially when they ask, it would be cruel not to bring help. But that other man indeed knows, by reason of our friendship, our ardor; yet he is so far away, and we are now so situated, that there is scarcely even the means of sending a letter to him. I believe that by now, in his Transalpine leisure, he has finished the poem by which the fear of death, charmed away, may flee, and the numbness and chill of the soul, hardened with ancient ice, may be driven off. But meanwhile, until these things come about which are not in our power, is it not most shameful that our leisure should be wasted, and the whole mind itself hang bound upon an uncertain decision?

**2. 15. 27.** What has become of our prayer to God, which we have offered and still offer, that he open a path for us as we seek, not riches, not bodily pleasures, not public distinction and honors, but our soul and himself? Will he thus forsake us, or be forsaken by us?  
Reason: It is wholly foreign to him to forsake those who desire such things; from which it ought also to be foreign to us to forsake so great a leader. So, if you please, let us briefly take up again from what those two points were established: either that truth always remains, or that truth is the method of disputation. For you said these wobble, so as to make us less secure about the sum of the whole matter. Or shall we rather ask this: how a discipline can be in an unskilled mind, which we cannot but call a mind? For from this you seemed disturbed, so that it became necessary for you to doubt again about the things you had conceded.

Augustine: Rather let us first thrash out those points, then we will see what this latter is. For so, I think, no controversy will remain.

Reason: Let it be so; but be wholly present and most cautious. For I know what happens to you when you attend: while you hang too eagerly upon the conclusion, and expect it to be drawn at any moment, you concede the things asked without examining them carefully.

Augustine: Perhaps you speak the truth; but I will strive against this kind of disease as much as I can. Only begin now to ask, that we may not linger over superfluous things.

**2. 15. 28.** Reason: From this, so far as I remember, we concluded that truth cannot perish: that not only if the whole world perishes, but even if truth itself perishes, it will be true that both the world and truth have perished. But nothing is true without truth; in no way, then, does truth perish.

Augustine: I recognize these things, and I wonder much if they are false.

Reason: Then let us look at the other point.

Augustine: Let me consider a little, I beg you, lest I shamefully come back here again.

Reason: Will it then not be true that truth has perished? If it will not be true, then truth does not perish. If it will be true, from what source will it be true after the fall of truth, when there is now no truth?

Augustine: I have nothing more to think over and consider; go on to something else. We will surely do, as far as we can, what we can, so that learned and prudent men may read these things and correct our rashness, if there is any; for I think that neither now nor ever shall I be able to find anything to say against this.

**2. 15. 29.** Reason: Is truth called anything, then, except that by which whatever is true is true?

Augustine: In no way.

Reason: Is anything rightly called true except what is not false?

Augustine: To doubt this is madness.

Reason: Is not the false that which is adapted to the likeness of something, and yet is not that to which it appears like?

Augustine: I see nothing else, indeed, that I would more gladly call false. But still, even what is far from a likeness of the true is wont to be called false.

Reason: Who denies it? But still, something that has some imitation of the true.

Augustine: How so? For when it is said that Medea flew with winged serpents yoked together, that matter in no part imitates the true; since it is nothing, and a thing that does not exist at all can imitate nothing.

Reason: You speak rightly; but you do not notice that a thing which is altogether nothing cannot even be called false. For if it is false, it exists; if it does not exist, it is not false.

Augustine: Shall we not, then, call that about Medea—I know not what monstrous thing—false?

Reason: Not at all; for if it is false, how is it a monstrous thing?

Augustine: I see a strange thing: so then, when I hear, *Huge winged serpents yoked to the yoke*, I do not call it false?

Reason: You do, plainly; for there is something you may call false.

Augustine: What, I beg?

Reason: That proposition, of course, which is enunciated in the verse itself.

Augustine: And what imitation of the true does it have, then?

Reason: Because it would be enunciated in the same way even if Medea had truly done that. So a false proposition, by its very enunciation, imitates true propositions. If it is not believed, it imitates the true ones only in this, that it is so stated, and is merely false, not also deceiving. But if it gains belief, it imitates also true things that are believed. Augustine: Now I understand that there is a great difference between the things we say and the things about which we say something; and so I now assent. For this alone was calling me back: that whatever we call false is not rightly so called unless it has an imitation of something true. For who would not justly be laughed at for saying that a stone is false silver? Yet if anyone says that a stone is silver, we say that he speaks falsely—that is, that he utters a false proposition. But tin or lead, I think, we not absurdly call false silver, because the thing itself, as it were, imitates it; nor is our proposition thereby false, but the very thing about which it is enunciated.

**2. 16. 30.** Reason: You understand well. But see whether we can fittingly call silver too by the name of false lead.

Augustine: It does not please me.

Reason: Why so?

Augustine: I do not know; except that I see it is said very much against my will.

Reason: Is it perhaps because silver is better, and the term is said as if in reproach of it; whereas it is a kind of honor, as it were, to lead, if it is called false silver?

Augustine: You have plainly explained what I meant. And therefore I believe those are rightly held infamous and not to be trusted who flaunt themselves in womanly dress—whom I do not know whether I should better call false women or false men. True actors, however, and truly infamous men, we can without doubt call them; or, if they lie hidden, and nothing is called infamous except from base repute, we call them truly worthless, not without truth, I think.

Reason: We will have another place for discussing these matters. For many things are done which seem, as it were, base to the popular eye, yet are shown to be honorable by some praiseworthy end. And it is a great question whether, for the sake of freeing his homeland, a man clothed in a womanly tunic ought to deceive the enemy—being by this very thing a false woman, but perhaps thereby a truer man; and whether a wise man who has somehow ascertained that his life will be necessary to human affairs would rather die of cold than, if there were no other way, clothe himself in feminine garments. But about this, as has been said, we will see elsewhere. For surely you discern how much inquiry it requires—how far these things ought to go, lest one fall into certain inexcusable basenesses. But now, what is enough for the present question, I think it already appears, and cannot be doubted, that nothing is false except by some imitation of the true.

**2. 17. 31.** Augustine: Proceed to the rest; for of this I am well persuaded.

Reason: Then I ask this: whether, apart from the disciplines by which we are educated—among which the very pursuit of wisdom should be reckoned—we can find anything so true that it is not, like the theatrical Achilles, false in some part, so that it can be true in another.

Augustine: Many things, it seems to me, can be found. For the disciplines do not have this stone; and yet, in order that it be a true stone, it does not imitate anything in respect of which it is called false. And once this one example is mentioned, you see that we may now pass over countless others, which occur of their own accord to those who think.

Reason: I see it plainly. But do they not seem to you to be included under the one name of body?

Augustine: They would seem so, if I either held for certain that the void is nothing, or thought that the mind itself is to be numbered among bodies, or even believed God to be some body. If all these things exist, I do not see that they are false and true by imitation of anything.

Reason: You send us far afield; but I will use a shortcut as far as I can. For surely what you call the void is one thing, and what you call truth another.

Augustine: Far other. For what is more void than I, if I think truth to be something void, or so greatly desire something void? For what else do I desire than to find truth?

Reason: Then perhaps you also concede this: that nothing is true that is not made true by truth.

Augustine: This was already plain some time ago.

Reason: Do you doubt that there is nothing void except the void itself, or surely body?

Augustine: I do not doubt it at all.

Reason: Then, I suppose, you believe truth to be some body.

Augustine: In no way.

Reason: What is in the body?

Augustine: I do not know; it is nothing to the matter. For I think you know this too: that, if there is a void, it is rather where no body is.

Reason: This indeed is plain.

Augustine: Why, then, do we delay?

Reason: Does it seem to you either that truth made the void, or that anything is true where truth is not?

Augustine: It does not seem so.

Reason: Then the void is not true, since neither can it be made void by that which is not void; and it is manifest that what lacks truth is not true; and in general, what is called void is so called from the fact that it is nothing. How, then, can that be true which is not? Or how can that be which is utterly nothing?

Augustine: Come now, let us leave the void aside as void.

**2. 18. 32.** Reason: What do you say of the rest?

Augustine: What?

Reason: That which you see supports me most. For there remain the mind and God; and if these two are true because truth is in them, no one doubts the immortality of God. The mind, however, is believed immortal if truth, which cannot perish, is proved to be in it also. So let us now look at this last point: whether body is not truly true—that is, whether truth is not in it, but only, as it were, a certain image of truth. For if even in a body, which is certain enough to admit of perishing, we find such a true thing as is in the disciplines, then the discipline of disputation will not straightway be the truth by which all disciplines are true. For body too is true, which does not seem to be formed by the method of disputation. But if body too is true by some imitation, and for that reason not truly true, perhaps there will be nothing to hinder the method of disputation from being taught to be truth itself.

Augustine: Meanwhile let us inquire about body; for not even when this is settled do I see this controversy ended.

Reason: How do you know what God wills? So attend: for I think a body is held together by some form and appearance, which if it did not have, it would not be a body; if it had a true one, it would be a mind. Or is it to be thought otherwise?

Augustine: I assent in part, but about the rest I doubt; for unless some figure is held, I concede that it is not a body. But how, if it had a true one, it would be a mind, I do not sufficiently understand.

Reason: Do you remember nothing at all of the beginning of the first book, and of that geometry of yours?

Augustine: You have reminded me well; I remember it plainly, and most gladly.

Reason: Are such figures found in bodies as that discipline shows?

Augustine: On the contrary, it is incredible how much more inferior they are proved to be.

Reason: Which of these, then, do you think true?

Augustine: Do not, I beg, think that even this needs to be asked of me. For who is so blind in mind that he does not see that those figures taught in geometry dwell in truth itself, or that truth is in them; whereas those bodily figures, since they seem, as it were, to tend toward the geometric ones, have some imitation of truth—I know not what—and are therefore false? For now I understand the whole of what you were working to show.

**2. 19. 33.** Reason: What need, then, is there now to inquire about the discipline of disputation? For whether the geometric figures are in truth, or truth is in them, no one doubts that they are contained in our soul—that is, in our understanding—and through this, truth too is compelled to be in our mind. But if any discipline whatever is so in the mind, as in a subject, inseparably, and truth cannot perish, why, I ask, do we doubt the perpetual life of the mind because of some intimacy, I know not what, with death? Or do that line, or square, or circle have other things they imitate in order to be true?

Augustine: I cannot believe that in any way—unless perhaps a line is something other than length without breadth, and a circle something other than a line drawn round on every side, verging equally toward the middle.

Reason: Why, then, do we hesitate? Or where these things are, is truth not?

Augustine: May God avert such madness.

Reason: Or is a discipline not in the mind?

Augustine: Who would say that?

Reason: But can it perhaps be that, the subject perishing, what is in the subject remains?

Augustine: When will this be persuaded to me?

Reason: It remains, then, that truth should perish.

Augustine: How can that come about?

Reason: Then the soul is immortal. Now at last believe your own reasonings, believe truth; it cries out both that it dwells in you, and that it is immortal, and that its seat cannot be taken from it by any death of the body. Turn away from your shadow, return into yourself; there is no perishing for you, except that you have forgotten that you cannot perish.

Augustine: I hear, I come to my senses, I begin to recollect. But unfold, I beg, the things that remain: how in an unskilled mind—for we cannot call it mortal—a discipline and truth are to be understood to be.

Reason: That question calls for another volume, if you wish it treated carefully; and at the same time I see that the things which, as best we could, have been investigated, must be reviewed by you. For if nothing of what has been conceded is doubtful, I think we have accomplished much, and with no small security may inquire into the rest.

**2. 20. 34.** Augustine: It is as you say, and I willingly obey your instructions. But let me obtain at least this, before you set a term to the volume: that you briefly explain what difference there is between the true figure, which is contained in the understanding, and the one which thought fashions for itself, which in Greek is called either phantasia or phantasm.

Reason: You ask what only the purest can see, and for the vision of which you are too little exercised; nor do we now work at anything through these circuits except your exercising, so that you may be fit to see it. Yet perhaps I can briefly make plain how great the difference is, so that it can be taught. Suppose you have forgotten something, and others wish, as it were, to recall it to your memory. They say, then: Is it this, or that?—offering different things as if they were alike. You, however, neither see the thing you wish to recall, and yet you see that it is not what is named. When this happens to you, does it seem like forgetfulness in every way? For that very discernment, by which you do not admit what you are falsely reminded of, is a certain part of recollection.

Augustine: It seems so.

Reason: Such, then, do not yet see the true; yet they cannot be deceived and misled, and they know well enough what they are seeking. But if someone says to you that a few days after you were born you laughed, you do not dare to say it is false; and if he is an author worthy of trust, you will not recollect it but believe it; for all that time is buried from you in deepest forgetfulness. Or do you think otherwise?

Augustine: I agree entirely.

Reason: This, then, differs very much from that forgetfulness; but that is a middle case. For there is another, nearer and closer to recollection and to the seeing again of truth; like it is when we see something and recognize for certain that we once saw it, and affirm that we know it; but where, or when, or how, or in whose presence it came into our knowledge, we busy ourselves to recover and recollect. As, if this happens to us concerning a man, we even ask where we knew him; and when he has reminded us, suddenly the whole matter is poured into the memory as if a light, and no further labor is needed to remember. Or is this kind unknown or obscure to you?

Augustine: What is plainer than this? Or what happens to me more often?

**2. 20. 35.** Reason: Such are those who are well educated in the liberal disciplines; since without doubt they unearth those things, buried in forgetfulness within themselves, by learning, and in a way dig them up again; yet they are not content, nor do they hold back, until they gaze most widely and most fully on the whole face of truth, of which a certain splendor already gleams reddening in those arts. But from these, certain false colors and forms pour themselves, as it were, into the mirror of thought, and often deceive and mislead inquirers who think that the whole is what they know, or what they are seeking. These are those imaginations to be avoided with great caution; they are caught out as deceptive when they vary as the mirror of thought, as it were, is varied, while that face of truth remains one and unchangeable. For then thought paints for itself a square of one size and another, and sets it, as it were, before the eyes; but the inner mind, which wishes to see the true, should rather turn itself, if it can, to that according to which it judges all those to be squares.

Augustine: What if someone tells us that it judges according to what it is accustomed to see with the eyes?

Reason: Why, then, does it judge—if at least it is well educated—that any true ball is touched by a true plane at a point? What such thing did the eye ever see, or can it see, when nothing of the kind can be fashioned by the very imagination of thought? Or do we not prove this when we describe even the smallest circle in imagination by the mind, and draw lines from it to the center? For when we have drawn two, between which scarcely a needle could be inserted, we cannot then by imaginary thought draw others in the middle, so as to reach the center without any commingling; whereas reason cries out that countless lines can be drawn, and that they cannot touch one another in those incredible straits except at the center, so that in every interval of them a circle too could be drawn. Since that phantasy cannot accomplish this, and fails more than the eyes themselves—since it is impressed upon the mind through them—it is manifest both that it differs much from truth, and that, while truth is seen, the phantasy is not seen.

**2. 20. 36.** These matters will be discussed more thoroughly and precisely when we begin to examine understanding, the part of our inquiry appointed for after we have explained and considered, as far as we can, everything that troubles us about the soul’s life. For I do not think you have little fear that human death, even if it does not kill the soul, may nevertheless bring forgetfulness of everything, even of truth itself, if any has been discovered.  
Augustine: It cannot be said enough how greatly this evil is to be feared. For what will that eternal life be, or what death is not to be preferred to it, if the soul lives as we see it live in a child just born?—to say nothing of that life which is lived in the womb; for I do not think it is none.

Reason: Be of good courage; God will be present, as we already feel, to us who seek—God who promises something most blessed after this body, and most full of truth, without any falsehood.

Augustine: May it be as we hope.
