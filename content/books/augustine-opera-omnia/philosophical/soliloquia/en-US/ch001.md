# Book I

**1. 1. 1.** As I had long been turning many different things over within myself, and for many days had been diligently seeking myself and my good, and what evil I ought to avoid, suddenly someone spoke to me—whether it was I myself, or someone else from without or within, I do not know, for this is precisely what I am striving so earnestly to know. He spoke to me, then:  
Reason: Look now—suppose you have discovered something. To whom will you entrust it, so that you may go on to other things?

Augustine: To memory, of course.

Reason: Is memory so great that it keeps everything you have thought out safely?

Augustine: That is hard—indeed, it cannot.

Reason: Then it must be written down. But what are you to do, since your health refuses the labor of writing? And these things ought not to be dictated, for they call for complete solitude.

Augustine: You speak the truth. And so I have no idea at all what to do.

Reason: Pray for health and for the help by which you may reach what you long for, and commit this very prayer to writing, so that you may grow more courageous through your own offspring. Then gather what you find briefly into a few short conclusions. And do not now trouble yourself to invite a crowd of readers; these things will be enough for a few of your fellow citizens.

Augustine: I will do so.

**1. 1. 2.** God, founder of the universe, grant me first that I may pray to you well, then that I may make myself worthy to be heard by you, and finally that you may set me free. God, through whom all things, which of themselves would not be, strive to be. God, who do not permit even that which destroys itself to perish utterly. God, who from nothing created this world, which the eyes of all perceive to be most beautiful. God, who do not make evil, and who make it so that it does not become the worst. God, who to the few who take refuge in that which truly is, show that evil is nothing. God, through whom the universe is perfect even with its baser part. God, from whom there is no discord even to the uttermost, since the worse things are in concord with the better. God, whom everything that can love loves, whether knowingly or unknowingly. God, in whom are all things, and to whom nevertheless the disgrace of no creature is disgraceful, nor does its malice harm you, nor its error lead you astray. God, who have willed that none but the pure should know the truth. God, father of truth, father of wisdom, father of true and supreme life, father of blessedness, father of the good and the beautiful, father of the light that is grasped by the mind, father of our awakening and enlightenment, father of the pledge by which we are reminded to return to you.

**1. 1. 3.** I call upon you, God the Truth, in whom and from whom and through whom all things are true that are true. God the Wisdom, in whom and from whom and through whom all things are wise that are wise. God, the true and supreme Life, in whom and from whom and through whom all things live that truly and supremely live. God the Blessedness, in whom and from whom and through whom all things are blessed that are blessed. God the Good and the Beautiful, in whom and from whom and through whom all things are good and beautiful that are good and beautiful. God, the light grasped by the mind, in whom and from whom and through whom all things shine intelligibly that shine intelligibly. God, whose kingdom is the whole world, which the senses do not know. God, from whose kingdom a law is copied down even into these earthly kingdoms. God, from whom to turn away is to fall, to whom to turn back is to rise again, in whom to remain is to stand firm. God, from whom to depart is to die, to whom to return is to come back to life, in whom to dwell is to live. God, whom no one loses unless deceived, whom no one seeks unless admonished, whom no one finds unless purified. God, whom to abandon is to perish, to attend to is to love, to see is to possess. God, to whom faith rouses us, hope lifts us up, charity joins us. God, through whom we conquer the enemy, I beseech you. God, whom we received that we might not perish utterly. God, by whom we are admonished to keep watch. God, through whom we distinguish good things from evil. God, through whom we flee evils and follow goods. God, through whom we do not yield to adversities. God, through whom we serve well and rule well. God, through whom we learn that things are foreign which we once thought our own, and that things are our own which we once thought foreign. God, through whom we do not cling to the baits and enticements of evils. God, through whom small things do not diminish us. God, through whom our better part is not made subject to the worse. God, through whom death is swallowed up in victory. God, who turn us back to yourself. God, who strip from us what is not, and clothe us in what is. God, who make us able to be heard. God, who unite us. God, who lead us into all truth. God, who speak to us all good things, who neither make us mad nor allow us to be made so by anyone. God, who call us back to the way. God, who lead us to the door. God, who bring it about that the door is opened to those who knock. God, who give us the bread of life. God, through whom we thirst for the drink which, once drunk, will let us thirst no more. God, who convict the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment. God, through whom we are not moved by those who do not believe. God, through whom we condemn the error of those who think the souls of men have no merits before you. God, through whom we do not serve the weak and beggarly elements. God, who purify us and prepare us for divine rewards, come to me in your favor.

**1. 1. 4.** Whatever I have said, you, the one God, come to my aid—one eternal true substance, where there is no discrepancy, no confusion, no passing away, no want, no death; where there is the highest concord, the highest clarity, the highest constancy, the highest fullness, the highest life; where nothing is lacking and nothing overflows; where the one who begets and the one whom he begets are one. God, whom all things serve that serve; whom every good soul obeys; by whose laws the heavens revolve, the stars run their courses, the sun governs the day, the moon tempers the night; and by whose laws the whole world, through days by the alternation of light and night, through months by the waxing and waning of the moon, through years by the succession of spring, summer, autumn, and winter, through cycles of years by the completion of the sun's course, through the great cosmic periods by the return of the stars to their risings—keeps the great constancy of things, so far as sensible matter allows, by the orderings and recurrences of the seasons. God, by whose laws, standing fast through the ages, the unsteady motion of changeable things is not allowed to be thrown into disorder, but is always called back to a likeness of stability by the reins of the circling ages; by whose laws the soul's choice is free, and rewards to the good and punishments to the evil are apportioned by necessities fixed throughout all things. God, from whom all good things flow even down to us, by whom all evils are kept away from us. God, above whom is nothing, beyond whom is nothing, without whom is nothing. God, beneath whom is the whole, in whom is the whole, with whom is the whole. You who made man to your own image and likeness, as anyone who knows himself recognizes. Hear, hear, hear me, my God, my Lord, my king, my father, my cause, my hope, my possession, my honor, my home, my homeland, my salvation, my light, my life. Hear, hear, hear me in that way of yours known to so few.

**1. 1. 5.** Now you alone I love, you alone I follow, you alone I seek, you alone I am ready to serve, for you alone rule justly; I long to be under your authority. Command, I beg, and order whatever you will, but heal and open my ears, that I may hear your voice. Heal and open my eyes, that I may see your bidding. Drive madness from me, that I may recognize you. Tell me where to look, that I may behold you; and I hope to do all that you command. Receive, I pray, your fugitive, Lord, most merciful Father; by now I have surely paid penalty enough, long enough have I served your enemies, whom you hold beneath your feet, long enough have I been the plaything of deceits. Receive me, your servant, fleeing from these things, for these same things received me as a stranger when I was fleeing from you. I feel that I must return to you: let your door lie open to me as I knock; teach me how one comes to you. I have nothing but will; I know nothing except that fleeting and perishable things are to be despised, and certain and eternal things sought. This I do, Father, because this alone I know; but from where one comes to you, I do not know. Suggest it to me, show it to me, supply me provisions for the road. If those who take refuge in you find you by faith, give me faith; if by virtue, virtue; if by knowledge, knowledge. Increase in me faith, increase hope, increase charity. O wonderful and singular goodness of yours!

**1. 1. 6.** Toward you I make my way, and from you again I ask the means by which one makes his way to you. For if you forsake us, one perishes; but you do not forsake us, for you are the highest good, whom no one has sought rightly and failed to find. And everyone has sought you rightly whom you have made to seek rightly. Make me, Father, seek you; deliver me from error; as I seek you, let nothing else meet me in your place. If I desire nothing else but you, let me find you now, I beg, Father. But if there is in me the craving for anything superfluous, cleanse me yourself, and make me fit to see you. As for the rest, concerning the health of this mortal body of mine, so long as I do not know what use it may be to me, or to those I love, I commit it to you, most wise and best Father, and I will pray for it according to what you advise for the time being. This only I ask of your most excellent mercy: that you turn me wholly to yourself, and let nothing resist me as I strive toward you, and that you command me, while I act and carry this very body, to be pure, magnanimous, just, and prudent, a perfect lover and receiver of your wisdom, and worthy of a dwelling place, and a dweller in your most blessed kingdom. Amen, amen.

**1. 2. 7.** Augustine: There, I have prayed to God.

Reason: What, then, do you wish to know?

Augustine: All these very things for which I have prayed.

Reason: Sum them up briefly.

Augustine: I desire to know God and the soul.

Reason: Nothing more?

Augustine: Nothing at all.

Reason: Then begin to seek. But first explain how, if God were shown to you, you could say: This is enough.

Augustine: I do not know how he must be shown to me, that I might say: This is enough. For I do not believe I know anything in the way I desire to know God.

Reason: What, then, are we to do? Do you not think you must first know how it would be enough for you to know God—so that, once you have arrived there, you seek no further?

Augustine: I do think so; but how it can be done, I do not see. For what have I ever understood that is like God, so that I could say: As I understand this, so I wish to understand God?

Reason: Since you do not yet know God, how do you know that you know nothing like God?

Augustine: Because if I knew something like God, I would surely love it; but as it is, I love nothing other than God and the soul, and I know neither of them.

Reason: Then you do not love your friends?

Augustine: How can I, loving the soul, not love them?

Reason: In that way, then, do you also love fleas and bedbugs?

Augustine: I said I love the soul, not animals.

Reason: Then either men are not your friends, or you do not love them; for every man is an animal, and you said you do not love animals.

Augustine: They are men, and I love them—not because they are animals, but because they are men; that is, because they have rational souls, which I love even in robbers. For I may love reason in anyone, while I justly hate the one who misuses what I love. And so I love my friends all the more, the more they make good use of their rational soul, or at least the more they desire to use it well.

**1. 3. 8.** Reason: I accept that. But still, if someone said to you, I will make you know God as you know Alypius, would you not give thanks and say, This is enough?

Augustine: I would indeed give thanks, but I would not say it is enough.

Reason: Why, may I ask?

Augustine: Because I do not know God even as I know Alypius, and yet I do not know Alypius well enough.

Reason: See, then, that you do not impudently wish to know God well enough, when you do not know Alypius well enough.

Augustine: That does not follow. For compared with the stars, what is cheaper than my dinner? And yet I do not know what I shall dine on tomorrow; but I profess, without impudence, to know under what sign the moon will be.

Reason: So it is enough for you to know God as you know under what sign the moon will run tomorrow?

Augustine: It is not enough; for this I confirm by the senses. But I do not know whether God, or some hidden cause of nature, may suddenly change the order and course of the moon; and if that happened, all that I had presumed would be false.

Reason: And do you believe this can happen?

Augustine: I do not believe it. But I am asking what I know, not what I believe. Now everything we know we may perhaps rightly be said also to believe; but not everything we believe do we also know.

Reason: In this matter, then, you reject all testimony of the senses?

Augustine: I reject it utterly.

Reason: Well? That friend of yours whom you said you still do not know—do you wish to know him by the senses, or by the understanding?

Augustine: What I know of him by the senses—if indeed anything is known by the senses—is both cheap and sufficient; but that part by which he is my friend, that is, his very mind, I desire to grasp by the understanding.

Reason: Can it be known otherwise?

Augustine: In no way.

Reason: Then do you dare to say that your friend, and a very close one, is unknown to you?

Augustine: Why should I not dare? For I judge that law of friendship most just which prescribes that one love a friend neither less nor more than oneself. And so, since I do not know myself, what insult can be felt by the one whom I have called unknown to me—especially since, as I believe, he does not even know himself?

Reason: If, then, the things you wish to know are of the kind that the understanding grasps, when I said that you wished impudently to know God while not knowing Alypius, you ought not to have offered me your dinner and the moon as a parallel, if these, as you said, pertain to the senses.

**1. 4. 9.** But what is that to us? Now answer this: if what Plato and Plotinus said about God is true, is it enough for you to know God as they knew him?  
Augustine: It does not follow that, if what they said is true, they must also have known it. For many speak copiously of what they do not know—just as I myself said I desire to know all that I prayed for, which I would not desire if I already knew it. So could I therefore not speak those things? For I spoke not what I grasped by understanding, but what, gathered from here and there, I committed to memory, and to which I gave as much trust as I could; but to know is something else.

Reason: Tell me, please—do you at least know, in the discipline of geometry, what a line is? Augustine: That I plainly know.

Reason: And in making this claim you do not fear the Academics?

Augustine: Not at all. For they did not want the wise man to err; but I am not wise. And so I do not yet fear to profess knowledge of the things I know. And if, as I desire, I reach wisdom, I will do what it advises.

Reason: I do not object. But, as I had begun to ask: just as you know a line, do you also know the ball they call a sphere?

Augustine: I do.

Reason: Do you know both equally, or one more or less than the other?

Augustine: Equally indeed. For in neither am I deceived.

Reason: These things—did you perceive them by the senses, or by the understanding?

Augustine: Indeed, in this matter I have used the senses as one uses a ship. For once they carried me to the place I was making for, and I dismissed them, and standing as if on solid ground I began to turn these things over in thought, my footsteps wavered for a long time. So it seems to me that one could sail on dry land sooner than perceive geometry by the senses—although at first they seem to help learners somewhat.

Reason: Then you do not hesitate to call the knowledge of these things, if you have any, a science?

Augustine: I do not—unless the Stoics forbid it, who grant science to none but the wise man. I do not deny that I have a perception of these things, which they concede even to folly; but I do not greatly fear these men either. I hold for certain as science the things you have asked about. Go on now; let me see where you are aiming with these questions.

Reason: Do not hurry; we are at leisure. Only attend closely, lest you concede anything rashly. I am eager to make you rejoice over things in which you fear no mishap—and do you bid me rush, as though it were a trifling matter?

Augustine: May God so grant, as you say. So question by your own judgment, and rebuke me more severely if I do anything of the sort again.

**1. 4. 10.** Reason: Is it clear to you, then, that a line can in no way be split lengthwise into two lines?

Augustine: It is clear.

Reason: What about crosswise?

Augustine: How else, except that it can be cut infinitely?

Reason: And is it equally clear to you that a sphere, cut from the middle on any one side, cannot have even two equal circles?

Augustine: Equally clear in every way.

Reason: What about a line and a sphere? Do they seem to you to be some one thing, or do they differ from each other in any way?

Augustine: Who would not see that they differ greatly?

Reason: But if you know the one as well as the other, and yet, as you admit, they differ greatly from each other, then is there a single, undifferentiated knowledge of differing things?

Augustine: Who has denied it?

Reason: You did, a little while ago. For when I asked you how you wished to know God, so that you could say, This is enough, you answered that you could not explain it, because you had grasped nothing in the way you desire to grasp God—for you knew nothing like God. So what now? Are a line and a sphere alike?

Augustine: Who would say that?

Reason: But I had asked not what you know that is like God, but what you know in the way you desire to know God. For you know a line as you know a sphere, though a line is not as a sphere is. Therefore answer: is it enough for you to know God as you know that geometric ball—that is, to have no doubt about God, as you have none about it?

**1. 5. 11.** Augustine: I beg you—however vehemently you press and convict me, I still do not dare to say that I wish to know God as I know these things. For not only the things, but the very knowledge of them, seems to me dissimilar. First, because a line and a ball do not differ so much that one discipline does not contain the knowledge of them both; but no geometer has professed to teach God. Next, if the knowledge of God and of these things were equal, I would rejoice as much that I know them as I presume I shall rejoice when God is known. But as it is, I despise these things very much in comparison with him, so that sometimes it seems to me that, if I understand him, and see him in the only way he can be seen, all these things will perish from my notice—since now, for love of him, they scarcely come to mind.

Reason: Granted that you will rejoice more, and much more, when God is known than over these things—still, not because of a dissimilarity in the understanding of them. Unless perhaps you behold the earth with one kind of sight and the clear sky with another, although the sight of the latter delights you much more than that of the former. But if the eyes are not deceived, I believe that, if you were asked whether it is as certain to you that you see the earth as that you see the sky, you would have to answer that it is just as certain—even though you take joy not so much in the beauty and splendor of the earth as in that of the sky.

Augustine: This comparison moves me, I confess, and I am led to agree that the true and certain objects of the disciplines differ from the intelligible majesty of God as much as the earth, in its own kind, differs from the sky.

**1. 6. 12.** Reason: You are rightly moved. For the reason that speaks with you promises to show God to your mind as the sun is shown to the eyes. For the senses of the soul are, as it were, the mind's own eyes; and whatever is most certain in the disciplines is like those things which are lit up by the sun so that they can be seen—such as the earth and all earthly things. But God himself is the one who gives the light. And I, reason, am in minds as sight is in the eyes. For to have eyes is not the same as to look, nor is to look the same as to see. So the soul needs three things: to have eyes it can now use well, to look, and to see. The eye of the soul is the mind purified of every stain of the body—that is, already withdrawn and cleansed from desires for mortal things—which nothing else affords it first but faith. For what cannot yet be shown to it while it is stained with vices and sick—since it cannot see unless it is healthy—if it does not believe that otherwise it will not see, it takes no pains for its own health. But what if it does believe that the matter is as it is said, and that it will see if it can see, yet despairs of being able to be healed? Does it not utterly cast itself down and despise itself, and refuse to obey the physician's instructions?

Augustine: It is altogether so, especially because the disease must feel those instructions to be harsh.

Reason: Therefore hope must be added to faith.

Augustine: So I believe.

Reason: What if it both believes that all is as it is said, and hopes that it can be healed, yet does not love the very light that is promised, does not desire it, and judges that it ought meanwhile to be content with its own darkness, which by habit has become pleasant? Does it not reject that physician nonetheless?

Augustine: It is altogether so.

Reason: Therefore a third thing, charity, is necessary.

Augustine: Nothing at all is so necessary.

Reason: Without these three, then, no soul is healed so as to be able to see its God—that is, to understand him.

**1. 6. 13.** When, then, he has healthy eyes, what remains?  
Augustine: To look.

Reason: The soul's looking is reason; but since it does not follow that everyone who looks sees, a right and perfect looking—that is, one which seeing follows—is called virtue; for virtue is reason either right or perfect. But this looking itself, even with eyes now healthy, cannot turn toward the light unless those three remain: faith, by which it believes that the matter is such that, once seen, it makes one blessed; hope, by which, having looked well, it presumes that it will see; charity, by which it desires to see and to enjoy fully. Then upon the looking follows the vision of God itself, which is the end of the looking—not that the looking no longer exists, but that it has nothing further to direct itself toward. And this is truly perfect virtue: reason reaching its end, which the blessed life accompanies. The vision itself is that understanding which is in the soul, made up of one who understands and that which is understood; just as, in the eyes, what is called seeing consists of the sense itself and what is sensed, and if either is taken away, nothing can be seen.

**1. 7. 14.** Therefore, when it has fallen to the soul to see God—that is, to understand God—let us see whether those three are still necessary to it. Why should faith be necessary, when it already sees? Hope likewise, since it already holds. But to charity not only will nothing be taken away; very much will even be added. For when the soul has seen that singular and true beauty, it will love it more; and unless it fixes its eye on it with immense love, and never anywhere turns from looking, it will not be able to remain in that most blessed vision. But while the soul is in this body, even if it sees—that is, understands—God most fully, nevertheless, because the senses of the body also do their own work, which, though of no avail to deceive, is yet not idle, there can still be said to be a faith by which they are resisted and that other thing is believed to be true instead. Likewise, because in this life, though the soul is already blessed in understanding God, it nevertheless endures many troubles of the body, it must hope that after death all these discomforts will be no more. So neither does hope, while it is in this life, forsake the soul. But when, after this life, the soul has gathered itself wholly into God, charity remains, by which it is held there. For it cannot be said to have faith that those things are true, when it is troubled by no interruption of falsehoods; nor does anything remain for it to hope, since it possesses the whole in security. Three things, then, pertain to the soul: that it be healthy, that it look, that it see. But the other three—faith, hope, and charity—are always necessary for the first and second of those; for the third, all three are necessary in this life, but after this life, charity alone.

**1. 8. 15.** Now, as far as the present occasion requires, learn from me something about God as well through that analogy with sensible things. God is intelligible, and the objects contemplated in the disciplines are also intelligible; yet they differ greatly. For both the earth and light are visible, but the earth cannot be seen unless illuminated by light. Thus we must believe that the things taught in the disciplines, which whoever understands them admits without any doubt to be most true, cannot be understood unless illuminated by something else, as though by their own sun. Therefore, just as three things may be noticed in this sun—that it exists, that it shines, and that it illuminates—so there are three things in that most hidden God whom you wish to understand: that he exists, that he is understood, and that he makes other things understood. I venture to teach you to understand these two things, yourself and God. But answer how you have received these statements: as probable or as true?  
Augustine: Probable indeed; and I have risen to greater hope, which I must confess. For apart from those two things about the line and the ball, you have said nothing that I would dare to say I know.

Reason: That is no wonder; for nothing has yet been so set out that perception of it should be demanded of you.

**1. 9. 16.** But why do we delay? We must set out on the way; yet let us first see, before everything else, whether we are healthy.  
Augustine: You must see to that, if you can look a little into yourself or into me; I will answer the questioner, if I have any sense of the matter.

Reason: Do you love anything besides the knowledge of yourself and of God?

Augustine: I could answer that I love nothing more, judging by the feeling I now have; but I answer more safely that I do not know. For it has often happened to me that, when I believed I was moved by nothing else, something would nevertheless come to mind that stung me quite differently than I had presumed. Likewise, often something falling into my thought has not pricked me, yet when it actually came, it disturbed me more than I had thought. But just now it seems to me that I can be moved by only three things: the fear of losing those I love, the fear of pain, the fear of death.

Reason: So you love both the life of your dearest ones with you, and your own good health, and your own life in this body; for otherwise you would not fear the loss of them.

Augustine: I confess it is so.

Reason: Then the fact that not all your friends are with you now, and that your health is less than whole, causes some distress in your mind; for I see that this too follows.

Augustine: You see rightly; I cannot deny it.

Reason: What if you suddenly felt and confirmed that you were sound in body, and saw all whom you love enjoying ample leisure together with you in harmony—would you not be moved to some exultation of joy?

Augustine: Truly, to some; indeed, if these things especially came about suddenly, as you say, how could I contain myself? How would I even be permitted to disguise that kind of joy?

Reason: Then you are still shaken by all the diseases and disturbances of the mind. So what impudence is it in such eyes to wish to see that sun?

Augustine: You have drawn your conclusion as though I had no sense at all of how far my health has advanced, or what plagues have withdrawn, and how much remains. Grant me that much.

**1. 10. 17.** Reason: Do you not see that these bodily eyes, even when healthy, are often struck back by the light of that sun and turned away, and flee to their own darkness? You think about how far you have advanced; you do not think about what you wish to see. And yet I will sift this very thing with you: what do you think we have cast off? Do you desire no riches?

Augustine: This indeed is not new to me now. For though I am in my thirty-third year, it is nearly fourteen years since I ceased to desire such things, nor have I thought of anything in them, if by chance they were offered, beyond necessary sustenance and a liberal use. A single book of Cicero's readily persuaded me that riches are by no means to be sought, but, if they come, are to be administered most wisely and carefully.

Reason: What of honors?

Augustine: I confess, I ceased to desire them only just now, almost in these very days.

Reason: What of a wife? Does it not sometimes delight you—a beautiful, chaste, compliant, educated woman, or one you could easily educate yourself, bringing too (since you despise riches) only so much dowry as would make her in no way burdensome to your leisure—especially if you could hope, and be certain, that you would suffer no trouble from her?

Augustine: However much you wish to paint her and heap her with all good things, I have resolved that there is nothing I must flee so much as the marriage bed. I feel there is nothing that casts down a manly mind from its citadel more than feminine charms and that contact of bodies without which a wife cannot be had. And so, if it pertains to the duty of the wise man (which I have not yet ascertained) to give care to children, then whoever has intercourse only for this reason may seem to me admirable, but by no means to be imitated; for to attempt this is more dangerous than to succeed at it is fortunate. Therefore, justly and usefully enough, I believe, for the freedom of my soul, I have commanded myself not to desire, not to seek, not to take a wife.

Reason: I am not now asking what you have resolved, but whether you still struggle, or have already conquered lust itself. For it is the health of your eyes that is at issue.

Augustine: I seek nothing of this kind at all, I desire nothing; I even recall such things with horror and disdain. What more do you want? And this good grows in me day by day; for the more the hope grows of seeing that beauty for which I ardently burn, the more all my love and pleasure are turned toward it.

Reason: What of the pleasantness of food? How much care does it cost you?

Augustine: The things I have resolved not to eat do not move me at all. As for those I have not cut off, I confess I am delighted by them when present, yet in such a way that they may be taken away, seen or tasted, without any disturbance of mind. And when they are altogether absent, this appetite does not dare to insert itself as a hindrance to my thoughts. But about food and drink, or baths, and the other pleasures of the body, do not question me at all; I ask of them only so much as can be of service to my health.

**1. 11. 18.** Reason: You have made much progress; yet the things that remain hinder very greatly your seeing that light. But I am working at something that seems to me easy to show: either that nothing remains for us to subdue, or that we have made no progress at all, and that the rot of all those things we believe pruned away still remains. For I ask you: if you were persuaded that you could not live in the pursuit of wisdom together with many dear ones unless some ample family estate could support your needs, would you not desire and wish for riches?

Augustine: I agree.

Reason: What if this also appeared: that you would persuade many of wisdom if authority accrued to you from honor, and that these same friends of yours could not set a limit to their desires and turn themselves wholly to seeking God unless they too were honored, and that this could not happen except through your honors and rank? Would not these things too have to be desired, and great effort made for them to come about?

Augustine: It is as you say.

Reason: I will not dispute about a wife now; for perhaps no such necessity for marrying can arise—although, if it were certain that all whom you desire to live with at leisure in one place could be supported by her ample patrimony, she herself harmoniously allowing it, especially if she were of such noble birth that through her you could easily attain those honors you have already granted to be necessary, I do not know whether it would pertain to your duty to despise these things.

Augustine: When would I dare to hope for such a thing?

**1. 11. 19.** Reason: You say this as though I were now asking what you hope for. I do not ask what does not delight you when refused, but what delights you when offered. For it is one thing for a plague to be drained out, another for it to be lulled to sleep. To this purpose applies what was said by certain learned men: that all fools are mad in the same way that all mire stinks—which you do not always perceive, but only when you stir it. It makes a great difference whether desire is overwhelmed by the mind's despair or driven out by health.

Augustine: Although I cannot answer you, you will nevertheless never persuade me to judge that, in this disposition of mind in which I now feel myself to be, I have made no progress.

Reason: I believe it seems so to you because, although you could wish for these things, they would nevertheless seem worth seeking not for their own sake but for the sake of something else.

Augustine: That is what I was wishing to say. For when I desired riches, I desired them in order to be rich; and those very honors, whose desire I just answered I had subdued, I wanted because I was delighted by some sheen of theirs; and in a wife I always looked to nothing else, when I looked, but what pleasure she would bring me along with good repute. Then there was in me a true desire for these things; now I utterly disdain them all. But if there is no passage given me to the things I desire except through these, I do not seek them as things to be embraced, but accept them as things to be endured.

Reason: Excellent altogether; for I too judge that nothing should be called a desire for things which are sought for the sake of something else.

**1. 12. 20.** But I ask you: why do you wish the people you love either to live or to live with you?  
Augustine: My wish is that we may seek our souls and God together in harmony. For thus, whoever first attains discovery easily leads the rest to it without labor.

Reason: What if they are unwilling to seek these things?

Augustine: I will persuade them to be willing.

Reason: What if you cannot—because they think they have already found, or that these things cannot be found, or because they are hindered by cares and desire for other things?

Augustine: I will keep them, and they me, as best we can.

Reason: What if their very presence hinders you from your inquiry? Will you not labor and wish, if they cannot be otherwise, that they not be with you rather than be so?

Augustine: I confess it is as you say.

Reason: So you desire their life or presence not for its own sake, but for the sake of finding wisdom?

Augustine: I agree completely.

Reason: Well? Your own life—if it were certain to you that it was a hindrance to grasping wisdom, would you wish it to remain?

Augustine: I would flee it altogether.

Reason: Well? If you were taught that you could reach wisdom as well after leaving this body as while set within it, would you care whether you enjoyed what you love here or in another life?

Augustine: If I understood that I would meet nothing worse to drive me back from where I have advanced, I would not care.

Reason: So now you fear to die for this reason: lest you be entangled in some worse evil by which divine knowledge would be taken from you.

Augustine: I fear not only lest it be taken away, if perhaps I have grasped something, but also lest the access be closed off to me to those things I gape to grasp—though I think that what I already hold will remain with me.

Reason: So you wish this life too to remain not for its own sake, but for the sake of wisdom.

Augustine: It is so.

**1. 12. 21.** Reason: There remains bodily pain, which perhaps moves you by its own force.

Augustine: And this too I dread vehemently for no other reason than that it hinders me from seeking. For although these days I have been tortured by a most acute toothache, I was not indeed prevented from turning over in mind the things I had perhaps already learned; but I was utterly hindered from learning, for which I need the whole attention of my mind. Still, it seemed to me that if that brilliance of truth opened itself to my mind, I would either not feel that pain, or surely endure it as nothing. But since, even though I have sometimes borne nothing greater, yet often, thinking how much heavier pains could befall, I am driven at times to agree with Cornelius Celsus, who says that the highest good is wisdom and the highest evil bodily pain. Nor does his reasoning seem to me absurd. For since, he says, we are composed of two parts, namely mind and body, of which the former, the mind, is the better part, while the body is the worse, the highest good is the best of the better part, and the highest evil the worst of the worse; and the best in the mind is wisdom, while the worst in the body is pain. Therefore that man's highest good is to be wise, and his highest evil to feel pain—which is concluded, I think, without any falsity.

Reason: We will look at these things later. For perhaps the very wisdom we are striving to reach will persuade us otherwise. But if it shows this to be true, we will hold this opinion about the highest good and the highest evil without doubt.

**1. 13. 22.** We are now asking what kind of lover you are of wisdom, which you desire to see and hold in the chastest gaze and embrace, with no veil between you, as though naked. She permits this to only a very few of her most chosen lovers. If you burned with love for a beautiful woman, would she not rightly refuse herself to you if she discovered that you loved anything besides her? Will wisdom’s most chaste beauty reveal itself to you unless you burn for her alone?  
Augustine: Why, then, am I still held in suspense, wretch that I am, and put off by a pitiable torment? I have surely already shown that I love nothing else, since what is not loved for its own sake is not loved. But I love wisdom alone for its own sake; the rest—life, rest, friends—I wish present to me, or fear absent, for its sake. What limit can there be to love of that beauty, in which I not only do not envy others, but even seek very many to long for it with me, to gape for it with me, to hold it with me, and to enjoy it fully with me—the more my friends, the more the beloved will be shared among us?

**1. 13. 23.** Reason: Such, indeed, the lovers of wisdom ought to be. Such does she seek, whose union is truly chaste and without any defilement. But one does not reach her by a single road. Each one grasps that singular and most true good according to his own health and strength. It is a certain light, ineffable and incomprehensible, of minds. Let this common light teach us, as far as it can, how that one stands. For there are some eyes so healthy and vigorous that, as soon as they are opened, they turn to the sun itself without any trembling. To these, in a way, the light itself is health, and they need no teacher, but perhaps only an admonition. For these, to believe, to hope, to love is enough. But others are struck by the very brilliance they vehemently desire to see, and, not seeing it, often return to darkness with delight. To these it is dangerous—though they may now rightly be called healthy—to wish to show what they are not yet strong enough to see. So these must first be exercised, and their love usefully deferred and nourished. First, certain things must be shown them that do not shine of themselves but can be seen by light, such as a garment, or a wall, or some such thing. Then something that shines more beautifully, not indeed of itself but by that light, such as gold, silver, and the like, yet not so radiant as to hurt the eyes. Then perhaps this earthly fire must be shown modestly, then the stars, then the moon, then the glow of dawn, and the brightness of the whitening sky. Amid these, whether sooner or later, whether through the whole order or with some steps passed over, each one, growing accustomed according to his own health, will without trembling and with great pleasure see the sun. Something like this the best teachers do for those most zealous for wisdom who see it now, though not yet sharply. For to reach it by a certain order is the office of good instruction, but to reach it without order is a matter of scarcely credible good fortune. But for today we have written enough, I think; we must spare your health.

**1. 14. 24.** And on another day:  
Augustine: Give me, I beg, now if you can, that order. Lead me, go where you will, through what you will, how you will. Command anything hard, anything arduous, provided it be in my power, by which I do not doubt I shall reach where I desire.

Reason: There is one thing I can charge you with; I know nothing more. These sensible things are to be utterly fled, and great care must be taken, while we act this body, that our wings be not entangled by any of their birdlime—wings that we need whole and perfect to fly up from this darkness to that light, which does not deign even to show itself to those shut in this cage, unless they are such that, with the cage broken or dissolved, they can escape into their own native air. And so, when you are such that nothing earthly delights you at all, then—believe me—at that very moment, at that very point of time, you will see what you desire.

Augustine: When will that be, I beg you? For I do not think these things can come into utter contempt for me unless I have seen that in comparison with which they grow base.

**1. 14. 25.** Reason: In this way this bodily eye too could say: I will not love darkness once I have seen the sun. For this also seems somehow to pertain to the order, though it is far otherwise. The eye loves darkness because it is not healthy; but it cannot see the sun unless it is healthy. And in this the mind is often deceived, so that it thinks itself healthy and boasts of it; and because it does not yet see, it complains as if rightly. But that beauty knows when to show itself. For it performs the office of physician, and understands better who are healthy than do those very ones who are being healed. As for us, we seem to ourselves to see as far as we have emerged; but how deeply we were sunk, and how far we had advanced, we are permitted neither to think nor to feel, and in comparison with a graver disease we believe ourselves healthy. Do you not see how, as if free of care, we declared yesterday that no plague now held us, and that we love nothing but wisdom, and seek or wish the rest only for its sake? How filthy, how foul, how detestable, how horrible the embrace of a woman seemed to you when we inquired among ourselves about the desire for a wife! Surely this very night, lying awake, when we again took up the same matters with ourselves, you felt how differently than you had presumed those imagined caresses and that bitter sweetness titillated you—far, far less than is usual, yet again far otherwise than you had thought; so that thus that most secret physician showed you both things: from what your cure has freed you, and what remains to be cured.

**1. 14. 26.** Augustine: Be silent, I beseech you, be silent. Why do you torture me? Why do you dig so deep and descend so far? Now I cannot keep from weeping; now I promise nothing, presume nothing—do not question me about these things. Surely you say that he himself, whom I burn to see, knows when I am healthy; let him do what pleases him. When it pleases him, let him show himself; now I commit myself wholly to his mercy and care. Once for all I have believed of him that he does not cease to lift up those so disposed toward him. I will declare nothing about my own health until I have seen that beauty.

Reason: Do nothing else at all. But now restrain yourself from tears, and gird up your mind. You have wept very much, and this chest of yours takes it gravely.

Augustine: You wish to set a limit to my tears, when I see no limit to my misery? Or do you bid me consider the health of my body, when I myself am wasted away with consumption? But I beg you, if you have any power over me, to try to lead me by some shortcuts, so that at least by some nearness of that light—which, if I have made any progress, I can now bear—my eyes may be loath to turn back to those shadows I have left; if indeed they should be called left, which still dare to flatter my blindness.

**1. 15. 27.** Reason: Let us conclude this first volume, if you please, so that in the second we may take up some path that proves convenient. For this disposition of yours must not cease from moderate exercise. Augustine: I will not allow this little book to be concluded at all unless you open to me some little nearness of the light, to which I may be intent.

Reason: That physician indulges you. For some brilliance—I know not what—invites and touches me as to where I should lead you. So receive it attentively.

Augustine: Lead, I beg you, and carry me off where you will.

Reason: You say, surely, that you wish to know the soul and God?

Augustine: This is my whole business.

Reason: Nothing more?

Augustine: Nothing at all.

Reason: What? Do you not wish to grasp the truth?

Augustine: As if I could know these things except through it.

Reason: Then that very thing must first be known, through which those things can be known.

Augustine: I refuse nothing.

Reason: First, then, let us see this: since "truth" and "the true" are two words, whether two things also seem to you to be signified by these words, or one.

Augustine: They seem two things. For just as chastity is one thing and the chaste another, and many things of this kind, so I believe truth is one thing, and what is called true another.

Reason: Which of these two do you think is the more excellent?

Augustine: Truth, I think. For the chaste does not make chastity, but chastity makes the chaste; so too, if anything is true, it is true by truth.

**1. 15. 28.** Reason: What? When some chaste person dies, do you think chastity also dies?

Augustine: In no way.

Reason: Then, when something that is true perishes, truth does not perish.

Augustine: But how does something true perish? For I do not see it.

Reason: I am amazed that you ask this. Do we not see thousands of things perish before our eyes? Unless perhaps you think this tree is either a tree but not a true one, or surely cannot perish. For although you do not trust the senses, and could answer that you do not know at all whether it is a tree, yet you will not deny, I think, that it is a true tree, if it is a tree; for this is judged not by the senses but by the understanding. For if it is a false tree, it is not a tree; but if it is a tree, it must be true.

Augustine: I concede that.

Reason: What of the other point? Do you not concede that a tree is the kind of thing that is born and perishes?

Augustine: I cannot deny it.

Reason: It is concluded, then, that something which is true perishes.

Augustine: I do not object.

Reason: What of this? Does it not seem to you that, when true things perish, truth does not perish—just as chastity does not die when a chaste person dies?

Augustine: Now I concede this too, and I eagerly await what you are working toward.

Reason: Then attend.

Augustine: I am here.

**1. 15. 29.** Reason: Does this proposition seem true to you: Whatever is, is compelled to be somewhere?

Augustine: Nothing draws me so to assent.

Reason: And you confess that truth exists?

Augustine: I confess it.

Reason: Then we must ask where it is; for it is not in a place, unless perhaps you think that something other than body can be in a place, or that truth is a body.

Augustine: I think neither of these.

Reason: Where, then, do you believe it to be? For it is not nowhere, since we have granted that it is.

Augustine: If I knew where it was, perhaps I would seek nothing further.

Reason: At least where it is not, can you know?

Augustine: If you remind me, perhaps I can.

Reason: It is surely not in mortal things. For whatever is cannot remain in something if that in which it is does not remain; but that truth remains, even when true things perish, was granted a little while ago. Therefore truth is not in mortal things. But truth is, and is not nowhere. Therefore immortal things exist. Now nothing is true in which truth is not. It follows, then, that nothing is true except what is immortal. And every false tree is not a tree, and false wood is not wood, and false silver is not silver, and in general whatever is false, is not. Now everything that is not true is false. Therefore nothing is rightly said to be, except immortal things. Consider this little argument with yourself carefully, lest anything in it seem not to be conceded. For if it holds, we have nearly finished the whole business, which will perhaps appear better in another book.

**1. 15. 30.** Augustine: I am grateful, and these things I will handle carefully and cautiously with myself, and indeed with you, when we are in silence—if no shadows steal in upon me, and, what I greatly fear, make me delight in them.

Reason: Believe in God constantly, and commit yourself wholly to him as far as you can. Do not wish to be, as it were, your own and in your own power, but profess yourself the servant of that most merciful and most helpful Lord. For so he will not cease to lift you up to himself, and will permit nothing to happen to you except what profits you, even if you do not know it.

Augustine: I hear, I believe, and as far as I can I obey; and I most earnestly pray to him that I may be most able—unless perhaps you desire something more of me.

Reason: It is well for now; afterward you will do whatever the vision itself prescribes.
