# Book I

**1. 1. 1.** O that virtue, Romanianus, could in turn snatch a man fit for her from fortune's opposition, just as she suffers no one to be snatched from herself! Already, surely, she would have laid hands on you, and, proclaiming that you are her own, and bringing you into the possession of the most certain goods, she would not allow you to be a slave even to prosperous turns of chance. But because it has been so arranged—whether for our deserts or by the necessity of nature—that the harbor of wisdom by no means receives the divine mind while it clings to mortal things, where it would be moved neither by the contrary blast of fortune nor by the favorable, unless fortune itself, whether favorable or as if adverse, brings it there: nothing remains for us on your behalf but prayers, by which we may, if we can, obtain from that God who has these things in his care that he restore you to yourself; for thus he will easily restore you to us as well, and let that mind of yours, which has long been in labor to breathe again, at last emerge into the airs of true freedom. For perhaps what is commonly called fortune is governed by a certain hidden order, and we call nothing in things "chance" except that whose reason and cause is secret; and nothing of advantage or disadvantage happens in a part that does not fit and agree with the whole. This opinion, set forth by the oracles of the most fruitful teachings, and removed very far from the understanding of the profane, philosophy promises to demonstrate to her true lovers—to which I invite you. Therefore, when many things unworthy of your mind befall you, do not despise yourself. For if divine providence extends all the way to us, which is least to be doubted, believe me, it is fitting that you be dealt with as you are being dealt with. For when, with so great a natural gift as yours, which I always admire, from your earliest youth you entered—with reason's footing still weak and slipping—upon a human life most full of all errors, an inflowing flood of riches received you, which had begun, with its enticing whirlpools, to swallow up that age and mind of yours, eagerly following what seemed beautiful and honorable, had not the blasts of that fortune which are thought adverse snatched you from there, all but drowning.

**1. 1. 2.** For truly, if theatrical applause—always most prosperous—had received you as you gave shows of bears and spectacles never before seen there to our fellow citizens; if by the blended and consenting voices of foolish men, whose throng is immense [^1], you were borne to the sky; if no one dared to be your enemy; if the municipal tablets marked you in bronze as the patron not only of citizens but even of neighbors; if statues were set up, honors flowed in, powers were added that overgrew the municipal measure; if at daily banquets rich tables were spread; if whatever anyone needed, whatever anyone's delights thirsted for, he asked for without hesitation, drew without hesitation, and much was poured out even on those who did not ask; and your very household estate, diligently and faithfully managed by your people, showed itself suitable and prepared for so great expenses; if you meanwhile lived among the most exquisite piles of buildings, in the splendor of baths, at the gaming-tables which honor does not reject, in hunts, in banquets, on the lips of clients, on the lips of citizens, finally on the lips of peoples, and were extolled as most humane, most generous, most refined, most fortunate—as you were: would anyone, Romanianus, dare to make mention to you of that other happy life, which alone is happy? Would anyone, I ask, be able to persuade you that not only are you not happy, but that you are most wretched for this very reason, that you seemed to yourself least so? But now, how briefly you need to be admonished, all those great adversities you have endured have brought about! For you do not need to be persuaded by others' examples how fleeting and fragile and full of calamities are all the things that mortals think good, since you have experienced it well enough in some measure, so that from you we can persuade the rest.

**1. 1. 3.** That, then—that quality of yours, by which you always desired what is comely and honorable; by which you preferred to be generous rather than rich; by which you never desired to be more powerful than just, never yielded to adversities and dishonesties—that very thing, I say, which is divine in you, lulled to sleep by I know not what slumber and lethargy of this life, a hidden providence has decreed to awaken by those varied and harsh tossings. Awake, awake, I beg you; you will, believe me, give much thanks that, with almost none of the prosperities by which the incautious are held, the gifts of this world have flattered you—which were daily contriving to capture me too, as I chanted these things, had not a pain in my chest forced me to throw off my windy profession and flee into the bosom of philosophy. Philosophy now nourishes and cherishes me in the leisure we vehemently longed for; she has freed me utterly from that superstition into which I had thrown you headlong along with me. For she teaches—and teaches truly—that nothing at all is to be worshipped, and that everything ought to be despised, whatever is discerned by mortal eyes, whatever any sense touches. She promises that she will clearly demonstrate the truest and most secret God, and now and again she deigns, as it were, to show him through bright clouds.

**1. 1. 4.** In her our Licentius lives most studiously with me: wholly turned to her from youthful enticements and pleasures, so that I would not rashly dare to set him before his father as a model to imitate. For philosophy it is, from whose breasts no age will complain of being shut out; and to make you the more eager to hold fast to her and drink her in—although I know your thirst well—I wanted nonetheless to send you a taste, which I ask I may not have hoped in vain will be most sweet to you and, so to speak, an introduction. For I have sent you, set down in writing, the discussion that Trygetius and Licentius had between themselves. For military service, as if to wipe away a distaste for the disciplines, had taken something of that young man too for itself, but restored him to us so most ardent and most voracious for the great and honorable arts. And so, after a very few days had passed, once we had begun to live in the country, when, exhorting and rousing them to their studies, I saw them more ready than I had wished and downright gaping, I wanted to test what they could do, in proportion to their age—especially since Cicero's book the *Hortensius* already seemed to have reconciled them in great part to philosophy. And so, a stenographer being employed, lest the breezes scatter our labor, I let nothing perish. In this book, to be sure, you will read both the substance and the opinions of those young men, but the very words of me and of Alypius.

**1. 2. 5.** When, then, all of us had at my urging gathered into one place for this purpose, where it seemed convenient, I said, "Do you doubt at all that we ought to know the truth?" "Not at all," said Trygetius; and the rest signified by their very looks that they approved. "What if," I said, "we can be happy even without truth being grasped—do you judge the grasping of truth necessary?" Here Alypius said, "I think it safer that I be the judge of this question. For since a journey to the city has been arranged for me, it is fitting that I be relieved of the burden of taking up some side; at the same time because I can more easily delegate to someone the parts of a judge than of any advocate. Therefore from now on expect nothing from me on either side." When this had been granted him by all, and I had repeated the question, Trygetius said, "Certainly we want to be happy; and if we can come to this thing without the truth, the truth need not be sought by us." "What of this very point?" I said. "Do you think we can be happy even without truth being found?" Then Licentius said, "We can, if we seek the truth." Here, when I had by a nod demanded the opinion of the rest, Navigius said, "What Licentius said moves me. For perhaps this very thing—to live in the search for truth—can be to live happily." "Define, then," said Trygetius, "what the happy life is, that from it I may gather what it is fitting to answer." "What else do you think it is to live happily," I said, "than to live according to that which is best in a human being?" "I will not pour out words rashly," he said; "for this very 'best,' what it is, I think must be defined for me by you." "Who would doubt," I said, "that the best in a human being is nothing other than that part of the mind to whose rule the other things in a human being ought to submit? And this part—lest you ask for another definition—can be called mind or reason. But if this does not seem so to you, find how you yourself would define either the happy life or the best in a human being." "I agree," he said.

**1. 2. 6.** "What then?—that we may return to our subject," I said. "Does it seem to you that one can live happily without the truth being found, if it is only sought?" "I repeat that opinion of mine," he said: "it does not seem so at all." "You—what do you think?" I said. Then Licentius said, "To me it seems so altogether: for our ancestors, whom we have received as wise and happy, lived well and happily by this alone, that they sought the truth." "I give thanks," I said, "that you have made me, along with Alypius, a judge—whom, I confess, I had already begun to envy. Since, then, to one of you it seems that the happy life can come about by the mere investigation of truth, to the other only by its finding, while Navigius a little while ago showed that he wished to cross over to your side, Licentius, I greatly await what sort of patrons of your opinions you can be. For the matter is great, and most worthy of diligent discussion." "If the matter is great," said Licentius, "it requires great men." "Do not seek," I said, "especially in this country house, what is hard to find anywhere among the nations; but rather unfold why what you brought forward—not rashly, I think—seems so to you, and for what reason. For even the greatest matters, when sought by the small, are wont to make them great."

**1. 3. 7.** "Since," he said, "I see you greatly urge us to dispute against one another, which I trust you usefully wish, I ask why one cannot be happy who seeks the truth, even if he does not find it." "Because," said Trygetius, "we want the happy man to be a wise man perfect in all things. But one who is still seeking is not perfect. How, then, you can assert him to be happy, I do not at all see." And the other said, "Can the authority of our forebears prevail with you?" "Not of all," said Trygetius. "Of whom, then?" He: "Of those, of course, who were wise." Then Licentius said, "Does Carneades not seem to you wise?" "I am not a Greek," he said; "I do not know who this Carneades was." "What," said Licentius, "do you think of that Cicero of ours?" Here, when he had long been silent, he said, "He was wise." And the other: "Then his opinion on this matter has some weight with you?" "It has," he said. "Hear, then, what it is, for I think it has slipped your mind. For it pleased our Cicero that he is happy who investigates the truth, even if he cannot reach its finding." "Where did Cicero say this?" he said. And Licentius: "Who does not know that he strongly affirmed that nothing can be perceived by a human being, and that nothing remains for the wise man but the most diligent inquiry into truth; for this reason, that if he assented to uncertain things, even if perhaps they were true, he could not be freed from error?—which is the greatest fault of the wise man [^2]. Therefore, if both the wise man must necessarily be believed happy, and the mere inquiry into truth is the perfect office of wisdom, why do we hesitate to judge that the happy life can come about even of itself, by the investigation of truth?"

**1. 3. 8.** Then he said, "Is it permitted, after all, to return again to the things rashly conceded?" Here I said, "Those do not usually allow this who are driven to dispute not by desire of finding the truth, but by the boastfulness of a boyish wit. And so, with me—especially since you are still to be nourished and brought up—it is not only allowed, but I want you to hold it among your precepts that you ought to return to discuss the things you have conceded too incautiously." And Licentius said, "I think it no small progress in philosophy when, in comparison with finding the right and the true, victory is despised by the disputant. And so I gladly obey your precepts and your opinion, and I permit Trygetius to return to that which he thinks he conceded rashly—for the matter is within my right." Then Alypius said, "You yourselves recognize with me that it is not yet the part of the office I have taken up. But since a departure long since arranged compels me to interrupt, by my office too the partner with me in judgment will not refuse a doubled power for himself until my return: for I see that this contest of yours will go on rather long." And when he had departed, Licentius said, "Bring out what you conceded rashly." And the other said, "I granted rashly that Cicero was wise. Then was Cicero not wise, by whom philosophy in the Latin tongue was both begun and perfected?" "Even if I grant," he said, "that he was wise, I do not, however, approve everything of his." "But you ought to refute many other things of his, so that you may not seem shamelessly to disapprove this one which is at issue." "What if I am prepared to affirm that he did not rightly think this one thing alone? It makes no difference, I think, to you, except what weight the reasons I bring carry toward asserting what I want." "Go on," said the other. "For what should I dare against him who professes himself Cicero's adversary?"

**1. 3. 9.** Here Trygetius said, "I want you to attend, you our judge, to how you defined the happy life above: for you said that he is happy who lives according to that part of the mind which it is fitting should command the rest. But you, Licentius, I want even now to concede to me (for I have already, in the freedom into which philosophy especially promises to vindicate us, shaken off that yoke of authority) that he is not perfect who still seeks the truth." Then he, after a long silence, said, "I do not concede it." And Trygetius: "Why, I ask? Explain. For I am right here, and eager to hear in what way a man can be both perfect and still seek the truth." Here he said, "He who has not come to the end, I confess, is not perfect. But that truth I think God alone knows, or perhaps a human being's soul, when it has left this body—that is, this dark prison. But the end of a human being is to seek the truth perfectly: for we are seeking one who is perfect, but nonetheless a human being." And Trygetius: "Then a human being cannot be happy. For how can he, when he cannot attain what he greatly desires? But a human being can live happily, since he can live according to that part of the mind which it is right should rule in a human being. Therefore he can find the truth. Or let him gather himself in and not desire the truth, lest, when he cannot attain it, he be necessarily wretched." "But this very thing is the happiness of a human being," said the other, "to seek the truth perfectly: for this is to come to the end beyond which he cannot advance. Whoever, then, seeks the truth less insistently than he ought does not come to the end of a human being; but whoever gives effort to finding the truth, as much as a human being can and ought, he, even if he does not find it, is happy; for he does everything he is born to do. But if the finding is lacking, that will be lacking which nature did not give. Finally, since a human being must necessarily be either happy or wretched, is it not the mark of a madman to call wretched one who day and night insists, as much as he can, on investigating the truth? He will therefore be happy. Then that definition, as I think, supports me more richly: for if he is happy, as he is, who lives according to that part of the mind which it is fitting should reign over the rest, and this part is called reason, I ask whether he who seeks the truth perfectly does not live according to reason. And if that is absurd, why do we hesitate to call a human being happy by the very investigation of truth alone?"

**1. 4. 10.** "To me," said the other, "whoever errs seems neither to live according to reason nor to be happy at all. But everyone errs who always seeks and does not find. Whence you must show me one of two things: either that one who errs can be happy, or that one who never finds what he seeks does not err." Here he said, "A happy man cannot err." And when he had long been silent, he said, "But he does not err when he seeks; because, in order not to err, he seeks perfectly." And Trygetius said, "He seeks indeed in order not to err; but he errs when he does not find. And so you thought it would profit you that he does not wish to err—as if no one errs unwillingly, or anyone errs at all except unwillingly." Then I, when he hesitated long over what to answer, said, "You must define what error is: for you can more easily see the bounds of that into which you have now deeply entered." "I," said Licentius, "am not fit to define anything—although to define error is easier than to put an end to it." "I," said the other, "will define it; which is most easy for me, not from my wit, but from my cause being the best. For to err is surely always to seek, never to find." "I," said Licentius, "if I could easily refute even that definition, would long since not have failed my cause. But because either the matter itself is hard of itself, or so appears to me, I ask of you that the question be deferred until tomorrow's light, if I can find nothing today to answer, since I shall diligently turn it over with myself." When I thought this had to be granted, the rest not refusing, we rose to go for a walk; and while we talked among ourselves of many and various things, he stayed fixed in thought. When he felt this to be in vain, he preferred to relax his mind and join in our conversation. Afterward, when it was now growing dark, they had returned to the same struggle; but I set a limit to it, and persuaded them to put it off to another day.

**1. 4. 11.** The next day, when we had sat down, I said, "Bring out what you began yesterday." Then Licentius said, "We had deferred the discussion, unless I am mistaken, at my request, since the definition of error was most difficult for me." "Here," I said, "you plainly do not err; which I would gladly wish to be an omen for you for the rest." "Hear, then," he said, "what I would have brought forth yesterday too, had you not interrupted. Error seems to me to be the approval of the false as true; into which he by no means falls who thinks the truth must always be sought: for one who approves nothing cannot approve the false; therefore he cannot err. But he can most easily be happy: for, not to go too far afield, if it were permitted us to live daily as it was permitted yesterday, nothing occurs to me why we should hesitate to call ourselves happy. For we lived with great tranquility of mind, freeing the mind from every defilement of the body, and most far removed from the torches of desires, giving—as far as a human being may—effort to reason; that is, living according to that divine part of the mind, which by yesterday's definition we agreed to be the happy life: and yet, I think, we found nothing, but only sought the truth. Therefore by the mere investigation of truth, even if he cannot find it at all, the happy life can come to a human being. For see with how great ease your definition is excluded by a common notion. For you said that to err is to seek always and never to find. What if someone seeks nothing, and being asked, for example, whether it is now day, rashly and at once should think and answer that it is night?—does he not seem to you to err? This kind of error, then, even the most monstrous, your definition has not included. What if it has even included those who do not err? Can any definition be more faulty? For if someone is seeking Alexandria, and goes straight toward it, you cannot, I think, call him erring. What if he should walk the same road, with various hindrances, for a long time, and be overtaken by death on it?—did he not both always seek and never find, and yet not err?" "He did not," said the other, "always seek."

**1. 4. 12.** "You say rightly," said Licentius, "and you warn well. For thence your definition pertains to the matter not at all: for I did not say that he is happy who always seeks the truth. Which cannot even happen: first, because a human being does not always exist; then, because not from the time he begins to be a human being can he yet, his age hindering, seek the truth. Or if you think it should always be said, provided he allows none of the time in which he can already seek to perish, again you must return to Alexandria. For suppose someone, from the time when he is permitted by age or by occupation to make the journey, begins to take that road, and, as I said above, when he nowhere goes astray, yet departs life before he arrives: you will greatly err if this man shall seem to you to have erred, although, in all the time he could, he neither ceased to seek nor was able to find where he was going. Therefore, if both my description is true, and according to it he does not err who seeks perfectly, even though he does not find the truth, and he is happy for this reason, that he lives according to reason; while your definition both has failed, and, even if it had not, I should pay it no heed, if from what I alone have defined the cause is sufficiently established: why, I ask, is this question not yet resolved between us?"

**1. 5. 13.** Here Trygetius said, "Do you grant that wisdom is the right way of life?" "I grant it," he said, "without doubt: but yet I want you to define wisdom for me, that I may know whether the same thing seems to you to be wisdom as seems so to me." And the other said, "Does it seem to you too little defined by this very thing of which you have just been asked? You have even conceded what I wanted. For, unless I am mistaken, wisdom is named the right way of life." Then Licentius said, "Nothing seems to me so ridiculous as that definition." "Perhaps," said the other; "but step by step, I beg, let reason go before your laughter: for nothing is fouler than laughter most worthy of being laughed at. For what?" he said. "Do you not confess that death is contrary to life?" "I confess it," he said. "To me, then," said he, "no way of life seems more such than that by which someone goes so as not to fall into death." Trygetius agreed. "Then, if some traveler, avoiding a byway which he had heard was beset by robbers, should go straight on and so escape death, has he not followed both a way of life and a right one—and no one calls that wisdom? How, then, is every right way of life wisdom? For I conceded that it is, but not that it alone is. But a definition ought to include nothing foreign. So define again, if you please, what seems to you to be wisdom."

**1. 5. 14.** He was silent for a long time; then he said, "See, I define again, since you have resolved never to make an end of this. Wisdom is the right way which leads to truth." "This too is refuted in like manner," said the other; "for when in Virgil it was said to Aeneas by his mother,  
*Only go on, and where the way leads you, direct your step* [^3],  
following this way he came to what had been spoken—that is, to the truth. Contend, if you please, that the place where he set his foot as he advanced can be called wisdom: although it is altogether foolish for me to try to break down this description of yours; for nothing aids my cause more. For you said that wisdom is not truth itself, but the way that leads to it. Whoever, then, uses this way surely uses wisdom; and whoever uses wisdom must be wise: therefore he will be wise who has sought the truth perfectly, even if he has not yet reached it. For the way that leads to truth is, I think, understood as nothing better than the diligent inquiry into truth. Using this way alone, then, this man will now be wise: but no wise man is wretched; and every human being is either wretched or happy: therefore not only the finding, but the investigation itself of truth, of itself, will make one happy."

**1. 5. 15.** Then he, smiling, said, "Deservedly do these things befall me, while I confidently agree with my adversary on a point not necessary—as if I were a great definer, or thought anything more superfluous in disputing. For what limit will there be, if I should again want something defined by you, and again the words of that same definition, and likewise all the words that follow, one by one, pretending that I understand nothing, demand to be defined? For what most plain thing would I not compel to be defined by my right, if by right a definition of wisdom is demanded of me? For of what word has nature willed there to be in our minds a more open notion than of wisdom? But somehow, when the notion itself has, as it were, left the harbor of our mind and spread, as it were, the sails of words for itself, at once a thousand shipwrecks of false charges meet it. Therefore either let no definition of wisdom be demanded, or let our judge deign to come down into its defense." Then I, when night now hindered my pen, and I saw something great, as if from the beginning, arising to be discussed, deferred it to another day: for we had begun to dispute when the sun was already declining toward setting, and nearly the whole day had been spent both in ordering rustic affairs and in reviewing the first book of Virgil.

**1. 6. 16.** Then soon, as it grew light—for things had been so arranged the day before that there was ample leisure—at once the business undertaken was to be carried through. Then I said, "Yesterday, Trygetius, you demanded that I come down from the office of judge to the defense of wisdom: as if, indeed, wisdom would suffer any adversary in your conversation, or, with anyone defending it, labored so as to need to implore greater help. For neither has anything else arisen to be sought between you than what wisdom is; in which neither of you attacks it, because each desires it. Nor, if you think you have failed in defining wisdom, must you therefore desert the rest of the defense of your opinion. And so from me you will have nothing else than a definition of wisdom, which is neither mine nor new, but of the men of old, and which I wonder you do not recall. For it is not now for the first time that you hear that wisdom is the knowledge of things human and divine [^4]."

**1. 6. 17.** Here Licentius, whom after that definition I long thought would be seeking what to say, at once added, "Why then, I ask, do we not call wise that most disgraceful man, whom we ourselves know well to be wont to dissolve himself among countless harlots—I mean that Albicerius, who at Carthage for many years gave to those consulting him certain wondrous and sure answers? I could recall countless instances, were I not speaking among those who have experienced them, and were not a few enough now for what I want. Did he not—when a spoon-case could not be found at home—being asked at your bidding, most quickly and most truly answer not only what was sought, but even by name whose it was and where it lay hidden? Likewise, in my presence—I pass over the fact that in what was asked of him he was in no way at all false—when the boy who carried the coins had stolen a certain part of them as we were going to him, he ordered them all to be counted out to himself, and forced the boy, before our eyes, to give back what he had taken, before he himself had either seen those same coins or heard from us how much had been brought to him."

**1. 6. 18.** "What of this, that we have heard from you that the most learned and most famous man Flaccianus was wont to marvel? Who, when he had been speaking of buying an estate, brought the matter to that diviner in such a way that he might tell, if he could, what he had done. And the man at once pronounced not only the kind of business, but even—at which the other, marveling, vehemently cried out—the very name of the estate, though it was so absurd that Flaccianus himself scarcely remembered it. And this I cannot say without amazement of mind: that to a friend of ours, your pupil, who wished to provoke him and insolently demanded that he say what he himself was silently turning over within himself, he answered that he was thinking of a verse of Virgil. When the man, astounded, could not deny it, he went on to ask which verse it was. Nor did Albicerius, who had scarcely ever glanced at a grammarian's school in passing, hesitate, careless and chatty, to recite the verse itself. Were, then, the things about which he was consulted not human affairs; or did he answer those consulting him so surely and truly without knowledge of divine things? But both are absurd. For human affairs are nothing other than the affairs of human beings—such as silver, coins, an estate, finally even thought itself; and who would not rightly judge that those are divine things through which divination itself comes to a human being? Albicerius, then, was wise, if by that definition we concede that wisdom is the knowledge of things human and divine."

**1. 7. 19.** Here I said, "First, I do not call knowledge that in which the one who professes it is sometimes deceived. For knowledge consists not only of things grasped, but of things so grasped that no one can err in it, nor be made to waver by the impulse of any opposing arguments. Whence it is most truly said by certain philosophers that it can be found in no one but the wise man, who ought not only to hold perceived what he watches over and follows, but to hold it unshaken. But we know that the man you mentioned often said many false things—which I learned not only from others reporting to me, but sometimes perceived myself, being present. Shall I, then, call him knowing, when he often said false things—him whom I would not call so even if he had hesitantly said true ones? Take it that I have said this of soothsayers and augurs, and of all who consult the stars, and of interpreters of dreams. Or bring forth, if you can, someone of this kind of men who, when consulted, never doubted his own answers, never, finally, answered falsely. For about seers I think I need not trouble myself, who speak with another's mind."

**1. 7. 20.** "Then, that I may grant that human affairs are the affairs of human beings—do you think anything is ours that chance can either give to us or snatch from us? Or, when the knowledge of human affairs is spoken of, is that meant by which anyone knows either how many or what kind of estates we have, what gold, what silver, and finally what verses of others we are thinking of? That is the knowledge of human affairs which knows the light of prudence, the beauty of temperance, the strength of fortitude, the holiness of justice. For these are the things which, fearing no fortune, we dare truly to call ours: which if that Albicerius had learned, he would never, believe me, have lived so luxuriously and foully. But as to his saying what verse the one who consulted him was turning over in his mind—neither do I think this should be counted among our possessions: not that I deny that the most honorable disciplines pertain to a certain possession of our mind, but because to chant and recite another's verse is granted even to the most ignorant. And therefore, when such things run into our memory, it is no wonder if they can be sensed by certain most worthless animals of this air, which they call demons—by whom I grant we can be surpassed in keenness and subtlety of sense, but in reason I deny it; and that this happens in some most secret way, most remote from our senses. For if we marvel at the little bee, when honey is set out, by what shrewdness—by which it surpasses a human being—it knows whence to fly to it, we ought not therefore to set it before us, or even compare it with us."

**1. 7. 21.** "And so I would rather that this Albicerius, asked by one who desired to learn, had taught the meters themselves; or, forced by one of those consulting him, had sung verses of his own on a matter set before him on the spot. This, you are wont to recall, that same Flaccianus often said, when with great loftiness of mind he derided and despised that kind of divination, and attributed it to some most abject little soul—for so he spoke—by which the man, as if warned or inflated by a spirit, was wont to give these answers. For that most learned man would ask of those who marveled at such things whether Albicerius could teach grammar or music or geometry. But who knew him and would not confess him the most ignorant of all these? Therefore in the end he would exhort that those who had learned such things should without hesitation prefer their own minds to that divination, and should give effort to instructing and supporting their minds with those disciplines by which they might leap over and fly above that airy nature of invisible living beings."

**1. 8. 22.** "Now since divine things, by everyone's concession, are far better and more august than human ones, how could he attain them, who did not know what he himself was? Unless perhaps he thought the stars which we daily contemplate something great in comparison with the truest and most secret God, whom rarely perhaps the understanding, but no sense at all, touches—whereas these stars are present to our eyes. Nor, then, are these stars those divine things which wisdom alone professes to know; but the rest, which those diviners (whatever they are) misuse, whether for empty boasting or for gain, are surely baser than the stars. Albicerius, then, was not a sharer in the knowledge of things human and divine, and in vain has our definition been attacked by you in that way. Finally, since whatever there is besides things human and divine we ought to count most worthless and altogether despise, I ask: in what things does that wise man of yours seek the truth?" "In divine things," said the other; "for virtue too, even in a human being, is without doubt divine." "Did Albicerius, then, already know these divine things which your wise man will always seek?" Then Licentius said, "He knew divine things too, but not those which are to be sought by the wise man. For who would not overturn all the custom of speaking, if he should concede divination to him and take away from him the divine things from which divination is named? Therefore that definition of yours, unless I am mistaken, included something else, I know not what, that did not pertain to wisdom."

**1. 8. 23.** Then Trygetius said, "That definition let him defend, if he pleases, who brought it forth. Now you answer me, that we may at last come to what is at issue." "Here I am," said the other. "Do you grant," said Trygetius, "that Albicerius knew the truth?" "I grant it," he said. "Then he is better than your wise man." "By no means," said the other: "for the kind of truth that the wise man seeks, not only does that raving soothsayer not attain, but not even the wise man himself while he lives in this body; which yet is so great that it is far more excellent always to seek this than sometime to find that." "It is necessary," said Trygetius, "that that definition come to my aid in my straits. If it seemed faulty to you because it included one whom we cannot call wise, I ask whether you approve it if we say that wisdom is the knowledge of things human and divine, but of those which pertain to the happy life." "That too," he said, "is wisdom, but not it alone: whence the earlier definition seized on something foreign, but this one has deserted something proper; so that the former can be convicted of greed, this one of folly. For—that I myself may now explain by a definition what I feel—wisdom seems to me to be not only the knowledge, but also the diligent inquiry, into things human and divine which pertain to the happy life. If you wish to divide this description, the first part, which holds knowledge, is God's; but this part, which is content with inquiry, is the human being's. By the former, then, God is happy, but by this, the human being." Then he said, "I wonder how you assert that your wise man spends his labor in vain." "How," said Licentius, "does he spend his labor in vain, when he gathers with so great a reward? For by this very thing, that he seeks, he is wise; and by which he is wise, by that he is happy: when he unfolds his mind, as far as he can, from all the wrappings of the body, and gathers himself into himself; when he does not permit himself to be torn by desires, but is always, tranquil, intent upon himself and upon God; so that both here he enjoys, by reason, that which we agreed above to be happy, and at the last day of life he may be found prepared for attaining what he desired, and deservedly enjoys divine blessedness, who has first enjoyed the human."

**1. 9. 24.** Then I, when Trygetius was long seeking what to answer, said, "I do not think, Licentius, that arguments will be lacking to him either, if we permit him to seek at leisure: for what was lacking to him at any point for answering? For first he himself brought in—since the question arose about the happy life, and only the wise man must necessarily be happy, seeing that folly is wretched even by the judgment of the foolish—that the wise man ought to be perfect, but that one is not perfect who is still inquiring what the truth is; whence he is not even happy. To which point, when you set against it the mass of authority, he, somewhat disturbed at Cicero's name, yet at once raised himself up, and with a certain noble defiance leaped up to the summit of liberty, and seized again what had been violently struck from his hands; and he asked of you whether one who was still seeking seemed to you perfect; so that, if you confessed he was not perfect, he might run back to the head of the matter, and show, if he could, by that definition, that a human being is perfect who governs his life according to the law of the mind, and through this, that no one can be happy unless perfect. From which snare, when you had freed yourself more cautiously than I thought, and said that the perfect human being is the most diligent inquirer of truth, and had fought, by that very definition by which we had said that that is the happy life which is conducted according to reason, more confidently and more openly; he plainly threw it back at you: for he occupied your stronghold, from which, driven out, you had altogether lost the sum of the matter, had not the truce restored you. For where did the Academics, whose opinion you defend, place their citadel, but in the definition of error? Which, had it not perhaps returned to your mind by night through a dream, you now had nothing to answer—since in expounding Cicero's opinion you yourself had earlier recalled that very thing. Then we came to the definition of wisdom, which, when you tried to overthrow it with so great cunning that perhaps not even your helper Albicerius would have caught your thefts, with what vigilance, with what strength did he resist you? How nearly he wrapped you up and pressed you down; had you not at last protected yourself with your new definition, and said that human wisdom is the inquiry into truth, from which, on account of the tranquility of mind, the happy life comes about? To this opinion he will not respond, especially if, in extending what remains of the day, or part of it, he demands that the favor be returned to him."

**1. 9. 25.** "But not to make it long, let this conversation now, if you please, be closed, in which I think it even superfluous to linger. For the matter has been treated enough for the business undertaken; which could be finished in a very few words altogether, did I not wish to exercise you, and to test your sinews and your zeal, which are a great care to me. For when I had resolved greatly to exhort you to seek the truth, I had begun to ask of you how much weight you placed in it: and you all placed so much that I desire no more. For since we desire to be happy, whether that can come about only by truth being found, or only by truth being diligently sought, all other things being set aside, it must be sought out by us, if we wish to be happy. Therefore let us now, as I said, end this discussion, and, set down in writing, send it, Licentius, above all to your father, whose mind toward philosophy I already wholly hold. But I still ask what fortune may allow him. He can be kindled to these studies more vehemently when he learns that you yourself, now intent, are living thus with me—not only by hearing, but also by reading these things. But you, if, as I perceive, the Academics please you, prepare stronger forces for defending them; for I have resolved to accuse them." When these things had been said, it was announced that the meal was ready, and we rose.
