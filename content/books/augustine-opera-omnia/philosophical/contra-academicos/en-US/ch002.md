# Book II

**2. 1. 1.** If it were as necessary that the wise man, who cannot be void of the discipline and knowledge of wisdom, should find that wisdom while it is being sought, as it is necessary, then surely all the Academics' cavil, or stubbornness, or obstinacy, or—as I sometimes think—reasoning suited to that time, would have been buried together with that time and with the very bodies of Carneades and Cicero. But because, whether by the many and various tossings of this life, Romanianus, as you yourself prove in your own case, or by a certain numbness of minds grown torpid through sloth or slowness, or by despair of finding—because the star of wisdom does not rise upon minds as easily as this light upon the eyes—or even by that error of all peoples, by the false opinion of having found the truth themselves, human beings do not seek diligently, if any do seek, and are turned away from the will to seek; it comes about that knowledge comes rarely and to few: and thence it happens that the weapons of the Academics, when one comes to grips with them, seem—not to ordinary men, but to keen and well-learned ones—unconquered and, as it were, Vulcanian. Therefore against those waves and storms of fortune, both must one strive with the oars of whatever virtues, and especially must divine help be implored with all devotion and piety; that the most constant intent of good studies may hold its course, from which no chance may shake it out, so that the safest and most delightful harbor of philosophy may receive it. This is your first cause; from this I fear for you, from this I desire you freed, from this—if only I am worthy to obtain it—I do not cease with daily prayers to ask for you favorable breezes; and I pray to the very Virtue and Wisdom of the highest God [^1]. For what else is he whom the mysteries hand down to us as the Son of God?

**2. 1. 2.** But you will greatly help me as I pray on your behalf, if you do not despair that we can be heard, and strive with us not only with prayers but also with will, and with that natural loftiness of mind of yours, on account of which I seek you, in which I am singularly delighted, which I always admire, which in you—alas!—is wrapped, like a lightning bolt, in those clouds of domestic affairs, and lies hidden from many, indeed from nearly all: but it cannot be hidden from me, and from one other, or a third, your most intimate friends, who have often not only attentively heard your mutterings, but have even beheld some flashes nearer to lightning bolts. For who—to pass over the rest as the time requires, and to recall one thing—who, I say, ever thundered so suddenly so much, and flashed forth so much with the light of mind, that under one roar of reason and a certain coruscation of temperance, in one day a lust most savage the day before utterly died? Will not, then, that virtue someday burst forth, and turn the laughter of many despairing into horror and astonishment; and, having spoken on earth, as it were, certain signs of things to come, again, the burden of the whole body cast off, run back to heaven? Did Augustine, then, say these things of Romanianus in vain? He will not allow it, to whom I have given myself wholly, whom I have now begun somewhat to recognize.

**2. 2. 3.** Approach philosophy with me, then: here is whatever has so wondrously been wont to move you, often anxious and doubting. For I fear nothing for your wit from sluggishness of character or slowness. For who, when you were granted to breathe again a little, was more vigilant in our conversations? Who appeared more keen? Shall I not repay you the favor? Or do I perhaps owe but a little? You received me, a poor young man going to study abroad, both with your home and with your means, and—what is more—with your heart. You consoled me, bereft of my father, with your friendship, roused me with your exhortation, aided me with your help. In our very town you made me, by your favor, your intimacy, the sharing of your home, almost as distinguished and a leading citizen as yourself. When I was returning to Carthage for the sake of a more illustrious profession, and had opened my plan and hope to you alone, and to none of my own, although for some time you hesitated out of that love of fatherland implanted in you—because I was already teaching there—yet, when you could not overcome the young man's desire, striving toward what seemed better, you were changed, with a wondrous moderation of benevolence, from a dissuader into a helper. You supported my journey with all necessaries. You there again, who had cherished the cradle and, as it were, the nest of my studies, supported the first efforts of one now daring to fly. You too, when I had sailed in your absence and without your knowledge, taking no offense that I had not, as I was wont, shared it with you, and suspecting anything rather than obstinacy, remained unshaken in friendship; nor were children deserted by their teacher more before your eyes than the inner depths and purity of my mind.

**2. 2. 4.** Finally, whatever I now rejoice in regarding my leisure—that I have flown out from the bonds of superfluous desires, that, the burdens of dead cares laid down, I breathe again, recover my senses, return to myself; that I seek the truth most intently, that I am now beginning to find it, that I trust I shall reach the very highest measure itself: you roused me, you impelled me, you brought it about. But whose minister you were, I have conceived more by faith than grasped by reason. For when, present to you present, I had set forth the inner motions of my mind, and vehemently and often asserted that no fortune seemed to me prosperous except that which gave leisure for philosophizing, no life happy except that in which one lived in philosophy; but that I was reined in by so great a burden of my own people, on whose welfare, by my office, life depended, and by many necessities, whether of shame or of the foolish wretchedness of my people: you were lifted up with so great joy, so inflamed with a holy ardor for this life, that you said, if you could in some way be released from the bonds of those troublesome lawsuits, you would break all my bonds too, by sharing your patrimony with me.

**2. 2. 5.** And so, when you had departed, having applied the kindling to us, we never ceased, gaping after philosophy and after that life which had pleased and been agreed upon among us, to think of nothing else at all: and this we did constantly indeed, but less keenly; yet we thought we were doing enough. And because that flame was not yet present which was to seize us at the highest, the one by which we slowly burned we judged to be even the greatest. When behold, certain books full—as Celsinus says—of good Arabian things, when they had breathed upon us, when they had instilled into that little flame a very few drops of most precious ointment: incredible, Romanianus, incredible, and beyond what perhaps even you believe of me—what more shall I say?—they kindled in me, even in my own eyes, an incredible blaze about myself. What honor then moved me, what human pomp, what desire of empty fame, what, finally, comfort and restraint of this mortal life? Wholly and swiftly I was returning into myself. I looked back, however—I confess—as if from a journey, upon that religion which was implanted in us as children and woven into our very marrow: but it was drawing me, unknowing, to itself. And so, tottering, hastening, hesitating, I seized the apostle Paul. For truly, I said, those men could not have done such great things, nor have lived as it is manifest they lived, if their writings and reasonings were opposed to this so great good. I read the whole through most intently and most cautiously.

**2. 2. 6.** But then indeed, with however little light now sprinkled on me, so great a face of philosophy opened itself to me that, I will not say to you, who have always burned with hunger for it though unknown, but if I could have shown it even to your very adversary—by whom I know not whether you are more exercised than hindered—then he, casting off and leaving Baiae, and pleasant orchards, and dainty and elegant banquets, and household players, finally whatever sharply moves him to any delights whatever, would fly to its beauty, a fond and holy lover, marveling, panting, burning. For he too has, it must be confessed, a certain comeliness of mind, or rather, as it were, the seed of comeliness, which, striving to burst forth into true beauty, sends out its foliage tortuously and deformedly among the rough thorns of vices and the thickets of false opinions: yet it does not cease to put forth foliage, and, to the few who keenly and diligently look into the thickets, to stand out as far as it is allowed. Thence comes that hospitality of his; thence at banquets the many seasonings of humanity; thence that very elegance, polish, the most refined appearance of all things, and on every side a courtliness suffusing all things with a shaded charm.

**2. 3. 7.** This is commonly called philocalia: do not despise this name because it comes from the name of the common crowd: for philocalia and philosophy are named nearly alike, and wish to seem—and are—as if of one family with each other. For what is philosophy? Love of wisdom. What is philocalia? Love of beauty. Ask the Greeks. What, then, is wisdom? Is it not itself true beauty? They are, then, sisters indeed, and begotten of the same parent: but the one, dragged from her own heaven by the birdlime of lust and shut up in a popular cage, yet kept the nearness of name, to warn the bird-catcher not to despise her. This one, then—without wings, soiled and needy—her sister, flying free, often recognizes, but rarely sets free: for that philocalia does not recognize whence she draws her descent, except through philosophy. This whole fable (for I have suddenly become Aesop) Licentius will more sweetly set forth to you in verse: for he is an almost perfect poet. Therefore he, if he could behold the true beauty of which he is a lover of the false, with eyes a little healed and laid bare, with how great pleasure would he wrap himself in the bosom of philosophy! How there would he embrace you, recognized, like a true brother! You marvel at these things, and perhaps laugh. What if I explained them as I wished? What if at least the voice—if the face cannot yet be seen by you—of philosophy herself could be heard? You would surely marvel; but you would not laugh, you would not despair. Believe me, of no one must we despair, and of such men least of all. There are altogether examples; easily does this kind of bird escape, easily fly back, while many shut up marvel much.

**2. 3. 8.** But let me return to ourselves—to ourselves, I say, Romanianus: let us philosophize. I will repay you the favor: your son has now begun to philosophize; I hold him back, that, first cultivated in the necessary disciplines, he may rise up more vigorous and firmer; of which—do not fear that you are without them, if I know you well—I only wish you free breezes. For what shall I say of your natural gift? Would that it were not as rare among human beings as it is certain in you! There remain two vices and hindrances to finding the truth, from which I do not much fear for you; yet I fear lest you despise yourself, and despair of being going to find, or at least lest you believe you have found. The first of these, if it is in you at all, this discussion will perhaps remove from you. For you have rather often been angry at the Academics—the more gravely, indeed, the less learned you were; but the more gladly, because you were enticed by love of truth. And so now, with Alypius, you favoring me, I will fight, and easily persuade you of what I want—probably, however. For the truth itself you will not see, unless you enter wholly into philosophy. But as for that other point—that you perhaps presume to have found something, although you departed from us already seeking and doubting—yet if any superstition has rolled back into your mind, it will surely be cast out, either when I send you some discussion among us about religion, or when, present, I confer many things with you.

**2. 3. 9.** For I now do nothing else than purge myself of empty and ruinous opinions. And so I do not doubt that it is better with me than with you. There is one thing only for which I envy your fortune: that you alone enjoy my Lucilianus—or do you too envy me because I said "my"? But what else did I say than "yours," and the property of all of us who are one? Yet, that you may come to the aid of this longing of mine, what shall I ask of you? Or do I earn it of myself? You know this much only, that you owe it. But now I say to both of you: beware lest you think you know anything except what you have learned so well, at least, as you know that one, two, three, four, gathered together, make ten in the sum. But likewise beware lest you think you will either not come to know the truth in philosophy, or that it can in no way be so known. For believe me, or rather him who says, *Seek and you will find* [^2], that knowledge is not to be despaired of, and that it will be more manifest than those numbers. Now let us come to our subject. For I have begun, somewhat late, to fear that this beginning may exceed measure, and it is no light matter. For measure is without doubt divine: but should it deceive me while it sweetly leads, I shall be more cautious when I have become wise.

**2. 4. 10.** After the earlier conversation, which we set down in the first book, we were at leisure from disputing for about seven days, while yet we reviewed three books of Virgil after the first, and treated them as seemed to suit the time. By this work, however, Licentius was so inflamed with zeal for poetry that he seemed to me even to need some restraining. For now he could not gladly bear to be called away from this intent to any other thing. At last, however, when the light of philosophy was praised by me as much as I could, he came not unwillingly to take up again the question about the Academics which we had deferred: and by chance the day had dawned so serene that it seemed to suit no thing more than the making-serene of our minds. And so we left our beds earlier than usual, and dealt a little with the country folk, since the season pressed. Then Alypius said, "Before I hear you disputing about the Academics, I want that conversation of yours read to me, which you say was completed in my absence: for otherwise, since the occasion of this dispute arose from it, I cannot, in hearing you, but either err or at least labor." When this had been done, and we saw that nearly the whole forenoon had been consumed in it, we resolved to return home from the field, which had received us as we walked. And Licentius said, "I ask that you not be loath, before the meal, to repeat to me briefly, by setting it forth, the whole opinion of the Academics, lest anything in it escape me that may be for my side." "I will," I said, "and the more gladly because, while you think about this matter, you will eat little." "Do not," he said, "be confident on that score: for I have often noticed that many, and especially my father, eat the more, the more stuffed with cares they are. Besides, you too, thinking about those meters, I have not so experienced that my care leaves my table free of care. Which indeed I am wont to wonder at in myself: for what does it mean, that we then seek food more persistently when we bend the mind to something else? Or what is it that, while our hands and teeth are occupied, the mind becomes imperious for us?" "Hear rather," I said, "what you had asked about the Academics, lest, while you turn over those meters, I have to suffer you not only at table without meter, but also in our questions. But if I hide anything for my side, Alypius will betray it." "Your good faith is needed," said Alypius: "for if it is to be feared that you hide anything, I think it hard for him—from whom no one who knows me is ignorant that I learned these things—to be caught out by me, especially since in betraying the truth you will be consulting not so much for victory as for your own mind."

**2. 5. 11.** "I will act in good faith," I said, "since you prescribe it by right. For it pleased the Academics both that knowledge cannot come to a human being—at least of those things which pertain to philosophy; for the rest Carneades said he did not care about—and yet that a human being can be wise, and that the whole office of the wise man, as was discussed by you too, Licentius, in that conversation, is unfolded in the search for truth. From which it is concluded that the wise man assents to nothing at all: for he must necessarily err—which is forbidden to the wise man—if he assents to uncertain things. And they not only said all things were uncertain, but affirmed it with the most copious reasons. But that the truth cannot be grasped they seemed to have seized from that definition of the Stoic Zeno, who says that that can be perceived as true which is so impressed on the mind from the source whence it is, that it could not be from a source whence it is not. Which is said more briefly and plainly thus: the truth can be grasped by those marks which what is false cannot have. That this can in no way be found, they bent all their force most vehemently to establish. Thence the disagreements of philosophers, thence the deceptions of the senses, thence dreams and madnesses, thence the "liars" and the "sorites" flourished in the support of that cause. And since from the same Zeno they had received that nothing is baser than to opine, they most cunningly concluded that, if nothing could be perceived, and to opine was most base, the wise man would never approve anything."

**2. 5. 12.** "Hence great ill-will was stirred up against them: for it seemed to follow that he would do nothing who approved nothing. Whence the Academics seemed to describe their wise man as always asleep and a deserter of all his duties, since they judged that he approved nothing. Here they, introducing a certain "probable," which they also called "verisimilar," asserted that the wise man in no way ceases from his duties, since he has something to follow; but that the truth, whether on account of certain shadows of nature or on account of the likeness of things, lay hidden, either buried or confused. Although even that very reining-in and, as it were, suspension of assent they said was a great action of the wise man. I seem to myself to have set forth the whole briefly, as you wished, and to have departed in nothing from your prescription, Alypius—that is, to have acted, as the saying goes, in good faith. For if I said anything otherwise than it is, or perhaps did not say it, nothing was done by me on purpose. Good faith, then, is from the intention of the mind. For one human being ought to seem to another a false teacher to be taught, a deceiving one to be guarded against: of which the former needs a good teacher, the latter a cautious pupil."

**2. 5. 13.** Then Alypius said, "I am grateful, since both Licentius has been satisfied by you, and you have relieved me of the burden imposed on me. For you had no more to fear lest, for the sake of testing me, you say too little (for how else could it be done?) than I had, if it had been necessary to betray you in anything. Therefore please—that which is lacking, not so much to the inquiry as to the inquirer—do not be loath to set forth the difference between the new and the old Academy." "I confess," I said, "I am loath. So you will do me a kindness (for I cannot deny that what you mention pertains very much to the matter) if, while I rest a little, you will distinguish these names among yourselves and lay open the cause of the new Academy." "I would believe," he said, "that I too wished to call you away from your meal, did I not rather think you frightened a while ago by Licentius, and his request had so prescribed to us that whatever there was of this complication should be unraveled for him before the meal." And when he was about to say the rest, our mother (for we were now at home) began so to push us to the meal that there was no room for making speeches.

**2. 6. 14.** Then, when we had taken as much nourishment as was enough to check hunger, and had returned to the meadow, Alypius said, "I will obey your wish, and would not dare to refuse. For if nothing escapes me, I shall congratulate it both to your teaching and to my own memory. But if perhaps I go astray in anything, you will set it right, so that henceforth I may not dread a delegation of this kind. The split of the new Academy, I think, was stirred up not so much against the old—conceived as it was—as against the Stoics. Nor indeed should it be thought a split, since the new question raised by Zeno had to be resolved and discussed. For about not-perceiving, although agitated by no conflicts, yet the opinion, dwelling even in the minds of the old Academics, was not shamelessly held. Which is easy to prove even by the authority of Socrates himself and of Plato and the rest of the ancients, who believed themselves defended from error this far, if they had not rashly committed themselves to assent: although they did not introduce a proper discussion of this matter into their schools, nor was it ever clearly inquired by them whether the truth can be perceived or not. And when Zeno had brought this in as something raw and new, and contended that nothing can be perceived except what is so true that it is distinguished from the false by unlike marks, and that the wise man must not fall into opinion, and Arcesilaus heard this, he denied that anything of this kind could be found by a human being, and that the wise man's life must not be committed to the shipwreck of opinion. Whence he also concluded that assent must be given to nothing."

**2. 6. 15.** "But while the matter stood so, that the old Academy seemed rather augmented than assailed, there arose Antiochus, a hearer of Philo, who, as he seemed to some, more desirous of glory than of truth, brought the opinions of both Academies into rivalry. For he said that the new Academics had tried to introduce something unusual and most remote from the opinion of the ancients. For which matter he implored the trust of the old natural philosophers and other great philosophers; even attacking the Academics themselves, who contended that they followed the verisimilar, while they confessed they were ignorant of the truth itself. And he had gathered many arguments, which I think must now be passed over: yet he defended nothing more than that the wise man can perceive. This, I think, was the controversy between the new and old Academics. If it is otherwise—so that you may fully instruct Licentius—I would have asked it on behalf of both. But if it is as I have been able to say, carry through the discussion you have undertaken."

**2. 7. 16.** Then I said, "How long, Licentius, will you rest in this conversation of ours, longer than I thought? You have heard who your Academics are." But he, smiling bashfully and somewhat more disturbed by this address, said, "It repents me that I so greatly affirmed against Trygetius that the happy life consists in the inquiry into truth. For this question so disturbs me that I am all but wretched—who certainly, if you have any humanity, seem to you to be pitied. But why do I, fool that I am, torture myself? Or why do I shudder, propped up by so great a goodness of cause? I will not yield except to the truth." "Do the new Academics please you?" I said. "Very much," he said. "Then they seem to you to speak the truth?" Then he, when he was about to assent, made more cautious by Alypius's smile, hesitated somewhat. And then he said, "Repeat the little question." "Do the Academics seem to you to speak the truth?" I said. And again, when he had long been silent, "Whether it is true," he said, "I do not know: it is probable, however. For I see nothing more to follow." "Probable," I said, "do you know it is also called by them 'verisimilar'?" "So it seems," he said. "Then," I said, "the opinion of the Academics is verisimilar." "So," he said. "Now, I beg, attend more diligently," I said. "If anyone affirmed that your brother seemed like your father, and did not know your father himself, would he not seem to you insane or inept?" And here he was long silent. Then he said, "This does not seem to me absurd."

**2. 7. 17.** When I had begun to answer him, "Wait, I beg," he said, "a little." And then, smiling, "Tell me, I pray you," he said, "are you now certain of your victory?" Then I said, "Suppose me to be certain: yet you ought not therefore to desert your cause, especially since this discussion was undertaken among us for the sake of exercising you, and of provoking your mind to be refined." "Have I," he said, "either read the Academics, or been instructed in so many disciplines, as you come to me equipped with?" "The Academics," I said, "neither had those read them by whom that opinion was first defended. But if learning and an abundance of disciplines fail you, your wit ought nonetheless not to be so weak that, with no charge made, you should succumb to my very few words and questions. For I have now begun to fear lest Alypius succeed you sooner than I wish—an adversary with whom I shall not walk about so securely." "Then would," he said, "that I were already conquered, that I might at last hear you disputing, and—what is more—see it; than which spectacle nothing more happy can be set before me. For since it has pleased you to pour these things forth, rather than to pour them away—since what bursts from your mouth you catch with the pen, and do not let fall to the ground, as the saying goes [^3]—it will be permitted to read them too: but somehow, when the very same eyes are applied among which the conversation is carried on, a good disputation suffuses the mind, if not more usefully, at least more joyfully."

**2. 7. 18.** "I am grateful," I said: "but those sudden joys of yours have rashly forced you to abandon that opinion by which you said that no spectacle more happy could be set before you. For what if you shall see that father of yours—than whom surely no one, after so long a thirst, will drink in philosophy more ardently—seeking and disputing these things with us, when I shall never think myself more fortunate—what, after all, is it fitting for you to feel and say?" Here indeed he wept somewhat, and when he could speak, stretching out his hand and looking up to heaven, "And when, O God," he said, "shall I see this? But of you nothing is to be despaired." Here, when we had nearly all begun to be let down from the intent of disputation into tears, struggling with myself and scarcely collecting myself, I said, "Come rather, and return to your strength; which, that you might gather it from wherever you could, being about to be the Academy's patron, I had long warned: not, I think, that now, before the trumpet, a tremor should seize your limbs [^4]; or that, from desire to behold another's fight, you should so quickly wish yourself to be a captive." Here Trygetius, when he had attended well enough that our faces were now made serene, said, "Why should he not wish it, a man so holy that God has granted him this before his prayers? Believe it now, Licentius; for you who do not find what to answer, and still wish to be conquered, seem to me of little faith [^5]." We smiled. Then Licentius said, "Speak, you who are happy—not by finding the truth, but certainly by not seeking it."

**2. 7. 19.** When, at this cheerfulness of the young men, we were the merrier, I said, "Attend to the question, and return to the road firmer and stronger, if you can." "Here I am," he said, "as much as I can be." "For what if that viewer of my brother should have learned by report that he is like his father—can he be insane or inept if he believes it?" "Can he," I said, "at least be called foolish?" "Not at once," he said, "unless he should contend that he knows it. For if, as something probable, he follows what frequent report has spread abroad, he can be accused of no rashness." Then I said, "Let us consider the matter itself a little, and, as it were, set it before our eyes. See, suppose that man, whatever he is, whom we are describing, to be present: your brother arrives from somewhere; there this man says, 'Whose son is this boy?' It is answered, 'A certain Romanianus's.' But this man: 'How like his father he is! how not rashly had report brought me this!' Here you, or someone else: 'Do you know Romanianus, good fellow?' 'I do not know him,' he says: 'yet he seems to me like him.' Could anyone hold his laughter?" "In no way," he said. "Then," I said, "you see what follows." "I have long seen it," he said. "But yet I want to hear the conclusion itself from you: for you ought to begin to nourish the one you have captured." "Why should I not," I said, "conclude? The matter itself cries out that your Academics are to be laughed at in like manner, who say they follow in life a likeness of truth, while they are ignorant of what the truth itself is."

**2. 8. 20.** Then Trygetius said, "Far different, it seems to me, is the caution of the Academics from the ineptitude of this man you have described. For they attain by reasonings what they say is verisimilar; but this inept fellow followed report, than whose authority nothing is cheaper." "As if, indeed," I said, "he would not be more inept if he said, 'I do not at all know his father, nor have I learned by report how like his father he is, and yet he seems to me like him.'" "More inept, certainly," he said. "But to what end is this?" "Because such," I said, "are those who say: 'The truth indeed we do not know; but this which we see is like that which we do not know.'" "They say 'probable,'" he said. To which I said, "How do you say that? Do you deny that they say 'verisimilar'?" And he said, "I wanted to say it for this reason, to exclude that likeness. For it seemed to me that report had wickedly rushed into your question, when the Academics trust not even human eyes, much less report—not, indeed, with a thousand eyes, as the poets feign, but with monstrous ones. For who, after all, am I, a defender of the Academy? Or do you, in this question, envy my security? Behold, you have Alypius, whose arrival, I beg, may give us a holiday—whom we think you have long, not in vain, dreaded."

**2. 8. 21.** Then, silence being made, both turned their eyes upon Alypius. Then he said, "I would wish indeed, as far as my strength allows, to aid your side somewhat, were not your omen a terror to me. But this dread, unless hope deceive me, I will easily put to flight. For at the same time it consoles me that the present assailant of the Academics has nearly taken up the burden of the conquered Trygetius, and now, by your confession, it is probable that he is the victor. This I rather fear, lest I be unable to avoid both the negligence of a deserted office and the shamelessness of an invaded one. For I do not believe you have forgotten that the office of judge was conferred on me." Here Trygetius said, "That is one thing, and this another; so we ask that you suffer yourself at last to be a private person." "Let me not refuse," he said; "lest, while I desire to avoid shamelessness or negligence, I fall into the snares of pride—than which vice nothing is more monstrous—if I should hold the honor granted me by you longer than you permit."

**2. 9. 22.** "Accordingly, I would have you set forth to me, good accuser of the Academics, your office; that is, in whose defense you assail these men. For I fear lest, in refuting the Academics, you wish to prove yourself an Academic. For of accusers," I said, "I think you know well there are two kinds: for if it was most modestly said by Cicero that he was the accuser of Verres in such a way that he was the defender of the Sicilians, for that reason it is necessary that he who accuses someone has another whom he defends." And he said, "But have you at least anything in which your opinion now stands firmly founded?" "It is easy," I said, "to answer this question, especially for me, to whom it is not sudden: I have already dealt with this whole matter within myself, and long and much turned it over in mind. Therefore hear, Alypius, what, I think, you already know very well: I do not want this discussion to be undertaken for the sake of disputing; let it be enough that we have skirmished with these young men, where philosophy has gladly, as it were, jested with us. Therefore let childish little tales be taken from our hands. The matter at issue is our life, our morals, our mind; which presumes that it will overcome the enmities of all deceptions, and, the truth being grasped, as if returning to the region of its origin, will triumph over lusts, and, having taken temperance as a spouse, will so reign that it will return more securely to heaven. Do you see what I say? Let us now take away all these things from the midst: *arms must be made for a keen man* [^6]; nor have I ever wished anything less than that among those who have lived much together, and conversed much, anything should arise from which a new conflict, as it were, should rise up. But for the sake of memory, which is an unfaithful guardian of things thought out, I wanted to be set down in writing what we have often discussed among ourselves, and at the same time that these young men might both learn to attend to these matters, and try to approach and undergo them."

**2. 9. 23.** "Then do you not know that I have nothing certain yet which I hold, but am hindered from seeking it by the arguments and disputations of the Academics? For somehow they have made in my mind a certain probability (to use their word, that I may not yet depart from it) that a human being cannot find the truth: whence I had become sluggish and altogether idle, nor dared to seek what most keen and most learned men were not permitted to find. Unless, then, I shall first have persuaded myself that the truth can be found, as much as they persuaded themselves that it cannot, I will not dare to seek, nor have I anything to defend. And so remove that question, if you please, and rather let us discuss among ourselves, as shrewdly as we can, whether the truth can be found. And for my part I seem to myself to have already many things by which I strive to lean against the reasoning of the Academics: between whom and me just now there is no difference, except that to them it seemed probable that the truth cannot be found; but to me it is probable that it can be found. For the ignorance of the truth is either peculiar to me, if they were pretending, or at least common to both of us."

**2. 10. 24.** Then Alypius said, "Now I will walk on secure: for I see you will be not so much an accuser as a helper. And so, that we may not go too far, let us see, I beg, first, lest through this question—in which I seem to have succeeded those who yielded to you—we fall into a controversy of words, which, on your own suggestion and by that Tullian authority, we have often confessed to be most base. For when, unless I am mistaken, Licentius said that the Academics' opinion about probability pleased him, you added what he without doubt confirmed—whether he knew that this is also called by them 'verisimilitude.' And I know well, since these things are known to me from you, that the opinions of the Academics are not without you. Since these, as I said, are fixed in your mind, I do not know why you pursue words." "It is not," I said, "believe me, a great controversy of words, but of the things themselves: for I do not think those men were such as did not know how to impose names on things; but they seem to me to have chosen these words both to conceal their opinion from the slower and to signify it to the more vigilant. Why and how this seems so to me I will set forth, when I have first discussed those things which men think were said by them as enemies, as it were, of human knowledge. And so I am very glad that our conversation has today advanced this far, that it stands open and clear enough what was being sought between us. For those men seem to me to have been altogether grave and prudent men. But if there is anything we shall now dispute, it will be against those who have believed the Academics opposed to the finding of truth. And lest you think me frightened, even against those very men I will not unwillingly arm myself, if they defended—not for the sake of concealing their opinion, lest by minds rashly profaned, and, as it were, unhallowed, certain sacred things of truth should be betrayed—but from their heart, those things which we read in their books. This I would do today, did not the sun's setting now compel us to return home." Thus far was it disputed that day.

**2. 11. 25.** The next day, although a day no less mild and tranquil had dawned, we scarcely extricated ourselves from domestic affairs. For we had consumed a great part of it especially in the writing of letters. And when scarcely two hours were left, we went forth to the meadow. For the great serenity of the sky invited us, and it pleased us not to suffer even that time which remained to perish. And so, when we had come to the usual tree, and had stayed in the place, I said, "I would have you, young men—since today no great matter is to be taken up—recall to my memory how Alypius answered yesterday the little question that disturbed you." Here Licentius said, "It is so brief that it is no trouble to recall it; but how slight it is you may see. For, as I think, he forbade you, since the matter was agreed, to raise a question about words." And I said, "This very thing—what it is, and what force it has—have you noticed enough?" "I seem to myself," he said, "to see what it is; but I beg you to set it forth a little. For I have often heard from you that it is base for disputants to linger over a question of words, when no contest about things remains. But this is too subtle for it to be demanded of me to explain."

**2. 11. 26.** "Hear, then," I said, "what it is, all of you. The Academics call that 'probable' or 'verisimilar' which can invite us to act without assent. But by 'without assent' I mean: that we do not opine that what we do is true, or think that we know it, yet we do it. For example, if someone should ask us whether, after last night so clear and pure, today the sun would rise so cheerful: I believe we would deny that we knew it, yet we would say it seemed so. 'Such,' says the Academic, 'seem to me all the things which I thought ought to be called probable or verisimilar; which, if you wish to call by another name, I do not fight. For it is enough for me that you have now well understood what I mean—that is, on what things I impose these names. For it does not befit the wise man to be a maker of words, but an inquirer into things' [^7]. Have you understood well enough how those playthings, with which I was harassing you, have been struck from my hands?" Here, when both had answered that they understood, and by their very look demanded my answer, I said, "What do you think? That Cicero, whose these words are, was so poor in the Latin tongue as to impose on things he perceived names less fitting?"

**2. 12. 27.** Then Trygetius said, "It now pleases us, since the matter is known, to stir no calumnies about words. So see rather what you answer to this man who freed us, upon whom, driven on, you try again to rush." And Licentius said, "Wait, I beg: for something, I know not what, gleams faintly upon me, by which I see that so great an argument ought not to have been so easily snatched from you." And when, fixed in thought, he had been silent for a while, "I ask," he said, "nothing seems more absurd than to say that he follows the verisimilar who is ignorant of what the truth is: nor does that simile of yours confound me. For when I am rightly asked whether, from this temperate state of the sky, no rain is forced for tomorrow, I answer that it is verisimilar, I who do not deny that I know something true. For I know that this tree cannot now become silver, and many such true things I say I know without shamelessness, of which I see that those things are like which I call verisimilar. But you, Carneades, or whatever other Greek plague—that I may spare our own (for why should I hesitate to cross to this side, to him to whom I am owed a captive by the right of victory?)—you, then, when you say you know nothing true, whence do you follow this verisimilar? 'But I could impose no other name on it.' Why, then, must we dispute with one who cannot even speak?"

**2. 12. 28.** "I will not," said Alypius, "fear deserters: how much less that Carneades, against whom—moved by I know not whether youthful or boyish levity—you thought that curses rather than any weapon should be hurled? For he, to strengthen his opinion, which was always founded only on the probable, would for the moment easily have this against you: that we are placed so far from the finding of truth that you yourself can be a great argument for it, who were so dislodged from your place by one little question that you wholly knew not where you should stand. But these things, and your knowledge, which a little while ago you confessed impressed upon you about this tree, let us defer to another time. For although you have now chosen the other side, yet you must be diligently taught what I said a little while ago. For we had not yet, I think, advanced into that question in which it is asked whether the truth can be found: but I thought only this should be prescribed in the very vestibule of my defense—in which I had seen you weary and prostrate—namely, that whether 'verisimilar,' or 'probable,' or by whatever other name it can be called, which the Academics say is enough for them, must not be inquired into. For if you now seem to yourself the best finder of truth, it is nothing to me. Afterward, if you are not ungrateful for this patronage of mine, you will perhaps teach me the same things."

**2. 13. 29.** Here I, when Licentius bashfully dreaded the onset of Alypius, said, "You preferred, Alypius, to say anything rather than how we must dispute with those who do not know how to speak." And he said, "Since both long ago, to me and to all, it was known, and now by your own profession you sufficiently indicate that you are skilled in speaking, I would have you explain first the usefulness of this inquiry of yours, which is either superfluous, I think—and to answer it is much more superfluous—or, if it shall seem advantageous and cannot be explained by me, I would obtain of you, as a suppliant, that you not be loath to do the office of a teacher." "You remember," I said, "that I promised yesterday to deal with these words afterward. And now that sun warns that the playthings I set out for the boys I should put back into their boxes; especially since I now set them out rather to display than to sell them. Now, before the darkness, which is wont to be the patroness of the Academics, takes hold of our pen, I want it to be fully agreed among us today to what question we must rise in the morning to unfold. So answer, I beg: whether it seems to you that the Academics held a certain opinion about the truth, and were unwilling to betray it rashly to unknown or unpurged minds; or whether they really felt as their disputations stand."

**2. 13. 30.** Then he said, "What was in their mind I will not rashly affirm. For, as far as can be gathered from the books, you know better in what words they were wont to bring out their opinion: but if you consult me about myself, I think the truth has not yet been found. I add also—what you were demanding about the Academics—that I think it cannot be found, not only by my opinion grown ingrained, which you have nearly always noticed, but also by the authority of great and more excellent philosophers, to whom to yield our necks either our own weakness or their shrewdness somehow compels us—beyond which it must be believed that nothing more can now be found." "This," I said, "is what I wanted. For I feared lest, when this seemed to you too which seems so to me, our discussion should remain maimed, no one existing who would force the matter to come to grips from the other side, so that it might be diligently, as far as we can, turned over. And so, if that had happened, I was prepared to ask you to take up the part of the Academics in such a way that they should seem to you not only to have disputed, but to have felt, that the truth cannot be grasped. It is asked, then, between us, whether by their arguments it is probable that nothing can be perceived and that assent must be given to nothing. If you maintain this, I will gladly yield: but if I shall be able to show that it is much more probable both that the wise man can come to the truth, and that assent must not always be withheld, you will have nothing, I think, why you should not suffer yourself to cross over to my opinion." When this had pleased him and the rest who were present, now overshadowed by evening, we returned home.
