# Book III

**3. 1. 1.** When, after that conversation which the second book contains, we had on another day sat down in the baths—for the day was too gloomy for us to be willing to go down to the meadow—I began thus: "I think you have now noticed well enough on what matter the question has been set up to be discussed among us. But before I come to my own part, which pertains to unfolding it, hear gladly, I beg, a few things—not foreign to the matter—about our hope, our life, our resolve. I judge our business no light or superfluous one, but necessary and supreme: to seek the truth greatly. On this Alypius and I agree. For both the other philosophers thought their wise man had found it, and the Academics professed that it must be sought by their wise man with the utmost effort, and that he does this diligently; but because the truth lay either buried or, confused, did not stand out, for the conduct of life he follows that which occurs as probable and verisimilar. This too was settled by your earlier debate. For when one asserted that a human being is made happy by the truth being found, the other only by its being diligently sought, none of us doubts that nothing is to be set by us before this business. Therefore, how, I ask, do we seem to you to have spent yesterday? For you were permitted to live in your studies. For both you, Trygetius, delighted yourself in the songs of Virgil, and Licentius was at leisure to compose verses, by love of which he is so smitten that I thought I must bring in this discourse especially for his sake—by which, in his mind, philosophy (for now is the time) may claim and vindicate for itself the greater part, not only over poetry, but over any other discipline whatever."

**3. 2. 2.** "But I ask you: did you not pity us, when the day before we had gone to bed so as to rise to nothing but the deferred question, and altogether to nothing else, since on account of so great a domestic matter, necessarily to be carried through, there arose such things that, wholly occupied with these, we could scarcely breathe again into ourselves in the two last hours of the day? Therefore it has always been my opinion that the wise man, now made such, needs nothing; but that, in order to become wise, fortune is most necessary—unless something else seems so to Alypius." Then he said, "How much right you assign to fortune I do not yet know well. For if you think that, for despising fortune, fortune itself is needed, I too give you my company in this opinion. But if you concede nothing else to fortune than those things which cannot, except with fortune willing, supply the body's need, I do not feel so. For either it is permitted—even with the same fortune resisting and unwilling—for one not yet wise, but desirous of wisdom, to take those things which we confess necessary to life; or it must be conceded that even in the whole life of the wise man she lords it, since the wise man himself cannot but stand in need of those things necessary to the body."

**3. 2. 3.** "You say, then," I said, "that fortune is necessary to one zealous for wisdom, but you deny it to the wise man." "It is not beside the point to repeat the same things," he said. "And so now too I ask of you whether you reckon that fortune helps anything toward the despising of fortune itself. And if you think so, I say that the one desirous of wisdom greatly needs fortune." "I think so," I said, "since through her he will become such as can despise her. Nor is it absurd: for thus, even to us as little ones, the breasts are necessary, by which it is brought about that afterward we can live and be strong without them." "If the conception of our minds does not disagree," he said, "it is clear to me that our opinions agree; unless perhaps it seems to someone that we must discuss this—that it is not the fortune or the breasts themselves, but some other thing, that makes us despisers either of fortune or of the breasts." "It is no great thing," I said, "to use another simile. For just as without a ship, or any vehicle, or altogether—lest I should even fear Daedalus himself—without any instruments suited to this matter, or some more hidden power, no one crosses the Aegean Sea, though he propose nothing else than to arrive; which, when it has happened to him, he is ready to throw away and despise all those things by which he was carried: so whoever has wished to come to the harbor of wisdom, and, as it were, to its most firm and most quiet soil—since, to omit other things, if he be blind and deaf he cannot, which lies in the power of fortune—seems to me to have fortune necessary for that which he has desired. Which, when he has obtained it, although he be thought to stand in need of certain things pertaining to the body's health, yet this is agreed: that he needs these not in order to be wise, but in order to live among human beings." "Nay," he said, "if he be blind and deaf, both the attaining of wisdom and that very life for the sake of which wisdom is sought, in my opinion, he will rightly despise."

**3. 2. 4.** "Yet," I said, "since our very life, while we live here, is in the power of fortune, and no one can become wise except living: must it not be confessed that we need her favor, by which we are conveyed to wisdom?" "But since wisdom," he said, "is necessary only to the living, and, life being removed, there is no need of wisdom, I dread fortune not at all in the prolonging of life. For because I live, therefore I want wisdom; not because I desire wisdom, do I want life. Whence, if fortune takes my life from me, she will take away the cause of seeking wisdom. Therefore I have nothing why, in order to become wise, I should either wish for fortune's favor or dread its hindrances—unless perhaps you bring forward something else." Then I said, "Then you do not think that one zealous for wisdom can be hindered by fortune from coming to wisdom, even if she does not take his life from him?" "I do not think so," he said.

**3. 3. 5.** "I want," I said, "you to lay open for me a little what difference seems to you to be between the wise man and the philosopher." "The wise man," he said, "I think differs in nothing from the lover of wisdom, except that of the things of which there is in the wise man a certain settled state (*habitus*), in the lover of wisdom there is only an ardent longing." "What, after all, are those things?" I said. "For to me nothing else seems to be at issue, except that the one knows wisdom, the other desires to know it." "If you determine 'knowledge' by a modest limit," he said, "you have stated the matter more plainly." "In whatever way I determine it," I said, "this pleased everyone—that there can be no knowledge of false things." "Here," he said, "I thought a prescription should be set against you, lest by my unconsidered consent your speech ride easily over the fields of that principal question." "Plainly," I said, "you have left me nowhere I might ride. For unless I am mistaken, what I have long been contriving, we have come to the very end. For if, as you said subtly and truly, there is no difference between the lover of wisdom and the wise man, except that the one loves, the other has the discipline of wisdom—whence too you did not hesitate to express the very word, that is, a certain settled state; but no one can have a discipline in his mind who has learned nothing; and no one has learned anything who knows nothing; and no one can know the false: therefore the wise man knows the truth, whom you yourself have now confessed to have in his mind the discipline of wisdom, that is, a settled state." "I do not know," he said, "of what shamelessness I should be, if I should wish to deny that I confessed there is in the wise man a settled state of the inquiry into things divine and human. But why it should not seem to you a settled state of probable findings, I do not see." "Do you grant me," I said, "that no one knows false things?" "Easily indeed," he said. "Say now, if you can," I said, "that the wise man does not know wisdom." "What?" he said. "Do you with this boundary enclose all things, so that it cannot seem to him that he has grasped wisdom?" "Give me your right hand," I said. "For if you remember, this is what I said yesterday I would bring about—which now I rejoice was concluded not by me, but offered to me of your own accord. For when I had said that this is the difference between me and the Academics—that to them it seemed probable that the truth cannot be grasped; but to me it seems that, though not yet found by me, it can nonetheless be found by the wise man: now you, when you were pressed by my question whether the wise man does not know wisdom, said, 'It seems to him that he knows.'" "What then, afterward?" he said. "Because if," I said, "it seems to him that he knows wisdom, it does not seem to him that the wise man can know nothing. Or, if wisdom is nothing, I want you to affirm it."

**3. 3. 6.** "I would truly believe," he said, "that we had come to the lime and the end; but suddenly, when you interposed our right hands, I see that we are most disjoined and have advanced into a long course: namely, because yesterday no other question seemed to be set up by us than this—that the wise man can come to the grasping of truth, you affirming, I denying. But now I think I have conceded nothing to you except that it can seem to the wise man that he has attained the wisdom of probable things: yet that I have placed this wisdom in the investigation of things divine and human, I think is doubtful to none of us." "It is not," I said, "because you wrap it up, that you escape: for you now seem to me to dispute for the sake of exercising yourself. And because you know well that these young men can scarcely yet discern what is discussed keenly and subtly, you abuse, as it were, the ignorance of the judges, so that you may speak as long as you please with no one crying out against you. For a little while ago you said, when I asked whether the wise man knows wisdom, that it seemed to him that he knows. To him, then, to whom it seems that the wise man knows wisdom, it surely does not seem that the wise man knows nothing. For this cannot be contended unless someone dare to say that wisdom is nothing. From which it follows that this seems to you which also seems to me: for it seems to me that the wise man knows something, and to you, I think—to whom it is pleasing—it seems that it seems to the wise man that the wise man knows wisdom." Then he said, "I do not think I wish to exercise my wit more than you; and at this I wonder, for you have no need of any exercise in this matter. For to me, perhaps still blind, there seems to be a difference between 'it seems to him that he knows' and 'he knows'; and between the wisdom which is placed in investigation and the truth: which, when either of the two is spoken of by us, how they square with each other I do not find." Then I, when we were now called to the meal, said, "I am not displeased that you resist so much: for either we both do not know what we are saying, and effort must be given that we be not so base; or one of us does, which it is no less base to leave and neglect. But in the afternoon hours we will return to one another. For when it seemed to me that we had now come to the lime, you mixed in fists too." Here, when they had smiled, we departed.

**3. 4. 7.** And when we had returned, we found Licentius—to whom, never thirsting, would Helicon have come—gaping after the contriving of verses. For from nearly the middle of the meal—although the beginning of our meal was the same as its end—he had secretly risen, and had drunk nothing. To him I said, "I wish indeed for you that you may someday embrace that poetry which you have desired: not that this perfection delights me too much, but because I see you have so caught fire that you cannot escape from this love except by satiety; which is wont easily to come about after perfection. Then, since you are well tuneful, I would rather you dinned your verses into our ears than that, in those Greek tragedies, after the manner of the little birds we see shut up in cages, you chant words you do not understand. Yet I advise you to go on and drink, if you will, and return to our school, if *Hortensius* and philosophy now deserve anything of you—to which you have already, by that conversation of yours, offered the sweetest first-fruits, which had moved you more vehemently than this poetry to the knowledge of great and truly fruitful things. But while I desire to call you back to those disciplines by which minds are cultivated, I fear lest it become a labyrinth for you, and I almost repent of having checked you from that impulse." He blushed, and went off to drink. For he was both very thirsty, and the occasion was given of avoiding me, who would perhaps say more and harsher things.

**3. 4. 8.** And when he had returned, all attentive, I began thus: "Is it so, Alypius, that about a matter now, as it seems to me, most manifest, we do not agree?" "It is no wonder," he said, "if what you assert is ready to hand for you is obscure to me: since most manifest things can be more manifest to others, and likewise certain obscure things more obscure to some. For if even this is truly manifest to you, believe me, there is someone else to whom even this manifest thing of yours is more manifest; and likewise another to whom my obscure thing is more obscure. But lest you think me too combative, I would beg that you set forth this manifest thing more manifestly." "Attend, I beg, diligently," I said, "and lay aside a little your care for answering. For if I know you and myself well, what I say will, by an effort given, easily grow clear, and one will quickly persuade the other. Did you say at last—or perhaps I had grown deaf—that it seems to the wise man that he knows wisdom?" He nodded. "Let us leave that wise man aside a little," I said. "Are you yourself wise, or not?" "Nothing less," he said. "Yet I want," I said, "you to answer me what you yourself feel about the Academic wise man: does it seem to you that he knows wisdom?" "Whether 'it seems to him that he knows' or 'he knows,'" he said, "do you think it one thing or different? For I fear lest this confusion offer some refuge to one of us."

**3. 4. 9.** "This," I said, "is that Tuscan wrangle, as it is wont to be called, when to a question raised not its solution but the raising of another seems to heal it. Which our poet too (that I may give myself a little to Licentius's ears) judged in the Bucolic poem to be becoming, this rustic and plainly pastoral way, when one asks the other where the space of heaven opens no wider than three ells; but the other,  
*Tell me in what lands the flowers spring up inscribed with the names of kings* [^1].  
And I beg, Alypius, that you not think this permitted us in the country: surely let even these little baths make some recollection of the comeliness of the gymnasia: to that, if you please, which I ask, answer. Does the Academics' wise man seem to you to know wisdom?" "Lest, by returning words for words, we go on at length," he said, "it seems that it seems to him that he knows." "Then," I said, "it seems to you that he does not know? For I am not asking what it seems to you seems to the wise man, but whether it seems to you that the wise man knows wisdom. Here, I think, you can either affirm or deny." "O that," he said, "it were either as easy for me as for you, or as hard for you as for me! then you would neither be so troublesome, nor hope for anything in these matters. For when you asked me what seemed to me about the Academic wise man, I answered, 'It seems to me that it seems to him that he knows wisdom,' lest either I rashly affirm that I know, or rashly say that he knows." "I beg you, grant me as a great kindness," I said, "first, that you deign to answer what I, not what you yourself, ask. Then, that you set aside a little now my hope, which I know is no less a care to you than your own. Certainly, if I deceive myself in this question, I will quickly cross to your side, and we will end the controversy. Finally, that, with some anxiety driven off by which I see you touched, you attend more diligently, that you may easily understand what I want answered me by you. For you said you did not affirm or deny—which must surely be done for what I ask—lest you rashly say you know what you do not know: as if, indeed, I had asked what you know, and not what seems to you. And so now I ask the same thing more plainly (if it can be said more plainly): does it seem to you that the wise man knows wisdom, or does it not seem so?" "If the wise man," he said, "such as reason sets forth, can be found, he can seem to me to know wisdom." "Reason, then," I said, "sets forth to you that the wise man is such as does not not know wisdom: and rightly so. For it was not fitting that it should otherwise seem to you."

**3. 4. 10.** "I ask, then, now, whether the wise man can be found. For if he can, he can also know wisdom, and the whole question between us is resolved. But if you say he cannot, then it will no longer be asked whether the wise man knows anything, but whether anyone can be wise. And this being settled, we must now depart from the Academics, and this question must be turned over with you diligently and cautiously, as far as we are able. For it pleased them, or rather it seemed to them, both that a human being can be wise, and yet that knowledge cannot fall upon a human being. Therefore they affirmed that the wise man knows nothing. But to you it seems that he knows wisdom, which is surely not to know nothing. For it pleased us at the same time—as also all the ancients, and the Academics themselves—that no one can know false things: whence this now remains, that you either contend that wisdom is nothing, or confess that such a wise man is described by the Academics as reason does not allow."

**3. 5. 11.** "And these things being set aside, you would consent that we inquire whether such wisdom as reason sets forth can come to a human being. For we ought not, nor can we, rightly call anything else wisdom. Even if I grant," he said, "what I see you greatly striving for—that wisdom is known by the wise man, and that something has been caught between us which the wise man can perceive—yet by no means does the whole intent of the Academics seem to me overthrown. For I foresee that no small place for defense is reserved for them, and that suspension of assent is not cut off, since by this very thing they cannot fail their own cause by which you think them convicted. For they will say that so far is nothing grasped, and assent to be given to nothing, that even this about nothing being perceived—which through nearly their whole life, up to your time, they had probably persuaded themselves—has now, by this conclusion, been wrung from them: so that, whether then or then the force of this argument is unconquered, whether by the slowness of my wit or really by its own strength, it cannot move them from their place, since they can still boldly affirm that not even now is consent to be given to anything. For perhaps at some time something can be found, even by themselves or by another, against this too, which is keenly and probably said: and they ought to notice their own image, and as it were a certain mirror, in that Proteus, who is reported to be wont to be caught by that whereby he was least caught, and his investigators never to have held him fast except by the index of some kind of divine power. May it be present, and deign to demonstrate to us that truth which is so great a care; then I too, even against their will—which I least think—will confess that they are overcome."

**3. 5. 12.** "It is well," I said; "I have wished for nothing more at all. For see, I beg, how many and how great goods have come to me. The first is that the Academics are now said to be so convicted that nothing remains to them for defense except what cannot happen. For who could either in any way understand or believe this—that one who has been conquered should, by the very thing whereby he has been conquered, boast himself the conqueror? Then, if any conflict now remains with them, it is not from what they say—that nothing can be known—but from what they contend, that assent must be given to nothing. Now, therefore, we are in agreement. For as to me, so also to them, it seems that the wise man knows wisdom. But yet they warn that one must abstain from assent. For they say only that it seems so to them, but that they know it in no way: as if I professed that I know. I too say that it seems so to me: for I am foolish, as they too are, if they do not know wisdom. But that we ought to approve something, I think—that is, the truth. About which I consult them whether they deny it—that is, whether it pleases them that assent must not be given to the truth. They will never say this, but will assert that it cannot be found. Therefore here too in some part they hold me as an ally, since to both it is not displeasing—nay, necessarily pleasing—that assent must be given to the truth. 'But who will demonstrate it?' they say. Here I will not care to contend with them; it is enough for me that it is now not probable that the wise man knows nothing, lest they be forced to say a most absurd thing—either that wisdom is nothing, or that the wise man does not know wisdom."

**3. 6. 13.** "But who can show the truth was said by you, Alypius—from whom I must greatly labor not to dissent. For you said that some divine power alone can show a human being what the truth is: which was said both briefly and piously. And so in this conversation of ours I have heard nothing more gladly, nothing weightier, nothing more probable, and—if that divine power, as I trust, be present—nothing more true. For with what loftiness of mind was that Proteus mentioned by you, with what intent toward the best kind of philosophy! For that Proteus—that you young men may see that the poets are not wholly to be despised by philosophy—is brought in as an image of truth. Of truth, I say, Proteus displays and sustains the role in the poems, which no one can hold who, deceived by false images, has either loosened or let go the knots of his grasp. For these are the images which, by the habit of corporeal things, through these senses by which we use what is necessary to this life, contrive to deceive and mock us even when the truth is held, and as it were is had in our hands. This, then, is the third good that has come to me, which I do not find at what to value. For my most intimate friend agrees with me not only about the probability of human life, but also about religion itself, which is the most manifest sign of a true friend. Since indeed friendship has been most rightly and most holily defined as agreement about things human and divine, with benevolence and love [^2]."

**3. 7. 14.** "Nevertheless, lest either the arguments of the Academics seem to spread certain fogs over us, or we seem proudly to resist for some the authority of most learned men—among whom Cicero especially cannot but move us—if it please you, I will first say a few things against those points by which those disputations seem to be opposed to the truth. Then, as it seems to me, I will show what cause the Academics had for concealing their opinion. And so, Alypius, although I see you wholly on my side, yet take up their part a little, and answer me." "Since today," he said, "you have set out, as they say, with good auspices, I will not hinder your fullest victory, and those parts—since they are imposed on you by me—I will now try to take up more securely; if only this, which you signify you will do by questions, you would, if it is convenient for you, prefer to turn into continuous speech; lest truly, like a stubborn adversary—which is most far from your humanity—I, now your captive, be tortured by those little darts of yours."

**3. 7. 15.** And I, when I noticed that the others too expected this, as if entering on another beginning, said, "I will humor you. And although, after that labor of the rhetorical school, I had presumed that in this light armor I would rest somewhat, so as to do these things by questioning rather than by speaking; yet, both because we are very few, so that I need not strain my voice against my health, and because I wished this pen, for the sake of the same health, to be, as it were, the charioteer and moderator of my speech, lest I be carried away by my mind more swiftly than care for the body demands: hear in continuous speech, as you wish, what I feel. But first let us see of what sort it is on which the lovers of the Academics are wont to boast too much. For there is in Cicero's books, which he wrote in patronage of this cause, a certain passage, seasoned, as it seems to me, with wondrous urbanity, but, as to some, even strengthened with firmness. It is altogether difficult that anyone should not be moved by what is said there: that to the Academic wise man the second place is given by all of the other sects who seem to themselves wise, since each must necessarily claim the first place for himself. From which it can probably be concluded that he is rightly first by his own judgment who is second by the judgment of all the rest."

**3. 7. 16.** "For suppose, for example, that a Stoic wise man is present; for against them especially the Academics' wit blazed. Therefore, if Zeno or Chrysippus be asked who the wise man is, he will answer that he is the one whom he himself described. On the contrary, Epicurus or some other of the adversaries will deny it, and will rather contend that his own most skilled hunter of pleasures is the wise man. Thence to a quarrel: Zeno cries out, and that whole Porch is in tumult, that a human being is born for nothing else than honorableness; that it draws minds to itself by its own splendor, with no advantage at all set without and, as it were, pandering as a reward; and that that pleasure of Epicurus is common only to the beasts among themselves, into whose fellowship to thrust both man and the wise man is wicked. On the other side, that one, having summoned from his little gardens to his aid a crowd, like that of Bacchus, of drunkards—seeking, however, someone whom, raving with unkempt nails and rough mouth, they may tear apart—pressing hard, exalting, with the people as witness, the name of pleasure, sweetness, repose, insists keenly that without these no one can seem to be happy. If the Academic should run into their brawl, he will hear both dragging him to their sides; but if he should go over to these or to those, he will be cried out by those he deserts as insane, unskilled, rash. And so, when he has diligently applied his ear both this way and that, being asked what seems to him, he will say he doubts. Now ask the Stoic who is better—Epicurus, who cries that he is raving, or the Academic, who pronounces that he must still deliberate about so great a matter? No one doubts that the Academic will be preferred. Again turn yourself to that other one, and ask whom he loves more: Zeno, by whom he is called a beast, or Arcesilaus, from whom he hears, 'You perhaps speak the truth, but I will inquire more diligently.' Is it not plain that that whole Porch seems insane, but the Academics, beside them, modest and cautious men, to Epicurus? [^3] Thus, about nearly all sects equally, Cicero most copiously affords a most delightful spectacle, as it were, to his readers, showing, as it were, that there is none of them who, when he has given himself the first place—which is necessary—does not say he gives the second to him whom he has beheld not resisting, but doubting. In which I will oppose nothing, nor take from them any glory. Let it indeed seem to whomever pleases that Cicero here did not jest, but wished to follow and gather certain empty and windy things—which is foreign to the levity of those very little Greeks."

**3. 8. 17.** Let it indeed seem to whomever pleases that Cicero here did not jest, but wished to follow and gather certain empty and windy things, which is foreign to the levity of those very little Greeks. For what hinders me—if I should wish to resist this vanity—from easily showing how much less an evil it is to be unlearned than unteachable? Whence it comes about that, when that boastful little Academic has given himself to each, as it were, as a pupil, and no one has been able to persuade him of what he thinks he knows, he is afterward, by their great consensus, laughed at. For now each one will judge any other of his adversaries to have learned nothing, but this man to be able to learn nothing. From which thereafter he will be thrown out of the schools of all—not with rods, which would be more disgraceful than troublesome, but with the cudgels and clubs of those cloaked fellows. For it will be no great business, against the common pest, to demand, as it were, certain Herculean aids of the Cynics. But if it pleases me to contend with them about this cheapest glory—which must be granted with easier pardon to me, now indeed philosophizing, but not yet wise—what will they have that they can refute? For see, let us suppose that I and an Academic have rushed into those quarrels of the philosophers: let all be present, let them briefly, as the time allows, set forth their opinions. Let it be asked of Carneades what he thinks. He will say he doubts. And so each one will prefer him to the rest. Therefore all to all: great glory indeed, and most lofty. Who would not wish to imitate him? And so I too, asked, will answer the same; the praise will be equal. In that glory, then, does the wise man rejoice, in which the fool is made equal to him? What if he even easily surpasses? For shame does nothing? For I will hold that Academic now departing from the judgment. For folly is more greedy of victory of this kind. Therefore, holding him back, I will betray to the judges what they do not know, and will say: 'I, best of men, have this in common with this man, that I doubt which of you follows the truth. But we have also opinions of our own, about which I ask you to judge. For to me it is uncertain indeed, though I have heard your decrees, where the truth is: but for this reason, that I do not know who among you is wise. But this man denies that even the wise man himself knows anything—not even that very wisdom from which he is called wise.' Who would not see whose the palm is? For if my adversary says this, I shall win by glory; but if, blushing, he confesses that the wise man knows wisdom, I shall win by opinion."

**3. 9. 18.** "But let us now withdraw from this litigious tribunal into some place where no crowd is troublesome to us; and would that into the very school of Plato, which is said to have received its name from this, that it was secluded from the people. Here let us now discuss, as far as we are able among ourselves, not about glory, which is light and childish, but about life itself, and about some hope of a happy mind. The Academics deny that anything can be known. Whence did this please you, men most studious and most learned? 'Zeno's definition moved us,' they say. Why, I ask? For if it is true, he who knows even it knows something true; but if it is false, it ought not to have moved most steadfast men. But let us see what Zeno says: namely, that such a presentation can be grasped and perceived as, when true, has no marks in common with the false. Did this move you, Platonic man, so that with all your forces you should drag the studious back from the hope of learning, so that they might desert the whole business of philosophizing, with the help also, as it were, of a certain groaning torpor of mind?"

**3. 9. 19.** "But how would it not move him deeply, if both nothing of the kind can be found, and nothing can be perceived except what is of that kind? If this is so, it should rather have been said that wisdom cannot fall upon a human being, than that the wise man does not know why he lives, does not know how he lives, does not know whether he lives; finally—than which nothing more perverse and more delirious and insane can be said—that he is at once wise and ignorant of wisdom. For which is harder: that a human being cannot be wise, or that the wise man does not know wisdom? Nothing need be disputed here, if the matter, so placed, is not enough for deciding. But that, perhaps, if it were said, would turn human beings utterly away from philosophizing: but now they are to be led on by the sweetest and most holy name of wisdom, so that, when, their lifetime worn away, they have learned nothing, they may afterward pursue you with the bitterest curses—you whom, having at least left the pleasures of the body, they followed to the torments of the mind."

**3. 9. 20.** "But let us see by which one they are rather deterred from philosophy. By the one who said: 'Hear, friend; philosophy is called not wisdom itself, but the study of wisdom; to which if you betake yourself, you will not indeed, while you live here, be wise (for wisdom is with God, nor can it come to a human being), but when you have sufficiently exercised and cleansed yourself by such study, your mind will easily enjoy these things after this life—that is, when you have ceased to be a human being'? Or by the one who said: 'Come, mortals, to philosophy; great is the fruit here: for what is dearer to a human being than wisdom? Come, then, that you may be wise—and may not know wisdom'? 'It will not be said so by me,' he says. 'That is to deceive, for nothing else will be found with you.' Thus it comes about that, if you say this, they flee as from a madman; if in another way you lead them to this, you make them mad. But let us believe that, on account of either opinion, human beings equally are unwilling to philosophize. If Zeno's definition forced philosophy to say something pernicious, my good man, was that to be said to a human being by which he should grieve, or that by which he should laugh at you?"

**3. 9. 21.** "Yet what Zeno defined, let us discuss, fools as we are. He says that that presentation can be grasped which so appears that the false cannot appear. It is manifest that nothing else comes into perception. 'This,' says Arcesilaus, 'I too see, and by this very thing I teach that nothing is perceived. For nothing of the kind can be found.' Perhaps by you and by other fools: but by the wise man, why can it not? Although even to a fool himself I think nothing can be answered, if he should say to you that, by that memorable keenness of yours, you should refute this very definition of Zeno, and show that it too can be false: which if you cannot do, you have this very thing as something you perceive; but if you refute it, you have nothing by which you are hindered from perceiving. I do not see that it can be refuted, and judge it altogether most true. And so, when I know it, fool though I am, I know something. But suppose it yields to your cunning. I will use a most secure complex: it is either true or false. If true, I hold it well; if false, something can be perceived, even if it has marks in common with the false. 'Whence,' he says, 'can it?' Most truly, then, did Zeno define, nor did whoever agreed with him even in this err. Or shall we think it a definition of small praise and sincerity which, against those who were going to say many things against perception, while it designated of what sort that was which could be perceived, showed itself to be such? And so there is both a definition and an example of comprehensible things. 'Whether,' he says, 'it itself too is true I do not know: but because it is probable, therefore, following it, I show that nothing is of the kind which it expressed could be grasped.' You show it perhaps besides it, and you see, I think, what follows. For if of even that we are uncertain, knowledge does not so desert us; for we know that it is either true or false: therefore we do not know nothing. Although it will never bring it about that I should be ungrateful; I altogether judge that definition most true. For either even false things can be perceived—which the Academics vehemently fear, and which is really absurd—or even those which are most like false things cannot: whence that definition is true. But now let us see the rest."

**3. 10. 22.** "Although these things, unless I am mistaken, may be enough for victory, yet perhaps not enough for satiety of victory. There are two things which are said by the Academics, against which, as far as we are able, we have resolved to come: that nothing can be perceived, and that assent must be given to nothing. About assenting presently; now we will say a few other things about perception. Do you say that nothing at all can be grasped? Here Carneades awoke: for none of those men slept less deeply than he, and he looked around at the evidence of things. And so, I believe, speaking with himself, as happens: 'Are you, then, Carneades,' he says, 'going to say that you do not know whether you are a human being or an ant? Or shall Chrysippus triumph over you? Let us say that we do not know those things which are inquired into among philosophers; the rest do not pertain to us, so that, if I have tottered in the daily and common light, I may appeal to those shadows of the unlearned, where only certain divine eyes see: who, even if they have beheld me trembling and falling, cannot betray me to the blind, especially the arrogant, and such as are ashamed to be taught anything.' Splendidly indeed, O Greek industry, girt and ready you go forth; but you do not regard that that definition was both invented by a philosopher, and fixed and founded in the vestibule of philosophy. If you try to cut it down, the axe will return upon your own legs: for, with it shaken, not only can something be perceived, but even that can be which is most like the false, if you do not dare to overthrow it. For it is your lurking-place, whence you violently burst and leap out upon the incautious wishing to cross over: some Hercules will strangle you, in your own cave, like the half-human Cacus, and crush you with its very masses, teaching that there is something in philosophy which, like a thing similar to the false, cannot be made uncertain by you. Certainly I was hastening to other things; whoever presses this point does you, Carneades, great insult—you whom he thinks can, by me, like one dead, be overcome anywhere or from anywhere. But if he does not think so, he is merciless, who forces me everywhere to desert my defenses and to contend with you in the field: into which, when I had begun to descend, frightened by your name alone, I drew back my foot, and from the higher ground hurled I know not what—whether it reached you, or what it did, let those see under whose examination we fight. But why do I fear, fool that I am? If I remember well, you are dead, and Alypius does not now rightly fight for your tomb: God will easily help me against your shade."

**3. 10. 23.** "You say nothing in philosophy can be perceived. And, to spread your speech far and wide, you seize on the brawls and disagreements of philosophers, and think they furnish you arms against those very men. For how shall we decide the quarrel between Democritus and the earlier natural philosophers about one world and innumerable ones, since between him and his heir Epicurus concord could not remain? For that luxurious fellow, when he permits the atoms—his little maidservants, as it were, that is, the little bodies which he gladly embraces in the dark—not to hold their own way, but to swerve here and there of their own accord into others' borders, has squandered his whole patrimony, even through quarrels. But this is nothing to me. For if it pertains to wisdom to know any of these things, it cannot lie hidden from the wise man. But if it is something else, the wise man knows that wisdom, and despises these things. Yet I, who am still far even from the neighborhood of the wise man, know something in these natural matters. For I hold it certain that either there is one world, or not one; and if not one, that the number is either finite or infinite. Let Carneades teach that this opinion is like the false. Likewise I know that this world of ours is so arranged either by the nature of bodies or by some providence; and that it either always was and will be, or began to be and will by no means cease; or has no origin in time, but will have an end; or both began to abide and will not abide forever: and innumerable natural things I know in this way. For these disjunctions are true, nor can anyone confound them by any likeness of the false. 'But assume something,' says the Academic. I will not: for this is to say, 'Leave what you know, say what you do not know.' But the statement hangs in suspense. Certainly it better hangs than falls: surely it is plain; surely it can now be called either false or true. This, then, I say I know. You, who both deny that these things pertain to philosophy and assert that nothing of them can be known, show that I do not know these things: say that these disjunctions are either false, or have something in common with the false, by which they cannot be distinguished at all."

**3. 11. 24.** "'Whence,' he says, 'do you know that this world exists, if the senses are deceived?' Your reasonings could never so refute the force of the senses as to convince us that nothing seems to us; nor have you ever at all dared to attempt that: but you have insisted vehemently on persuading us that a thing can be other than it seems. So I call this whole, whatever it is, that contains and nourishes us—this, I say, which appears to my eyes and is sensed by me to have earth and sky, or as-it-were earth and as-it-were sky—I call the world. If you say nothing seems to me, I shall never err. For he errs who rashly approves what seems to him. For you say that what is false can seem to those sensing; you do not say that nothing seems. For the whole cause of all disputation will surely be taken away, where you wish to reign, if not only do we know nothing, but also nothing seems to us. But if you deny that this which seems to me is a world, you make a controversy about a name, since I said that it is called by me 'world.'"

**3. 11. 25.** "'Even,' you will say, 'if you are asleep, is that a world which you see?' It has already been said that whatever appears to me of the kind I call the world. But if it pleases you to call 'world' only that which is seen by the waking, or even by the sane, contend, if you can, that those who are asleep and raving do not rave and sleep in the world. Therefore I say this: that this whole mass and machine of bodies in which we are—whether asleep, or raving, or waking, or sane—is either one or not one. Explain how that opinion can be false. For if I am asleep, it can be that I have said nothing; or, if even words have escaped a sleeper's mouth, as is wont to happen, it can be that I have not said it here, not sitting thus, not with these listening: but that this should be false, cannot be. Nor do I say I have perceived this while awake. For you can say that this could have seemed to me even asleep; and therefore this can be most like the false. But if there is one world and six worlds, that there are seven worlds, however I am affected, is manifest, and that I know it I affirm not shamelessly. Therefore show either this connection or those disjunctions above to be able, by sleep or madness or vanity of the senses, to be false; and if, awakened, I remember these things, I will grant that I am conquered. For I believe it is now clear enough what things seem false through sleep and madness—namely, those which pertain to the bodily senses: for that three times three is nine, and the square of intelligible numbers, must be true even if the human race be snoring. Although I see that much can be said even on behalf of the senses themselves, which we do not find reprehended by the Academics. For I believe the senses are not accused either because the raving suffer false imaginations, or because we see false things in dreams. For if they reported true things to the waking and sane, it is nothing to them what the mind of a sleeping or insane man feigns to itself."

**3. 11. 26.** "It remains to ask whether, when they themselves report, they report the truth. Come, if some Epicurean should say: 'I have nothing to complain of about the senses: for it is unjust to demand of them more than they can; but whatever the eyes can see, they see truly.' Then is what they see of the oar in the water true? Wholly true. For, a cause being added why it should so appear, if the oar plunged in the wave appeared straight, I would rather accuse my eyes of false reporting. For they would not see what, such causes existing, ought to be seen. What need of many words? This can be said of the movement of towers, of the little feathers of birds, of countless other things. 'Yet I am deceived if I assent,' says someone. Do not assent more than to persuade yourself that it so appears to you; and there is no deception. For I do not see how the Academic refutes him who says: 'This I know, that this seems white to me; this, that my hearing is delighted by this; this, that this smells pleasantly to me; this, that this tastes sweet to me; this, that this is cold to me.' Say rather whether the leaves of the wild olive, which the goat so persistently seeks, are bitter in themselves. O shameless man! is not the goat himself more modest? I do not know how they are to the flock; to me they are bitter: what more do you ask? But perhaps there is even some human being to whom they are not bitter. Do you press into trouble? Did I say they are bitter to all? I said to me, and this I do not always affirm. For what if at one time and another time, for one cause and another, now something sweet, now something bitter, be sensed in the mouth? This I say: that a human being can, when he tastes something, in good faith swear that he knows by his own palate that this is sweet to him, or the contrary, nor can he be led away from this knowledge by any Greek calumny. For who would be so shameless as to say to me, savoring something with delight, 'Perhaps you do not taste, but this is a dream'? Do I resist? But yet that would delight me even in dreams. Therefore that which I said I know, no likeness of false things confounds. And the Epicurean, or the Cyrenaics, may say many other things perhaps on behalf of the senses, against which I have learned nothing was said by the Academics. But what is that to me? If they wish these things, and if they can, even with me favoring, let them tear them down. For whatever is disputed by them against the senses does not avail against all philosophers. For there are those who confess that all those things which the mind receives by the bodily sense can beget opinion, but deny that they beget knowledge; which yet they wish to be contained by understanding, and, removed from the senses, to live in the mind. And perhaps among their number is that wise man whom we seek. But of this elsewhere. Now let us go on to the rest, which on account of these things now said we shall, unless I am mistaken, explain in few words."

**3. 12. 27.** "For what does the bodily sense either help or hinder one inquiring about morals? Unless perhaps those very men who placed the highest true good of a human being in pleasure—nothing hinders, neither the dove's neck, nor the uncertain voice, nor a weight heavy to a human being that is light to camels, nor six hundred other things—from saying that they know they are delighted by that by which they are delighted, or offended by that by which they are offended (which I do not see can be refuted); will they move him who embraces the end of good with his mind? Which of these do you choose? If you ask what seems to me, I think the highest good of a human being is in the mind. But now we are inquiring about knowledge. Therefore ask the wise man, who cannot be ignorant of wisdom: yet to me—slow and foolish as I am—it is meanwhile permitted to know that the end of human good, in which the happy life dwells, is either none, or in the mind, or in the body, or in both. Convince me, if you can, that I do not know this; which those most famous reasonings of yours by no means do. But if you cannot—for you will not find anything to which it is like the false—shall I hesitate to conclude that it rightly seems to me that the wise man knows whatever is true in philosophy, since I have come to know so many true things from that source?"

**3. 12. 28.** "But he fears, perhaps, lest he choose the highest good while asleep. There is no danger: when he has awakened, he will reject it if it displeases him, hold it if it pleases. For who will rightly blame him because he saw something false in dreams? Or will you perhaps dread this—lest the sleeper lose wisdom, if he approves the false as true? This now not even a sleeper dares to dream: that he call the waking man wise, and deny it if he sleeps. These things can be said of madness too: but the discourse hastens to other things. Yet these I do not leave without a most secure conclusion. For either wisdom is lost in madness, and then he will no longer be wise—whom you cry out to be ignorant of the truth: or his knowledge remains in the understanding, even if the rest of his mind imagines, as if in dreams, that which it has received from the senses."

**3. 13. 29.** "There remains dialectic, which the wise man certainly knows well, and no one can know the false. But if he does not know it, the knowledge of it does not pertain to his wisdom, without which he could be wise; and superfluously do we ask whether it is true or can be perceived. Here perhaps someone may say to me: 'You, fool, are wont to betray what you know: could you know nothing of dialectic?' I indeed know more than of any part of philosophy. For first, it taught me that all those propositions which I used above are true. Then, through it, I have come to know many other true things. Count them, if you can, how many they are. If there are four elements in the world, there are not five. If there is one sun, there are not two. One soul cannot both die and be immortal. A human being cannot be at once both happy and wretched. Here the sun does not at once shine and it be night. We are now either awake or asleep. That which I seem to myself to see is either a body or not a body. These, and many others which it would be very long to recall, I have learned through it to be true, in themselves true, however our senses are affected. It taught me that, if the antecedent part of any of those things which I just now set forth by connection is assumed, it necessarily draws after it that which is annexed. But those things which I stated by opposition or disjunction have this nature: that, when the rest are removed, whether one or more, there remains something that is confirmed by their removal. It taught me also that, when the matter for which words are spoken is agreed, one ought not to contend about words: and that whoever does this, if he does it from ignorance, must be taught; if from malice, deserted; if he cannot be taught, warned to do something else rather than consume time and labor on superfluous things; if he does not obey, neglected. About captious and deceptive little reasonings, the precept is brief: if they are inferred by ill conceding, one must return to the things conceded. If the true and the false conflict in one conclusion, what is understood must be accepted from it, what cannot be unraveled left alone. But if the measure (*modus*) of some matters lies wholly hidden from a human being, the knowledge of it must not be sought. These things indeed I have from dialectic, and many others which it is not necessary to recall. For I ought not to be ungrateful. But that wise man either neglects these, or—if perfected dialectic is itself the knowledge of truth—so knows it that he, despising and not pitying that most beggarly calumny of theirs, 'If it is true, it is false; if it is false, it is true,' starves it to death. Enough, I think, has been said about perception, for this reason, that when I shall have begun to speak about assenting, the whole cause will there again be turned over."

**3. 14. 30.** "Now then let us come to that part in which Alypius still seems to doubt. And first let us examine of what sort that is which moves you most keenly and most cautiously. For if this finding of yours, by which we are forced to confess that it is much more probable that the wise man knows wisdom, overthrows the Academics' opinion, strengthened (for so you said) by so many and so great reasons, by which it pleased them that the wise man knows nothing, then assent must rather be withheld. For by this very thing it is shown that nothing can be persuaded, however copious and subtle the arguments, to which, from the contrary side, if there be wit, there is not resisted no less keenly, or perhaps more keenly. From this it comes about that, when the Academic is conquered, he has conquered. O that he be conquered! He will never bring it about, by any Pelasgian art, that he depart from me at once both conquered and conqueror. Certainly let nothing else be found that can be said against these things, and of my own accord I profess myself conquered. For we are not dealing with the acquiring of glory, but with the finding of truth. It is enough for me somehow to surmount that mass which sets itself against those entering philosophy, and, hiding the darkness in I know not what receptacles, threatens that the whole of philosophy is such, and does not permit one to hope that any light will be found in it. But what more I should desire, I have nothing, if it is now probable that the wise man knows something. For there was no other cause why it seemed verisimilar that he ought to withhold assent, except that it was verisimilar that nothing could be grasped. This being taken away—for the wise man perceives even, as is now conceded, wisdom itself—no cause will now remain why the wise man should not assent even to wisdom itself. For it is without doubt more monstrous that the wise man should not approve wisdom than that the wise man should not know wisdom."

**3. 14. 31.** "For, I beg, let us set, as it were, before our eyes such a spectacle, if we can—a certain brawl of the wise man and wisdom. What else does wisdom say than that she is wisdom? But on the contrary that one says, 'I do not believe it.' Who says to wisdom, 'I do not believe you are wisdom'? Who, except the one with whom she could speak, and in whom she deigned to dwell—namely, the wise man? Go now, and seek me, who fight with the Academics: you now have a new contest, the wise man and wisdom fight with themselves. The wise man does not wish to consent to wisdom; I, with you, await secure. For who would not believe that wisdom is unconquered? Yet let us fortify ourselves with some complex. For either in this contest the Academic will conquer wisdom: and he will be conquered by me, because he will not be wise: or he will be overcome by her; and we shall teach that the wise man consents to wisdom. Either, then, the Academic is not wise, or the wise man will assent to something: unless perhaps one who was ashamed to say that the wise man does not know wisdom will not be ashamed to say that the wise man does not consent to wisdom. But if it is now verisimilar that even the perception of wisdom herself falls upon the wise man, and there is no cause why he should not assent to that which can be perceived, I see what I wanted to be verisimilar—namely, that the wise man will assent to wisdom. If you ask where he may find wisdom herself, I will answer, In himself. If you say he does not know what he has, you return to that absurdity, that the wise man does not know wisdom. If you deny that the wise man himself can be found, then no longer with the Academics, but with you, whoever you are who feel this, we shall discuss in another conversation. For when they dispute these things, they surely dispute about the wise man. Cicero cries out that he himself is a great opiner, but that he inquires about the wise man [^4]. And if this is still unknown to you, young men, surely you have read in the *Hortensius*: *If, then, there is nothing certain, and it is not the part of the wise man to opine, the wise man will never approve anything* [^5]. From which it is manifest that, in those disputations of theirs against which we strive, they inquire about the wise man."

**3. 14. 32.** "I think, then, that to the wise man wisdom is certain—that is, that the wise man has perceived wisdom; and on this account he does not opine, when he assents to wisdom: for he assents to that thing which, had he not perceived it, he would not be wise. Nor do those men affirm that anyone ought not to assent except to things which cannot be perceived. But wisdom is not nothing. Since, then, he both knows wisdom and assents to wisdom, he neither knows nothing nor assents to nothing. What more do you want? Or do we inquire something about that error which they say is utterly avoided, if assent turns the mind toward nothing? For he errs, they say, whoever approves not only a false thing, but even a doubtful one, however true it be: but I find nothing that is not doubtful. But the wise man finds wisdom herself, as we were saying."

**3. 15. 33.** "But from this now you perhaps wish me to depart. Most secure things are not easily to be left, when we deal with most cunning men: yet I will humor you. But what shall I say here? What? What indeed? That old thing, surely, must be said, where they too have what they can say. For what shall I do, whom you thrust forth from my camp? Shall I implore the aid of the learned, with whom, if I cannot conquer, I shall perhaps be less ashamed to be conquered? I will therefore hurl, with what force I can, a dart smoky indeed and rough, but, unless I am mistaken, most strong: whoever approves nothing does nothing [^6]. O rustic fellow! And where is the probable? Where is the verisimilar? This is what you wanted. Do you hear how the Greek-fashioned shields ring? It has been caught—that which is indeed most robust: but with what hand did we hurl it? And these clients of mine suggest to me nothing more powerful; nor have we, as I see, made any wound. I will turn myself to those things which the country house and the field supply: they burden me rather than prepare me."

**3. 15. 34.** "For when, at leisure, I had long thought in this countryside how the probable or verisimilar could defend our acts from error, at first it seemed to me, as it was wont to seem when I sold such things, prettily roofed and fortified. Then, when I looked round the whole more cautiously, I seemed to myself to have seen one entrance by which error might rush in upon the secure. For I think that not only he errs who follows a false way, but also he who does not follow the true one. Let us suppose two travelers going to one place, of whom one has resolved to believe no one, the other is too credulous. They have come to some fork in the road: here that credulous one says to a shepherd who was present, or to some countryman, 'Hail, good fellow; tell me, I pray, by which way one goes well to that place.' It is answered: 'If you go this way, you will not err at all.' And he to his companion: 'He speaks the truth; let us go this way.' The most cautious man laughs, and most wittily mocks the assent so quickly given, and meanwhile, as the other departs, stays fixed at the fork: and now it begins to seem disgraceful to delay, when behold, from another arm of the road a certain elegant and urbane man, sitting on a horse, comes into view and begins to draw near; this man rejoices. Then, to the one arriving and greeted, he shows his purpose, asks the way; he tells even the cause of his remaining, to make him more benevolent, preferring him to the shepherd. But that man was by chance a deceiver, of those whom the crowd now calls "samardoci." The wretched fellow kept his custom even for nothing. 'Go this way,' he says: 'for I come from there.' He deceived him, and went off. But when would this man be deceived? 'For I do not approve that direction as true,' he says, 'but because it is verisimilar. And to be idle here is neither honorable nor useful; let me go this way.' Meanwhile that one who erred by assenting, thinking so quickly that the shepherd's words were true, was already refreshing himself in the place to which they were going; but this man, not erring—since he follows the probable—wanders round I know not what woods, and does not yet find anyone to whom that place is known to which he had purposed to come. Truly I will tell you: when I thought of these things, I could not hold my laughter, that by the Academics' words it somehow comes about that he errs who holds the true way even by chance; but that he who has been led probably through pathless mountains, and has not found the region he sought, does not seem to err. So that, even granting that I rightly condemn rash consent, both err more easily than this man does not err. Henceforth, more vigilant against these words, I began to consider the very deeds and morals of human beings. Then indeed so many and so capital things against these men came into my mind that I no longer laughed, but was partly indignant, partly grieved that men most learned and most keen had rolled down into so great wickednesses and disgraces of opinions."

**3. 16. 35.** "For certainly—perhaps not everyone who errs sins; yet everyone who sins is granted either to err or to do something worse. What then, if some young man, when he has heard these men saying, 'It is base to err, and therefore we ought to consent to nothing; but yet, when anyone does what seems to him probable, he neither sins nor errs: let him only remember this, that whatever occurs either to the mind or to the senses is not to be approved as true'—hearing this, then, that young man will lie in wait for the chastity of another's wife. You, you I consult, Marcus Tullius: we are dealing with the morals and life of young men, for whose rearing and instruction all those writings of yours kept watch [^7]. What else are you going to say than that it is not probable to you that the young man should do this? But to him it is probable. For if we live by another's probable, then you too ought not to have administered the republic, because it seemed to Epicurus that it ought not to be done. That young man, then, will commit adultery with another's wife: and if he be caught, where will he find you to be defended by? Although, even if he should find you, what will you say? You will surely deny it. What if it is so plain that you deny in vain? You wish, no doubt, to persuade—as if in the Cumaean gymnasium, or rather the Neapolitan—that he has not sinned, nay, that he has not even erred. For he did not persuade himself, as true, that adultery ought to be done; the probable occurred, he followed it, he did it: or perhaps he did not do it, but only seemed to himself to have done it. But that husband, foolish man, throws everything into confusion with lawsuits, crying out for his wife's chastity—with whom by chance he is now sleeping, and does not know. If the judges understand this, they will either neglect the Academics, and punish the man as a most certain crime; or, obeying these same men, will verisimilarly and probably condemn the man—so that now this patron does not at all know what he is doing. For he will have no one to be angry at, since all say they have erred in nothing, since by not assenting they have made probable that which seemed so. He will, then, lay aside the role of patron, and take up that of philosopher-consoler: and so he will easily persuade the young man, who has now made so much progress in the Academy, to think himself condemned as if in a dream. But you think I am jesting: I am ready to swear by all that is divine that I do not at all know in what way this man has sinned, if whoever does what seems probable does not sin. Unless perhaps they say that to err is wholly one thing, to sin another; and that they gave those precepts that we might not err, but reckoned it no great thing to sin."

**3. 16. 36.** "I say nothing of murders, parricides, sacrileges, and absolutely all the disgraces and crimes that can be done or thought of, which are defended in few words, and—what is graver—before the wisest judges: 'I consented to nothing, and therefore I did not err. But how should I not do what seemed probable?' But those who think these things cannot be probably persuaded—let them read Catiline's speech, by which he persuaded the parricide of his fatherland, in which alone all crimes are contained. Now who does not laugh at this? They themselves say that in acting they follow nothing but the probable, and they greatly seek the truth, while it is probable to them that it cannot be found. O wondrous monster! But let us pass this by; it pertains less to us, less to the peril of our life, less to the danger of our fortunes. That is capital, that is to be dreaded, that is to be feared by every best man: that every wickedness, if this reasoning of the probable shall hold, when it shall have seemed probable to anyone that it ought to be done—provided he assent to nothing as if to a truth—he commits not only without blame of crime, but even without blame of error. What then? Did they not see these things? Nay, most skillfully and most prudently they saw them; nor would I in any way arrogate so much to myself as to follow Marcus Tullius in any part in industry, vigilance, wit, learning: to whom yet, asserting that a human being can know nothing, if this only were said—'I know that it so seems to me'—he would have nothing whence to refute it."

**3. 17. 37.** "Why, then, did it please men so great to deal, by perpetual and stubborn contentions, that the knowledge of the truth might seem to fall upon no one? Hear now a little more attentively—not what I know, but what I think: for I had reserved this to the last, that I might explain, if I could, of what sort the whole counsel of the Academics seems to me to be. Plato, the wisest and most learned man of his times, who both so spoke that whatever he said became great, and spoke such things that, however he said them, they did not become small, is said, after the death of his master Socrates, whom he had singularly loved, to have learned much also from the Pythagoreans. But Pythagoras, not content with Greek philosophy—which then was either almost nothing, or at least most hidden—after, moved by the disputations of a certain Pherecydes the Syrian, he believed the mind immortal, had heard many wise men, traveling far and wide. So Plato, adding to the charm and subtlety of Socrates, which he had in moral matters, the skill in natural and divine things, which he had diligently received from those I mentioned; and joining, as the shaper of those parts and their judge, dialectic, which is either wisdom itself, or that without which wisdom cannot at all be: is said to have composed a perfect discipline of philosophy, about which it is not now the time to discourse. For it is enough for what I want, that Plato felt there are two worlds: one intelligible, in which the truth itself dwells; but this sensible one, which it is manifest we sense by sight and touch. And so that the former is true, this verisimilar and made to the image of that one. And therefore that from the former, in the soul which knew itself, truth was, as it were, polished and as it were made serene; but from this, in the minds of the foolish, not knowledge but opinion could be begotten. Yet whatever was done in this world by those virtues which he called civil—like the other true virtues, which, except to a few wise men, were unknown—could not be named except verisimilar."

**3. 17. 38.** "These and other things of this kind seem to me to have been preserved among his successors, as far as they could, and guarded as mysteries. For either these things are not easily perceived except by those who, cleansing themselves from all vices, have vindicated themselves into a certain more-than-human manner of life; or he sins not lightly who, knowing them, has wished to teach any human beings whatever. And so I suspect that Zeno, prince of the Stoics, when, having heard and believed certain things, he had come to the school left by Plato, which Polemo then held, was held suspect, and did not seem such a man to whom those, as it were, sacrosanct decrees of Plato should easily be betrayed and committed, before he had unlearned those things which he had brought into that school, received from others. Polemo dies, Arcesilaus succeeds him—a fellow-pupil indeed of Zeno, but under the teaching of Polemo. Therefore, when Zeno was delighting in a certain opinion of his own about the world, and especially about the soul, for whose sake true philosophy keeps watch—saying that it is mortal, and that there is nothing beyond this sensible world, and that nothing is done in it except by body; for he thought God himself fire—most prudently and most usefully, it seems to me, Arcesilaus, when that evil was creeping widely, hid the opinion of the Academy utterly, and, like gold to be found sometime, buried it for posterity. Therefore, since the crowd is more prone to rush into false opinions, and, by the habit of bodies, all things are most easily, but harmfully, believed to be corporeal, that most keen and most humane man resolved rather to unteach those whom he found ill-taught than to teach those whom he did not judge teachable. Thence were born all those things which are attributed to the new Academy, of which the ancients had no need."

**3. 17. 39.** "But if Zeno had at some time been awakened, and had seen both that nothing can be grasped except what is such as he himself defined, and that nothing of the kind can be found in bodies, to which he attributed all things; long since indeed this kind of disputations, which blazed up by great necessity, would have been extinguished. But Zeno, deceived—as it seemed to the Academics themselves, and as it does not also not seem to me—by an image of constancy, was stubborn: and that pernicious trust in bodies somehow lived on into Chrysippus, who (for he most could) gave it great strength of spreading itself more widely; had not, on that side, Carneades, keener and more vigilant than all the rest before, so resisted that I wonder that opinion availed anything even afterward. For Carneades first laid aside that, as it were, shamelessness of caviling, by which he saw Arcesilaus not a little defamed, lest he should seem to wish to speak against all things, as if for the sake of ostentation: but he set himself to overthrow and destroy properly the Stoics themselves, and Chrysippus."

**3. 18. 40.** "Then, when he was pressed on all sides—that if he assented to nothing, the wise man would do nothing (O wondrous man! and yet not wondrous, for it flowed from the very fountains of Plato!)—he attended wisely what actions those men approved, and seeing them like to certain true ones, named that which in this world he might follow for acting 'verisimilar.' To what it was like he both skillfully knew and prudently concealed, and this he also called 'probable.' For whoever beholds its model approves an image well. How, then, does the wise man approve, or how does he follow what is like the true, when he is ignorant of what the truth itself is? Therefore those men knew, and approved false things in which they noticed a praiseworthy imitation of true things. But because it was neither lawful nor easy to show this to the profane, they left to posterity, and to those whom they could at that time, a certain sign of their opinion. But those who were good dialecticians they forbade, insulting and mocking, to raise a question about words. On these accounts Carneades is said to have been also the prince and author of the third Academy."

**3. 18. 41.** "Then this conflict lasted down to our Cicero—now plainly wounded, and about to inflate Latin letters with its last breath. For nothing seems to me more inflated than to say so many things so most copiously and most ornately, not feeling so. By which winds, however, that hay-stuffed Platonic, Antiochus, was, as it seems to me, sufficiently scattered and dispersed. For the flocks of the Epicureans set their sunny stalls in the minds of delight-loving peoples. For Antiochus, a hearer of Philo—a man, as I think, most circumspect, who had now, as it were, begun to open the gates to the retreating enemy, and to recall the Academy to the authority and laws of Plato (although Metrodorus too had before tried to do this, who is said first to have confessed that it had not pleased the Academics by decree that nothing could be grasped, but that they had necessarily taken up arms of this kind against the Stoics): Antiochus, then, as I had begun to say, having heard Philo the Academic and Mnesarchus the Stoic, had crept into the old Academy—as if empty of defenders, and as if secure with no enemy—like a helper and citizen, bringing in I know not what evil from the ashes of the Stoics, by which to violate the threshold of Plato. But against him, those arms being seized again, both Philo resisted until he died, and our Cicero suppressed all his remnants, impatient that, while he lived, anything he had loved should be shaken or contaminated: so that, after those times, no long interval after, all stubbornness and obstinacy being dead, that face of Plato, which in philosophy is most purified and most lucid, the clouds of error being dispersed, flashed forth, especially in Plotinus—a Platonic philosopher judged so like him that one would think they lived at the same time; but so great is the interval of time that he must be thought to have revived in this man."

**3. 19. 42.** "And so now we do not nearly see philosophers, except either Cynics, or Peripatetics, or Platonists: and Cynics indeed, because a certain freedom and license of life delights them. But as for what pertains to learning and doctrine, and to the morals by which the soul is cared for, because there have not been lacking most keen and most skillful men, who taught by their disputations that Aristotle and Plato so accord with each other that to the unskilled and less attentive they seem to disagree—through many ages indeed and many contentions, but yet there has been filtered out, as I think, one discipline of the truest philosophy. For it is not that philosophy of this world, which our sacred rites most deservedly detest, but of the other, intelligible world; to which souls, blinded by the manifold darkness of error and besmeared with the deepest filth from the body, that most subtle reasoning would never recall, had not the highest God, by a certain popular clemency, bent down and lowered the authority of the divine intellect even to the human body itself; by whose precepts—and not only precepts, but deeds too—souls, roused, could return into themselves and recover their fatherland, even without the wrangling of disputations."

**3. 20. 43.** "This about the Academics I have for the meantime probably, as I could, persuaded myself. If it is false, it is nothing to me, for whom it is now enough not to think that the truth cannot be found by a human being. But whoever thinks that the Academics felt thus, let him hear Cicero himself. For he says it was their custom to conceal their opinion, and not to be wont to lay it open to anyone except one who had lived with them up to old age [^8]. But what that opinion is, God will see; I think, however, that it was Plato's. But that you may briefly grasp my whole purpose: in whatever way human wisdom stands, I see that I have not yet perceived it. But since I am in my thirty-third year of age, I do not think I ought to despair that I shall someday attain it. For, all the other things which mortals think good being despised, I have resolved to give myself to seeking it out. And since the reasonings of the Academics were deterring me not a little from this business, I am, I think, sufficiently fortified against them by this discussion. But it is doubtful to none that we are impelled to learning by a twofold weight, of authority and of reason. To me, then, it is certain never at all to depart from the authority of Christ: for I find none more powerful. But as for what is to be pursued by most subtle reasoning—for I am now so disposed that I impatiently desire to apprehend what is true not by believing only, but also by understanding—I trust that for the meantime I shall find among the Platonists what does not conflict with our sacred rites."

**3. 20. 44.** Here, after they saw that I had made an end of speaking, although it was now night, and something had been written even after the lamp was brought in, yet those young men were most intently waiting to see whether Alypius would promise to answer on another day. Then he said, "I am prepared to affirm that nothing ever came out so to my mind as that I depart from today's discussion overcome. Nor do I think this joy ought to be only mine. I will therefore share it with you, my fellow contenders, or our judges. For perhaps the Academics themselves wished to be conquered in this way by their own posterity. For what could seem or be shown to us more delightful than this charm of speech, more weighed than this gravity of opinions, more ready than this benevolence, more skilled than this learning? I altogether cannot worthily marvel that things so harsh were treated so wittily, things so desperate so bravely, things so confuted so moderately, things so obscure so clearly. Therefore now, my companions, turn your expectation—by which you were provoking me to answer—with me, with surer hope, toward learning. We have a leader who will lead us into the very secret places of truth, God now showing the way."

**3. 20. 45.** Here I, when those young men, with a certain boyish eagerness, showed by their faces that they felt cheated, as it were, because Alypius did not seem about to answer, said, smiling, "Do you envy my praises? But since, now secure about Alypius's constancy, I fear nothing from him, so that you too may give me thanks, I equip you against him who has offended so great an intentness of your expectation. Read the Academics; and when you have found there Cicero the victor (for what is easier?) of these trifles, let him be compelled by you to defend this conversation of ours against those unconquered ones. This hard reward, Alypius, I render you for my false praise." Here, when they had smiled, we made an end of so great a conflict—whether a most firm one I do not know, yet more modestly and more quickly than I had hoped.
