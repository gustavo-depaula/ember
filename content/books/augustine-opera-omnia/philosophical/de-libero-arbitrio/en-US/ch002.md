# Book II

**2. 1. 1.** Evodius: Now, if it can be done, explain to me why God gave a human being free choice of the will; for surely, if he had not received it, he could not sin.

Augustine: Is it now certain and known to you that God gave a human being this thing which you think ought not to have been given?

Evodius: As far as I seemed to understand in the previous book, we both have free choice of the will, and it is by it alone that we sin.

Augustine: I too remember that this was already made clear to us. But just now I asked whether you know that God gave us this thing which we plainly have, and by which we sin.

Evodius: I think it was no other. For we are from him; and whether we sin or do right, we merit from him either punishment or reward.

Augustine: I want to know this too: whether you know this clearly, or whether, moved by authority, you gladly believe it even though it is unknown to you.

Evodius: I affirm that on this matter I first believed on authority. But what is truer than that every good is from God, and that everything just is good, and that punishment for sinners and reward for those who do right is just? From which it follows that God afflicts sinners with wretchedness and those who do right with blessedness.

**2. 1. 2.** Augustine: I do not resist; but I ask that other thing: how you know that we are from him. For just now you explained not this, but that we merit from him either punishment or reward.

Evodius: This too I see is plain from no other source than that it is already established that God avenges sins. For all justice is from him. For just as it does not belong to a certain goodness to bestow benefits on strangers, so it does not belong to justice to take vengeance on strangers. Hence it is plain that we belong to him, since he is not only most kind to us in bestowing, but also most just in avenging. Then, from what I posited and you granted—that every good is from God—it can also be understood that a human being is from God. For a human being himself, insofar as he is a human being, is some good; because he can, when he wills, live rightly.

**2. 1. 3.** Augustine: Plainly, if these things are so, the question you proposed is resolved. For if a human being is some good, and could not do right except when he willed, he must have had a free will, without which he could not do right. For we must not believe that God gave it for this purpose, that one might sin by it. There is reason enough, then, why it ought to have been given, since without it a human being cannot live rightly. And that it was given for this purpose can be understood even from this: that if anyone uses it for sinning, he is divinely punished for it. This would be done unjustly if the will had been given as free not only for living rightly but also for sinning. For how could he be justly punished who used the will for that very thing for which it was given? But now, when God punishes a sinner, what else does he seem to you to say but: "Why did you not use free will for the very thing for which I gave it to you—that is, for doing right?" Then again, that good, by which justice itself is commended in condemning sins and honoring right deeds—how could it exist if a human being lacked free choice of the will? For there would be neither sin nor right deed that was not done by the will. And on this account both punishment and reward would be unjust, if a human being did not have a free will. But there had to be justice in both punishment and reward, since this is one of the goods that are from God. God, therefore, had to give a human being a free will.

**2. 2. 4.** Evodius: Now I grant that God gave it. But does it not seem to you, I ask you—if it was given for doing right—that it ought not to have been able to be turned to sinning, just like that justice which was given to a human being for living well? For surely no one can live badly through his justice. So no one could sin through the will, if the will was given for doing right.

Augustine: God will indeed grant, I hope, that I may be able to answer you—or rather, that you yourself may answer, with Truth, the supreme teacher of all, teaching within. But for a little while I want you to tell me this: if you hold for certain and known the thing I asked you about, that God gave us a free will, ought we to say that it should not have been given, when we confess that God gave it? For if it is uncertain whether he gave it, we rightly ask whether it was given well, so that, when we have found it was given well, we may also find that he gave it, from whom all good things are given to souls; but if we find it was not given well, let us understand that he did not give it, whom it is wrong to blame. But if it is certain that he himself gave it, we must confess that, in whatever way it was given, it neither ought not to have been given, nor to have been given otherwise than it was. For he gave it whose work can in no way be rightly faulted.

**2. 2. 5.** Evodius: Although I hold these things by an unshaken faith, yet since I do not yet hold them by knowledge, let us inquire as though all were uncertain. For I see that, from this being uncertain—whether the free will was given for doing right, since by it we can also sin—it becomes uncertain too whether it ought to have been given. For if it is uncertain that it was given for doing right, it is uncertain too that it ought to have been given; and through this it will also be uncertain whether God gave it, because if it is uncertain that it ought to have been given, it is uncertain that it was given by him, whom it is wrong to believe gave anything that ought not to have been given.

Augustine: At least it is certain to you that God exists.

Evodius: Even this I hold unshaken, not by contemplating it, but by believing it.

Augustine: If, then, one of those foolish people, of whom it is written, *the fool said in his heart, There is no God* [^1], were to say this to you, and were unwilling to believe with you what you believe, but wanted to know whether you had believed truly—would you leave the man, or would you think that in some way what you hold unshaken should be made convincing to him, especially if he wished not to fight back stubbornly, but earnestly to come to know it?

Evodius: This last point you put sufficiently reminds me what I ought to answer him. For surely, however absurd he might be, he would grant me that one must not argue, about any matter at all, and least of all about so great a matter, with a deceitful and stubborn person. With that granted, he would first deal with me, so that I might believe he was asking this in good faith, and that there lay hidden in him, as far as this matter is concerned, no deceit or stubbornness. Then I would show—which I think very easy for anyone—how much fairer it was that, when on the hidden matters of his own soul, which he himself knew, he wished another, who did not know them, to believe him, he himself should also believe in the existence of God on the books of such great men, who left it attested in their writings that they had lived with the Son of God; for they wrote that they had seen things that in no way could happen if there were no God; and he would be exceedingly foolish if he reproached me for having believed those men, while wanting me to believe him. And now, since he could not rightly reproach this, he would in no way find why he should also be unwilling to imitate it.

Augustine: If, then, as to whether God exists, you think it enough that we judged we ought not rashly to believe such great men, why, I ask you, do you not likewise think that on those matters too which we have set out to inquire into as uncertain and quite unknown, we ought to believe the authority of those same men in such a way that we need labor no further over investigating them?

Evodius: But we desire to know and understand what we believe.

**2. 2. 6.** Augustine: You rightly remember what we cannot deny was set down by us at the very beginning of the previous discussion. For unless it were one thing to believe and another to understand, and unless we must first believe the great and divine thing we desire to understand, the prophet would have said in vain, *unless you believe, you will not understand* [^2]. Our Lord himself, too, both by words and by deeds, exhorted those whom he called to salvation first to believe. But afterward, when he spoke of the gift itself, which he was going to give to those who believe, he did not say, "But this is eternal life, that they believe"; rather he said, *This is eternal life, that they know you the true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent* [^3]. Then, to those who already believe, he says, *Seek and you will find* [^4]. For what is believed without being known cannot be called found; nor does anyone become fit for finding God unless he has first believed what he is afterward to know. Therefore, obeying the Lord's precepts, let us seek earnestly. For what we seek at his urging, we will find at his showing, as far as such things can be found in this life, and by people such as we are. For we must believe that these things are discerned and obtained more evidently and more perfectly by the better, even while they dwell on this earth, and certainly by all the good and pious after this life; and we ought so to hope for ourselves, and, despising things earthly and human, to desire and love these things in every way.

**2. 3. 7.** Let us investigate in this order, if you please: first, how it is evident that God exists; next, whether everything that is good, to whatever degree, comes from him; and finally, whether free will is to be numbered among good things. Once we know these things, it will, I think, be clear enough whether it was rightly given to the human being. So, to begin with what is most evident, I first ask whether you yourself exist. Or are you perhaps afraid that you may be mistaken in answering this question, although if you did not exist you could not possibly be mistaken?  
Evodius: Go on rather to the rest.

Augustine: Then, since it is plain that you exist, and it would not be plain to you otherwise unless you were alive, this too is plain, that you are alive. Do you understand that these two things are most true?

Evodius: I understand entirely.

Augustine: Then this third thing too is plain—namely, that you understand.

Evodius: It is plain.

Augustine: Which of these three seems to you to excel?

Evodius: Understanding.

Augustine: Why does this seem so to you?

Evodius: Because, while there are these three—to be, to live, to understand—a stone too exists, and a beast lives, yet I do not think a stone lives or a beast understands; but one who understands, it is most certain, both exists and lives. Therefore I do not doubt to judge that more excellent in which all three are present, than that in which even one is lacking. For what lives surely also exists, but it does not follow that it also understands; such, I think, is the life of a beast. But what exists does not surely follow that it also lives and understands; for I can admit that corpses exist, but no one would say they live. And what does not live, much less does it understand.

Augustine: We hold, then, that of these three, two are lacking to a corpse, one to a beast, and none to a human being.

Evodius: It is true.

Augustine: We hold also that the most excellent of these three is that which a human being has along with the other two—that is, to understand—which, where it is present, is followed by both being and living.

Evodius: We hold it indeed.

**2. 3. 8.** Augustine: Now tell me whether you know that you have those most common senses of the body—seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, and touching.

Evodius: I know it.

Augustine: What do you think belongs to the sense of sight? That is, what do you think we perceive by seeing?

Evodius: Whatever is corporeal.

Augustine: Do we also perceive hard and soft things by seeing?

Evodius: No.

Augustine: What, then, properly belongs to the eyes, which we perceive through them?

Evodius: Color.

Augustine: What to the ears?

Evodius: Sound.

Augustine: What to smell?

Evodius: Odor.

Augustine: What to taste?

Evodius: Flavor.

Augustine: What to touch?

Evodius: Soft or hard, smooth or rough, and many such things.

Augustine: What of this? The shapes of bodies—large, small, square, round, and any of this kind—do we not perceive them both by touching and by seeing, and so they can be assigned properly neither to sight nor to touch, but to both?

Evodius: I understand.

Augustine: Then you understand that some things the several senses have as their own, of which they give report, and some things certain of them have in common?

Evodius: I understand this too.

Augustine: Then, what belongs to each sense, and what either all of them or certain of them have in common among themselves—can we discern any of this by any one of those senses?

Evodius: By no means; rather, these things are discerned by some inner sense.

Augustine: Is it perhaps reason itself, which beasts lack? For, as I think, it is by reason that we grasp these things and know them to be so.

Evodius: I rather think that by reason we grasp that there is some inner sense, to which all things are referred from those five most familiar ones. For it is one thing by which a beast sees, and another by which it either avoids or seeks what it perceives by seeing. For that sense is in the eyes, but the other is within, in the soul itself; by which animals, not only the things that are seen, but also the things that are heard, and that are grasped by the other bodily senses, either seek and take up when delighted, or, offended, shun and reject. This latter can be called neither sight, nor hearing, nor smell, nor taste, nor touch, but something else, I know not what, which presides in common over all of them. And although we grasp this very thing by reason, as I said, I cannot for all that call it reason, since it is plain that it is present in beasts too.

**2. 3. 9.** Augustine: I recognize this thing, whatever it is, and I do not hesitate to call it the inner sense. But unless what is reported to us by the bodily senses passes beyond this too, it cannot reach knowledge. For whatever we know, we hold grasped by reason. We know, however—to be silent about the rest—that colors cannot be perceived by hearing, nor sounds by sight. And when we know this, we know it neither by the eyes, nor by the ears, nor by that inner sense which beasts too do not lack. For we must not believe that they know that light is not perceived by the ears, nor sound by the eyes; since we discern these things only by rational attention and thought.

Evodius: I cannot say I have grasped this. For what if, by that inner sense, which you grant they do not lack, they also discern this too—that colors cannot be perceived by hearing nor sounds by sight?

Augustine: Do you also think that they can distinguish from one another the color that is perceived, and the sense that is in the eye, and that inner sense in the soul, and the reason by which these things are severally defined and enumerated?

Evodius: By no means.

Augustine: What? Could this reason distinguish these four from one another and bound them with definitions, unless both color was referred to it through the sense of the eyes, and that sense in turn through that inner sense which presides over it, and that same inner sense through itself—provided that nothing else is interposed?

Evodius: I do not see how it could otherwise.

Augustine: What? Do you see this—that color is perceived by the sense of the eyes, but that same sense is not perceived by that same sense? For by the sense with which you see color, you do not also see the very seeing.

Evodius: Not at all.

Augustine: Try to distinguish these too. For I believe you do not deny that color is one thing, and to see color another, and likewise yet another thing, when color is not present, to have a sense by which it could be seen, if it were present.

Evodius: I discern these too, and grant that they differ among themselves.

Augustine: Of these three, do you see any with your eyes except color?

Evodius: Nothing else.

Augustine: Tell me, then, by what you see the other two; for you could not distinguish them if they were not seen.

Evodius: I do not know by what; I know that they exist, nothing more.

Augustine: Then you do not know whether it is reason itself, or that life which we call the inner sense, surpassing the senses of the body, or something else?

Evodius: I do not know.

Augustine: This, however, you know: that these things can be defined only by reason, and that reason does not do this except concerning the things that are offered to it for examination.

Evodius: It is certain.

Augustine: Whatever, then, that other thing is by which everything we know can be perceived, it is a servant of reason, to which it presents and reports whatever it touches, so that the things perceived may be discerned within their own bounds, and grasped not only by being perceived but also by being known.

Evodius: So it is.

Augustine: What? Does reason itself, which distinguishes its servants and the things they report from one another, and likewise recognizes what difference there is between these and itself, and affirms itself to be far more powerful than they—does it grasp itself by anything other than itself, that is, by reason? Or would you know that you have reason except by perceiving it through reason?

Evodius: That is most true.

Augustine: Since, then, when we perceive color, we do not likewise by that very sense perceive that we are perceiving; nor, when we hear a sound, do we also hear our own hearing; nor, when we smell a rose, does the smelling itself have any scent for us; nor, to those tasting, does the taste itself have any savor in the mouth; nor, when we touch something, can we touch the very sense of touching: it is plain that these five senses cannot be perceived by any sense of theirs, although by them all corporeal things are perceived.

Evodius: It is plain.

**2. 4. 10.** Augustine: I think this too is plain: that that inner sense perceives not only the things it has received from the five bodily senses, but that these senses are also perceived by it. For a beast would not move itself, either by seeking something or by fleeing, unless it perceived that it was perceiving—not for the sake of knowing, for that belongs to reason, but only for the sake of moving, which it surely does not do by any one of those five senses. If this is still obscure, it will become clear if you notice what, for the sake of example, is enough in some one sense, such as sight. For a beast could in no way open its eye and move it in looking toward what it desires to see, unless, with the eye closed or not so moved, it perceived that it did not see that thing. But if it perceives that it does not see while it does not see, then it must also perceive that it sees while it does see; for since it does not move its eye with the same appetite when seeing as when not seeing, it shows that it perceives both. But whether this life, which perceives that it perceives corporeal things, also perceives itself, is not so clear—except that everyone, questioning himself within, finds that every living thing flees death; and since death is contrary to life, it must be that life perceives even itself, since it flees its own contrary. But if this is not yet clear, let it be passed over, that we may strive toward what we will only by sure and plain proofs. For these things are plain: that corporeal things are perceived by a bodily sense; that the same sense cannot be perceived by this same sense; that by the inner sense both corporeal things are perceived through the bodily sense, and the bodily sense itself; and that by reason all those things, and that very reason itself, are made known and contained in knowledge. Or does it not seem so to you?

Evodius: It does seem so.

Augustine: Come now, answer: from what does the question arise, to whose solution we have long been laboring our way, desiring to come?

**2. 5. 11.** Evodius: As far as I remember, of those three questions which we posed a little while ago to weave the order of this discussion, the first is now in hand—that is, how it can be made plain, although it is to be believed most tenaciously and firmly, that God exists.

Augustine: You hold this rightly. But I also want you to hold carefully this: that when I asked you about yourself, whether you know that you exist, not only this, but two other things appeared to us that you know.

Evodius: I hold this too.

Augustine: Now, then, see to which of these three you understand that everything which the sense of the body touches belongs—that is, in what kind of thing it seems to you should be placed whatever our sense touches by the eyes or by any other bodily instrument: whether in that which only exists, or in that which also lives, or in that which also understands.

Evodius: In that which only exists.

Augustine: What? In which kind of these three do you judge the sense itself to be?

Evodius: In that which lives.

Augustine: Which, then, of these two do you judge better—the sense itself, or what the sense touches?

Evodius: The sense, of course.

Augustine: Why?

Evodius: Because that which also lives is better than that which only exists.

**2. 5. 12.** Augustine: What? That inner sense, which we tracked down above as below reason and still common to us with beasts—will you hesitate to set it above this sense by which we touch the body, and which you have already said is to be set above the body itself?

Evodius: I would in no way hesitate.

Augustine: I want to hear from you why you do not hesitate about this too. For you will not be able to say that this inner sense is to be placed in that one of those three kinds which also understands, but rather in that which both exists and lives, though it lacks understanding; for this sense is present in beasts too, in which understanding is not present. Since this is so, I ask why you set the inner sense above this sense by which corporeal things are perceived, when both are in that kind which lives. Now, the sense that touches bodies you set above bodies because they are in the kind that only exists, while it is in the kind that also lives; but since that inner sense too is found in this same kind, tell me why you think it the better. For if you say it is because the inner sense perceives this one, I do not think you will find a rule by which we can trust that everything that perceives is better than what is perceived by it—lest perhaps from this we be even compelled to say that everything that understands is better than what is understood by it. For this is false, since a human being understands wisdom and is not better than wisdom itself. So consider for what reason it seemed to you that the inner sense is to be preferred to this sense by which we perceive bodies.

Evodius: Because I recognize that it is a kind of governor and judge of the other. For if anything is lacking to that other in its office, this one demands it, as a debt, from its servant, as was argued a little while ago. For the sense of the eye does not see that it sees or does not see; and because it does not see this, it cannot judge what is lacking to it or what is enough; but that inner one does, by which the soul of a beast too is prompted to open the closed eye and to supply what it perceives to be lacking. And no one doubts that the one who judges is better than that about which it judges.

Augustine: Do you see, then, that this bodily sense too judges in a way about bodies? For to it belong pleasure and pain, when it is touched, whether gently or roughly, by a body. For just as that inner one judges what is lacking or enough to the sense of the eyes, so the sense of the eyes itself judges what is lacking or enough to colors. Likewise, just as that inner one judges about our hearing, whether it is too little or sufficiently attentive, so hearing itself judges about sounds, which of them flows in gently or rattles harshly. There is no need to follow through the other bodily senses; for now, as I think, you notice what I want to say—namely, that the inner sense judges about these bodily senses, when it both approves their soundness and demands its due, just as the bodily senses themselves judge about bodies, taking up in them the gentle touch and rejecting the contrary.

Evodius: I see indeed, and I agree that these things are most true.

**2. 6. 13.** Augustine: Attend now to whether reason also judges about this inner sense. For I am not now asking whether you doubt that reason is better than it, because I do not doubt that you judge so; although I think we need not even ask whether reason judges about this sense. For in the very things that are below it—that is, in bodies, and in the bodily senses, and in this inner sense—how is one better than another, and how far reason itself is more excellent than they, what in the end reports this, if not reason itself? And this it surely could in no way do, unless it itself judged about these things.

Evodius: It is plain.

Augustine: Since, then, the nature that only exists, and neither lives nor understands—such as a lifeless body—is preceded by the nature that not only exists but also lives, though it does not understand, such as the soul of beasts; and this in turn is preceded by the one that at once exists and lives and understands, such as the rational mind in a human being—do you think that in us, that is, in those things by which our nature is completed so that we are human beings, anything more excellent can be found than this, which we placed third among these three? For it is plain that we have a body, and a certain life by which the body itself is animated and quickened—both of which we recognize in beasts too—and a third thing, like the head or the eye of our soul, or whatever can be more fittingly said of reason and understanding, which the nature of beasts does not have. So see, I beg you, whether you can find anything in human nature loftier than reason.

Evodius: I see nothing at all better.

**2. 6. 14.** Augustine: What if we are able to find something that you not only do not doubt to exist, but is even more excellent than our reason itself? Will you hesitate to call that, whatever it is, God?

Evodius: I would not at once say that God is whatever I could find better than that which is best in my nature. For it does not please me to call God that to which my reason is inferior, but that to which none is superior.

Augustine: Quite so; for he himself gave to this reason of yours to think of him so piously and truly. But, I ask you, if you should find that there is nothing above our reason except what is eternal and unchangeable, will you hesitate to call this God? For you know that bodies are changeable; and it is plain that the very life by which the body is animated is not free of changeableness, through its various affections; and reason itself is plainly proved to be changeable, since now it strives to reach the truth and now does not strive, and sometimes reaches it and sometimes does not. If reason, with no bodily instrument employed—neither by touch, nor by taste, nor by smell, nor by the ears, nor by the eyes, nor by any sense inferior to itself, but through itself—discerns something eternal and unchangeable, then at the same time it must confess both that it is itself inferior, and that this is its God.

Evodius: I will plainly confess this to be God, than which it is established that nothing is higher.

Augustine: Well and good; for it will be enough for me to show that there is something of this kind, which you will either confess to be God, or, if there is something above it, you will grant that this very thing is God. Therefore, whether there is something above it or not, it will be plain that God exists, when I have shown, with his own help, the thing I promised—that there is something above reason.

Evodius: Then demonstrate what you promise.

**2. 7. 15.** Augustine: I will. But first I ask whether my bodily sense is the same as yours, or rather mine is only mine, and yours only yours. For if it were not so, I could not see anything with my eyes that you would not see.

Evodius: I grant entirely that, though they are of the same kind, we each have our own senses of seeing, or hearing, or any of the others. For one person can not only see, but also hear, what another does not hear, and perceive by any other sense something that another does not perceive. Hence it is plain that your sense is only yours, and mine only mine.

Augustine: Will you answer the same about that inner sense too, or something else?

Evodius: Nothing else, indeed. For mine surely perceives my sense, and yours perceives yours. For this is why I am often asked by someone who sees something, whether I too see it, because I perceive that I see or do not see, not he who asks.

Augustine: What? Does not each of us have his own reason? For it can happen that I understand something when you do not understand it; nor can you know whether I understand, while I myself know it.

Evodius: It is plain that each of us has his own rational mind too.

**2. 7. 16.** Augustine: Can you also say that we each have our own suns that we see, or moons, or morning stars, or other such things, although each sees them by his own and proper sense?

Evodius: I would in no way say that.

Augustine: We can, then, many of us at once see some one thing, although our senses are several, one to each of us, by all of which we perceive that one thing which we see together; so that, although my sense is one and yours another, it can nevertheless come about that what we see is not one thing for me and another for you, but that one thing is present to both of us and seen at once by both.

Evodius: It is most plain.

Augustine: We can also hear some one voice at once, so that, although my hearing is one and yours another, the voice we hear together is nevertheless not one for me and another for you, nor is one part of it caught by my hearing and another by yours; but whatever sounds is present, whole and one, to both of us at once, to be heard.

Evodius: This too is plain.

**2. 7. 17.** Augustine: Now you may notice, regarding the other bodily senses too, what we are saying: that, as far as this matter is concerned, they are not altogether as those two of the eyes and ears are, nor altogether not so. For since both you and I can fill our breath from one air, and perceive the quality of that air by smell; and likewise since we can both taste from one honey, or any other food or drink, and perceive its quality by flavor—although that thing is one, while our senses are several, yours yours and mine mine—so that, although we both perceive one odor or one flavor, you nevertheless do not perceive it by my sense, nor I by yours, nor by any one sense that could be common to both of us, but my sense is altogether mine, and yours yours, even though one odor or one flavor is perceived by both: hence, then, these senses are found to have something of the kind that those two of sight and hearing have. But in this they differ, as far as concerns what we are now doing: that although we both draw in one air by the nostrils, or take in one food by tasting, I nevertheless do not draw in the same part of the air that you do, nor take the same part of the food that you do, but I one and you another. And so, when I breathe, I draw from the whole air the part that is enough for me, and you likewise draw from the whole another part that is enough for you; and although one whole food is consumed by both, it cannot be consumed wholly both by me and by you—as a word is heard whole both by me and by you at once, and an appearance, as great as I see it, just so great you see at once; but a part of the food or drink must pass, one into me, another into you. Or do you understand these things too little?

Evodius: On the contrary, I agree that they are most clear and most certain.

**2. 7. 18.** Augustine: Do you think the sense of touch is to be compared with the senses of the eyes and ears in the matter we are now treating? For by it not only can we both perceive one body by touching, but you too can touch the same part that I have touched, so that not only the same body, but the same part of the body, we can both perceive by touching. For it does not happen in touching as with some food set before us, which I cannot take whole, and you whole, when we both feed on it; rather, both the one and the whole that I have touched, you too can touch, so that we both touch it—not in several parts, but each the whole.

Evodius: I confess that in this way the sense of touch is very like those two higher senses. But in this I see it is unlike: that at once, that is, at one time, we can both see and hear some one whole thing; whereas we can indeed both touch some whole thing at one time, but each in several parts, and the same part only at several times. For to no part that you grasp by touch can I apply my touch, unless you remove yours.

**2. 7. 19.** Augustine: You have answered most attentively. But you must also see this: of all the things we perceive, some are perceived by both of us, others by each of us singly; but our senses themselves we each perceive singly, so that I do not perceive your sense, nor you mine. Now of those things that are perceived by us through the bodily senses—that is, of corporeal things—what is it that we cannot perceive both of us, but only each singly, except that which is so made ours that we can turn it into ourselves and change it, as is food and drink, no part of which that I have taken in can you too take in? For although nurses give chewed food to infants, yet what taste has snatched from it and changed into the vitals of the chewer can in no way be recalled to be poured back into the infant's food. For the throat, when something tastes pleasant, claims for itself a small but irrecoverable part, and forces this to be done, as suits the nature of the body; otherwise, no taste would remain in the mouth after the chewed food was given back and spat out. The same can rightly be said of the parts of air that we draw in by the nostrils: for even if some of the air I breathe out, you too can draw in, you cannot for all that draw in what has passed from it into my nourishment, because it cannot be given back. For physicians teach that we take nourishment by the nostrils too; and this nourishment I alone can perceive by breathing, and cannot restore by breathing out, so that, drawn in by your nostrils too, it could be perceived by you. For the other perceptible things, which, though we perceive them, we do not by perceiving change and corrupt into our body, we can both perceive, either at one time or each in turn at several times, so that the whole, or the very part that I perceive, may be perceived by you too; such are light, or sound, or bodies that we touch but do not harm.

Evodius: I understand.

Augustine: It is plain, then, that the things we do not change, and yet perceive by the bodily senses, do not belong to the nature of our senses, and are therefore more common to us, because they are not turned and changed into our own and, as it were, private possession.

Evodius: I agree entirely.

Augustine: We must understand, then, as one's own and, as it were, private, that which each of us is to himself, and which he alone perceives in himself, which belongs properly to his own nature; and as common and, as it were, public, that which is perceived by all who perceive without any corruption or change of it.

Evodius: So it is.

**2. 8. 20.** Augustine: Come now, attend, and tell me whether anything is found that all who reason see in common, each by his own reason and mind, while that which is seen is present to all, and is not changed for the use of those to whom it is present, like food or drink, but remains uncorrupted and whole, whether they see it or do not see it. Or do you perhaps think there is nothing of this kind?

Evodius: On the contrary, I see there are many; one of which it is enough to mention—that the reason and truth of number is present to all who reason, so that every calculator tries to grasp it, each by his own reason and understanding; and one can do this more easily, another with more difficulty, another not at all; yet it offers itself equally to all who are able to grasp it; nor, when anyone perceives it, is it turned and changed, as it were, into the nourishment of its perceiver; nor, when anyone errs in it, does it itself fail, but, while it remains true and whole, that person is in error all the more, the less he sees it.

**2. 8. 21.** Augustine: Quite rightly; but I see that, as one not unversed in these matters, you have quickly found what to say. Yet if someone should tell you that these numbers are impressed on our mind not from some nature of their own, but from those things we touch by the bodily sense—as certain images of visible things—what would you answer? Or do you too think this?

Evodius: I would in no way think this. For even if I perceived numbers by the bodily sense, I could not on that account perceive by the bodily sense the reason of dividing or joining numbers. For by this light of the mind I refute anyone who, in adding or subtracting while he calculates, reports a false sum. And whatever I touch by the bodily sense, such as this sky and this earth, and whatever other bodies I perceive in them, I do not know how long they will last; but seven and three are ten, and not only now but always; nor in any way were seven and three ever not ten, nor will seven and three ever not be ten. This incorruptible truth of number, then, I said is common to me and to any who reason.

**2. 8. 22.** Augustine: I do not resist you, who answer most truly and most certainly. But you will easily see that numbers themselves too are not drawn in through the bodily senses, if you consider that every number is called by as many units as it has: for example, if it has one twice, it is called two; if three times, three; and if they have one ten times, then they are called ten; and any number whatever, however many times it has one, from this it has its name, and is called by that number. But whoever thinks most truly of one surely finds that it cannot be perceived by the bodily senses. For whatever is touched by such a sense is shown to be not one, but many; for it is a body, and therefore has innumerable parts. But, not to pursue the tiny and less distinct parts, however small that little body may be, it certainly has one part on the right, another on the left; one above, another below; or one farther, another nearer; or some at the ends, another in the middle: for these we must confess to be present in however small a measure of body; and therefore we grant that no body is truly and purely one—in which, nevertheless, so many parts could not be counted unless they were distinguished by the knowledge of that one. For when I seek one in a body, and do not doubt that I do not find it, I surely know what I am seeking there, and what I do not find there, and that it cannot be found, or rather that it is not there at all. So where I know that a body is not one, I know what one is; for if I did not know it there, I could not count many in a body. But wherever I know one, I do not know it through the bodily sense; because through the bodily sense I know only a body, which we prove is not truly and purely one. Moreover, if we did not perceive one by the bodily sense, we perceived no number by that sense, of those numbers at least which we discern by the understanding. For there is none of them that is not called by as many units as it has one, the perception of which is not made by the bodily sense. For of any little body, the half part, although the whole consists of two, has itself its own half. So there are those two parts in a body in such a way that they themselves are not simply two. But that number which is called two, since it has twice that which is simply one, its half part—that is, that very thing which is simply one—cannot again have a half part, or a third, or any fraction whatever, since it is simple and truly one.

**2. 8. 23.** Then, since, keeping the order of numbers, after one we see two, which number, compared to one, is found to be double, the double of two is not joined next in sequence, but, with three interposed, four follows, which is the double of two. And this reasoning runs through all the other numbers by a most certain and unchangeable law: that after one, that is, after the first of all numbers, excepting it, the first is the one that has its double; for two follows. After the second, that is, after two, excepting it, the second is the one that has its double; for after two the first is three, the second four, the double of the second. After the third, that is, three, excepting it, the third is the one that is its double; for after the third, that is, after three, the first is four, the second five, the third six, which is the double of the third. And so after the fourth, excepting it, the fourth has its double; for after the fourth, that is, four, the first is five, the second six, the third seven, the fourth eight, which is the double of the fourth. And so through all the rest you will find this which was found in the first pair of numbers, that is, in one and two: that whatever its place a number holds from the very beginning, just so far after it is its double. This, then, which we behold to be immovable, firm, and incorruptible through all numbers—whence do we behold it? For no one touches all numbers by any bodily sense, since they are innumerable: whence, then, do we know that this is so through all, or by what fancy or phantasm is so certain a truth of number beheld so confidently through innumerable cases, unless by that inner light which the bodily sense does not know?

**2. 8. 24.** By these and many such proofs, those to whom God has given talent for disputing, and on whom stubbornness draws no fog, are compelled to confess that the reason and truth of numbers both does not belong to the bodily senses, and stands unalterable and pure, and is common to all who reason, to be seen. Therefore, although many other things could occur that are present in common and, as it were, publicly to those who reason, and are seen by the mind and reason of each who discerns them, and remain inviolate and unchangeable—nevertheless I am not unwilling that this reason and truth of number occurred to you above all, when you wished to answer what I asked. For not without cause, in the holy books, is number joined to wisdom, where it is said, *I turned my heart about, to know and consider and seek out wisdom and number* [^5].

**2. 9. 25.** Yet tell me, please, what you think we ought to hold about wisdom itself. Do you suppose that each individual person has a wisdom of his own, or that one wisdom is available in common to all, and that each person is wiser the more he participates in it?  
Evodius: I do not yet know what you mean by wisdom; for I see that people have various views as to what is done or said wisely. Those who serve as soldiers seem to themselves to act wisely; those who, despising soldiering, spend care and labor on cultivating a field, rather praise this and attribute it to wisdom; those who are clever at devising ways of getting money seem to themselves to be wise; those who neglect or cast off all these things and all such temporal things, and bend their whole zeal to the search for truth, that they may come to know themselves and God, judge this to be the great gift of wisdom; those who are unwilling to give themselves to this leisure of seeking and contemplating the truth, but rather are busy with most laborious cares and duties, in order to take counsel for people, and are engaged in the just regulating and governing of human affairs, think themselves wise; and those who do both of these—who live partly in the contemplation of truth, partly in dutiful labors which they think owed to human society—seem to themselves to hold the palm of wisdom. I pass over the countless sects, of which there is none that does not, preferring its own followers to the rest, wish them alone to be wise. Therefore, since what we are now doing between us is that we must answer not what we believe but what we hold by clear understanding, I shall in no way be able to answer what you asked, unless what I hold by believing, I have also come to know by contemplating and discerning by reason—namely, what wisdom itself is.

**2. 9. 26.** Augustine: Do you think wisdom is anything other than the truth in which the highest good is discerned and held? For all those you mentioned, following different things, seek the good and flee evil; but they follow different things because to different people a different thing seems good. Whoever, then, seeks what ought not to be sought—even though he would not seek it unless it seemed good to him—nevertheless errs. But neither can he err who seeks nothing, nor he who seeks what he ought to seek. Insofar, therefore, as all people seek the blessed life, they do not err. But insofar as anyone does not hold the way of life that leads to blessedness, while confessing and professing that he wills nothing but to arrive at blessedness, so far he errs. For error is when we follow something that does not lead to that at which we wish to arrive. And the more one errs in the way of life, the less he is wise. For he is so much the farther from the truth, in which the highest good is discerned and held. But when the highest good has been pursued and attained, everyone becomes blessed; which, without controversy, we all will. As, then, it is established that we will to be blessed, so it is established that we will to be wise; for no one is blessed without wisdom. For no one is blessed except by the highest good, which is discerned and held in that truth which we call wisdom. As, therefore, before we are blessed, the notion of blessedness is nevertheless impressed on our minds—for by it we know, and confidently and without any doubt say, that we will to be blessed—so too, before we are wise, we have the notion of wisdom impressed on our mind, by which each of us, if asked whether he wills to be wise, answers without any fog of doubt that he does.

**2. 9. 27.** Therefore, if we now agree on what wisdom is, which perhaps you could not explain in words—for if you did not perceive it in your mind at all, you could not know that you wish to be wise and ought to wish it, which I do not think you will deny—I now want you to tell me whether you think wisdom too, like the rational principle and truth of number, presents itself in common to all who reason; or whether, because there are as many human minds as there are human beings, so that I perceive nothing of your mind and you nothing of mine, you think there are also as many wisdoms as there can be wise people.  
Evodius: If the highest good is one for all, then the truth too, in which it is discerned and held—that is, wisdom—must be one and common to all.

Augustine: Do you doubt that the highest good, whatever it is, is one for all people?

Evodius: I do doubt it indeed, because I see different people rejoicing in different things as in their highest goods.

Augustine: I would indeed wish that no one doubted about the highest good, just as no one doubts that, whatever it is, a person cannot become blessed except by attaining it. But since it is a great question, and perhaps demands a long discourse, let us suppose that there are altogether as many highest goods as there are the various things that are sought by various people as highest goods. Does it then follow that wisdom itself is also not one and common to all, because those goods which people discern and choose in it are many and various? For if you think this, you could also doubt that the light of the sun is one, because the things we discern in it are many and various; of which many things each chooses, according to his will, what he may enjoy through the sense of the eyes: one gladly gazes on the height of some mountain and delights in that sight; another on the flatness of a plain; another on the hollows of valleys; another on the greenness of woods; another on the moving evenness of the sea; another brings together all these, or several of them at once, for the gladness of seeing. As, then, these things that people see and choose to enjoy in the light of the sun are many and various, yet the light itself is one, in which the gaze of each beholder sees and holds what he may enjoy: so, even if there are many and various goods, from which each may choose what he wills, and, by seeing and holding it for enjoyment, rightly and truly establish it as his highest good, it can nevertheless be that the very light of wisdom, in which these things can be seen and held, is one and common to all the wise.

Evodius: I confess it can be so, and that nothing prevents one wisdom from being common to all, even if the highest goods are many and various. But I would like to know whether it is so. For what we grant can be so, we do not at once grant is so.

Augustine: Meanwhile we hold that wisdom exists; but whether it is one common to all, or each wise person has his own, as he has his own soul or mind, this we do not yet hold.

Evodius: So it is.

**2. 10. 28.** Augustine: What then? This which we hold—that wisdom or the wise exist, and that all people will to be blessed—where do we see it? For I would in no way doubt that you see this, and that it is true. Do you see this truth, then, as you see your own thought, which, if you do not declare it to me, I am wholly ignorant of? Or do you see it in such a way that you understand it can be seen by me too, even though it is not told me by you?

Evodius: On the contrary, in such a way that I do not doubt it can be seen by you too, even against my will.

Augustine: Then this one truth, which we both see, each with his own mind—is it not common to both of us?

Evodius: Most plainly.

Augustine: Likewise, I believe you do not deny that wisdom is to be pursued, and you grant this to be true.

Evodius: I do not doubt it at all.

Augustine: Can we deny that this truth likewise is one, and common to all who know it to be seen, although each beholds it not with my mind, nor yours, nor anyone else's, but with his own, since the thing beheld is present in common to all who behold it?

Evodius: By no means.

Augustine: Likewise, that one must live justly, that worse things must be made subject to better, that like must be compared with like, and that to each must be rendered what is his own—will you not confess that these are most true, and present in common to me and to you and to all who see them?

Evodius: I agree.

Augustine: Can you deny that the incorrupt is better than the corrupt, the eternal than the temporal, the inviolable than the violable?

Evodius: Who can?

Augustine: Then can anyone call this truth his own, when it is present, unchangeably, to be contemplated by all who are able to contemplate it?

Evodius: No one could truly say it was his own, since it is as much one and common to all as it is true.

Augustine: Likewise, that the soul must be turned away from corruption and turned toward incorruption—that is, that not corruption but incorruption must be loved—who denies it? Or who, when he confesses it to be true, does not also understand it to be unchangeable, and see that it is present in common to all minds that are able to behold it?

Evodius: That is most true.

Augustine: What? Will anyone doubt that the life which is moved by no adversities from its sure and honorable resolve is better than the one that is easily broken and overturned by temporal misfortunes?

Evodius: Who would doubt it?

**2. 10. 29.** Augustine: I will not now ask for more things of this kind; for it is enough that you see together with me, and grant to be most certain, that these, as it were, rules and certain lights of the virtues are both true and unchangeable, and, whether singly or all together, present in common to be contemplated by those who are able to behold them, each by his own reason and mind. But I do ask whether these things seem to you to pertain to wisdom. For I believe it seems to you that one who has attained wisdom is wise.

Evodius: It seems so entirely.

Augustine: What? Could one who lives justly so live, unless he saw which lower things he should subject to which higher, and which equal things he should join to one another, and which proper things he should distribute to each?

Evodius: He could not.

Augustine: One, then, who sees these things—will you deny that he sees wisely?

Evodius: I do not deny it.

Augustine: What? Does not one who lives prudently choose incorruption, and judge that it is to be preferred to corruption?

Evodius: Most plainly.

Augustine: When, then, he chooses that toward which he turns his soul—which no one doubts is to be chosen—can it be denied that he chooses wisely?

Evodius: I would in no way deny it.

Augustine: When, then, he turns his soul toward that which he wisely chooses, he surely turns it wisely.

Evodius: It is most certain.

Augustine: And one who is driven away by no terrors or penalties from that which he wisely chooses and toward which he wisely turns himself, doubtless acts wisely.

Evodius: Without any doubt.

Augustine: It is most plain, then, that all these things which we have called rules and lights of the virtues pertain to wisdom; since the more anyone uses them for the conduct of life, and conducts his life according to them, the more wisely he lives and acts; and everything that is done wisely cannot rightly be said to be separated from wisdom.

Evodius: It is altogether so.

Augustine: As, then, the rules of numbers are true and unchangeable, whose reason and truth you said is present unchangeably and in common to all who discern them, so the rules of wisdom are true and unchangeable—the rules about which, questioned just now one by one, you answered that they are true and evident, and you grant that they are present in common to be contemplated by all who are able to behold them.

**2. 11. 30.** Evodius: I cannot doubt it. But I would very much like to know whether these two—wisdom, that is, and number—are contained under some one kind, because you mentioned that they are placed together in the holy Scriptures; or whether one exists from the other, or one consists in the other, as number from wisdom, or in wisdom. For that wisdom exists from number, or consists in number, I would not dare to say; for I do not know how, since I have known many number-workers, or calculators, or by whatever other name they should be called, who reckon supremely and wonderfully, but very few wise people, or perhaps none. And so wisdom seems to me far more venerable than number.

Augustine: You speak of a thing that I too am wont to marvel at. For when I consider with myself the unchangeable truth of numbers, and its, as it were, lair and inner chamber, or some region, or whatever other fitting name can be found by which we may name, as it were, a certain dwelling and seat of numbers, I am borne far away from the body; and finding perhaps something I can think, yet not finding anything I am able to express in words, I return, as if wearied, to these things of ours, that I may be able to speak, and I say the things set before my eyes, as they are usually said. This happens to me also when I think of wisdom as attentively and intently as I am able. And therefore I greatly marvel—since these two are in a most hidden and most certain truth, with the testimony of the Scriptures also added, in which I mentioned that they are set together—I marvel exceedingly, as I said, why number is held cheap by the multitude of people, and wisdom dear. But no doubt it is because they are some one and the same thing; and yet, since nonetheless it is said in the divine books of wisdom that it *reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly* [^6], that power by which it reaches mightily from end to end is perhaps called number, while that by which it orders all things sweetly is properly called wisdom—though both belong to one and the same wisdom.

**2. 11. 31.** But because it gave numbers to all things, even to the lowest and those set at the bottom of things—for all bodies too, although they are the most extreme of things, have their own numbers—but it did not give to bodies, nor to all souls, but only to rational ones, the power to be wise, as though it had set up its seat in them, from which it might order all those even lowest things to which it gave numbers: therefore, since we easily judge about bodies, as about things ordered below us, on which we discern numbers impressed below us, we hold them on that account more cheaply. But when we begin, as it were, to run back upward, we find that those numbers transcend even our minds, and remain unchangeable in truth itself. And because few can be wise, while even to fools it is granted to count, people marvel at wisdom and despise numbers. But the learned and studious, the further removed they are from earthly stain, the more they behold both number and wisdom in truth itself, and hold both dear; and in comparison with that truth, not gold and silver and the other things over which people fight, but they themselves even grow cheap to themselves.

**2. 11. 32.** And do not marvel that numbers have therefore grown cheap to people, and wisdom is dear, because they can more easily count than be wise, when you see that they hold gold dearer than the light of a lamp, in comparison with which gold is laughable. But a far inferior thing is more honored, because even a beggar lights a lamp for himself, while few have gold—although far be it that wisdom should be found inferior in comparison with number, since it is the same; but it seeks an eye by which it can be discerned. But as in one fire there is felt, so to speak, a consubstantial brightness and heat, and they cannot be separated from each other, yet the heat reaches the things brought near, while the brightness is diffused even farther and wider: so the power of understanding that is in wisdom warms the things nearer it, such as rational souls; but the things that are more remote, such as bodies, it does not reach with the heat of being wise, but floods with the light of numbers. This is perhaps obscure to you. For no visible likeness can be fitted to an invisible thing in every respect of agreement. Only attend to this, which is enough both for the question we have taken up, and which shows itself even to humbler minds, such as ours: that although it cannot be clear to us whether number is in wisdom or from wisdom, or whether wisdom itself is from number, or in number, or whether both names can be shown to be of one thing—this at least is plain, that both are true, and unchangeably true.

**2. 12. 33.** Therefore you must in no way deny that there is an unchangeable truth containing all these things which are unchangeably true. You cannot call it yours or mine or any particular person’s, but it is present and offers itself in common to all who perceive unchangeably true things, like a light that in wondrous ways is both secret and public. But who would say that anything present in common to all who reason and understand belongs to the individual nature of any one of them? For you remember, I think, what we discussed a little earlier about the bodily senses: that the things we apprehend in common by sight or hearing, such as colors and sounds which you and I see or hear at the same time, do not belong to the nature of our eyes or ears, but are common objects of perception for us. Likewise, then, you must not say that the things you and I perceive in common, each with his own mind, belong to the nature of either of our minds. For what the eyes of two people see at once you cannot call the eyes of either one, but something third toward which the sight of both is directed.  
Evodius: It is most clear and most true.

**2. 12. 34.** Augustine: This truth, then, of which we have now long been speaking, and in which, one as it is, we behold so many things—do you think it is more excellent than our mind, or equal to our minds, or even inferior? But if it were inferior, we would judge not according to it, but about it, as we judge about bodies because they are below us, and we say of them, for the most part, not only that they are so or not so, but that they ought to be so or not so. So too about our souls: we know not only that the soul is so, but for the most part that it ought to be so. And about bodies we judge thus when we say, It is less white than it ought to be; or, less square, and many such things; and about souls, He is less apt than he ought to be; or, less gentle; or less vehement, as the rule of our character should bear. And we judge these things according to those inner rules of truth which we discern in common; but about those rules themselves no one in any way judges. For when someone says that eternal things are to be preferred to temporal, or that seven and three are ten, no one says it ought to have been so, but, knowing only that it is so, he does not, like an examiner, correct it, but, like a discoverer, only rejoices. But if this truth were equal to our minds, it too would be changeable. For our minds sometimes see it more, sometimes less, and by this they confess themselves changeable; whereas it, abiding in itself, neither gains when it is seen more by us, nor loses when less, but, whole and uncorrupted, gladdens with its light those who are turned toward it, and punishes with blindness those turned away. What of this, that we judge even about our own minds according to it, while about it we can in no way judge? For we say, He understands less than he ought; or, He understands as much as he ought. But a mind ought to understand the more, the nearer it can be brought and the more it can cling to unchangeable truth. Therefore, if it is neither inferior nor equal, it remains that it is superior and more excellent.

**2. 13. 35.** I had promised, if you remember, that I would show you something that is loftier than our mind and reason. Behold, here is truth itself: embrace it if you can, and enjoy it, and *delight in the Lord, and he will give you the petitions of your heart* [^7]. For what more do you ask than to be blessed? And what is more blessed than one who enjoys the unshaken, unchangeable, and most excellent truth? People cry out that they are blessed when they embrace beautiful bodies greatly desired, whether of spouses or even of harlots; and shall we doubt that we are blessed in the embrace of truth? People cry out that they are blessed when, with throats parched by heat, they come to an abundant and wholesome spring, or, hungering, find a dinner or supper richly and copiously set out; and shall we deny that we are blessed when we are watered and fed by truth? We are wont to hear the voices of those who cry that they are blessed if they lie among roses and other flowers, or even enjoy most fragrant ointments: what is more fragrant, what more pleasant, than the breathing of truth? And do we hesitate to call ourselves blessed when we are breathed upon by it? Many set their blessed life in the singing of voices and strings and pipes, and when these are lacking to them they judge themselves wretched; but when present, they are carried away with gladness: and shall we, when into our minds, without any noise, so to speak, a certain melodious and eloquent silence of truth glides, seek another blessed life, and not enjoy the one so certain and present? People, delighted by the brightness and pleasantness of the light of gold and silver, of gems and other colors, or of the very light that belongs to these eyes—whether in earthly fires, or in the stars or moon or sun—when they are called away from this gladness by no troubles, no want, seem to themselves blessed, and for the sake of these things wish to live forever: and shall we be afraid to set our blessed life in the light of truth?

**2. 13. 36.** On the contrary, since in truth the highest good is known and held, and that truth is wisdom, let us discern in it, and hold, the highest good, and fully enjoy it. For he is blessed who enjoys the highest good. For this truth shows all the goods that are true, which intelligent people, according to their own capacity, choose, whether singly or several, to enjoy. But as those who, in the light of the sun, choose what they gladly behold, and are gladdened by that sight—among whom, if there happen to be any endowed with more vigorous, sound, and very strong eyes, behold nothing more gladly than the sun itself, which also illumines the other things in which weaker eyes delight—so a strong and vigorous keenness of mind, when with sure reason it has beheld many true and unchangeable things, directs itself to truth itself, by which all things are shown, and, clinging to it, as it were forgets the rest, and in it enjoys all things at once. For whatever is pleasant in the other true things is pleasant by truth itself.

**2. 13. 37.** This is our freedom, when we are subject to this truth; and it is God himself, our God, who frees us from death, that is, from the condition of sin. For Truth itself, speaking as a man with men, says to those who believe in him: *If you remain in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free* [^8]. For the soul enjoys nothing with freedom unless it enjoys it with security.

**2. 14. 37.** But no one is secure in those goods that he can lose against his will. Now truth and wisdom no one loses against his will; for no one can be separated from it by places. Rather, what is called separation from truth and wisdom is a perverse will, by which lower things are loved. But no one wills anything unwillingly. We have, therefore, that which we may all enjoy equally and in common: there are no straits in it, no want. It receives all its lovers without any envy of one toward another, and is common to all, and chaste to each. No one says to another: Step back, that I too may approach; take away your hands, that I too may embrace. All cling to it; all touch the selfsame. Its food is in no part torn off; you drink nothing from it that I cannot drink. For you do not change anything from its common store into your own private possession; but what you take from it remains whole for me. What it breathes upon you, I do not wait to be given back by you, that I may be breathed upon from it: for no part of it ever becomes the property of any one or some persons, but the whole of it is at once common to all.

**2. 14. 38.** Less like this truth, then, are the things we touch, or taste, or smell, and more like it the things we hear and see; for every word is heard whole by all who hear it, and at once whole by each; and every appearance that lies before the eyes, as great as it is seen by one, just so great by another at once. But these are like it only by a very long interval. For no voice sounds whole all at once, since it is stretched out and drawn through times, and one part of it sounds first, another later; and every visible appearance, as it were, swells out through places, and is not whole everywhere. And certainly all these things are taken away against our will, and we are hindered by certain straits from being able to enjoy them. For even if anyone's sweet song could be everlasting, and its devotees came eagerly to hear it, they would crowd together and fight over places, the more numerous they were, that each might be nearer the singer; and in hearing they would hold nothing remaining with them, but would be touched by all the voices as they fled. But if I wished to gaze on this sun, and could do so persistently, it would desert me at its setting, and be veiled by cloud, and by many other obstacles I would against my will lose the pleasure of seeing it. Finally, even if the sweetness of light to the seer and of voice to the hearer were always present, what great thing would come to me, when it would be common to me with the beasts? But that beauty of truth and wisdom—provided only there be a persevering will to enjoy it—neither shuts out those who come by the crowding of a multitude of hearers, nor is run through by time, nor moves from place to place, nor is cut off by night, nor shut in by shadow, nor subject to the bodily senses. To all who are turned to it from the whole world, and who love it, it is nearest of all, everlasting to all; it is in no place, nowhere is it absent; outwardly it admonishes, inwardly it teaches; it changes all who behold it for the better, and is changed by none for the worse; no one judges about it, and no one judges well without it. And hence it is plain that it, by which alone our minds, individually, are made wise, and which are judges not about it but, through it, about other things, is beyond doubt more excellent than our minds.

**2. 15. 39.** But you had granted that, if I showed there was something above our minds, you would acknowledge it to be God, provided nothing else stood higher. Accepting that concession, I said that it was enough for me to demonstrate this. For if something more excellent exists, that is rather God; but if nothing does, truth itself is God. Therefore, whether that higher thing exists or not, you will nevertheless be unable to deny that God exists; and this was the question we undertook to discuss and examine. If you are troubled because we have accepted in faith, within Christ’s most sacred teaching, that there is a Father of Wisdom, remember that we have also accepted in faith that the Wisdom begotten by him is equal to the eternal Father. We must not investigate this now, but hold it with unshaken faith. For God exists, and exists truly and supremely. This we now not only hold without doubt by faith, as I think, but also touch through a certain, though still very slight, form of knowledge. This suffices for the question we undertook, so that we may explain the remaining matters relevant to it—unless you have something to object to these arguments.  
Evodius: But I, suffused with an altogether incredible joy, which I cannot explain to you in words, receive these things, and cry out that they are most certain. And I cry out with an inner voice, by which I desire to be heard by truth itself, and to cling to it; for I grant that it is not only a good, but the highest good, and the blessing-bestowing good.

**2. 15. 40.** Augustine: Quite rightly; I too rejoice greatly. But, I ask you, are we now wise and blessed? Or are we still tending toward it, that it may come to be ours?

Evodius: I think rather that we are tending toward it.

Augustine: Whence, then, do you grasp these things, which you cry out you rejoice in as true and certain, and which you grant pertain to wisdom? Or can any foolish person know wisdom?

Evodius: As long as he is foolish, he cannot.

Augustine: Then you are already wise, or you do not yet know wisdom.

Evodius: I am not indeed yet wise, but neither would I call myself foolish, insofar as I know wisdom; since the things I know are certain, and I cannot deny that they pertain to wisdom.

Augustine: Tell me, I ask you, will you not confess that one who is not just is unjust, and one who is not prudent imprudent, and one who is not temperate intemperate? Or can anything be doubted about these?

Evodius: I confess that a person, when he is not just, is unjust; and I would answer the same of the prudent and the temperate.

Augustine: Why, then, is he not foolish when he is not wise?

Evodius: This too I confess, that when anyone is not wise, he is foolish.

Augustine: Which, then, of these are you now?

Evodius: Whichever of these you call me, I do not yet dare to call myself wise; and from the things I have granted, I see it follows that I should not hesitate to call myself foolish.

Augustine: Then the foolish person knows wisdom. For, as has already been said, he would not be certain that he wills to be wise, and that he ought to be, unless the notion of wisdom clung to his mind—as did the notion of those things about which, questioned one by one, you answered, which pertain to wisdom itself, and in the knowledge of which you rejoiced.

Evodius: It is as you say.

**2. 16. 41.** Augustine: What else, then, do we do when we are zealous to be wise, but, with all the eagerness we can, gather our whole soul, as it were, to that which we touch with the mind, and set it there, and fix it steadfastly, so that it may no longer rejoice in its own private possession, which it has entangled with passing things, but, stripped of all affections for times and places, may lay hold of that which is one and the same always? For as the soul is the whole life of the body, so God is the blessed life of the soul. While we do this, until we accomplish it, we are on the way. And that it is granted us to rejoice in these true and certain goods, gleaming even now amid this dark journey, see whether this is what is written of wisdom—what she does with her lovers when they come to her and seek her; for it is said, *In their ways she will show herself to them cheerfully, and in all foresight she will meet them* [^9]. For wherever you turn, she speaks to you by certain traces that she has impressed on her works, and, when you slip back into outward things, she calls you back within by the very forms of outward things; so that whatever delights you in a body, and entices you through the bodily senses, you may see to be numerical, and may ask whence it is, and may return into yourself, and understand that you cannot approve or disapprove what you touch by the bodily senses unless you have within you certain laws of beauty, to which you refer whatever beautiful things you perceive outwardly.

**2. 16. 42.** Behold the sky and the earth and the sea, and whatever in them shines above, or creeps below, or flies or swims; they have forms because they have numbers: take these away from them, and they will be nothing. From whom, then, are they, if not from him from whom number is; since they have being only insofar as they have number? And indeed the makers of all corporeal forms have in their art the numbers by which they fit together their works; and they keep moving their hands and instruments in fashioning until that which is formed outwardly, referred to the light of numbers that is within, attains, as far as it can, its completion, and pleases, through the interpreting sense, the inner judge who looks upon the higher numbers. Ask next who moves the very limbs of the craftsman: it will be number, for they too are moved by number. And if you take away the work from the hands, and from the mind the intention of fashioning, and that motion of the limbs is directed to delight, it will be called dancing. Ask, then, what delights in dancing; number will answer you: Behold, it is I. Look now at the beauty of a formed body; numbers are held fast in place. Look at the beauty of mobility in a body; numbers are at work in time. Enter into the art whence these proceed; seek in it time and place; never will it be, nowhere will it be; yet number lives in it: its region is not of spaces, nor its age of days; and yet, when those who wish to become craftsmen apply themselves to learning the art, they move their body through places and times, and their mind through times; for by the passage of time they become more skilled. Transcend, then, even the mind of the craftsman, that you may see the everlasting number; then wisdom will shine for you from its very inner seat, and from the very sanctuary of truth. And if it still beats back your too feeble gaze, turn the eye of the mind to that way in which it showed itself cheerfully. Only remember that you have deferred a vision which, stronger and sounder, you may seek again.

**2. 16. 43.** Woe to those who forsake you as their guide, and wander among your traces, who love your beckonings instead of you, and forget what you signify, O wisdom, most sweet light of the cleansed mind! For you do not cease to beckon to us what and how great you are; and your beckonings are all the beauty of created things. For even the craftsman in a way beckons to the beholder of his work, from the very beauty of the work, not to stick fast there wholly, but so to run over with his eyes the appearance of the fashioned body that he may recur with affection to the one who fashioned it. But like those craftsmen are the people who love the things you make instead of you—who, when they hear someone eloquent and wise, while they too eagerly listen to the sweetness of his voice and the structures of aptly placed syllables, lose the sovereignty of the meanings, of which those words sounded as, so to speak, signs. Woe to those who turn away from your light, and cling sweetly to their own darkness! For, as it were setting their backs to you, they are fixed in carnal work as in their own shadow; and yet even there, what delights them they still have from the surrounding radiance of your light. But the shadow, while it is loved, makes the eye of the soul more feeble and weaker for enduring the sight of you. And so a person is more and more darkened, while he the more gladly follows whatever, being weaker, receives him more tolerantly. From this he begins to be unable to see what supremely is, and to think evil whatever deceives the unforeseeing, or entices the needy, or torments the captive—when he suffers these things as the desert of his own turning away, and nothing that is just can be evil.

**2. 16. 44.** If, then, whatever changeable thing you behold, you cannot grasp, whether by the bodily sense or by the consideration of the mind, unless it is held together by some form of numbers, which being taken away it falls back into nothing—do not doubt that, in order that those changeable things may not be cut off, but, by measured motions and a distinct variety of forms, may run through, as it were, certain verses of time, there is some eternal and unchangeable form; which is neither contained and, as it were, diffused through places, nor stretched out and varied through times, but through which all these things are able to be formed, and, after their own kind, to fill out and run through the numbers of places and times.

**2. 17. 45.** For every changeable thing must also be formable. But just as we call changeable that which can be changed, so I would call formable that which can be formed. But no thing can form itself; because no thing can give what it does not have, and surely it is in order to have form that anything is formed. Therefore, any thing whatever, if it has some form, has no need to receive what it has; but if it does not have any form, it cannot receive from itself what it does not have. No thing, then, as we said, can form itself. But what more shall we say of the changeableness of body and soul? Enough has been said above. It follows, then, that body and soul are formed by a certain unchangeable and ever-abiding form. To which form it is said, *You will change them, and they shall be changed; but you are the same, and your years shall not fail* [^10]. The prophetic utterance put "years without failing" for eternity. Of this same form it is said that, *abiding in itself, it makes all things new* [^11]. Hence, too, it is grasped that all things are governed by providence. For if all things that are will be nothing once the form is utterly withdrawn, then the unchangeable form itself, through which all changeable things subsist so that they are filled out and run through by the numbers of their forms, is their providence; for these things would not be if it were not. Therefore, whoever makes the journey toward wisdom, beholding and considering the whole creation, feels that wisdom shows herself to him cheerfully on the way, and meets him in all foresight; and the more eagerly he burns to complete this way, the more beautiful that way too is through her, to whom he is afire to come.

**2. 17. 46.** But if, besides that which exists and does not live, and that which exists and lives but does not understand, and that which exists and lives and understands, you find some other kind of creature, then dare to say that there is some good that is not from God. For these three can be expressed by two names as well, if they are called body and life: for that which only lives, and does not understand, such as belongs to beasts, and that which understands, such as belongs to human beings, are most rightly called life. But these two, that is, body and life, which are indeed reckoned among creatures—for the life of the Creator himself too is called life, and that is the highest life—these two creatures, then, body and life, since they are formable, as the things said above have taught, and, with their form utterly lost, fall back into nothing, sufficiently show that they subsist from that form which is always of the same kind. Therefore, however great goods they may be—whether great or least—they can only be from God. For what is greater among creatures than intelligent life, or what can be less than body? However much they fail and tend toward not being, yet some form remains to them, so that they exist in some way. But whatever form remains to any failing thing is from that form which does not know how to fail, and does not allow the very movements of failing or advancing things to exceed the laws of their numbers. Whatever, therefore, is found praiseworthy in the nature of things, whether judged worthy of little or much praise, is to be referred to the most excellent and ineffable praise of the Creator—unless you have something to say against this.

**2. 18. 47.** Evodius: I confess that I am sufficiently persuaded—both how it is made plain, as far as in this life and among such as we are it can be, that God exists, and that all goods are from God; since all things that are—whether those that understand and live and exist, or those that only live and exist, or those that only exist—are from God. Now let us look at the third question, whether it can be settled that the free will is to be numbered among goods. When this is shown, I shall without hesitation grant that God gave it to us, and that it had to be given.

Augustine: You have remembered well what was proposed, and you have noticed attentively that the second question is already explained. But you should also have seen that this third one is already resolved. For you had said that it seemed to you that free choice of the will ought not to have been given, because by it each person sins. When, against this opinion of yours, I had replied that it cannot rightly be done except by this same free choice of the will, and asserted that God gave it rather for this purpose, you answered that the free will ought to have been given to us as justice was given, which no one can use except rightly. This answer of yours compelled us to go through such long circuits of discussion, in which we proved to you that goods, both greater and lesser, are only from God. This could not be shown so clearly unless first, against the opinions of impious folly, by which *the fool says in his heart, There is no God* [^12], some reasoning, of whatever sort, entered into so great a matter according to our small measure—God himself helping us on so perilous a journey—were directed toward something plain. Yet these two things, that is, that God exists and that all goods are from him, although they were held even before by an unshaken faith, have nevertheless been so treated that this third too—that the free will is to be numbered among goods—appears most plainly.

**2. 18. 48.** For it was already made clear in the previous discussion, and established between us, that the nature of body is in a lower grade than the nature of the soul, and that on this account the soul is a greater good than the body. If, then, among the goods of the body we find some that a person can use not rightly, and yet do not on that account say that they ought not to have been given, since we confess them to be goods, what wonder is it if there are also in the soul certain goods that we may use not rightly, but which, because they are goods, could only have been given by him from whom all goods are? For you see how great a good is lacking to a body that lacks hands; and yet one who works either cruel or shameful things with his hands uses them badly. If you saw someone without feet, you would confess that a very great good was lacking to the wholeness of the body; and yet you would not deny that one who used his feet to harm someone, or to dishonor himself, used his feet badly. With our eyes we see this light, and distinguish the forms of bodies; and this is the most beautiful thing in our body, for which reason these members are set on a kind of pinnacle of dignity; and the use of the eyes serves to protect health, and brings many other conveniences of life: yet very many people do many shameful things with their eyes, and force them to serve lust. And you see how great a good is lacking in the face if the eyes are lacking; but when they are present, who gave them, if not God, the giver of all goods? As, then, you approve these things in the body, and, not looking at those who use them badly, praise him who gave these goods, so you ought to confess that the free will, without which no one can live rightly, is a good and divinely given, and that those who use this good badly are rather to be condemned than that the one who gave it ought not to have given it.

**2. 18. 49.** Evodius: Then I would first wish you to prove to me that free will is some good, and I would grant that God gave it to us, since I confess that all goods are from God.

Augustine: Did I not, then, prove it at last, with such effort in the previous discussion, when you confessed that every shape and form of a body subsists from the highest form of all things, that is, from truth, and granted that it is good? For Truth itself in the Gospel says that even the hairs of our head are numbered [^13]. Has what we said about the supremacy of number, and the power reaching from end to end, slipped from you? What perversity is this, then—to number our hairs, though they are among the tiny and altogether most abject things, yet among goods, and to find no author to whom they should be attributed except God the founder of all goods, since both the greatest and the least goods are from him from whom is every good; and yet to doubt about the free will, without which even those who live most wickedly grant that no one can live rightly? And now answer, I ask: which seems to you the better thing in us—that without which one can live rightly, or that without which one cannot live rightly?

Evodius: Spare me now, I beg you; I am ashamed of my blindness. For who would doubt that that is far more excellent without which there is no right life at all?

Augustine: Will you now deny that a one-eyed man can live rightly?

Evodius: Far be such monstrous madness.

Augustine: Since, then, you grant that the eye is some good in the body, the loss of which nevertheless does not hinder living rightly—will the free will seem to you no good, without which no one lives rightly?

**2. 18. 50.** For you behold justice, which no one uses badly. This is numbered among the highest goods that are in a human being himself, and so are all the virtues of the soul, by which the right and honorable life itself consists. For no one uses prudence, or fortitude, or temperance badly; for in all these too, as in that justice you mentioned, right reason flourishes, without which the virtues cannot exist. And right reason no one can use badly.

**2. 19. 50.** These, then, are great goods. But you must remember that not only great goods, but even the least, cannot be except from him from whom all goods are, that is, God. For this the previous discussion persuaded us of, to which you so often and so gladly assented. The virtues, then, by which one lives rightly, are great goods; the forms of any bodies whatever, without which one can live rightly, are the least goods; and the powers of the soul, without which one cannot live rightly, are intermediate goods. The virtues no one uses badly; but the other goods, that is, the intermediate and the least, anyone can use not only well but also badly. And therefore no one uses virtue badly, because the work of virtue is the good use of those things which we can also use not well. But no one, by using well, uses badly. Therefore the abundance and greatness of God's goodness has provided not only great goods, but also intermediate and least goods. His goodness is more to be praised in the great than in the intermediate, and more in the intermediate than in the least goods; but more in all than if he had not bestowed all.

**2. 19. 51.** Evodius: I agree. But this troubles me: since the question is about the free will, and we see that it itself uses the other goods well or not well, how is it that it too is to be numbered among those things which we use?

Augustine: In the same way that all the things we know unto knowledge, we know by reason, and yet reason itself too is numbered among those things which we know by reason. Or have you forgotten that, when we were inquiring what things are known by reason, you confessed that reason too is known by reason? Do not marvel, then, if we use the other things through the free will, that we can use the free will itself through it as well; so that, in a way, the will, which uses the other things, uses itself—just as reason, which knows the other things, knows itself. For memory too not only comprehends all the other things we remember, but also, because we do not forget that we have memory, memory in a way holds itself in us, which remembers not only other things but also itself; or rather, we remember both the other things and itself through itself.

**2. 19. 52.** The will, then, which is an intermediate good, when it clings to the unchangeable good—and that not its own private good, but one common, such as is the truth of which we have spoken much and said nothing worthy—a person holds the blessed life; and this very blessed life, that is, the disposition of a soul clinging to the unchangeable good, is the proper and first good of a human being. In it are also all the virtues, which no one can use badly. For although these are great and first in a human being, yet it is sufficiently understood that they are proper to each individual person, and not common. For by truth and wisdom, which is common to all, all become wise and blessed by clinging to it. But one person is not made blessed by the blessedness of another; for even when one imitates another in order to be blessed, he seeks to become blessed from the same source from which he sees the other was made so—namely, from that unchangeable and common truth. Nor is anyone made prudent by another's prudence, or brave by another's fortitude, or temperate by another's temperance, or just by another's justice; but by conforming his soul to those unchangeable rules and lights of the virtues which live incorruptibly in truth itself and in the common wisdom, to which the very person also conformed and fixed his soul whom, endowed with those virtues, he set before himself for imitation.

**2. 19. 53.** The will, then, clinging to the common and unchangeable good, obtains the first and great goods of a human being, while it is itself a certain intermediate good. But the will turned away from the unchangeable and common good, and turned toward its own private good, or toward what is outer, or toward what is lower, sins. It is turned toward its own private good when it wills to be its own master; toward what is outer, when it is zealous to know the private affairs of others, or whatever does not pertain to it; toward what is lower, when it loves the pleasure of the body. And so a person, made proud and curious and lascivious, is taken up by another life, which in comparison with the higher life is death; which, nevertheless, is governed by the administration of divine providence, which sets all things in their fitting seats, and distributes to each what is his own according to his merits. So it comes about that neither those goods that are sought by sinners are in any way evil, nor the free will itself, which we have found is to be numbered among certain intermediate goods; but the evil is the turning of it away from the unchangeable good, and its turning toward changeable goods—which turning away and turning toward, since it is not compelled but voluntary, is followed by a fitting and just penalty of wretchedness.

**2. 20. 54.** But perhaps you will ask: since the will moves when it turns away from the unchangeable good toward a changeable good, where does that movement arise? It is certainly evil, although free will must be numbered among good things, since without it one cannot live rightly either. For if that movement, that is, the will’s turning away from the Lord God, is undoubtedly sin, can we call God the author of sin? That movement, then, will not be from God. Where, therefore, will it come from? If I answer your question by saying that I do not know, you may be more disappointed; yet I will have answered truly. For what is nothing cannot be known. Only hold fast to unshaken piety, so that no good may occur to you in sensing, understanding, or thinking in any way which is not from God. In this way no nature occurs to you which is not from God. For wherever you see measure, number, and order in anything, do not hesitate to attribute it to God the maker. But where you have removed these entirely, absolutely nothing will remain. Even if some beginning of a form remains in which you find neither measure nor number nor order—for wherever these exist, there is a completed form—you must remove even that beginning of form, which seems to lie as material before the maker to be completed. For if the completion of form is good, the beginning of form is already some good. Thus, when every good is entirely removed, not something but absolutely nothing will remain. Every good, however, comes from God; therefore there is no nature which does not come from God. That movement of turning away, then, which we acknowledge to be sin, is a defective movement, and every defect is from nothing. See where it belongs, and do not doubt that it does not belong to God. Yet because that defect is voluntary, it lies within our power. If you fear it, you must be unwilling to have it; and if you are unwilling, it will not occur. What, then, is safer than to live a life in which what you do not wish cannot happen to you? But since a human being cannot rise voluntarily in the same way as he fell voluntarily, let us hold with firm faith the right hand of God stretched down to us from above, that is, our Lord Jesus Christ; let us await him with certain hope and long for him with ardent charity. If you think anything about the origin of sin still needs more careful inquiry—for I do not think there is any further need—nevertheless, if you think there is something, it must be deferred to another discussion.  
Evodius: I follow your wish indeed, that we should defer to another time whatever arises from here. For I will not grant you this—that you should think enough has now been inquired into from it.
