# Book I

**1. 1. 1.** Evodius: Tell me, I beg you, whether God is not the author of evil.

Augustine: I will tell you, if you first make plain which evil you are asking about. For we usually speak of evil in two ways: in one, when we say that someone has done evil; in the other, when someone has suffered something evil.

Evodius: I want to know about both.

Augustine: But if you know or believe that God is good—and it is not lawful to think otherwise—he does not do evil. Again, if we confess that God is just—and to deny this too is sacrilege—then just as he grants rewards to the good, so he assigns punishments to the wicked; and these punishments are indeed evils to those who suffer them. Therefore, if no one pays a penalty unjustly—which we must believe, since we believe that this universe is governed by divine providence—God is in no way the author of that first kind of evils, but he is the author of this second kind.

Evodius: Is there, then, some other author of that evil of which God has been shown not to be the author?

Augustine: There certainly is, for it could not come about with no author at all. But if you ask who that author is, it cannot be named, for it is not any single one; rather, each wicked person is the author of his own misdeed. If you doubt this, attend to what was said above: that misdeeds are punished by the justice of God. They would not be punished justly unless they were done by the will.

**1. 1. 2.** Evodius: I do not know whether anyone sins who has not learned to. And if that is true, I ask who it is from whom we have learned to sin.

Augustine: Do you consider learning to be something good?

Evodius: Who would dare to say that learning is evil?

Augustine: What if it is neither good nor evil?

Evodius: It seems good to me.

Augustine: Quite right, since knowledge is given or awakened through it, and no one learns anything except through learning. Or do you think otherwise?

Evodius: I judge that only good things are learned through learning.

Augustine: Then take care that evil things are not learned, for learning is so called only from a learning that is good.

Evodius: How, then, do people come to do evil things, if they are not learned?

Augustine: Perhaps by this: that a person turns himself away and estranges himself from learning, that is, from learning. But whether it is this or something else, one thing at least is clear: since learning is good, and learning takes its name from learning, evil things cannot be learned at all. For if they are learned, they are contained within learning, and so learning will not be a good thing—yet it is good, as you yourself grant. Therefore evil things are not learned, and you are searching in vain for the one from whom we learn to do evil; or if evil things are learned, they are learned so as to be avoided, not done. From which it follows that to do evil is nothing other than to turn aside from learning.

**1. 1. 3.** Evodius: I think there are really two kinds of learning: one through which we learn to do good, the other through which we learn to do evil. But when you asked whether learning was good, the love of the good itself seized my attention, so that I had in view the learning by which one does good, and on that account I answered that it was good. Now, however, I am reminded that there is another, which I confirm beyond doubt to be evil, and whose author I am seeking.

Augustine: At least you think that understanding is nothing if not good?

Evodius: I think it so plainly good that I cannot see what could be more excellent in a human being, and I would in no way say that any understanding could be evil.

Augustine: What then? When someone is taught, if he does not understand, can he seem to you to be learned?

Evodius: He certainly cannot.

Augustine: If, then, all understanding is good, and no one who does not understand learns, then everyone who learns does good; for everyone who learns understands, and everyone who understands does good. Whoever, therefore, seeks the author through whom we learn something is in fact seeking the author through whom we do good. So stop wanting to track down some evil teacher or other. For if he is evil, he is no teacher; and if he is a teacher, he is not evil.

**1. 2. 4.** Evodius: Come now: since you press me hard enough to confess that we do not learn to do evil, tell me whence it is that we do evil.

Augustine: You raise the very question that vexed me severely as a young man, and that, wearying me, drove and cast me down among the heretics. By that fall I was so afflicted, and so buried under such great heaps of empty fables, that had not the love of finding the truth obtained divine help for me, I could not have emerged from there and breathed again in that first freedom of inquiry. And since I was diligently brought to be freed from this question, I will proceed with you by the same path I followed in escaping. For God will be present, and will make us understand what we have believed. We are well aware in our own conscience that we hold to the step prescribed by the prophet, who says, *unless you believe, you will not understand* [^1]. Now we believe that all things that exist are from one God, and yet that God is not the author of sins. But the mind is troubled: if sins come from those souls that God created, and those souls are from God, how is it that sins are not, at no great remove, traced back to God?

**1. 2. 5.** Evodius: That is now plainly what you have said—the very thing that, when I think on it, torments me enough, and that has driven and dragged me into this inquiry.

Augustine: Be of a manly spirit, and believe what you believe; for nothing better is believed, even if the reason why it is so lies hidden. To think of God in the best way is the truest beginning of piety; and no one thinks of him in the best way who does not believe him to be almighty, and unchangeable in not so much as a single part; the creator also of all good things, to which he himself is superior; the most just ruler too of all that he created; and aided by no nature in creating, as though he were one who was not sufficient for himself. From this it follows that he created all things out of nothing; but out of himself he did not create, but rather begot, that which would be equal to himself, whom we call the only Son of God, and whom, when we try to declare him more plainly, we name the Power of God and the Wisdom of God, through whom he made all things, which were made out of nothing. With these things settled, let us, with God's help, strive toward an understanding of the matter you are inquiring into, in this way.

**1. 3. 6.** You are surely asking where our evil actions come from. We must therefore first examine what it is to do evil. Tell me what you think about this. If you cannot briefly encompass the whole matter in words at once, at least make your view known to me by mentioning particular evil deeds one by one.  
Evodius: Adulteries and murders and sacrileges—to pass over the rest, for which there is neither time nor memory enough to list them—who is there to whom these do not seem to be evil deeds?

Augustine: Then tell me first why you think adultery is an evil deed. Is it because the law forbids doing it?

Evodius: It is surely not evil because it is forbidden by law; rather, it is forbidden by law because it is evil.

Augustine: What if someone should harass us, exaggerating the pleasures of adultery and asking us why we judge this to be evil and worthy of condemnation? Do you think we should take refuge in the authority of the law, when dealing with people who now desire not merely to believe but to understand? For I too believe with you, and believe it unshakably, and proclaim that all peoples and nations must believe, that adultery is evil. But now we are laboring to know and hold most firmly, by understanding as well, the very thing we have received in faith. So consider as best you can, and tell me, by what reasoning you have come to know that adultery is evil.

Evodius: I know it is evil by this: that I would not be willing to suffer it myself in the case of my own wife. And whoever does to another what he does not want done to himself surely does evil.

Augustine: What if a man's lust were such that he offered his own wife to another, and willingly allowed her to be corrupted by one in whose wife he in turn desired to have an equal license? Does it seem to you that he does no evil?

Evodius: On the contrary, very great evil.

Augustine: Yet this man does not sin by that rule, for he does not do what he is unwilling to suffer. So you must look for something else by which to prove that adultery is evil.

**1. 3. 7.** Evodius: It seems to me evil because I have often seen people condemned for this crime.

Augustine: What of this: have not people often been condemned for doing what is right? Review the history—I need not send you to other books—that very history which excels by its divine authority; and you will at once find how badly we think of the apostles and of all the martyrs, if it pleases us to take condemnation as a sure sign of an evil deed, since all of them were judged worthy of condemnation for their confession. Therefore, if whatever is condemned is evil, then to believe in Christ and to confess that faith was evil at that time. But if not everything that is condemned is evil, look for some other ground on which to teach that adultery is evil.

Evodius: I cannot find what to answer you.

**1. 3. 8.** Augustine: Then perhaps the evil in adultery is lust. But while you look for the evil outside, in the deed itself, which can already be seen, you find yourself in difficulties. For to make you understand that the lust in adultery is the evil, consider this: even if a man should not have the opportunity to lie with another's wife, but it is in some way plain that he desires it, and that, were the chance given, he would do it, he is no less guilty than if he were caught in the act itself.

Evodius: Nothing is plainer than this, and I now see that there is no need for a long discourse to persuade me concerning murder and sacrilege, and indeed concerning all sins. For it is now clear that nothing other than lust holds sway in the whole class of evildoing.

**1. 4. 9.** Augustine: Do you also know that this lust is called by another name, "desire"?

Evodius: I know.

Augustine: What then? Do you think there is no difference between this and fear, or some difference?

Evodius: On the contrary, I judge that these two differ greatly from each other.

Augustine: I suppose you judge so because desire pursues, while fear flees.

Evodius: It is as you say.

Augustine: What, then, if someone kills a man not out of a desire to gain something, but in fear that some evil may befall him? Will he not be a murderer?

Evodius: He will indeed; but the deed does not on that account lack the dominion of desire. For one who kills a man out of fear surely desires to live without fear.

Augustine: And does it seem to you a small good to live without fear?

Evodius: It is a great good, but this can in no way come to that murderer through his crime.

Augustine: I am not asking what can come to him, but what he himself desires. For surely he desires a good who desires a life free of fear; and therefore this desire is not to be blamed—otherwise we would blame all lovers of the good. Hence we are compelled to admit that there is a murder in which the dominion of that evil desire cannot be found; and either it will be false that lust holds sway in all sins so as to make them evil, or there will be some murder that may not be a sin.

Evodius: If murder is the killing of a man, it can sometimes happen without sin; for a soldier who kills an enemy, a judge or his officer who kills a criminal, and one whose weapon slips by accident, against his will and without his knowing, do not seem to me to sin when they kill a man.

Augustine: I agree; but these are not usually called murderers. So answer me: do you think that the man who killed his master, from whom he feared grievous torments, should be counted among those who kill a man in such a way that they do not even deserve the name of murderers?

Evodius: I see that he differs greatly from them. For they act either in accordance with the laws or not against the laws, whereas no law approves this man's deed.

**1. 4. 10.** Augustine: Again you call me back to authority. But you should remember that we have now taken this up so that we may understand what we believe; and we believe the laws. So we must try, if in any way we can, to understand this very thing: whether the law that punishes this deed punishes it wrongly.

Evodius: It in no way punishes wrongly, since it punishes one who willingly and knowingly kills his master, which none of those others does.

Augustine: And what of this—do you recall that you said a little while ago that lust holds sway in every evil deed, and that by this very fact it is evil?

Evodius: I do recall it.

Augustine: What then? Did you not yourself grant this same point, that one who desires to live without fear does not have an evil desire?

Evodius: I recall this too.

Augustine: When, then, a master is slain by his slave out of this desire, he is not slain out of that blameworthy desire. And so we have not yet found out why this deed is evil. For we agreed between us that all evil deeds are evil for no other reason than that they are done out of lust, that is, out of a desire that is to be condemned.

Evodius: It now seems to me that the man is condemned unjustly—which I would not dare to say if I had anything else to say.

Augustine: Is that so? Did you persuade yourself that so great a crime ought to go unpunished, before you considered whether that slave wished to be free of the fear of his master in order to satisfy his lusts? For to desire to live without fear belongs not only to the good but to all the wicked as well. The difference, however, is this: the good seek it by turning their love away from those things that cannot be possessed without the danger of losing them; the wicked, on the other hand, strive to remove the obstacles, so that they may rest in the enjoyment of these things with security, and on that account they lead a life of crime and wickedness, which would better be called death.

Evodius: I come to my senses, and I rejoice greatly that I have so plainly come to know what that blameworthy desire is, which is called lust. It now appears to be the love of those things that anyone can lose against his will.

**1. 5. 11.** Come, then, let us now investigate, if you please, whether disordered desire also rules in acts of sacrilege, many of which we see committed through superstition.  
Augustine: Take care that this is not too hasty. For it seems to me that we must first examine whether an attacking enemy, or an ambushing assassin, can be killed—whether for the sake of life, or freedom, or chastity—without any lust.

Evodius: How can I think that those people lack lust who fight for the sake of things they can lose against their will? Or if they cannot lose them, what need is there to go so far as to kill a man for their sake?

Augustine: Then the law is not just that grants power to a traveler to kill a robber, lest he himself be killed by him; or to any man or woman to slay, if able, an assailant rushing in to rape them, before the rape is committed. For the soldier too is commanded by law to kill the enemy; and if he refrains from that slaughter, he pays the penalty at the commander's order. Shall we then dare to call these laws unjust, or rather no laws at all? For to me what is not just does not seem to be a law.

**1. 5. 12.** Evodius: I see well enough that the law is sufficiently fortified against an accusation of this kind, since, among the people it governs, it has given license for lesser evil deeds so that greater ones may not be committed. For it is far less harsh that one who lies in wait for another's life be killed than that he who defends his own life be killed. And it is far more monstrous for a man to suffer rape against his will than for the one who tries to force it to be slain by the one on whom he tries to force it. As for the soldier, in killing the enemy he is the agent of the law, and so he fulfilled his office without any lust. Furthermore, the law itself, which was enacted for the protection of the people, cannot be charged with any lust. For if the one who enacted it enacted it at God's command—that is, what eternal justice prescribes—he could have done it wholly free of lust. And if he established it with some lust, it does not follow that one must obey that law with lust, since a good law can be enacted even by one who is not good. For suppose someone who has seized tyrannical power should take a bribe from a person to whom it is profitable, so as to decree that no one may seize a woman even for marriage; the law will not be evil on that account, because an unjust and corrupt man enacted it. Therefore that law, which for the protection of citizens commands that hostile force be repelled with equal force, can be obeyed without lust; and the same can be said of all officers who by right and order are subject to the various authorities. But how those men can be guiltless under a law that does not blame them, I do not see. For the law does not compel them to kill, but leaves it in their power. They are therefore free to kill no one for the sake of things they can lose against their will, and for that reason ought not to love. As for life, it may perhaps be a matter of doubt to someone whether it is taken from the soul at all when this body is slain; but if it can be taken away, it is to be despised; if it cannot, there is nothing to fear. As for chastity, who would doubt that it is established in the soul itself, since it is a virtue? Hence it cannot be snatched away even by a violent rapist. Whatever, then, the man who is killed was going to snatch away is entirely not within our power; and so I do not understand how it is to be called ours. Therefore I do not reproach the law that permits such people to be killed; but how to defend those who do the killing, I cannot find.

**1. 5. 13.** Augustine: Much less can I find why you seek a defense for people whom no law holds guilty.

Evodius: No law, perhaps—of those laws that are visible and read by men. But I do not know whether they are not held by some more powerful and most hidden law, if there is nothing that divine providence does not administer. For how, in its sight, are those people free of sin who, for the sake of things that ought to be despised, are stained with human blood? It seems to me, then, both that this law, which is written for governing a people, rightly permits these things, and that divine providence avenges them. For this law takes upon itself to avenge only those things that are sufficient to secure peace among ignorant people, and so far as they can be governed by a man. But those other faults have other fitting penalties, from which it seems to me that wisdom alone can free us.

Augustine: I praise and approve this distinction of yours—unfinished and less than perfect, yet confident and reaching after certain lofty things. For it seems to you that this law, which is enacted for governing states, concedes much and leaves much unpunished that is nevertheless avenged by divine providence; and rightly so. For it is not the case that, because it does not do everything, the things it does are to be disapproved.

**1. 6. 14.** But let us carefully examine, if you please, how far evil deeds should be punished by that law which restrains peoples in this life; then what remains to be punished more inescapably and secretly by divine providence.  
Evodius: I desire to go on, if only we can reach the limits of so great a matter; for I think it is boundless.

Augustine: On the contrary, be of good heart, and enter upon the ways of reason, relying on piety. For nothing is so arduous and difficult that it does not become wholly plain and easy with God's help. So, depending on him and beseeching his aid, let us seek what we have set out to seek. And first answer me: does this law, which is promulgated in writing, give aid to people who are living this present life?

Evodius: It is plain that it does, for it is from such people that peoples and states are made up.

Augustine: What then? Are these people and peoples of the kind of thing that cannot perish or change, and is altogether eternal? Or are they rather changeable and subject to time?

Evodius: Who would doubt that this kind of thing is plainly changeable and subject to time?

Augustine: Then if a people is well ordered and serious, and a most diligent guardian of the common good, in which each person values his private affairs less than the public good, is not a law rightly enacted by which this very people is allowed to appoint magistrates for itself, through whom its affairs—that is, the public affairs—may be administered?

Evodius: Quite rightly.

Augustine: Furthermore, if that same people, gradually grown corrupt, should prefer private affairs to the public good, and put its vote up for sale, and, corrupted by those who love honors, should hand over its government to scoundrels and criminals—then if some good man should arise who had great power, would it not likewise be right for him to take away from this people the power of conferring honors, and reduce it to the choice of a few good men, or even of one?

Evodius: That too is right.

Augustine: Since, then, these two laws seem so contrary to each other—one granting the people the power of conferring honors, the other taking it away—and since this second was enacted in such a way that both can in no manner exist at once in one state, shall we say that one of them is unjust and ought never to have been enacted?

Evodius: By no means.

Augustine: Let us call this law, then, if you please, "temporal," which, although it is just, can nevertheless justly be changed with the times.

Evodius: Let us so call it.

**1. 6. 15.** Augustine: What of that law which is called the highest reason, which must always be obeyed, and through which the wicked merit a wretched life and the good a blessed one, and through which, finally, the law we said should be called temporal is rightly enacted and rightly changed—can it seem to anyone who understands to be anything but unchangeable and eternal? Or can it ever be unjust that the wicked be wretched and the good blessed, or that a temperate and serious people appoint its own magistrates, while a dissolute and worthless one lacks that license?

Evodius: I see that this law is eternal and unchangeable.

Augustine: At the same time I think you also see that in that temporal law there is nothing just and lawful that people have not derived for themselves from this eternal one. For if at one time that people justly conferred honors, and at another justly did not, this temporal variation, in order to be just, was drawn from that eternity by which it is always just that a serious people confer honors and a fickle one not confer them. Or does it seem otherwise to you?

Evodius: I agree.

Augustine: Then, to explain in words, as briefly and as well as I can, the notion of the eternal law that is impressed upon us: it is that by which it is just that all things be most perfectly ordered. If you think otherwise, say so.

Evodius: I have nothing to set against what you say, since it is true.

Augustine: Since, then, this is the one law from which all those temporal laws for governing people are varied, can it itself in any way be varied?

Evodius: I understand that it altogether cannot. For no force, no chance, no decay of things will ever bring it about that it is not just for all things to be most perfectly ordered.

**1. 7. 16.** Augustine: Come now, let us see how a human being is himself most perfectly ordered within himself; for from human beings bound together by one law a people is made up, and that law, as has been said, is temporal. And tell me whether it is most certain to you that you are alive.

Evodius: What could I answer more certainly than this?

Augustine: What then? Can you distinguish that it is one thing to be alive, and another to know that one is alive?

Evodius: I know indeed that no one knows that he is alive except a living being; but whether every living being knows that it is alive, I do not know.

Augustine: How I wish that, as you believe, so you also knew that beasts lack reason; our discussion would soon pass on from this question. But since you say you do not know, you set a long discourse going. For it is not a thing such that, passing it over, we may be allowed to proceed to what we aim at with the close-knit chain of reasoning that I sense is needed. So tell me: since we have often seen beasts tamed by men—that is, not only the body of a beast but also its soul so subjugated to a man that it serves his will by a kind of perception and habit—does it seem to you possible in any way that any beast, however savage in ferocity or in body, or however keen in any sense, should in turn try to subjugate a man to itself, though many of them are able to slay his body either by force or by stealth?

Evodius: I agree that this can in no way happen.

Augustine: Quite right. But likewise tell me: since it is plain that in bodily strength and the other functions of the body a man is easily surpassed by very many beasts, what is the thing in which a man so excels that no beast can rule over him, while he can rule over many? Is it perhaps that which is usually called reason or understanding?

Evodius: I can find nothing else, since it is in the soul that we have what makes us excel the beasts. If they were without souls, I would say we excel them because we have a soul. But as it is, since they too are animate beings, what is not present in their souls—namely, that they be subject to us—but is present in ours—namely, that we be better than they—since this is evidently neither nothing nor anything small, what could I more rightly call it than reason?

Augustine: See how easily, with God's help, what people think most difficult is done. For I confess to you, I had thought that this question, which, as I understand, is now settled, would hold us as long, perhaps, as all that has been said from the very beginning of our discussion. So accept now, that the reasoning may go on connectedly; for I believe you are not unaware that what we call knowing is nothing other than to have grasped something by reason.

Evodius: So it is.

Augustine: He, then, who knows that he is alive does not lack reason.

Evodius: That follows.

Augustine: But beasts are alive, and, as has already been shown, they are without reason.

Evodius: That is plain.

Augustine: Behold, then, you now know what you had answered that you did not know: that not everything that is alive knows that it is alive, although everything that knows that it is alive necessarily is alive.

**1. 7. 17.** Evodius: I no longer doubt it; go on to what you intended. For I have learned well enough that it is one thing to be alive, another to know that one is alive.

Augustine: Which, then, of these two seems to you the more excellent?

Evodius: What do you suppose, if not the knowledge of life?

Augustine: Does the knowledge of life seem to you better than life itself? Or do you perhaps understand that knowledge is a certain higher and purer life, since no one can know unless he understands? And what is it to understand, if not to live more brightly and more perfectly by the very light of the mind? So you have, unless I am mistaken, set before me not something other than life, but a life better than a certain life.

Evodius: You have understood and explained my meaning excellently well—provided that knowledge can never be evil.

Augustine: I think it can in no way be evil, unless by a transferred sense we call experience "knowledge"; for to experience is not always good, as to experience punishments. But that which is properly and purely called knowledge, since it is acquired by reason and understanding—how can that be evil?

Evodius: I grasp this distinction too; go on with the rest.

**1. 8. 18.** Augustine: This is what I want to say: this thing, whatever it is, by which a human being is set above the beasts—whether it is more rightly called mind, or spirit, or both (for we find both in the divine Scriptures)—if it rules and commands the other things of which a human being is composed, then a human being is most perfectly ordered. For we see that we have much in common not only with the beasts but even with shrubs and plants. To take food for the body, to grow, to reproduce, to flourish—we see these granted also to trees, which are bound within a certain lowest kind of life. And we discern and admit that beasts can see and hear, and perceive bodily things by smell, taste, and touch, and most of them more keenly than we do. Add strength and vigor and firmness of limbs, and swift and very easy movements of the body, in all of which we surpass some of them, are equal to others, and are even outdone by a few. Yet that very class of things is certainly common to us with the beasts. And indeed to seek bodily pleasures and to avoid hardships is the whole activity of the life of beasts. There are certain other things that no longer seem to fall to wild animals, yet are not the highest things in the human being either, such as joking and laughing—things human indeed, but which anyone who judges most rightly of human nature judges to be the lowest in a human being. Then there is the love of praise and glory, and the craving to dominate; and although these are not found in beasts, we are not to think ourselves better than beasts by reason of the lust for these things. For this appetite too, when it is not subject to reason, makes people wretched; and no one ever thought himself to be set above another by his wretchedness. When, therefore, reason dominates these movements of the soul, a human being is to be called ordered. For there is no right order, nor is it to be called order at all, where the better are made subject to the worse. Or does it not seem so to you?

Evodius: It is plain.

Augustine: When this reason, then, or mind, or spirit governs the irrational movements of the soul, that which dominates in a human being is the very thing to which dominion is owed by the law we have found to be eternal.

Evodius: I understand and follow.

**1. 9. 19.** Augustine: When, then, a human being is so constituted and ordered, does he not seem to you to be wise?

Evodius: I do not know who else could seem to me a wise man, if this one does not.

Augustine: I believe you also know this, that very many people are foolish.

Evodius: This too is sufficiently established.

Augustine: But if the fool is the contrary of the wise, then, since we have found out who is wise, you surely now understand who is foolish.

Evodius: To whom would it not be obvious that it is the one in whom the mind does not hold the highest power?

Augustine: What, then, must be said when a human being is in this condition—that he lacks a mind, or that, though it is present, it lacks dominion?

Evodius: This latter, rather, which you placed last.

Augustine: I would very much like to hear from you by what proofs you have grasped that there is a mind present in a human being which does not exercise its rule.

Evodius: If only you would take that part yourself, for it is not easy for me to sustain what you press upon me.

Augustine: At least it is easy for you to recall what we said a little while ago: how beasts that have been tamed and domesticated by people serve them; and how people would in turn be subject to them, as reason has shown, unless they excelled in something. We did not find that something in the body; and so, since it appeared to be in the soul, and we did not discover what to call it but reason, we afterward recalled that this is also called mind and spirit. But if reason is one thing and mind another, it is at any rate certain that nothing but the mind can use reason. From which it follows that one who has reason cannot lack a mind.

Evodius: I recall and hold these things well.

Augustine: What then? Do you believe that the tamers of wild animals can only be wise men? For I call those wise whom the truth bids to be called so—that is, those who, by the reign of the mind, are at peace through having subjugated all lust.

Evodius: It is laughable to think such people wise—those whom common usage calls animal-keepers, or even herdsmen, oxherds, or charioteers, all of whom we see have tamed cattle subject to them, and by whose industry the untamed are subdued.

Augustine: There, then, you have a most certain proof by which it becomes plain that a mind is present in a human being without dominion. For such people have it, since they do things that could not be done without a mind; yet it does not reign, for they are foolish, and it is well known that the reign of the mind belongs to none but the wise.

Evodius: It is remarkable that this had already been worked out by us in what went before, and yet I could not call to mind what to answer.

**1. 10. 20.** But let us connect this with other points. For we have now established both that the rule of the human mind is human wisdom and that it can also fail to rule.  
Augustine: Do you think that lust is more powerful than that mind to which we know dominion over the lusts has been granted by the eternal law? For my part, I think it in no way is. For it would not be most perfectly ordered for the less powerful to command the more powerful. So I judge it necessary that the mind be more powerful than desire, by the very fact that it rightly and justly dominates desire.

Evodius: I think so too.

Augustine: What then? Shall we doubt that we ought to set every virtue above every vice in such a way that the better and loftier the virtue, the firmer and more invincible it is?

Evodius: Who would doubt it?

Augustine: Then no vicious soul overcomes a soul armed with virtue.

Evodius: That is most true.

Augustine: I do not think you will deny that any soul whatever is better and more powerful than any body.

Evodius: No one denies it who sees—as is easy—that a living substance is to be preferred to one not living, or that which gives life to that which receives it.

Augustine: Then much less does a body, of whatever kind, overcome a soul endowed with virtue.

Evodius: It is most evident.

Augustine: What then? Can a just soul, a mind that keeps its own right and command, cast another mind that reigns with equal fairness and virtue down from its citadel and subjugate it to lust?

Evodius: By no means—not only on account of the equal excellence in both, but also because the mind that tried to make the other vicious would first fall away from justice and become vicious, and by that very fact would be the weaker.

**1. 10. 21.** Augustine: You understand well. So it remains for you to answer, if you can, whether anything seems to you more excellent than a rational and wise mind.

Evodius: Nothing, I think, except God.

Augustine: That is my view too. But since the matter is arduous, and it is not now opportune to inquire into it so as to come to an understanding—although it is held by a most robust faith—let our careful and cautious treatment of this question remain intact for us.

**1. 11. 21.** For the present, we can know that whatever that nature may be which can rightly surpass a mind strong in virtue, it cannot in any way be unjust. Therefore even that nature, although it has the power, will not compel the mind to serve disordered desire.  
Evodius: There is no one at all who would not confess this without any hesitation.

Augustine: It remains, then—since to a reigning mind possessed of virtue, whatever is equal or superior does not make it a slave of lust, on account of justice, and whatever is inferior cannot do this, on account of its weakness, as the points established between us show—that nothing else makes the mind a companion of desire than its own will and free choice.

Evodius: I see that nothing so necessary remains to be said.

**1. 11. 22.** Augustine: It follows, then, that it now seems to you just that the mind pay the penalty for so great a sin.

Evodius: I cannot deny it.

Augustine: What then? Is this very penalty to be thought a small one—that lust dominates the mind, and, having despoiled it of the riches of virtue, drags it this way and that, needy and destitute: now approving false things as true, now even defending them, now disapproving what it had earlier approved, and nonetheless rushing into other false things; now suspending its assent, and for the most part dreading clear reasonings; now despairing of finding the truth at all, and clinging fast to the darkness of folly; now striving toward the light of understanding, and again falling back through weariness? Meanwhile that reign of the desires rages tyrannically, and disturbs the whole soul and life of a human being with various and contrary storms—here with fear, there with longing; here with anxiety, there with empty and false gladness; here with the torment of a lost thing that was loved, there with the burning to obtain what was not possessed; here with the pains of an injury received, there with the fires of avenging it. Wherever it can, greed straitens, extravagance squanders, ambition enslaves, pride puffs up, envy torments, sloth buries, obstinacy provokes, subjection harasses, and whatever other countless things crowd and busy that reign of lust. Can we, in the end, count this no penalty—which, as you see, all who do not cling to wisdom must necessarily suffer?

**1. 11. 23.** Evodius: I judge this to be a great penalty indeed, and altogether just, if someone already set on the height of wisdom should choose to come down from there and be a slave to lust. But whether there could be anyone who has either wished to do this or wishes it, is uncertain. For although we believe that a human being was so perfectly fashioned by God, and established in the blessed life, that he himself slipped down from there by his own will into the hardships of mortal life—still, while I hold this by a most firm faith, I have not yet attained it by understanding. If you think a careful inquiry into this matter should now be deferred, you do so against my wish.

**1. 12. 24.** But as for what troubles me most—why we, who are certainly foolish and have never been wise, suffer such bitter punishments, so that we could be said to deserve them for abandoning the stronghold of virtue and choosing slavery under disordered desire—I could in no way allow you to put off explaining this by argument, if you can.  
Augustine: You say this as though you had it clearly established that we were never wise; for you are looking at the time since we were born into this life. But since wisdom is in the soul, whether before its partnership with this body the soul lived in some other life, and whether it ever lived wisely, is a great question, a great secret, to be considered in its own place. Yet the matter we now have in hand is not on that account hindered from being opened up as far as it can be.

**1. 12. 25.** For I ask you whether we have any will.  
Evodius: I do not know.

Augustine: Do you want to know this?

Evodius: I do not know that either.

Augustine: Then question me no further.

Evodius: Why?

Augustine: Because I ought not to answer you when you ask, unless you want to know what you ask. Furthermore, unless you want to attain wisdom, there can be no discussion with you about matters of this kind. And finally, you cannot be my friend unless you want it to be well with me. Now as for yourself, see whether you have no will for your own blessed life.

Evodius: I confess, it cannot be denied that we have a will. Go on now; let us see what you conclude from this.

Augustine: I will. But tell me first this too: do you sense that you have a good will as well?

Evodius: What is a good will?

Augustine: The will by which we seek to live rightly and honorably and to attain to the highest wisdom. Just see whether you do not seek a right and honorable life, or do not vehemently wish to be wise, or whether you dare to deny that, when we will these things, we have a good will.

Evodius: I deny none of these things, and therefore I now confess that I have not only a will, but also a good will.

Augustine: How highly, I ask you, do you value this will? Do you think that riches, or honors, or bodily pleasures, or all these together, are in any way to be compared to it?

Evodius: God avert that wicked madness!

Augustine: Then ought we to rejoice but little that we have something in the soul—I mean this very good will—in comparison with which the things we mentioned are utterly worthless, things for the obtaining of which we see a multitude of people refuse no labors and no dangers?

Evodius: We ought indeed to rejoice, and greatly.

Augustine: What then? Do you think that those who do not enjoy this joy are afflicted with only a small loss of so great a good?

Evodius: On the contrary, with a very great one.

**1. 12. 26.** Augustine: You see, then, as I think, that it is set within our will whether we enjoy or are without so great and so true a good. For what is so within the will as the will itself? Whoever has it good has, surely, something that is to be set far above all earthly kingdoms and all bodily pleasures. But whoever does not have it lacks that thing which his will alone, by itself, would give him—a thing more excellent than all the goods that are not within our power. And so, although he judges himself most wretched if he has lost a glorious reputation, vast wealth, and any bodily goods whatever, will you not judge him most wretched—even if he abounds in all such things—when he clings to things he can most easily lose, and does not have, though he wills it, the good will, which is not even to be compared with those things, and which, since it is so great a good, requires only willing in order to be had?

Evodius: That is most true.

Augustine: By right, then, and deservedly, foolish people—even if they were never wise (for this is doubtful and most hidden)—are afflicted with wretchedness of this kind.

Evodius: I agree.

**1. 13. 27.** Augustine: Consider now whether prudence seems to you to be the knowledge of things to be sought and things to be avoided.

Evodius: It does.

Augustine: What then? Is not fortitude that disposition of the soul by which we despise all hardships and the loss of things not set within our power?

Evodius: So I judge.

Augustine: Further, temperance is the disposition that restrains and checks the appetite from those things that are basely sought. Or do you think otherwise?

Evodius: No, I think as you say.

Augustine: And justice—what shall we say it is, if not the virtue by which to each is rendered what is his own?

Evodius: I have no other notion of justice.

Augustine: Whoever, then, having a good will—of whose excellence we have now long been speaking—embraces this one thing with love, than which meanwhile he has nothing better, delights in it, and finally enjoys it fully and rejoices in it, considering and judging how great it is, and how it can neither be torn nor stolen from him against his will: can we doubt that such a person is opposed to all the things that are enemies to this one good?

Evodius: It is altogether necessary that he be opposed to them.

Augustine: Shall we think that this person is endowed with no prudence, when he sees that this good is to be sought and that the things hostile to it are to be avoided?

Evodius: It seems to me in no way possible that anyone could do this without prudence.

Augustine: Rightly. But why do we not also grant him fortitude? For all those things that are not within our power he cannot love or value highly. For they are loved by an evil will, which he must resist as an enemy to his dearest good. But since he does not love these things, he does not grieve at their loss, and altogether despises them—which we have said and granted to be the work of fortitude.

Evodius: Let us grant it indeed; for I do not understand whom I could more truly call brave than the one who, with an even and tranquil soul, does without those things which it is not within us to obtain or to keep—which we have found that this person necessarily does.

Augustine: See now whether we can keep temperance from him, since it is the virtue that checks the lusts. And what is so hostile to a good will as lust? From which you surely understand that this lover of his own good will resists in every way, and opposes the lusts, and is therefore rightly called temperate.

Evodius: Go on; I agree.

Augustine: Justice remains; and I really do not see how it could be lacking to this man. For one who has and loves a good will, and resists, as has been said, the things hostile to it, can wish ill to no one. It will follow, then, that he does injury to no one—which no one can do except one who renders to each what is his own. When I said that this pertains to justice, you approved it, as I think you remember.

Evodius: I do indeed remember, and I confess that in this man, who values and loves his own good will highly, all four virtues, which were described by you a little while ago with my agreement, are found.

**1. 13. 28.** Augustine: What, then, prevents us from granting that this man's life is praiseworthy?

Evodius: Nothing at all; rather, all things urge, and even compel us to it.

Augustine: What then? Can you in any way not judge that a wretched life is to be fled?

Evodius: I judge it greatly to be fled, and think that nothing else should be done.

Augustine: But a praiseworthy life, surely, you do not think is to be fled.

Evodius: On the contrary, I judge it is to be diligently sought.

Augustine: Then a praiseworthy life is not a wretched one.

Evodius: That certainly follows.

Augustine: Then nothing, I think, now remains difficult for you to agree to—namely, that the life which is not wretched is the blessed life.

Evodius: It is most plain.

Augustine: It is agreed, then, that the man who is a lover of his own good will, and despises in comparison with it whatever else is called good—the loss of which can happen even while the will to keep it remains—is blessed.

Evodius: Why should it not be agreed, since the things we conceded above necessarily lead to it?

Augustine: You understand well. But tell me, I ask: is not this very thing—to love one's good will and to value it as highly as has been said—itself a good will?

Evodius: You speak the truth.

Augustine: And if we rightly judge this man blessed, do we not rightly judge wretched the one whose will is the contrary?

Evodius: Most rightly.

Augustine: What reason, then, is there why we should think it must be doubted that—even if we were never before wise—we nevertheless merit and lead, by our will, a praiseworthy and blessed life, and by our will a base and wretched one?

Evodius: I confess that we have arrived here by sure and undeniable steps.

**1. 13. 29.** Augustine: Consider also this: for I believe you hold in memory what we said a good will is. I think it was said to be the will by which we seek to live rightly and honorably.

Evodius: I remember it so.

Augustine: If, then, we love and embrace this will with a likewise good will, and prefer it to all the things we cannot keep merely because we will to, those virtues, as reason has taught, will dwell in our soul; and to have them is the very thing that it is to live rightly and honorably. From which it follows that whoever wills to live rightly and honorably, if he wills this willing of his above the fleeting goods, attains so great a thing with such ease that to have what he willed is nothing other than the very willing of it.

Evodius: Truly I tell you, I can scarcely keep from crying out for joy at so great a good, so suddenly arising for me and so easily set within reach.

Augustine: And yet this very joy, which is begotten by the attaining of this good, when it lifts the soul calmly and quietly and steadily, is called the blessed life—unless you think that to live blessedly is anything other than to rejoice in true and certain goods.

Evodius: So I think.

**1. 14. 30.** Augustine: Rightly. But do you suppose that any human being does not in every way will and wish for the blessed life?

Evodius: Who doubts that every human being wills it?

Augustine: Why, then, do not all attain it? For we had said, and agreed between us, that people merit it by their will, and the wretched life too by their will, and merit them so as to receive them. But now there arises some sort of contradiction, and unless we look carefully, it strives to throw into confusion the earlier reasoning, so wide-awake and firm. For how does anyone suffer a wretched life by his will, when no one at all wills to live wretchedly? Or how does a human being attain the blessed life by his will, when so many are wretched, and all will to be blessed? Does it come about because it is one thing to will well or ill, and another to merit something through a good or evil will? For those who are blessed, who must also be good, are not blessed because they willed to live blessedly—for the wicked also will this—but because they willed to live rightly, which the wicked do not will. Therefore it is no wonder that wretched people do not attain what they will, that is, the blessed life. For they do not likewise will that which is its companion, and without which no one is worthy of it and no one attains it—namely, to live rightly. For that eternal law, to whose consideration it is now time to return, has fixed this with unchangeable stability: that merit lies in the will, but reward and punishment in blessedness and wretchedness. And so, when we say that people are wretched by their will, we do not say it because they will to be wretched, but because they are in a will such that wretchedness must follow it, even against their will. Therefore it does not contradict the earlier reasoning that all will to be blessed and cannot be; for not all will to live rightly, to which one will alone the blessed life is owed—unless you have something to say against this.

Evodius: I have nothing.

**1. 15. 31.** But let us now see how these things relate to that question we proposed concerning the two laws.  
Augustine: So be it. But tell me first whether one who loves to live rightly, and so delights in it that to him it is not only right but also sweet and pleasant, loves this law, and holds it most dear—the law by which he sees that the blessed life is granted to a good will, and a wretched life to an evil one.

Evodius: He loves it altogether and vehemently; for it is by following this very law that he lives so.

Augustine: What then? When he loves it, does he love something changeable and temporal, or something stable and everlasting?

Evodius: Something eternal, surely, and unchangeable.

Augustine: What of those who, persevering in an evil will, nonetheless desire to be blessed? Can they love that law by which wretchedness is deservedly repaid to such people?

Evodius: In no way, I think.

Augustine: Do they love nothing else?

Evodius: On the contrary, very many things—namely, those in the obtaining or keeping of which that evil will persists.

Augustine: I suppose you mean riches, honors, pleasures, and the beauty of the body, and all the other things that they can both fail to obtain though they will them, and lose against their will.

Evodius: Those are the very things.

Augustine: Do you think these things are eternal, when you see them subject to the turning of time?

Evodius: Who, even of the most demented, would think so?

Augustine: Since, then, it is plain that some people are lovers of eternal things, others of temporal things, and since we have agreed that there are two laws, one eternal, the other temporal—if you have any sense of fairness, which of these do you judge ought to be subject to the eternal law, and which to the temporal?

Evodius: I think what you ask is obvious. For I judge that the blessed, on account of their love of eternal things, live under the eternal law, while the temporal is imposed on the wretched.

Augustine: You judge rightly, provided you hold unshaken what reason has already most clearly shown: that those who serve the temporal law cannot be free of the eternal—from which, we said, everything that is just, and is justly varied, is derived—while those who, by a good will, cling to the eternal law have no need of the temporal law. This, as it appears, you understand well enough.

Evodius: I grasp what you say.

**1. 15. 32.** Augustine: The eternal law, then, commands us to turn our love away from temporal things and, when it is cleansed, to turn it toward the eternal.

Evodius: It does command this.

Augustine: What, then, do you think the temporal law commands, except that those things which can be called ours for a time—when people cling to them by desire—they may possess by that right by which peace and human society are preserved, as far as they can be preserved in such matters? These things are: first, this body and what are called its goods, such as sound health, keenness of the senses, strength, beauty, and whatever others there are, some necessary for the good arts and therefore to be valued more highly, others of less worth. Then liberty—though indeed there is no true liberty except that of the blessed and those who cling to the eternal law; but I now mention the liberty by which those think themselves free who have no human masters, and which those long for who wish to be set free from human masters. Then parents, brothers, spouse, children, kinsmen, relations by marriage, intimates, and whoever are joined to us by some bond. Then the state itself, which is usually held in the place of a parent; honors too, and praise, and what is called popular glory. And lastly, money, under which one name are contained all the things of which we are by right the owners, and which we seem to have the power to sell or give away. How that law distributes to each his own among all these is difficult and long to explain, and plainly not necessary for what we have proposed. For it is enough to see that the power of this law in punishing reaches no further than to take and carry off these things, or some of them, from the one it punishes. It coerces, then, by fear, and twists and turns to its purpose the souls of the wretched, for whose governing it is fitted. For while they fear to lose these things, they keep a certain measure in using them, suited to the bond of the state, of such kind as can be formed out of people of this sort. It does not, however, punish the sin when these things are loved, but when they are taken from others by wickedness. So consider whether we have now reached what you thought boundless. For we had set out to inquire how far that law, by which earthly peoples and states are governed, has the right of punishing.

Evodius: I see that we have reached it.

**1. 15. 33.** Augustine: Do you also see this, then—that there would be no punishment, whether inflicted on people by injury or by such legal vengeance, if they did not love the things that can be taken from them against their will?

Evodius: I see this too.

Augustine: Since, then, of the same things one person makes evil use and another good use—and the one who makes evil use clings to them and is entangled by love, that is, made subject to the things that ought to be subject to him, and setting up as goods for himself the things in the ordering and right handling of which he himself ought surely to be a good; whereas the one who uses them rightly shows that they are indeed goods, but not for him, for they do not make him good or better, but are rather made so by him; and therefore he is not glued to them by love, nor makes them, as it were, members of his own soul (which happens by loving), lest, when they begin to be cut away, they defile him with torment and decay; rather, he rises wholly above them, ready to have them and to govern them when there is need, and readier still to lose them and not have them—since this is so, do you think that silver and gold should be blamed on account of the greedy, or food on account of gluttons, or wine on account of drunkards, or women's beauty on account of fornicators and adulterers, and so on in like manner—especially when you see that a physician makes good use of fire, and a poisoner makes wicked use of bread?

Evodius: It is most true that not the things themselves, but the people who use them badly, are to be blamed.

**1. 16. 34.** Augustine: Rightly. But since we have now, as I think, begun to see what the eternal law is capable of, and have found out how far the temporal law can go in punishing; and since the two kinds of things, eternal and temporal, and again the two kinds of people—those who follow and love eternal things, and those who follow temporal things—have been distinguished clearly enough; and since it has been established that what each chooses to pursue and embrace is set within the will, and that the mind is cast down from the citadel of its rule and from right order by nothing but the will, and it is plain that not any thing, when someone uses it badly, but the one who uses it badly, is to be blamed: let us return, if you please, to the question proposed at the beginning of this discourse, and see whether it has been resolved. For we had set out to inquire what it is to do evil, and on this account we said all that we have said. So now we may notice and consider whether to do evil is anything other than to neglect eternal things—which the mind enjoys through itself, and perceives through itself, and which, loving them, it cannot lose—and to pursue temporal things, things perceived through the body, the lowest part of a human being, things that can never be certain, as though they were great and wondrous. For in this one class all evil deeds, that is, sins, seem to me to be included. But I wait to learn what seems so to you.

**1. 16. 35.** Evodius: It is as you say, and I agree that all sins are contained in this one class, when someone turns away from divine and truly abiding things and turns to changeable and uncertain ones. These, although they are rightly placed in their own order and accomplish a certain beauty of their own, it is nevertheless the mark of a perverse and disordered soul to be made subject to them in following them, when by the divine order and right it has rather been set over them, to lead them at its own bidding. And at the same time I now seem to see settled and found out the very thing we had set out to inquire after that question, what it is to do evil—namely, whence it is that we do evil. For, unless I am mistaken, as the reasoning we have pursued has shown, we do it by the free choice of the will. But I ask whether free choice itself, by which we are shown to have the capacity for sinning, ought to have been given to us by him who made us. For it seems we would not have been going to sin if we lacked it; and it is to be feared that in this way God may even be thought the author of our evil deeds.

Augustine: Have no fear of that at all. But for a more careful inquiry, another time must be taken. For this discourse now calls for a measure and a limit; and I would have you believe that we have, as it were, knocked at the doors of great and hidden things to be inquired into. When, with God as our guide, we begin to come into their inmost recesses, you will surely judge how great a difference there is between this discussion and those that follow, and how far those excel—not only in the keenness of the inquiry, but also in the majesty of the things and the most brilliant light of truth. Only let piety be present, that divine providence may permit us to hold and complete the course we have begun.

Evodius: I yield to your wish, and most gladly join my own to it in judgment and desire.
