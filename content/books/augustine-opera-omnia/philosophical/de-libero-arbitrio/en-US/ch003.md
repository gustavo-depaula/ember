# Book III

**3. 1. 1.** Evodius: Since it has been made plain enough to me that the free will is to be numbered among goods, and indeed not the least of them—from which we are also compelled to confess that it was divinely given, and had to be given—now, if you think it opportune, I desire to learn from you whence that movement arises by which the will itself turns away from the common and unchangeable good, and turns toward its own, or another's, or lower goods, all of them changeable.

Augustine: But what need is there to know this?

Evodius: Because, if it was so given that it has this movement naturally, then it is by necessity turned to these things; and no fault can be found where nature and necessity hold sway.

Augustine: Does this movement please you, or displease you?

Evodius: It displeases me.

Augustine: Then you blame it.

Evodius: I do indeed blame it.

Augustine: Then you blame a blameless movement of the soul.

Evodius: I do not blame a blameless movement of the soul; but I do not know whether there is any fault in forsaking the unchangeable good and turning to changeable goods.

Augustine: Then you blame what you do not know.

Evodius: Do not press the word. For I said "I do not know whether there is any fault" in such a way that I meant to be understood as having no doubt that there is a fault. For by this word that I used, "I do not know," I plainly mocked any doubt about so plain a matter.

Augustine: See what most certain truth is, which has compelled you so quickly to forget what you said a little while ago. For if that movement exists by nature or by necessity, it can in no way be blameworthy. Yet you hold so most firmly that it is blameworthy, that you thought even a doubt about so certain a matter should be mocked. Why, then, did it seem to you that you should affirm, or at least say with some doubt, what you yourself plainly prove to be false? For you said: If the free will was so given that it has this movement naturally, then it is by necessity turned to these things; and no fault can be found where nature and necessity hold sway. But you ought in no way to have doubted that it was not so given, since you do not doubt that this movement is blameworthy.

Evodius: I said that the movement itself was blameworthy, and therefore that it displeases me, and I cannot doubt that it is to be blamed; but I deny that the soul, which by this movement is dragged from the unchangeable good to changeable goods, is to be blamed, if its nature is such that it is moved by necessity.

**3. 1. 2.** Augustine: Whose is this movement, which you grant must surely be blamed?

Evodius: I see it in the soul, but whose it is I do not know.

Augustine: Surely you do not deny that the soul is moved by this movement?

Evodius: I do not deny it.

Augustine: Do you deny, then, that the movement by which a stone is moved is the stone's movement? I do not mean the movement by which we move it, or by which it is moved by some external force, as when it is thrown into the air, but that by which, by its own inclination, it tends and falls toward the earth.

Evodius: I do not indeed deny that the movement by which, as you say, it is inclined and seeks the depths, is the stone's movement; but it is natural. If the soul too has its movement in this way, then it too surely is natural; and it cannot rightly be reproached for what it does naturally; for even if it is moved to its own ruin, yet it is compelled by the necessity of its nature. But since we do not doubt that this movement is blameworthy, it must in every way be denied to be natural; and so it is not like that movement by which a stone is naturally moved.

Augustine: Did we accomplish anything in the two earlier discussions?

Evodius: We did indeed.

Augustine: I believe, then, you remember that in the first discussion it was sufficiently found out that the mind is made a slave of lust by nothing other than its own will: for it can be forced to this disgrace neither by anything higher nor by anything equal, since that is unjust; nor by anything lower, since it cannot. It remains, then, that this movement is its own—the movement by which it turns its will to enjoyment from the Creator toward the creature. If this movement is reckoned as fault—and one who doubts it seemed to you worthy of mockery—then surely it is not natural but voluntary; and in this it is like that movement by which a stone is borne downward, for as that is the stone's own, so this is the soul's. Yet in this it is unlike, that a stone does not have it in its power to check the movement by which it is borne lower; whereas the soul, while it is unwilling, is not moved in such a way that, forsaking higher things, it loves lower ones. And so that movement is natural to the stone, but this one voluntary to the soul. Hence, if anyone should say that a stone sins because it tends toward the depths by its weight, I would judge him—I will not say duller than the stone itself, but plainly out of his mind; whereas we charge the soul with sin when we prove that it forsakes higher things and prefers lower ones for enjoyment. What need, then, is there to ask whence arises that movement by which the will turns away from the unchangeable good toward the changeable good, since we confess it to be only the soul's, and voluntary, and on that account blameworthy; and the whole useful teaching about this matter avails for this: that, this movement being disapproved and checked, we may turn our will from the lapse into temporal things toward the enjoyment of the everlasting good?

**3. 1. 3.** Evodius: I see, and in a way touch and hold, that what you say is true. For there is nothing I sense so firmly and so intimately as that I have a will, and that by it I am moved to enjoy something. And what I may call mine, I do not find at all, if the will by which I will and do not will is not mine. To whom, then, is it to be attributed, if I do anything evil through it, but to myself? For since a good God made me, and I do nothing good except through the will, it sufficiently appears that it was given by the good God rather for this. But the movement by which the will is turned this way or that, unless it were voluntary and set in our power, a person would be neither praiseworthy when he turns the, as it were, hinge of the will toward higher things, nor blameworthy when he turns it toward lower; nor would he need at all to be admonished to neglect these things and will to attain the eternal, and to will not to live badly but to live well. But whoever thinks that a person need not be so admonished is to be banished from the number of human beings.

**3. 2. 4.** Since these things are so, I am moved beyond words how it can be that both God is foreknowing of all things future, and we sin by no necessity. For whoever says that anything can happen otherwise than God foreknew it, strives with most insane impiety to destroy the foreknowledge of God. Therefore, if God foreknew that the good man was going to sin—which anyone must grant me who confesses with me that God is foreknowing of all things future—then if it is so, I do not say that God should not have made him, for he made him good, nor could the sin of one whom he himself made good harm God in anything; rather, in making him he had shown his goodness, and shows also, in punishing, his justice, and, in delivering, his mercy. So I do not say that God should not have made him; but this I say: since he had foreknown that he was going to sin, it was necessary that what God foreknew was going to be should come about. How, then, is the will free, where so unavoidable a necessity appears?

**3. 2. 5.** Augustine: You have knocked vehemently at the mercy of God. May it be present, and open to those who knock. But I would believe that the greater part of people are tormented by this question for no other reason than that they do not seek piously, and are swifter to excuse than to confess their sins. For some gladly hold that no divine providence presides over human affairs; and while they commit both their souls and their bodies to chance happenings, and give themselves to be struck and torn by lusts, denying divine judgments and deceiving human ones, they think to ward off, by the patronage of fortune, those by whom they are accused—which fortune, however, they are wont to fashion and paint as blind, so that either they are better than that fortune by which they think they are governed, or they confess that they too feel and speak these things with the same blindness. And it is not absurd to grant that such people manage everything by chance, since by acting they fall. But against this opinion, full of the most foolish and most demented error, enough, as I think, was discussed in our second conversation. Others, however, although they do not dare to deny that the providence of God presides over human life, prefer nevertheless to believe, by a wicked error, that it is either weak, or unjust, or evil, rather than to confess their sins with suppliant piety. If all these would suffer themselves to be persuaded that, when they think of the best and most just and most powerful, they should believe the goodness and justice and power of God to be far greater and higher than whatever they conceive in thought; and, considering themselves, would understand that they owe thanks to God, even if he had willed them to be something lower than they are, and would cry out with all the bones and marrow of their conscience: *I said, Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you* [^1]—then they would be led, by the sure paths of the divine mercy, into wisdom, so that, neither puffed up by things found nor disturbed by things not found, they would become both better instructed for seeing by knowing, and gentler for seeking by not knowing. But to you, of whom I do not doubt that this is now persuaded, see how easily I shall answer concerning so great a question, when you, who first questioned, have answered me a few things.

**3. 3. 6.** For this is certainly what troubles and puzzles you: how it is not contradictory and incompatible for God to foreknow all future things while we sin not by necessity but by will. For if God foreknows, you say, that a human being will sin, it is necessary that he sin; but if it is necessary, then in sinning there is no choice of the will, but rather an inevitable and fixed necessity. You evidently fear that this reasoning may force us either impiously to deny that God foreknows all future things, or, if we cannot deny that, to confess that we sin not by will but by necessity. Or is something else troubling you?  
Evodius: Nothing else for the moment.

Augustine: You think, then, that all the things of which God is foreknowing come about not by will but by necessity.

Evodius: I think so entirely.

Augustine: Wake up at last, and look upon yourself a little, and tell me, if you can, what sort of will you are going to have tomorrow—one for sinning, or one for doing right.

Evodius: I do not know.

Augustine: What? Do you think God likewise does not know this?

Evodius: I would in no way think that.

Augustine: If, then, he knows your will of tomorrow, and foresees the future wills of all people, both those who are and those who will be, much more does he foresee what he himself is going to do with the just and the impious.

Evodius: Surely, if I say that God is foreknowing of my works, I would say much more confidently that he foreknows his own works, and most certainly foresees what he is going to do.

Augustine: Do you not, then, beware lest it be said to you that he too will do whatever he is going to do not by will but by necessity, if all the things of which God is foreknowing come about by necessity and not by will?

Evodius: When I said that all things that God foreknew were going to be come about by necessity, I was looking only at those things that come about in his creation, not those in himself; for those do not come about, but are everlasting.

Augustine: Then God does nothing in his creation.

Evodius: He has already established, once for all, in what manner the order of that universe which he founded should be carried on; nor does he administer anything by a new will.

Augustine: Does he make no one blessed?

Evodius: He does indeed.

Augustine: And he does it surely then, when the person is made so.

Evodius: So it is.

Augustine: If, then, for example, you are going to be blessed a year from now, you will be made blessed a year from now.

Evodius: Just so.

Augustine: Then he foreknows today what he is going to do a year from now.

Evodius: He always foreknew this; and now too I agree that he foreknows it, if it is so going to be.

**3. 3. 7.** Augustine: Tell me, I ask: are you not his creature, or will your blessedness not come about in you?

Evodius: On the contrary, I am his creature, and it will come about in me that I shall be blessed.

Augustine: Then your blessedness will come about in you not by will but by necessity, with God making it.

Evodius: His will is necessity for me.

Augustine: Then you will be blessed against your will.

Evodius: If I had the power to be blessed, I would surely already be so; for I will it even now, and am not so, because not I, but he, makes me blessed.

Augustine: Most excellently does truth cry out from you. For you could not feel that anything is in our power except what we do when we will. So nothing is so within our power as the will itself. For it, surely, with no interval, is present the moment we will. And so we can rightly say, We grow old not by will but by necessity; or, We fall ill not by will but by necessity; or, We die not by will but by necessity; and anything else of this kind. But that we will not by will—who, even raving, would dare to say? Therefore, although God foreknows our future wills, it does not follow that we do not will something by will. For as to what you said about blessedness, that you are not made blessed by your own self, you said it as though I had denied this; but I say that, when you are going to be blessed, you will be so not against your will but willing it. Since, then, God is foreknowing of your future blessedness, and nothing can come about otherwise than he foreknew—otherwise there is no foreknowledge—we are nevertheless not on that account compelled to think (which is most absurd and far removed from the truth) that you will be blessed though unwilling. But just as the foreknowledge of God, which is certain even today about your future blessedness, does not take from you the will for blessedness when you begin to be blessed, so too a blameworthy will, if any will be in you in the future, will not for that reason cease to be a will, because God foreknew that it would be.

**3. 3. 8.** Consider, please, how blind it is to say: “If God foreknew my future will, since nothing can happen otherwise than he foreknew, I must will what he foreknew; but if I must, it must be acknowledged that I will it not by will but by necessity.” What extraordinary folly! How, then, can nothing happen otherwise than God foreknew, if the will which he foreknew would exist is not going to be a will? I pass over the equally monstrous thing I mentioned a little earlier: the very same person says, “I must will in this way,” who tries to remove the will by positing necessity. For if he must will, how will he will when there is no will? But if he does not speak in this way and instead says that because he must will, the will itself is not in his power, he can be answered from what you yourself said when I asked whether you would be happy unwillingly. You answered that you would already be happy if it were in your power; for you said you willed it but could not yet attain it. At this I remarked that truth had cried out through you. For we can deny that we have something in our power only when what we will is not present to us; but if, when we will, the will itself is absent, we certainly do not will. If it is impossible for us, while willing, not to will, the will is certainly present to those who will; and nothing else is in our power except what is present when we will it. Our will, then, would not even be will unless it were in our power. Moreover, because it is in our power, it is free for us. For what we do not have in our power is not free for us, nor can what we possess fail to be present. Thus we both refrain from denying that God foreknows all future things and nevertheless will what we will. For since he foreknows our will, the will he foreknows will itself exist. There will therefore be will, because it is will that he foreknows; and it could not be will unless it were in our power. Therefore he foreknows our power too. His foreknowledge, then, does not take away my power; rather, I shall possess it all the more certainly because he whose foreknowledge is never deceived foreknew that I would possess it.  
Evodius: Behold, I no longer deny that whatever God foreknew comes about of necessity, and that he foreknows our sins in such a way that nevertheless our will remains free to us, and set within our power.

**3. 4. 9.** Augustine: What, then, troubles you? Have you perhaps forgotten what our first discussion accomplished—that we sin by no one compelling us, whether higher or lower or equal, but by our own will?

Evodius: I dare to deny none of these things at all. But yet, I confess, I do not yet see how these two are not opposed to each other—the foreknowledge of God about our sins, and our free choice in sinning. For we must confess both that God is just, and that he is foreknowing. But I would like to know by what justice he punishes sins which must necessarily come about; or how it is that things he foreknew were going to be do not come about of necessity; or how whatever must necessarily come about in his creation is not to be attributed to the Creator.

**3. 4. 10.** Augustine: Why does it seem to you that our free choice is opposed to the foreknowledge of God? Because it is foreknowledge, or because it is the foreknowledge of God?

Evodius: Because it is God's, rather.

Augustine: What then? If you foreknew that someone was going to sin, would it not be necessary that he sin?

Evodius: On the contrary, it would be necessary that he sin; for my foreknowledge would not be foreknowledge unless I foreknew things certain.

Augustine: Then it is not because it is God's foreknowledge that what he foreknew must necessarily come about, but only because it is foreknowledge—which, if it does not foreknow things certain, is surely no foreknowledge at all.

Evodius: I agree; but where is this tending?

Augustine: Because, unless I am mistaken, you would not directly compel the person to sin whom you foreknew was going to sin; nor would your foreknowledge itself compel him to sin, though without doubt he was going to sin—for otherwise you would not foreknow that this was going to be. Therefore, just as these two are not opposed to each other—that you should by your foreknowledge know what another is going to do by his own will—so God, compelling no one to sin, nevertheless foresees those who are going to sin by their own will.

**3. 4. 11.** Why, then, should not he who is just avenge what he, being foreknowing, does not compel to be done? For just as you do not, by your memory, compel to have been done the things that have passed, so God does not, by his foreknowledge, compel to be done the things that are to come. And just as you remember certain things you have done, and yet have not done all the things you remember, so God foreknows all the things of which he himself is the author, and yet is not the author of all the things he foreknows. Of those things of which he is not the evil author, he is the just avenger. From this, then, understand now by what justice God punishes sins—because he does not do the things he knows are to come; for if he ought not to render punishment to sinners because he foresees they will sin, neither ought he to render rewards to those who do right, because he no less foresees that they will do right. Rather, let us confess both that it belongs to his foreknowledge that nothing future escapes him, and that it belongs to his justice that sin, since it is committed by the will, is not, by his judgment, left unpunished, just as it is not compelled to be done by his foreknowledge.

**3. 5. 12.** Now that which you put in the third place—how whatever must necessarily come about in his creation is not to be attributed to the Creator—that rule of piety will easily set aside, which it behooves us to remember: that we owe thanksgiving to our Creator. For his most lavish goodness would most justly be praised, even if he had founded us in some lower grade of creation. For although our soul is corrupted by sins, it is nevertheless higher and better than if it were turned into this visible light. And you surely see, from the eminence of this light, how greatly souls, even those given over to the bodily senses, praise God. Therefore let it not now trouble you that sinful souls are blamed, so that you say in your heart that it would have been better if they did not exist. For they are blamed in comparison with themselves, when it is considered what they would be like if they had been unwilling to sin. Yet God, their founder, is to be praised most gloriously, as far as human capacity allows, not only because he orders them justly when they sin, but also because he so founded them that, even when defiled by sins, they are in no way surpassed by the dignity of corporeal light, for which he is nonetheless rightly praised.

**3. 5. 13.** I also warn you to beware lest perhaps you say not that it would have been better if they did not exist, but that they ought to have been made otherwise. For whatever better thing occurs to you by true reason, know that God made it, as the founder of all goods. But it is not true reason, but envious weakness, when, having thought that something better ought to have been made, you then will nothing lower to be made at all—as if, beholding the sky, you were unwilling that the earth had been made; which is altogether unjust. For you would rightly find fault if you saw the earth made and the sky passed over, since you would say it ought to have been made such as you could conceive the sky to be. But since you see that the very thing to whose appearance you wished the earth brought has been made—but is called not earth but sky—I believe that, not defrauded of the better thing, you ought in no way to envy that some lower thing too was made and was earth. And in the earth there is again, according to its parts, such variety that nothing pertaining to the appearance of earth occurs to one's thought that God, the founder of all things, has not made in its whole mass. For from the most fertile and most pleasant land, by degrees, one comes through the intermediate kinds to the most salty and most barren, so that you would not dare to blame any, except in comparison with a better; and so you ascend through all the degrees of praise, so that, when you have found the highest kind of land, you would nevertheless not wish it alone to exist. Now between the whole earth and the sky, how great is the distance! For the moist and airy natures are interposed; and out of these four elements other countless kinds and forms of things—countless to us, but numbered by God—are varied. There can, then, be something in the nature of things that you do not conceive by your reason. But that what you conceive by true reason should not exist, cannot be. For you cannot conceive anything better in creation that escaped the maker of creation. For the human soul, naturally connected with the divine reasons on which it depends, when it says, This would be better made than that—if it speaks the truth, and sees what it says—sees it in those reasons to which it is connected. Let it believe, then, that God made what it knew by true reason ought to have been made by him, even if it does not see this among the things made. For even if it could not see the sky with its eyes, and yet gathered by true reason that some such thing ought to have been made, it ought to believe it was made, although it did not see it with its eyes. For it would not see in thought that it ought to have been made, unless it saw it in those reasons by which all things were made. But what is not there, no one can see by truthful thought, as surely as it is not true.

**3. 5. 14.** In this very many people err, that, when they have beheld better things with the mind, they seek them with the eyes in unfitting places. As if someone, grasping perfect roundness by reason, should be vexed that he did not find such a nut, if he had never beheld any round body except fruits of this kind. So some, when by most true reason they see that a creature is better which, although it has a free will, yet always fixed on God, has never sinned—then, looking upon the sins of people, grieve not that they may stop sinning, but that they were made at all, saying: He should have made us such that we always wished to enjoy his unchangeable truth, and never wished to sin. Let them not cry out, let them not grow angry: for he did not compel them to sin by the fact that he made them, to whom he gave the power to choose whether they would; and there are some such angels, who neither ever sinned nor ever will sin. Therefore, if a creature delights you that, by a most persevering will, does not sin, there is no doubt that, by right reason, you set it above one that sins; but just as you set it above in thought, so God, the Creator, set it above in his ordering. Believe that there is such a creature in the higher seats and in the height of the heavens; for if the Founder showed his goodness in founding that creature whose future sins he foresaw, in no way would he not show that goodness in founding a creature which he foreknew would not sin.

**3. 5. 15.** For that lofty creature has its own perpetual blessedness, forever enjoying its Creator, which it merits by a perpetual will to keep justice. Next, even that sinful creature has its own order—having lost blessedness in its sins, yet not dismissed from the capacity of recovering blessedness. This surely surpasses the one held by a perpetual will to sin; and between it and that former one, abiding in the will of justice, this one shows a certain middle state, which by the humility of repentance recovers its height. For God did not, from that creature which he foreknew would not only sin but also remain in the will of sinning, withhold the bounty of his goodness, so as not to found it. For just as a wandering horse is better than a stone that does not wander, because it lacks proper motion and sense, so a creature is more excellent that sins by a free will than one that does not sin because it has no free will. And as I would praise wine good in its kind, while I would blame a person made drunk on that wine, and yet would set that same person, already blamed and still drunk, above the wine I praised by which he was made drunk: so a corporeal creature is rightly to be praised in its degree, while those are to be blamed who, by its immoderate use, are turned away from the perception of truth; and yet those same perverse and, as it were, drunken people are still preferred to that creature, praiseworthy in its own order, by the greedy use of which they have vanished away—not now by the merit of their vices, but still by the dignity of their nature.

**3. 5. 16.** Since, then, every soul is better than every body, and every sinful soul, into whatever it falls, is by no change made a body, nor is what makes it a soul taken from it at all, and therefore it in no way loses that by which it is better than a body—while among bodies light holds the first place—it follows that the lowest soul is set above the first body; and it can come about that some other body be set before the body of a certain soul, but that another soul be set before that soul itself, in no way. Why, then, should not God be praised, and praised with unspeakable proclamation, who, when he made those souls that would abide in the laws of justice, made also other souls which he foresaw would either sin or even persevere in sins, since even such souls are still better than those which, because they have no rational and free choice of the will, cannot sin? And these latter are still better than the brightest splendor of any bodies whatever, which some, though with great error, venerate in place of the substance of the highest God himself. But if, in the order of corporeal creatures, from the very choirs of the stars down to the number of our hairs, the beauty of good things is so woven together by degrees that it is most ignorant to say, What is this? Why this?—since all things were created in their own order—how much more ignorant is it to say it of any soul, which, to whatever diminution and defect of its own beauty it may come, will without any doubt always surpass the dignity of all bodies?

**3. 5. 17.** For reason judges one way, use another. Reason judges by the light of truth, so as by right judgment to subject lesser things to greater; but use is for the most part inclined by the habit of convenience, so as to value more highly the things that truth proves to be lesser. For although reason sets the heavenly bodies far above earthly bodies, yet which of carnal people would not rather that more stars be missing from the sky than one little tree from his field, or a cow from his herd? But just as older people either wholly despise, or at least patiently wait to correct, the judgments of little children, who—except for certain ones in whose love they delight—would rather any other person whatever should die than their own sparrow; and much more so if that person were terrifying, but the sparrow tuneful and pretty: so those who, by the advance of the soul, have made progress toward wisdom, when they find unskilled appraisers of things praising God in lesser creatures, which they apply more fittingly to their carnal senses, but, in the higher and better things, partly not praising him, or praising him less, partly even trying to blame or amend, partly not believing that he is their founder—let them grow accustomed either wholly to despise the judgments of such people, if they cannot correct them, or, until they correct them, to bear and endure them with an even mind.

**3. 6. 18.** Since these things are so, so far is it from the truth that the sins of the creature are thought to be attributed to the Creator—although the things he foreknew were going to be must necessarily come about—that, whereas you said you could not find how whatever must necessarily come about in his creation is not attributed to him, I, on the contrary, do not find a way, and affirm that none can be found and that there is altogether none, by which whatever must necessarily come about in his creation, so as to come about by the will of those sinning, is attributed to him. For if anyone says, I would rather not exist than be wretched, I will answer: You lie. For even now you are wretched, and you do not wish to die for no other reason than to exist; so that, although you do not wish to be wretched, you nevertheless wish to exist. Give thanks, then, for the fact that you exist willingly, that what you are unwillingly may be taken away. For you exist willingly, and you are wretched unwillingly. If you are ungrateful for the fact that you wish to exist, you are rightly compelled to be what you do not wish. Therefore, from the fact that, even ungrateful, you have what you wish, I praise the goodness of the Creator; and from the fact that you suffer ungrateful what you do not wish, I praise the justice of the Orderer.

**3. 6. 19.** If he says: I do not refuse to die because I prefer to be wretched rather than not to exist at all, but lest after death I be more wretched—I will answer: If this is unjust, you will not be so; but if it is just, let us praise him by whose laws you will be so. If he says: From what should I presume that, if this is unjust, I shall not be so?—I will answer: Because, if you will be in your own power, either you will not be wretched, or, by ruling yourself unjustly, you will justly be wretched; or, willing to rule yourself justly and not being able, you will not be in your own power, and will be either in no one's power or in another's; if in no one's, then either unwillingly or willingly; but unwillingly you can be nothing, unless some force has overcome you; and one who is in no one's power can be overcome by no force. If, however, willingly you will be in no one's power, then again reason runs to this—that you are in your own; and either, by ruling yourself unjustly, you are justly wretched; or, since you will be whatever you are willingly, you still have whereby to give thanks to the goodness of your Creator. But if you will not be in your own power, then surely either one more powerful, or one weaker, will have you in his power. If weaker, it is your fault, and a just wretchedness, since you could overcome the weaker if you would. But if a more powerful will have you, the weaker, in his power, you will in no way think so right an ordering rightly unjust. Most truly, then, it was said: If this is unjust, you will not be so; if just, let us praise him by whose laws you will be so.

**3. 7. 20.** If he says: For this reason I prefer to be wretched rather than not to exist at all, because I already am; whereas, if I could have been consulted before I existed, I would choose not to exist rather than to be wretched. For the very fact that now I fear not to exist, though I am wretched, belongs to that very wretchedness, by which I do not will what I ought to will; for I ought rather to will not to exist than to be wretched. But now I confess that indeed I prefer to be wretched rather than nothing; but the more wretchedly, the more foolishly do I will this; and the more wretchedly, the more truly I see that I ought not to have willed this—I will answer: Take care rather that you do not err here, where you think you see the truth. For if you were blessed, you would surely prefer to be rather than not to be; and now, though you are wretched, you nonetheless prefer to be, even wretched, rather than not to be at all, though you do not will to be wretched. Consider, then, as far as you can, how great a good it is simply to be, which both the blessed and the wretched will. For if you consider this well, you will see that you are wretched insofar as you do not draw near to that which supremely is; and that you think it better not to be than to be wretched, insofar as you do not see that which supremely is; and yet that you will to be, because you are from him who supremely is.

**3. 7. 21.** If, then, you wish to flee wretchedness, love in yourself this very thing—that you wish to be. For if you will more and more to be, you will draw near to him who supremely is. And give thanks now that you are. For although you are inferior to the blessed, you are nevertheless superior to those things which do not have even the will for blessedness; many of which are praised even by the wretched. Yet all things, by the very fact that they are, are rightly to be praised; because by the very fact that they are, they are good. The more, then, you love to be, the more you will desire eternal life, and the more you will long to be so fashioned that your affections are not temporal, branded and impressed by the loves of temporal things—things which, before they exist, do not exist, and when they exist, flee, and when they have fled, will not be. And so, when they are future, they do not yet exist; and when they are past, they no longer exist. How, then, can they be held so as to abide, whose very beginning to be is a passing on toward not being? But one who loves to be approves these things insofar as they are, and loves that which always is. And if he was wavering in the love of these things, he is made firm in the love of that; and if he was flowing away in the love of passing things, he will be made solid in the love of the abiding, and will stand, and will obtain that very being which he willed when he feared not to be, and could not stand entangled in the love of fleeing things. Therefore let it not displease you—rather, let it greatly please you—that you prefer to be even wretched rather than, for that reason, not to be wretched, because you would be nothing. For to this beginning, by which you wish to be, if you add the willing to be more and more, you rise and are built up into that which supremely is; and so you will hold yourself back from every defect by which what is lowest passes into not being, and undermines with itself the strength of the one who loves it. Hence it comes about that he who prefers not to be, lest he be wretched, since he cannot not be, it remains that he be wretched. But he who loves to be more than he hates to be wretched, by adding to what he loves, excludes what he hates: for when he has begun to be perfectly in his own kind, he will not be wretched.

**3. 8. 22.** For see how absurdly and inconsistently it is said: I would rather not be than be wretched. For one who says, I would rather this than that, chooses something. But not to be is not something, but nothing; and therefore you can in no way rightly choose, when what you choose does not exist. You say that you indeed will to be, though you are wretched, but that you ought not to have willed this. What, then, ought you to have willed? Rather not to be, you say. If you ought to have willed this, this is better; but what does not exist cannot be better: therefore you ought not to have willed it; and the sense by which you do not will it is truer than the opinion by which you think you ought to have willed it. Then, what anyone rightly chooses to seek, when he has reached it, he must necessarily become better; but one who will not exist cannot be better: no one, therefore, can rightly choose not to be. Nor ought we to be moved by the judgment of those who, pressed by wretchedness, have killed themselves. For either they fled to where they thought it would be better for them—and this is not contrary to our reasoning, however they thought; or, if they believed they would be nothing at all, much less will the false choice of those who choose nothing move us. For how shall I follow one who chooses, of whom, if I ask what he chooses, he will answer, Nothing? For one who chooses not to be is surely proved to choose nothing, even if he is unwilling to give that answer.

**3. 8. 23.** But, to say what I think about this whole matter, if I can: no one seems to me, when he kills himself or in any way desires to die, to have in his perception that he will not exist after death—although he has something of this in his opinion. For opinion is in the error or the truth of one who reasons or believes; but perception holds sway either by habit or by nature. That it can happen that one thing is in opinion and another in perception is easy to recognize even from this: that very often we believe one thing ought to be done, and it delights us to do another. And sometimes perception is truer than opinion, if the latter is from error and the former from nature: as when a sick person is often profitably delighted by cold water, which he nevertheless believes, if he drinks it, will harm him. Sometimes opinion is truer than perception, if he believes, on the art of medicine, that cold water will hurt, when indeed it will hurt, and yet it delights him to drink it. Sometimes both are in the truth, when what profits is not only believed so, but also pleases; sometimes both are in error, when what harms is both believed to profit and does not cease to please. Now right opinion is wont to correct depraved habit, and depraved opinion to deprave right nature: so great is the force in the dominion and sovereignty of reason. When, therefore, anyone, believing that he will not exist after death, is nevertheless driven by intolerable troubles to a total desire for death, and decides upon and seizes death, he has in his opinion the error of utter ceasing-to-be, but in his perception the natural desire for rest. But that which is at rest is not nothing; rather, it is more than that which is unquiet. For unquiet varies the affections, so that one destroys another; but rest has constancy, in which is best understood that which is said, "it is." Therefore all that craving in the will for death is directed not so that one who dies should not exist, but so that he should rest. So, although by error he believes he will not exist, by nature he nevertheless desires to be at rest—that is, to be more fully. Wherefore, just as it can in no way come about that not to exist should please anyone, so it ought in no way to come about that anyone, from the fact that he exists, should be ungrateful to the goodness of the Creator.

**3. 9. 24.** If he says: Yet it was not difficult or laborious for almighty God so to make all things he made that each should have its own order, so that no creature should come to wretchedness; for either the almighty could not do this, or the good God begrudged it—I will answer that the order of creatures runs by just degrees from the highest to the lowest, so that he envies who says, That should not exist; and he too envies who says, That should be of such a kind. For if he wishes it to be such as the higher is, then that higher already exists, and is so great that nothing should be added to it, since it is perfect. He, then, who says, That too should be of such a kind, either wishes to add to the perfect higher one, and will be immoderate and unjust; or wishes to destroy that one, and will be evil and envious. But he who says, That should not exist, will be no less evil and envious, since he does not wish to exist that thing which he is still compelled to praise as superior to a lower one. As if he should say, There should be no moon—when even the brightness of a lamp, far inferior, yet beautiful in its kind, and comely for earthly darkness, and apt for nightly uses, and in all these in its own small measure praiseworthy, he must either confess or most foolishly or contentiously deny. How, then, will he dare rightly to say, The moon should not exist among things, when, if he said, The lamp should not exist, he would feel he was to be laughed at? But if he does not say, The moon should not exist, but says it ought to have been such as he sees the sun is, he does not understand that he is saying nothing other than, The moon should not exist, but there should be two suns. In which he errs doubly: both that he desires to add something to the perfection of things, when he longs for a second sun; and that he desires to diminish it, when he wishes the moon withdrawn.

**3. 9. 25.** Here perhaps he will say that he makes no complaint about the moon, because its splendor is so much less that it is not wretched; but that he grieves over the souls—not over their dimness, but over their wretchedness. Let him diligently consider that, just as the splendor of the moon is not wretched, so the splendor of the sun is not blessed. For although they are heavenly bodies, they are nevertheless bodies, as far as concerns this light which can be perceived through the bodily eyes. But no bodies, as far as concerns themselves, can be either blessed or wretched—although the bodies of the blessed or the wretched can exist. Yet the likeness drawn from those lights teaches this: that just as, in contemplating the differences of bodies, when you see some brighter, you unjustly demand that those you have beheld as dimmer be taken away, or made equal to the brighter—but, referring all things to the perfection of the whole, you see that all things exist the more, the more clear they are among themselves in greater and lesser degree; nor does a perfect whole occur to you except where the greater are present in such a way that the lesser are not lacking—so too you should think of the differences of souls; in which you will find this also: that the wretchedness over which you grieve avails for this too, that, for the perfection of the whole, those souls also are not lacking which were bound to become wretched, because they willed to be sinful. And so far is it from being the case that God ought not to have made such souls, that he made even the other creatures, far inferior to wretched souls, in a praiseworthy way.

**3. 9. 26.** But one less understanding seems still to have something to object to what has been said. For he says: If even our wretchedness completes the perfection of the whole, then something would have been lacking to this perfection if we were always blessed. Therefore, if the soul does not reach wretchedness except by sinning, then even our sins are necessary to the perfection of the whole which God founded. How, then, does he justly punish sins, which, had they been lacking, his creation would not be full and perfect? Here the answer is given that not the sins themselves, nor the wretchedness itself, are necessary to the perfection of the whole, but souls insofar as they are souls; which, if they will, sin, and, if they have sinned, become wretched. For if, when their sins are removed, wretchedness persists, or even precedes sins, then rightly the order and administration of the whole is said to be deformed. Again, if sins occur, and wretchedness is lacking, no less does iniquity dishonor the order. But when blessedness is present to those not sinning, the whole is perfect; and when wretchedness is present to those sinning, no less is the whole perfect. And that the souls themselves are not lacking—those which wretchedness follows when they sin, or blessedness when they do right—the whole is always full and perfect through all natures. For sin and the punishment of sin are not certain natures, but affections of natures—the one voluntary, the other penal. The voluntary affection that occurs in sin is a base affection. To it, therefore, a penal one is applied, that it may order it where it is not base for such a thing to be, and may compel it to fit the beauty of the whole, so that the punishment of sin amends the dishonor of sin.

**3. 9. 27.** Hence it comes about that the sinning superior creature is punished by inferior creatures, because they are so low that they can be adorned even by base souls, and so fit the beauty of the whole. For what is so great in a house as a human being? And what is so abject and lowly as the sewer of the house? Yet a slave detected in such a sin that he is held worthy to clean the sewer adorns it even by his disgrace; and both of these—that is, the slave's disgrace and the cleaning of the sewer—now joined together and reduced to a kind of unity of their own kind, are so fitted and woven into a well-arranged house that they suit its whole with most fitting beauty. Yet, if that slave had been unwilling to sin, there would not have been lacking to household discipline some other provision by which the necessary things were cleaned. What, then, is so lowly among things as every earthly body? Yet even a sinful soul so adorns this corruptible flesh that it gives it a most becoming appearance and a vital motion. A soul of this kind, then, does not suit a heavenly dwelling through sin, but suits an earthly one through punishment; so that, whichever it chooses, the whole is always beautiful, ordered in its most becoming parts, of which God is the founder and administrator. But the best souls, when they dwell in the lowest creatures, do not adorn them by their wretchedness, which they do not have, but by the good use of them. But if sinful souls were permitted to dwell in lofty places, it would be dishonorable; for they do not suit those things which they can neither use well nor confer any ornament upon.

**3. 9. 28.** Therefore, although this earthly world is assigned to corruptible things, yet, preserving as far as it can the image of the higher things, it does not cease to show us certain examples and indications. For if we see some good and great man, at the prompting of the duty of honor, burned by fire, as far as concerns the body, we do not call this the punishment of sin, but a proof of fortitude and patience, and we love him more when most foul corruption consumes his bodily members than if he suffered nothing of the kind; for we marvel that the nature of the soul is not changed by the changeableness of the body. But when we see the members of a most cruel robber undone by such a torment, we approve the order of the laws. Both these torments, then, adorn—the one by the merit of virtue, the other by that of sin. But if, after those fires, or even before them, we saw that best man changed, to suit a heavenly dwelling, and lifted up to the stars, we would surely rejoice. But if we saw a wicked robber, whether before or after his punishment, with the same malice of will, raised up to a seat of everlasting honor in heaven, who would not be offended? So it comes about that both could adorn the inferior creatures, but only one of them the higher. From this we are admonished to observe that the mortality of this flesh both adorned the first man, so that punishment suited his sin, and adorned our Lord, so that mercy might free us from sin. But not as the just man, abiding in justice itself, could have a mortal body, can the unjust man, while he is unjust, attain the immortality of the saints—that is, the lofty and angelic immortality; not of those angels of whom the Apostle says, *Do you not know that we shall judge angels?* [^2], but of those of whom the Lord says, *And they shall be equal to the angels of God* [^3]. For those who desire equality with the angels for the sake of their own vain glory do not on that account wish to be equal to the angels, but to themselves. And so, persevering in such a will, they will be made equal in punishment to the prevaricating angels, loving their own power rather than that of almighty God. To such, set on the left hand, because they did not seek God through the door of humility, which the Lord Jesus Christ showed in himself, and lived unmercifully and proudly, it will be said: *Depart into the eternal fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels* [^4].

**3. 10. 29.** For since there are two origins of sins—one from one's own thought, the other from the persuasion of another—to which I think pertains what the prophet says, *Cleanse me from my hidden faults, O Lord, and from those of others spare your servant* [^5]—both are indeed voluntary. For just as one does not sin unwillingly by his own thought, so, when he consents to one persuading him to evil, he surely consents only by will; but yet it is graver, not only to sin by one's own thought with no one persuading, but even to persuade another to sin through envy and deceit, than to be drawn to sinning by another's persuasion. The justice of the Lord, then, who punishes, is preserved in both sins. For this too was weighed in the balance of equity: that the human being should not be denied even to the power of the devil, whom by evil persuasion he had subjected to himself. For it was unjust that the devil should not have dominion over him whom he had captured. Nor can it in any way come about that the perfect justice of the highest and true God, which is stretched out everywhere, should forsake even the ruins of sinners that must be set in order. And yet, because the human being had sinned less than the devil, this very thing availed him for the recovery of salvation—that he was made over, even to the mortality of the flesh, to the prince of this world, that is, of the portion of these mortal and lowest things—the prince, that is, of all sinners and the overseer of death. For so, made fearful by the consciousness of mortality, and dreading harm and destruction even from the basest and most abject beasts, or even the tiniest, and uncertain of things to come, the human being grew accustomed to check both unlawful joys, and especially pride—by whose persuasion he was cast down, and which is the one vice through which the medicine of mercy is spurned. For what is so needy of mercy as the wretched? And what is so unworthy of mercy as the proud wretch?

**3. 10. 30.** From this it came about that that Word of God, through whom all things were made, and in whom all angelic blessedness rejoices, stretched out his mercy even to our wretchedness, and *the Word became flesh and dwelt among us* [^6]. For so could a human being eat the bread of angels, not yet made equal to the angels, if the very bread of angels deigned to be made equal to human beings. Nor did he so descend to us as to forsake them; but, whole to them at once and whole to us, he feeds them inwardly through that which he is God, and admonishes us outwardly through that which we are, making fit by faith those whom he may feed equally by sight. For since the rational creature is fed by that Word as by its best food, and the human soul is rational, which was held by the mortal bonds of the penalty of sin, reduced to such diminution that it strove toward understanding invisible things through conjectures of visible things—the food of the rational creature was made visible, not by a change of its own nature, but by the taking on of ours, so that, by drawing those who pursue visible things, it might call them back to itself, the invisible. So the soul found him outwardly humble whom, growing proud, it had inwardly forsaken—about to imitate his visible humility, and about to return to his invisible height.

**3. 10. 31.** And the Word of God, the only Son of God, having clothed himself with man, subjected to man also the devil, whom he always held and will hold under his laws—wresting nothing from him by violent dominion, but overcoming him by the law of justice. For since, the woman having been deceived, and the man having been cast down through the woman, the devil claimed all the offspring of the first man as sinful under the laws of death—with a malicious desire of harming, yet by a most just right—his power held good only so long as he should slay the just one, in whom he could show nothing worthy of death, both because he was slain without crime, and because he was born without lust. To this just one the devil had subjected those he had captured, so that whatever was born from there he might retain as the fruit of his own tree—by a depraved desire of having, yet by a not unjust right of possessing. Most justly, then, he is compelled to release those who believe in him whom he most unjustly slew; so that both, in that they die in time, they may pay their debt, and, in that they live forever, they may live in him who paid for them what he did not owe. But those whom he had persuaded to persevere in unbelief, he would justly have as companions with himself in everlasting damnation. So it came about that the human being was not snatched from the devil by force, whom the devil had captured not by force but by persuasion; and that the one who was justly the more humbled, so as to serve him to whom he had consented unto evil, was justly set free through him to whom he consented unto good; because this one sinned less in consenting than that one in evilly persuading.

**3. 11. 32.** All natures, then, God made—not only those that would abide in virtue and justice, but also those that would sin—not that they might sin, but that they might adorn the universe, whether they willed to sin or not to sin. For if there were lacking to things those souls which so hold the very pinnacle of order in the whole creation that, if they willed to sin, the whole would be weakened and shaken, something great would be lacking to creation: for there would be lacking that, with which removed, the stability of things and their connection would be disturbed. Such are the best, and holy, and lofty creatures of the heavenly or supercelestial powers, whom God alone commands, while the whole world is subject to them. Without their just and perfect services the whole cannot exist. Likewise, if there were lacking those which, whether they sinned or did not sin, would diminish nothing of the order of the whole, even so very much would be lacking. For these are rational souls, unequal indeed in office to those higher ones, but equal in nature; and below these still there are many, yet praiseworthy, grades of things appointed by the highest God.

**3. 11. 33.** That nature, then, is of a loftier office which would diminish the order of the whole not only if it did not exist, but even if it sinned. Of a lower office is this one, which only if it did not exist, but not if it sinned, the whole would have something less. To the former is given the power of holding all things together by its proper office, which cannot be lacking to the order of things: nor does it abide in a good will because it received this office, but it received it because it was foreseen by him who gave it that it would abide. Yet it does not hold all things together by its own majesty, but by clinging to his majesty, and by most devotedly obeying the commands of him by whom and through whom and in whom all things were made. To the latter, indeed, when it does not sin, is given the most powerful office of holding all things together; yet not as its own, but together with that former one, as to one foreknown as going to sin. Spiritual things, surely, have among themselves both a conjunction without increase, and a separation without diminution: so that the former is not aided in the ease of its action when the latter is joined to it, nor is its action made more difficult if the latter forsakes its office by sinning. For spiritual creatures can be joined not by places and the mass of bodies, but by likeness of affections, and separated by unlikeness, although they each possess their own bodies.

**3. 11. 34.** But ordered, after sin, among inferior and mortal bodies, the soul governs its body—not altogether at its own pleasure, but as the laws of the whole allow. Nor is such a soul on that account inferior to a heavenly body, to which body even earthly bodies are subject. For the ragged garment of a condemned slave is far inferior to the garment of one well deserving and set in great honor before his master; but the slave himself is better than any precious garment, because he is a human being. That former soul, then, clings to God, and, in a heavenly body, by angelic power, adorns and governs even an earthly body, as he commands whose beck it beholds ineffably. But this latter soul, burdened with mortal members, scarcely administers within the very thing by which it is pressed, and yet adorns it as far as it can; but the other things lying about it outwardly, by a far weaker working, it affects from without as it can.

**3. 12. 35.** Whence it is gathered that there would not have been lacking a most fitting adornment to the lowest corporeal creature, even if this soul had been unwilling to sin. For that which can govern the whole governs also a part; but that which can do less cannot at once do greater things. For a perfect physician heals even the itch effectively; but it does not at once follow that one who usefully attends to a person with the itch can heal the whole of human health. And if it is discerned by sure reason—by which it becomes plain that there ought to be a creature which has never sinned, and which never will sin—the same reason also reports that that creature refrains from sin by a free will, and does not refrain compelled, but of its own accord. Nevertheless, even if it sinned (although it did not sin, as God foreknew it would not sin)—yet even if it itself sinned, the power of God's ineffable might would suffice for the governing of this universe, so that, rendering to all things fitting and worthy returns, he would allow nothing in his whole dominion to be base and unseemly. For whether, through no powers founded for this very purpose—if the whole angelic nature had failed from his precepts by sinning—he would by his own majesty most fittingly and best govern all things; nor would he so begrudge the spiritual creature to exist, he who founded even the corporeal one, far inferior even to sinning spiritual creatures, with such lavishness of goodness that there is no one who reasonably beholds the sky and the earth, and all visible natures, measured, formed, and ordered in their kinds, who would either believe that the maker of all things is any other than God, or not confess that he is to be ineffably praised. Or whether there is no better ordering of things unless the angelic power excels in the disposition of the universe by the excellence of its nature and the goodness of its will—even if all the angels had sinned, they would cause no lack to the Creator of the angels for governing his dominion. For neither would his goodness fail, as from some weariness, nor his omnipotence from difficulty, in creating others, whom he would set in those seats which others had forsaken by sinning; nor could a spiritual creature, of however great a number, if it were condemned according to its merits, straiten the order which fittingly and becomingly receives whatever are to be condemned. Wherever, then, our consideration turns itself, it finds God ineffably to be praised, the best founder of all natures, and the most just administrator.

**3. 12. 36.** Finally, that we may leave the contemplation of the beauty of things to those who can see it by the divine gift, and not try to lead them by words to behold things ineffable, yet, on account of talkative or weak or treacherous people, let us go through so great a question with a most brief summary.

**3. 13. 36.** Every nature that can become less good is good; and every nature, while it is corrupted, becomes less good. For either corruption does not harm it, and it is not corrupted; or, if it is corrupted, corruption harms it; and if it harms it, it diminishes something of its good, and makes it less good. For if it utterly deprives it of all good, whatever of it remains will then be unable to be corrupted, because there will be no good by the taking away of which corruption could harm it; but that which corruption cannot harm is not corrupted. Now a nature that is not corrupted is incorruptible: therefore a nature will be—which is most absurd to say—made incorruptible by corruption. Wherefore it is most truly said that every nature, insofar as it is a nature, is good: for if it is incorruptible, it is better than the corruptible; but if it is corruptible, since, while it is corrupted, it becomes less good, it is without doubt good. But every nature is either corruptible or incorruptible. Every nature, therefore, is good. I call nature that which is also wont to be called substance. Every substance, therefore, is either God or from God; because every good is either God or from God.

**3. 13. 37.** These things being settled and established, as at the head of our reasoning, attend to what I say. Every rational nature, founded with free choice of the will, if it abides in the enjoyment of the highest and unchangeable good, is beyond doubt praiseworthy; and every one that strives to abide is itself also praiseworthy; but every one that does not abide in it, and does not will to act so as to abide, insofar as it is not there, and insofar as it does not act so as to be there, is blameworthy. If, then, a rational nature that has been made is praised, no one doubts that the one who made it is to be praised; and if it is blamed, no one doubts that its Founder is praised in its very blaming. For when we blame this nature because it does not will to enjoy the highest and unchangeable good, that is, its Creator, we without any doubt praise him. How great a good, then, and how—whether ineffably by all tongues, or ineffably by all thoughts—is the Creator of all, God, to be proclaimed and honored, without whose praise we can neither be praised nor blamed! For we cannot be blamed for not abiding in him, except because it is our great and highest and first good to abide in him; and whence is this, except because he is the ineffable good? What, then, can be found in our sins for which he should be blamed, since the blame of our sins is nothing unless he is praised?

**3. 13. 38.** What of this—that even in the very things that are blamed, nothing is blamed but the fault? But no thing's fault is blamed except where the nature is praised. For either what you blame is according to nature, and is no fault, and you rather are to be amended, that you may know how to blame rightly, than that which you wrongly blame; or, if it is a fault, so that it can rightly be blamed, it must also be against nature. For every fault, by the very fact that it is a fault, is against nature. For if it does not harm the nature, it is no fault; but if, because it harms it, it is therefore a fault, then it is a fault because it is against nature. But if some nature is corrupted not by its own fault but by another's, it is unjustly blamed; and it must be asked whether that nature is not corrupted by its own fault, by whose fault the other nature could be corrupted. But what else is it to be made faulty than to be corrupted by a fault? Now a nature that is not made faulty lacks fault; but that by whose fault another nature is corrupted surely has a fault. The former, then, is the faulty one, and is first corrupted by its own fault, by whose fault the other too can be corrupted. From which it is gathered that every fault is against nature, even of that thing whose fault it is. Wherefore, since in any thing whatever nothing is blamed but the fault, and it is a fault for this reason, because it is against the nature of that thing whose fault it is, no thing's fault is rightly blamed except one whose nature is praised. For a fault rightly displeases you only because it spoils what pleases you in the nature.

**3. 14. 39.** We must also see this: whether it is truly said that some nature is corrupted by the fault of another nature, with no fault of its own joined to it. For if the nature that comes, with its own fault, to corrupt another, finds nothing corruptible in it, it does not corrupt it. But if it finds it, it works its corruption with the other's fault joined. For a more powerful nature, if it is unwilling to be corrupted by a weaker, is not corrupted; but if it is willing, it begins to be corrupted first by its own fault rather than by another's. And a nature equal to an equal, no less, if it is unwilling to be corrupted, cannot be. For whatever nature comes with a fault to one that is without fault, in order to corrupt it, by that very fact does not come as an equal, but as weaker by its own fault. But if a more powerful one corrupts a weaker, it comes about either by the fault of both, if it is done by the depraved desire of both; or by the fault of the more powerful, if its nature is of such excellence that, even faulty, it surpasses the inferior one which it corrupts. For who would rightly blame the fruits of the earth because people do not use them well, and, corrupted by their own fault, corrupt them by abusing them to luxury, since it would be the mark of a madman to doubt that the nature of a human being, even faulty, is more excellent and more powerful than any fruits, however unfaulty?

**3. 14. 40.** It can also come about that a more powerful nature corrupts some inferior one, and that this happens by the fault of neither of them: since we call a fault that which is worthy of blame. But who would dare to blame either a frugal man who seeks nothing from the fruits but the supplying of his nature, or those same fruits because they are corrupted by the use of his food? For such corruption is not even usually called corruption, since corruption is most often the name of a fault. For this too can easily be observed in things—that for the most part a more powerful nature corrupts an inferior one for no use of supplying its own want, but by the order of justice, while it punishes a fault; from which rule the Apostle said, *If anyone corrupts the temple of God, God will corrupt him* [^7]—or by the order of changeable things yielding to one another according to the most fitting laws given for the strength of each part of the whole. For if the sun corrupts by its brightness the eyes of someone too weak, by the measure of their nature, to bear its light, neither is it to be thought to do this to supply its own want of light, nor to do it by any fault of its own; nor at least are the eyes themselves to be blamed, because they both yielded to their owner so as to be opened against the light, and to the light itself so as to be corrupted. Of all corruptions, then, only that which is faulty is rightly blamed; but the others either are not even to be called corruptions, or surely, because they are not faulty, cannot be worthy of blame. For blame itself, because it is prepared, that is, fitted and due, for a fault alone, is believed to have drawn its name from this, that it should be called vituperation [blame of a vice].

**3. 14. 41.** But a fault, as I had begun to say, is evil from no other source than that it is contrary to the nature of that very thing whose fault it is. Whence it is plain that this same thing whose fault is blamed is praiseworthy by nature; so that we confess that this very blaming of faults is the praise of the natures—namely, of those whose faults are blamed. For because a fault is contrary to nature, just so much is added to the malice of faults as is taken from the integrity of natures. When, therefore, you blame a fault, you surely praise that whose integrity you desire; and of what, if not of the nature? For a perfect nature is worthy not only of no blame, but even of praise in its kind. What, then, you perceive to be lacking to the perfection of a nature, that you call a fault, testifying that the nature pleases you well enough, since you would wish it perfect by your blame of its imperfection.

**3. 15. 42.** If, then, the blaming of faults commends the comeliness and dignity of the very natures whose faults they are, how much more is God, the founder of all natures, to be praised even in their faults; since both they have this from him, that they are natures, and they are faulty in just so far as they depart from the art by which they were made, and are rightly blamed in just so far as their blamer sees the art by which they were made, so that he blames in them that which he does not see there! And if that art itself, through which all things were made—that is, the highest and unchangeable Wisdom of God—truly and supremely is, as indeed it is, look to where whatever departs from it tends. Yet that defect would not be worthy of blame, unless it were voluntary. For attend, I beg, whether you rightly blame what is as it ought to be: I think not; but rather what is not as it ought to be. But no one owes what he has not received; and whoever owes, to whom does he owe but to him from whom he received what he should owe? For things too that are paid back by inheritance are paid back to him who bequeathed them. And what is paid back to the just successors of creditors is surely paid back to those very ones whom these rightly succeed. Otherwise it must be called not repayment, but ceding or losing, or whatever else of the kind. Wherefore it is most absurd to say that all temporal things, which are so placed in this order of things that, unless they fail, the future cannot succeed the past—so that the whole beauty of the ages may be completed in its kind—ought not to fail. For as much as they have received, so much they do, and so much they pay back to him to whom they owe what they are, in whatever measure they are. He who grieves that these things fail ought to attend to his own speech—that very speech, surely, in which he complains of these things, if he judges it to be just and proceeding from prudence; of which speech, as far as concerns its sound, if anyone should love one little particle, and be unwilling that it should give place by failing to the rest, by whose departing and succeeding the whole speech is woven together, he would be judged of marvelous madness.

**3. 15. 43.** In those things, then, which fail for this reason, because they did not receive being further, so that all things may be accomplished in their own times, no one rightly blames the failing; because no one can say, It ought to have remained, when it could not pass beyond the bounds it received. But in rational creatures, by whom—whether sinning or not sinning—the universal beauty is bounded in most fitting ways: either there are no sins (which it is most absurd to say, for he sins at least who even condemns as sins things that are no sins); or sins are not to be blamed (which is no less absurd; for then even right deeds will begin not to be praised, and the whole intention of the human mind will be disturbed, and life overthrown); or a deed will be blamed that was done as it ought to have been done (and an execrable madness, or, to speak more mildly, a most wretched error will arise); or, if most true reason compels, as it does, both that sins be blamed, and that whatever is rightly blamed be blamed for this reason, because it is not as it ought to be—seek what a sinful nature owes, and you will find a right deed; seek to whom it owes it, and you will find God. For from him from whom it received the power to do right when it wills, from him it received this also: that it be wretched if it has not done so, and blessed if it has.

**3. 15. 44.** For since no one surpasses the laws of the almighty Creator, the soul is not allowed not to pay its debt. For either it pays back by using well what it received, or it pays back by losing what it was unwilling to use well. So, if it does not pay back by doing justice, it will pay back by suffering wretchedness; because in both that word of debt sounds. For this too could be said, in what was said: If it will not pay back by doing what it ought, it will pay back by suffering what it ought. Nor are these divided by any interval of time, so that, as it were, at one time it does not do what it ought, and at another suffers what it ought, lest even for a point of time the universal beauty be defiled, with the dishonor of sin being in it without the comeliness of vengeance. But whatever is now most secretly avenged is reserved for the future judgment, for the manifestation and most keen sense of wretchedness. For just as one who does not keep awake sleeps, so whoever does not do what he ought suffers, without interval, what he ought; since the blessedness of justice is so great that no one can depart from it except into wretchedness. In all defects, then, either those things which fail did not receive being further, and there is no fault—as also, when they exist, because they did not receive more being than they have, there is nonetheless no fault; or they are unwilling to be what, if they willed, they received being to be; and because it is good, there is guilt if they are unwilling.

**3. 16. 45.** But God owes nothing to anyone, because he bestows all things freely. And if anyone says that something is owed him by his own merits, surely, that he should exist was not owed him; for there was no one to whom it could be owed. And yet, what merit is it to be turned to him from whom you are, that from him you may even be better—you who have it from him that you are? What, then, do you advance to him beforehand, so as to demand it as a debt; since, if you were unwilling to be turned to him, nothing would be lacking to him, but to you he himself, without whom you would be nothing, and from whom you are something in such a way that, unless by turning yourself to him you pay back to him that you are from him, you will indeed not be nothing, but will nevertheless be wretched? All things, then, owe to him, first, whatever they are, insofar as they are natures; then, whatever they can be better, if they will, of those things which they received that they should will; and whatever they ought to be. From that, then, which one did not receive, no one is guilty; but from that which one does not do that he ought, he is justly guilty. And he owes, if he has received both a free will and a most sufficient capacity.

**3. 16. 46.** When someone fails to do what he ought, the Creator is so far from being at fault that he deserves praise, because that person suffers what he ought; and in the very fact that he is blamed for not doing what he ought, the one to whom he owes it is only praised. For if you are praised for seeing what you ought to do, although you see it only in him who is unchangeable Truth, how much more should he be praised who both commanded you to will it, supplied the ability, and did not permit unwillingness to go unpunished? For if everyone owes what he has received, and a human being was made so that he necessarily sins, he owes it to sin. Therefore, when he sins, he does what he ought. Since it is impious to say this, no one’s own nature compels him to sin. Nor does another’s nature. For a person does not sin simply by suffering what he does not will. If he suffers justly, he does not sin in suffering unwillingly; rather, he sinned by voluntarily acting in such a way that he justly suffers what he did not will. But if he suffers unjustly, how does he sin? For sin consists not in suffering injustice but in doing injustice. If, then, neither one’s own nature nor another’s compels one to sin, it remains that one sins by one’s own will. If you wish to attribute this to the Creator, you will exonerate the sinner, who has done nothing beyond what his Creator established; but if he is rightly defended, he has not sinned. There is therefore nothing for you to attribute to the Creator. Let us praise the Creator, then, if the sinner can be defended; let us praise him if he cannot. For if he is rightly defended, he is not a sinner: therefore praise the Creator. But if he cannot be defended, he is a sinner insofar as he turns away from the Creator: therefore praise the Creator. Thus I find no way—and affirm that none can be found, and that there simply is none—in which our sins may be attributed to God our Creator; for even in them I find him worthy of praise, not only because he punishes them but also because they occur when one departs from his truth.  
Evodius: I receive these things most gladly, and approve them; and I agree that it is altogether true that in no way can our sins rightly be attributed to our Creator.

**3. 17. 47.** But yet I would like to know, if it could be done, why that nature does not sin which God foreknew would not sin, and why this one sins which was foreseen by him as going to sin. For I no longer think that, by the very foreknowledge of God, either this one is compelled to sin or that one not to sin. But yet, if there were no cause, the rational creature would not be so divided that one never sins, another perseveres in sinning, and another, as it were midway between both, sometimes sins, sometimes turns to doing right. What cause distributes it into these three parts? But I do not want to be answered, "The will": for I am seeking the cause of the will itself. For not without cause does that one never will to sin, this one never not will, while certain ones sometimes will, sometimes not will, though all are of the same kind. This alone I seem to see: that there is not without cause this threefold will of the rational creature; but what the cause is, I do not know.

**3. 17. 48.** Augustine: Since the will is the cause of sin, and you are inquiring into the cause of the will itself—if I should be able to find this, will you not then be going to seek the cause also of that cause which has been found? And what will be the measure of seeking, what the end of inquiring and discussing, since you ought to seek nothing beyond the root? For beware lest you think anything could be said more truly than what was said—that *the root of all evils is greed* [^8], that is, to wish more than is enough. But enough is as much as the measure of preserving each nature in its own kind requires. For greed, which in Greek is called philargyria, is to be understood not in silver or coins alone—from which it sounds as if it took its name the more, for among the ancients coins were more often made of silver or of mixed silver—but in all things that are immoderately desired, wherever, in general, anyone wishes more than is enough. And this greed is desire; but desire is a wicked will. Therefore a wicked will is the cause of all evils. If it were according to nature, it would surely preserve nature, and would not be destructive to it, and so it would not be wicked. From which it is gathered that the root of all evils is not according to nature; which is enough against all who wish to accuse natures. But if you seek the cause of this root, how will it be the root of all evils? For that will be the root which is the cause of this; and when you have found it, as I said, you will seek the cause of it too, and there will be no measure of seeking.

**3. 17. 49.** But what cause of the will can there finally be, prior to the will? For either it is itself a will, and the root of this will is not departed from; or it is not a will, and it has no sin. Either, then, the will itself is the first cause of sinning, or no sin is the first cause of sinning. Nor is there anyone to whom sin is rightly imputed, except the sinner. There is not, therefore, anyone to whom it is rightly imputed except one who wills; but I do not know why it pleases you to seek anything further. Then, whatever that cause of the will is, it is surely either just or unjust. If just, whoever obeys it will not sin; if unjust, let him not obey it, and he will not sin.

**3. 18. 50.** Is it perhaps violent, and does it compel the unwilling? Shall we so often go over the same things? Recall what was said above—so many things by us about sin and the free will. But if it is laborious to commit all things to memory, hold this most brief point. Whatever that cause of the will is, if it cannot be resisted, it is yielded to without sin; but if it can be, let it not be yielded to, and there will be no sin. Does it perhaps deceive the unwary? Then let him beware lest he be deceived. Or is the deception so great that it cannot be guarded against at all? If so, there are no sins. For who sins in that which can in no way be guarded against? But there is sinning: therefore it can be guarded against.

**3. 18. 51.** And yet certain things done even through ignorance are disapproved, and judged to need correction, as we read in the divine authorities; for the Apostle says, *I obtained mercy because I did it ignorantly* [^9]; and the prophet says, *Remember not the sins of my youth and my ignorances* [^10]. There are also things done of necessity that are to be disapproved, where a person wills to do right and cannot: for whence are those words, *For I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do* [^11]; and that, *To will is present with me, but to accomplish the good I find not* [^12]; and that, *The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other, so that you do not do the things you would* [^13]? But all these belong to people coming from that condemnation of death; for if this is not the punishment of a human being but his nature, then these are no sins. For if a person does not depart from that mode in which he was naturally made, so that he cannot be better, then he does what he ought when he does these things. But if a person were good, he would be otherwise; but now, because he is so, he is not good, nor does he have it in his power to be good—whether by not seeing what kind he ought to be, or by seeing and not being able to be what he sees he ought to be: who can doubt that this is a punishment? But every punishment, if it is just, is the punishment of sin, and is called a penalty; but if the punishment is unjust—since no one doubts it is a punishment—it was imposed on the human being by some unjust ruler. Furthermore, since to doubt about the omnipotence and justice of God is the mark of a madman, this punishment is just, and is paid for some sin. For no unjust ruler could either steal a human being away, as though God did not know; or wrest him away against his will, as though weaker, by terrifying or fighting, so as to torment a human being with an unjust punishment. It remains, then, that this just punishment comes from the condemnation of the human being.

**3. 18. 52.** Nor is it to be wondered at that a person, either through ignorance, does not have free choice of the will to choose what he may rightly do, or, with carnal habit resisting—which by the violence of mortal succession has, as it were, grown naturally ingrained—sees what is rightly to be done, and wills it, and cannot accomplish it. For that is the most just penalty of sin: that each lose what he was unwilling to use well, when he could have used it without any difficulty if he had willed. That is to say: that one who, knowing, does not do right, loses the knowing of what is right; and one who, when he could, was unwilling to do right, loses the power when he wills. For there are in truth two penal things for every sinning soul, ignorance and difficulty. From ignorance, error dishonors; from difficulty, torment afflicts. But to approve false things as true, so that one errs against his will, and, with the pain of the carnal bond resisting and tormenting, not to be able to refrain from lustful works, is not the nature of the human being as he was instituted, but the punishment of one condemned. But when we speak of the free will of doing right, we are surely speaking of that in which the human being was made.

**3. 19. 53.** Here arises that question which people, murmuring among themselves, are wont to gnaw at, who are ready to accuse anything else whatever in sinning rather than themselves. For they say: If Adam and Eve sinned, what did we wretches do, that we should be born with the blindness of ignorance and the torments of difficulty, and first should err, not knowing what we ought to do; then, when the precepts of justice begin to be opened to us, should will to do them, and, with some necessity of carnal concupiscence holding us back, not be able? To whom it is briefly answered, that they should be quiet, and cease to murmur against God. For perhaps they would rightly complain, if no human being existed as conqueror of error and lust; but since he is everywhere present who in many ways, through the creature serving him as Lord, calls back the one turned away, teaches the believer, consoles the hoper, exhorts the lover, helps the one striving, hears the one praying—it is not reckoned to you as a fault that you are unwillingly ignorant, but that you neglect to seek what you do not know; nor that you do not bind up your wounded members, but that you despise him who wills to heal you: these are your own proper sins. For to no human being is it taken away to know that it is useful to seek what it is useless to be ignorant of, and that one's weakness is to be humbly confessed, so that he who neither errs while he helps, nor labors, may come to the aid of the one seeking and confessing.

**3. 19. 54.** For that which anyone does not rightly through ignorance, and that which, willing to do rightly, he cannot do, are called sins because they take their origin from that sin of the free will: for that preceding sin merited these as its consequences. For just as we call "tongue" not only the member that we move in the mouth when we speak, but also that which follows the motion of this member—that is, the form and tenor of words, according to which manner one tongue is called Greek, another Latin—so we call sin not only that which is properly called sin, for it is committed by a free will and by one who knows, but also that which now must necessarily follow from the punishment of this. So too we speak of nature in one way when we speak properly of the nature of the human being, in which he was first made blameless in his kind; in another way of this nature, in which, from the punishment of that condemned man, we are born mortal and ignorant and subject to the flesh; according to which manner the Apostle says, *For we too were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest* [^14].

**3. 20. 55.** But that we are born from that first marriage with ignorance, and with difficulty, and with mortality, since they, when they had sinned, were cast headlong into error and into hardship and into death—it most justly pleased the highest God, the moderator of things, that both in the origin of the human being the justice of the one punishing might appear, and in his advancement the mercy of the one delivering. For blessedness was not so taken from the first man condemned that fruitfulness too was taken away. For there could be made, even from his offspring, though carnal and mortal, some adornment and ornament of the earth in its own kind. But that he should beget better than he himself was, was not of equity; but that, by conversion to God, each might overcome the punishment which his origin had merited by aversion, it behooved that the willing one be not only not hindered but even helped; for so too the Creator of things showed with how great ease the human being could, if he had willed, have retained what he was made, since his offspring could even surpass what it was born.

**3. 20. 56.** Then, if one soul was made, from which the souls of all who are born are drawn, who can say that he did not sin when that first one sinned? But if souls are made singly in each one who is born, then it is not perverse—rather, it appears most fitting and most ordered—that the evil desert of the prior be the nature of the one that follows, and the good desert of the one that follows be the nature of the prior. For what unworthy thing is there, if the Creator willed even thus to show that the dignity of the soul so excels corporeal creatures that one soul can have its rise from that grade to which another was brought down at its fall? For when that sinful soul comes to ignorance and difficulty, it is rightly called punishment, because it was better before this punishment. If, then, another begins to be such, not only before sin, but before all its own life, as the other was made after a blameworthy life, it has no small good for which to give thanks to its Creator; because even its very rise and beginning is better than any perfect body. For these are no mean goods—not only that it is a soul, by which nature it already precedes every body, but also that it has the capacity, by the help of the Creator, to cultivate itself, and by pious zeal to acquire and grasp all the virtues, through which it may be freed both from tormenting difficulty and from blinding ignorance. If this is so, ignorance and difficulty will not be, for souls being born, the punishment of sin, but the prompting to advance and the beginning of perfection. For it is no small thing, before any merit of good work, to have received the natural judgment by which one prefers wisdom to error, and rest to difficulty, so that to these it may come not by being born, but by being zealous. But if it is unwilling to do this, it will rightly be held guilty of sin, as one that did not use well the capacity it received. For although it was born in ignorance and difficulty, it is nevertheless compressed by no necessity to remain in that in which it was born: nor at all could there be any maker of such souls but God almighty, who makes them even when not loved by himself, and remakes them when loving them; who both grants to things not existing that they exist, and grants to those loving him from whom they are that they be blessed.

**3. 20. 57.** But if souls, already existing somewhere in some secret place of God, are sent to inspire and govern the bodies of each who is born, then they are sent surely for this office: that the body, which is born of the punishment of sin—that is, of the mortality of the first man—they may, by administering it well, that is, by chastising it through the virtues and subjecting it to a most ordered and lawful servitude, prepare for themselves also, in due order and at the opportune time, a place of heavenly incorruption. When these souls enter into this life and undergo the mortal members to be borne, they must of necessity also undergo both forgetfulness of their prior life and the labor of the present; from which will follow that ignorance and difficulty, which in the first man was the punishment of mortality, for the paying out of the soul's wretchedness, but in these is the door of ministry for restoring the incorruption of the body. For in this way too these are not called sins, except because the flesh, coming from the offspring of the sinner, causes this ignorance and difficulty to the souls coming to it; which is to be attributed as blame neither to these souls nor to the Creator. For he gave both the capacity for working well in laborious offices, and the way of faith in the blindness of forgetfulness; and that judgment above all, by which every soul both grants that it must seek what it uselessly does not know, and that it must perseveringly strive in dutiful labors to overcome the difficulty of doing right, and must implore the aid of the Creator that he help the one striving; who has commanded—whether outwardly by the law, or in the inmost places of the heart by his address—that one must strive, and prepares the glory of the most blessed city for those who triumph over him who, by most wicked persuasion, led the first man, conquered, to this wretchedness; which wretchedness these undertake, by the best faith, in order to conquer him. For it is no small glory of warfare to conquer the devil by the very punishment undertaken, by which he boasts to have led the conquered man. But whoever, captured by love of this life, neglects this, will in no way justly attribute the disgrace of his desertion to the command of the king of all; but he will rather be ordered, under the Lord of all, in the ranks of that one whose foul wages, that he might desert his own camp, he came to love.

**3. 20. 58.** But if souls, established elsewhere, are not sent by the Lord God, but of their own accord come to inhabit bodies, then it is now easy to see that whatever ignorance and difficulty has followed their own will, the Creator is in no way to be blamed for it; since, even if he himself had sent them—he who, even in that very ignorance and difficulty, did not take away the free will of asking and seeking and striving, being about to give to those who ask, to show to those who seek, to open to those who knock—he would be altogether beyond fault. For he would grant that this ignorance and difficulty avail, for the zealous and well-disposed who overcome it, unto a crown of glory; but to the negligent, and those wishing to defend their sins by their weakness, he would not cast in their teeth the ignorance and difficulty itself as a crime; but, because they preferred to remain in these rather than to come to truth and ease by the zeal of seeking and learning, and by the humility of confessing and praying, he would avenge it with a just punishment.

**3. 21. 59.** But of these four opinions about the soul—whether they come from the offspring, or are made new in each who is born, or, already existing somewhere, are either sent divinely into the bodies of those being born, or slip into them of their own accord—none ought to be rashly affirmed. For either this question has not yet been unfolded and made clear by the catholic treaters of the divine books, according to the desert of its obscurity and perplexity; or, if it has already been done, writings of this kind have not yet come into our hands. Only let there be present a faith of thinking nothing false and unworthy about the substance of the Creator. For toward him we tend by the journey of piety. If, then, we should think otherwise about him than is true, our intention would compel us to go not into blessedness but into vanity. But about the creature, if we should think anything otherwise than it is, provided we do not hold it as known and grasped, there is no danger. For we are not bidden to tend toward the creature in order to become blessed, but toward the Creator himself; about whom, if we are persuaded of anything other than is fitting and than the matter stands, we are deceived by a most pernicious error. For by going toward that which either does not exist, or, if it exists, does not make us blessed, no one can come to the blessed life.

**3. 21. 60.** But for contemplating the eternity of truth, that we may be able fully to enjoy it and to cling to it, a way has been provided for our weakness out of temporal things, so that, as far as suffices for the journey of those tending toward the eternal, we may believe things past and future. This discipline of faith, that it may prevail by authority, is governed by the divine mercy. But present things, as far as concerns the creature, are perceived, as it were, passing, in the mobility and changeableness of body and soul. Of these, whatever we do not experience, we cannot hold by any kind of knowledge. Whatever, therefore, is narrated to us about any creatures whatever, whether past or future, on divine authority to be believed, although partly it passed away before we could perceive it, and partly has not yet come into our senses—nevertheless, because it avails much for strengthening our hope and exhorting our love, while it commends to us, through the most ordered series of times, that God does not neglect our deliverance, it is to be believed without any doubt. But whatever error takes upon itself the person of divine authority is refuted especially by this reasoning: if it is proved to believe or affirm either some changeable appearance besides the creature of God, or some changeable appearance in the substance of God, and contends that the substance of God is either more or less than the Trinity; to the pious and sober understanding of which Trinity all Christian watchfulness keeps vigil, and to it all its advancement is directed. Of the unity and equality of that Trinity, and of a certain property of the several persons in it, this is not the place to discuss. For to mention certain things about the Lord God, the author and former and orderer of all things, which pertain to a most wholesome faith, and by which the intention, suckling and beginning to lift itself from earthly things to heavenly, may be usefully supported, both is most easy to do and has been done by very many already; but to treat this whole matter through, and so to handle the question that to clear reason, as far as is given in this life, all human understanding may be subjected, can seem easy and ready to be undertaken, not only in speech, but not even in thought, by any human being—or at least by us. Now, then, that we may accomplish what we set out to do, as far as we are helped and as far as we are permitted: whatever, as far as concerns the creature, is either narrated to us as past or foretold as future, which may avail to commend an entire religion, by rousing us to a most sincere love of God and of neighbor, is to be believed without doubt; but it is to be defended against unbelievers only so far that either by the weight of authority their unbelief may be crushed, or it may be shown to them, as far as can be, first how it is not foolish to believe such things, then how foolish it is not to believe them. But false teaching is to be refuted, not so much about past and future things as about present things, and especially about unchangeable things, and, as far as is given, to be convicted by clear reason.

**3. 21. 61.** Indeed, in the series of temporal things, the expectation of future things is to be preferred to the inquiry into things past: since even in the divine books, those things that are narrated as past bear before themselves either a prefiguration of things to come, or a promise, or a testimony. And in truth, even in those things that pertain to this life, in prosperity and adversity, each person is not much concerned what he was; but upon what is hoped to be future, the whole tide of his cares gathers itself. For by some inmost and natural sense, the things that have happened to us, because they are done with, are held, as to the moment of happiness and wretchedness, as if they had never happened. What, then, does it harm me, if I do not know when I began to be, when I know that I am, and do not despair that I shall be? For I do not attend to the past, so as to fear, as a most pernicious error, if I should think otherwise about it than it was; but toward that which I am to be, I direct my course, with the mercy of my Creator for guide. About this, then, which I am to be, and about him with whom I am to be, if I believe or think otherwise than the truth holds, the error is vehemently to be guarded against; lest I either not prepare the necessary things, or not be able to come to the very end of my purpose, while one thing seems to me instead of another. Wherefore, just as for procuring a garment it would not harm me if I had forgotten the past winter, but it would harm me if I did not believe the future cold to be threatening: so it will in no way harm my soul, if it has forgotten what perchance it has borne, provided only that it diligently attend to and hold to what it is admonished to prepare itself for next. As, for example, it would not hurt one sailing to Rome if it had slipped from his mind from what shore he loosed the ship, provided only that, from the place where he was, he did not fail to know whither to direct the prow; but it would profit him nothing to remember the shore from which he began his journey, if, thinking something false about the Roman port, he had fallen upon the rocks: so neither, if I do not hold the beginning of the time of my life, will it harm me at all, knowing the end in which I shall rest; nor would that memory or conjecture of the inchoate life profit anything, if, thinking otherwise than is worthy about God himself, who is the one end of the labors of the soul, I should rush upon the reefs of error.

**3. 21. 62.** Nor let this discourse avail so that anyone should think we forbid those who can to inquire, according to the divinely inspired Scriptures, whether the soul is propagated from the soul, or is made singly in each living thing, or, sent by the divine command from somewhere to rule and animate the body, or insinuating itself by its own will; if either the reasoning of some necessary question to be unraveled demands that these things be considered and discussed, or leisure from more necessary matters is granted for seeking and disputing these. But I have said these things rather for this: that no one should, in such a matter, either rashly grow angry at one who does not yield to his own opinion, perhaps with a more human doubting; or even, if anyone should grasp something certain and clear here, think on that account that another has lost hope of future things, because he does not recall the beginnings of his past.

**3. 22. 63.** But in whatever way this stands—whether it is to be altogether omitted, or now deferred and considered at another time—the present question is not hindered from making it appear that, by the most whole and most just and unshaken and unchangeable majesty and substance of the Creator, souls pay the penalties of their own sins; which sins, as we have long been discussing, are to be attributed only to their own will, and no further cause of sins is to be sought.

**3. 22. 64.** But ignorance and difficulty, if they are natural, the soul begins from them to advance, and to be promoted toward knowledge and rest, until the blessed life is perfected in it. If it neglects, by its own will, this advance in the best pursuits and in piety, the capacity for which has not been denied it, it is justly cast headlong into a graver ignorance and difficulty, which is now penal, being ordered among lower things by a most becoming and most fitting governance of things. For not that which it does not know naturally, and cannot do naturally, is reckoned to the soul as guilt; but that it did not study to know, and that it did not give worthy effort to acquire the ease for doing right. For not to know how to speak, and not to be able to, is natural to an infant; and this ignorance and difficulty of speech is not only blameless under the laws of grammarians, but even charming and pleasing to human affections; for it neglected to acquire that faculty by no fault, nor lost by any fault what it had acquired. Therefore, if our blessedness were set in eloquence, and it were imputed as a crime when one sins in the sounds of the tongue, as when one sins in the acts of life, no one surely would be charged with his infancy, because from it he set out toward attaining eloquence; but plainly he would deservedly be condemned if, by the perversity of his own will, he had either slipped back into it or remained in it. So too now, if ignorance of the true and difficulty of the right is natural to a human being, from which he begins to rise into the blessedness of wisdom and rest, no one rightly charges him with it from his natural beginning; but if he is unwilling to advance, or wills to slip back from his advance, he will justly and deservedly pay the penalties.

**3. 22. 65.** But its Creator is everywhere praised—either because he has begun it, from its very beginnings, with a capacity for the highest good; or because he aids its advance; or because he fills and perfects the one advancing; or because, the one sinning—that is, either refusing to lift itself from its beginnings to perfection, or already slipping back from some advance—he orders by a most just condemnation according to its deserts. For he did not create it evil because it is not yet so great as it received the power to be by advancing; since all the perfections of bodies are far inferior to its beginning, which whoever judges most soundly about things judges to be praiseworthy in their kind. That, then, it does not know what it ought to do comes from this: that it has not yet received it; but this too it will receive, if it has used well that which it has received. And it has received that it should piously and diligently seek, if it will. But that, recognizing what it ought to do, it cannot at once accomplish it—this too it has not yet received: for a certain loftier part of it went before, to perceive the good of the right deed; but a certain slower and carnal part is not consequently led into the same view; so that, from this very difficulty, it may be admonished to implore the helper of its perfection, the same whom it feels to be the author of its beginning; so that from this he may become dearer to it, while it is lifted up, not by its own powers, but by the mercy of him by whose goodness it has that it exists, to be blessed. But the dearer he is to it from whom it is, the more firmly it rests in him, and the more fully it enjoys his eternity. For if we in no way rightly call sterile the new and tender shoot of a tree, though it passes some summers without fruit, until at the opportune time it puts forth its fruitfulness, why should not the author of the soul be praised with due piety, if he has granted it such a beginning that, by being zealous and advancing, it may come to the fruit of wisdom and justice, and has bestowed on it so great a dignity that he has set it even within its own power, if it will, to tend toward blessedness?

**3. 23. 66.** But against this discussion a certain cavil is wont to be raised by the unskilled, concerning the deaths of little children, and concerning certain torments of the body by which we often see them afflicted. For they say: What need was there that one should be born who departed from life before he had entered upon any merit of life? Or how will he be reckoned in the future judgment, who has no place among the just, since he did nothing rightly, nor among the wicked, since he sinned in nothing? To whom it is answered: for the embracing of the whole, and the most ordered connection of the entire creation, whether through places or through times, no human being whatever can be created superfluously, where not a leaf of a tree is created superfluously; but it is surely superfluous to ask about the merits of one who has merited nothing. For we need not fear that there could be some life midway between right deed and sin, and that the sentence of the judge cannot be midway between reward and punishment.

**3. 23. 67.** At this point people are also wont to scrutinize what the sacrament of the baptism of Christ profits little children, since for the most part they die after receiving it, before they could have known anything of it. In this matter it is piously and rightly enough believed that the faith of those by whom the child is offered to be consecrated profits the child. And this the most wholesome authority of the Church commends, so that from it each may perceive what his own faith profits him, when, for the benefit of others too who do not yet have their own, another's can be lent. For what did his own faith profit the widow's son, which, being dead, he surely did not have? And yet his mother's faith profited him, that he should rise again [^15]. How much more, then, can another's faith take counsel for a little child, to whom his own faithlessness cannot be imputed?

**3. 23. 68.** But concerning the torments of the body by which little children are afflicted, whose sins through their age are none—if the souls by which they are animated did not begin to exist before they themselves began to be human beings—a greater and, as it were, merciful complaint is wont to be laid down, when it is said: What evil did they do that they should suffer these things? As if there could be a merit of innocence before anyone could do any harm. But when God works some good in the amendment of their elders, when these are scourged by the pains and deaths of their little ones who are dear to them, why should these things not come about, since, when they have passed, they will be, for those in whom they were done, as though not done; while those for whose sake they were done will either be better, if, amended by temporal troubles, they choose to live more rightly, or will have no excuse in the punishment of the future judgment, if, by the anguishes of this life, they were unwilling to turn their desire toward the eternal life? But who knows what good of recompense God reserves in the secret of his judgments for those little children themselves—by whose torments the hardness of their elders is either broken, or their faith exercised, or their mercy proved—since, although they did nothing rightly, yet they suffered these things while sinning in nothing? For not in vain does the Church commend, as received into the honor of martyrs, those infants too who were slain when the Lord Jesus Christ was being sought by Herod to be killed [^16].

**3. 23. 69.** Although these cavilers, not most zealous examiners of such questions but most talkative fanners of them, are wont also to trouble the faith of the less learned by the pains and labors of beasts, when they say: What evil have beasts deserved, that they should suffer such great troubles, or what good do they hope for, that they are tried by such great troubles? But they say or feel these things because they judge most wrongly about matters, who, since they cannot behold what and how great the highest good is, wish all things to be such as they think the highest good to be: for, besides the highest bodies, which are heavenly and less subject to corruption, they cannot conceive any highest good; and therefore they most disorderedly demand that the bodies of beasts suffer neither death nor any corruption, as if they were not mortal, since they are lowest; or as if they were evil, because the heavenly are better. But the pain which beasts feel commends, in its own kind, a certain wonderful and praiseworthy force of the souls even of beasts. For from this very thing it appears well enough how greatly desirous they are of unity in ruling and animating their bodies. For what else is pain but a certain sense impatient of division or corruption? Whence it appears clearer than light how greedy of unity, and how tenacious, that soul is in the whole of its own body, which is bent toward that suffering of its body neither gladly nor indifferently, but rather struggling and reluctant, by which it takes ill that its unity and wholeness are weakened. So the appetite for unity in the inferior animate creatures would not appear, unless by the pain of beasts. And if it did not appear, we would be admonished less than were needful that all these things are constituted by that highest and lofty and ineffable unity of the Creator.

**3. 23. 70.** And in truth, if you piously and diligently attend, every appearance and motion of the creature that falls under the consideration of the human mind speaks our instruction; by its diverse motions and affections, as by a certain variety of tongues, crying out and proclaiming on every side that the Creator is to be known. For there is none of those things which feel neither pain nor pleasure that does not, by some unity, attain the comeliness proper to its kind, or at least some sort of stability of its nature. Likewise there is none of those things which feel either the troubles of pain or the allurements of pleasure that does not, by the very fact that they flee pain and seek pleasure, confess that they flee separation and seek unity. And in rational souls themselves, every appetite for knowledge, by which that nature is gladdened, both refers to unity everything it perceives, and in error flees nothing else than to be confounded by an incomprehensible ambiguity. But whence is every ambiguity troublesome, unless because it has no sure unity? From which it appears that all things, whether when they give offense or are offended, whether when they delight or are delighted, intimate and proclaim the unity of the Creator. But if ignorance and difficulty, from which this life must necessarily begin, are not natural to souls, it remains that they were either undertaken as a duty, or imposed as a punishment. Of which things I think enough has now been discussed.

**3. 24. 71.** Wherefore we must rather inquire what the first man himself was made like, than how his posterity was propagated. For they think they propose the question very acutely who say: If the first man was made wise, why was he seduced? But if he was made foolish, how is God not the author of vices, since folly is the greatest vice? As if human nature received no middle affection besides folly and wisdom, which could be called neither folly nor wisdom. For a human being begins to be either foolish or wise—so that necessarily one of these is applied to him—when, were he not negligent, he could already have wisdom, so that the will is guilty of a faulty folly. For no one is so out of his senses as to call an infant foolish, although it would be more absurd to wish to call him wise. As, then, an infant can be called neither foolish nor wise, although he is already a human being—from which it appears that human nature receives some middle thing which you would rightly call neither folly nor wisdom—so too, if anyone were animated by such an affection as those have who, through negligence, lack wisdom, no one would rightly call him foolish whom he saw to be such not by vice but by nature. For folly is not any ignorance whatever of things to be sought and avoided, but a faulty ignorance. Hence we do not call an irrational animal foolish, because it did not receive the power to be wise. Yet we often, by likeness, call something not properly. For blindness, although it is the greatest vice of the eyes, is nevertheless not a vice in puppies being born, nor can it properly be called blindness.

**3. 24. 72.** If, then, the human being was so made that, although he was not yet wise, he could nevertheless receive a precept which he surely ought to obey, it is no wonder that he could be seduced; nor is it unjust that, not obeying the precept, he paid the penalty; nor is his Creator the author of vices, because not to have wisdom was not yet the vice of the human being, if he had not yet received the power to have it. Yet he had something whereby, if he willed to use it well, he might ascend to that which he did not have. For it is one thing to be rational, another to be wise. By reason each becomes capable of the precept, to which he owes faith, that he may do what is commanded. As, however, the nature of reason takes up the precept, so the observance of the precept takes up wisdom. And what nature is for taking up the precept, that the will is for observing it. And as the rational nature is, as it were, the merit of receiving the precept, so the observance of the precept is the merit of receiving wisdom. But from the point at which a human being begins to be capable of a precept, from that point he begins to be able to sin. He sins, however, in two ways before he becomes wise: either if he does not apply himself to receiving the precept, or if, having received it, he does not observe it. But the wise man sins if he turns himself away from wisdom. For just as the precept is not from him to whom it is given, but from him who gives it, so wisdom is not from him who is illumined, but from him who illumines. What is there, then, for which the Creator of the human being should not be praised? For a human being is some good, and better than a beast, from the fact that he is capable of a precept. And this is better, when he has now received the precept. And again, this is better, when he has obeyed the precept. And better than all these, when he is blessed by the eternal light of wisdom. But sin is the evil in negligence, whether toward receiving the precept, or toward observing it, or toward keeping the contemplation of wisdom. From which it is understood that, even if the first man was made wise, he could nevertheless be seduced. Which sin, since it was in the free choice, a just punishment followed by the divine law. So the apostle Paul too says: *Claiming to be wise, they became fools* [^17]. For pride turns away from wisdom; and folly follows the turning away. For folly is a kind of blindness, as the same one says: *And their foolish heart was darkened* [^18]. But whence is this darkening, if not from turning away from the light of wisdom? And whence this turning away, except while one to whom the good is God wills to be his own good to himself, as God is to himself? So, *My soul is troubled within me* [^19]; and, *Taste, and you will be as gods* [^20].

**3. 24. 73.** But it troubles those who consider it, that they ask thus: Did the first man depart from God by folly, or was he made foolish by departing? For if you answer that he departed from wisdom by folly, he will seem to have been foolish before he departed from wisdom, so that folly was the cause of his departing. Likewise, if you answer that he was made foolish by departing, they ask whether he acted foolishly or wisely in departing. For if wisely, he did rightly and sinned in nothing; but if foolishly, then, they say, there was already in him the folly by which it came about that he departed. For he could not do anything foolishly without folly. From which it appears that there is something midway, by which there is a transition from wisdom to folly, which can be called neither foolishly nor wisely done; which is given to be understood by people set in this life only from its contrary. For just as no mortal becomes wise except by passing from folly to wisdom, so, if that transition is made foolishly, it is surely not made well—which is most demented to say; but if it is made wisely, then wisdom was already in the human being before he passed to wisdom, which is no less absurd. From which it is understood that there is a middle thing which can be called neither: so too, from the citadel of wisdom, in order that the first man might pass to folly, that transition was neither foolish nor wise. As in sleep and waking, to fall asleep is not the same as to sleep, nor to wake the same as to be roused, but a certain transition from one to the other. But this difference there is: that for the most part these things happen without the will, but those never except through the will; whence most just retributions follow.

**3. 25. 74.** But because the will is enticed to do anything only by some thing seen—while what each takes up or rejects is in his power, but by what sight he is touched there is no power—it must be confessed that the soul is touched by sights both from higher and from lower things, so that the rational substance may take from either what it wills, and that from the merit of taking, either wretchedness or blessedness may follow. As in paradise, the sight from the higher things was the precept of God; the sight from the lower things, the suggestion of the serpent. For neither what was commanded him by the Lord, nor what was suggested by the serpent, was in the human being's power. But how free, and loosed from all bonds of difficulty, it is, for one established in the soundness of wisdom, not to yield to the sights of a lower enticement, can be understood even from this: that even the foolish, who are about to pass to wisdom, overcome them, even with the trouble of doing without pernicious habits with their pestilential sweetness.

**3. 25. 75.** But it can be asked at this point: if sights from both sides were present to the human being—one from the precept of God, the other from the suggestion of the serpent—whence was there suggested to the devil himself the counsel of seeking impiety, by which he fell from his lofty seats? For if he were touched by no sight, he would not choose to do what he did; for if nothing had come into his mind, he would in no way have turned his intention to wickedness. Whence, then, did it come into his mind—whatever it was that came into his mind—that he should contrive those things by which, from a good angel, he became the devil? For one who wills surely wills something; which, unless either he is admonished from without through the bodily sense, or comes into his mind by hidden ways, he cannot will. Therefore the kinds of sights are to be distinguished, of which one is that which proceeds from the will of one persuading, such as that of the devil, by consenting to whom the human being sinned; the other from things lying beneath either the intention of the mind or the senses of the body. The intention of the mind has lying beneath it—excepting the unchangeableness of the Trinity, which indeed does not lie beneath but rather rises above—first the mind itself, whence we feel that we live; then the body which it administers, whence, to do any work, it moves the member that is needed, when it is needed. And under the bodily senses lie all corporeal things.

**3. 25. 76.** But that in the contemplation of the highest wisdom (which surely is not the soul, for it is unchangeable) the soul, which is changeable, may behold even itself, and in a way come into its own mind, comes about only by that difference whereby it is not what God is, and yet is something that can please after God. But it is better when it forgets itself in love of the unchangeable God, or even utterly despises itself in comparison with him. But if, as if meeting itself, it pleases itself, so as perversely to imitate God, willing to enjoy its own power, it becomes the less, the more it desires to be the greater. And this is, *Pride is the beginning of all sin* [^21]; and, *The beginning of the pride of a human being is to apostatize from God* [^22]. To the pride of the devil there was added a most malevolent envy, that he should persuade this same pride to the human being, by which he felt himself to be damned. Whence it came about that punishment took up the human being as amending rather than destroying; so that, to the one to whom the devil had offered himself for the imitation of pride, the Lord might offer himself for the imitation of humility, through whom eternal life is promised to us; so that, with the blood of Christ given beforehand for us, after labors and ineffable wretchedness, we might cling with so great love to our deliverer, and be caught up with so great brightness of his into him, that no sight from lower things might bend us away from the higher vision; and, even if anything were suggested to this intention of ours from the appetite for lower things, the everlasting damnation and torment of the devil would call us back.

**3. 25. 77.** So great is the beauty of justice, so great the pleasantness of the eternal light—that is, of unchangeable truth and wisdom—that, even if it were not allowed to abide in it longer than the space of a single day, for this alone the innumerable years of this life, full of delights and an abundance of temporal goods, would rightly and deservedly be despised. For it was not from a false or small affection that it was said, *For one day in your courts is better than a thousand* [^23]. Although it can also be understood in another sense, so that "a thousand days" be understood in the changeableness of time, but by the name of "one day" the unchangeableness of eternity be called. I do not know that I have passed over anything that, from our answer—as far as the Lord has deigned to grant it—is lacking to your questions; although, even if anything occurs to you, the measure of the book now compels us to make an end, and at last to rest from this discussion.
