# On the Same Psalm 118 (16)

**1.** [v. 65.] These verses of this psalm, about which we are now going to discourse in the will of God, begin from here: *You have dealt sweetly with your servant, O Lord, according to your word;* or rather, *according to your utterance.* But what the Greek says our interpreters rendered sometimes *sweetness,* sometimes *goodness.* But because sweetness can be present even in evil, when illicit and unclean things give delight, and can be present also in that carnal pleasure which is permitted, we ought so to understand the *sweetness* which the Greeks call by that name as to reckon it among spiritual goods; for this reason our interpreters wished to call it *goodness* as well. So I think nothing else is said here in *You have dealt sweetly with your servant* than: you have brought it about that the good might delight me. For when the good gives delight, it is a great gift of God. But when the good work which the law commands is done from fear of punishment, not from delight in righteousness—when God is feared, not loved—it is done in a servile way, not freely. But a servant does not remain in the house for ever; a son remains for ever[^1]; because perfect love casts out fear[^2]. *You have dealt sweetly with your servant, O Lord,* then, by making a son of him who had been a servant; *according to your utterance,* that is, according to your promise; that the promise might be sure to all the seed according to faith[^3].

**2.** [v. 66.] *Teach me sweetness, and instruction, and knowledge,* he says, *for I have believed your commandments.* He begs that these be increased and perfected in him; for surely one who had already said, *You have dealt sweetly with your servant,* how does he say, *Teach me sweetness,* except that the grace of God may become more and more known to him through the sweetness of goodness? For those had faith who said, *Lord, increase our faith*[^4]. And as long as one lives in this world, this is the song of those who advance. He added, moreover, *and instruction;* or, as more manuscripts have, *discipline.* But *discipline,* which the Greeks call by that name, our Scriptures are accustomed to set down where instruction through hardships is to be understood; according to that, *For whom the Lord loves he corrects, and scourges every son whom he receives*[^5]. This in the ecclesiastical Writings is usually called discipline, rendered from the Greek where the word is read. For this word is put in Greek in the Epistle to the Hebrews, where the Latin interpreter says: *All discipline for the present seems to be not of joy but of sorrow; but afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have striven through it*[^6]. With whom, then, God deals sweetly, that is, into whom he favorably breathes delight in the good—and, to put it more openly, to whom is given by God the love of God, and for God's sake the love of neighbor—surely he ought to pray insistently that this gift be so much increased in him that not only for its sake he may despise other delights, but also for its sake may endure any sufferings whatever. Thus to sweetness discipline is healthfully added. For it is not to be begged and longed for in any little measure of sweetness or goodness, that is, of holy love; but so great that under its pressure it cannot be extinguished, but, like a great flame under the blast of wind, the more it is suppressed, the more ardently it is roused. Therefore it was too little to say, *You have dealt sweetly with your servant,* unless again he asked that he teach him sweetness—so great, surely, that it might be able most patiently to bear discipline. Third is set knowledge; since, if knowledge by its greatness goes before the greatness of love, it does not build up but puffs up[^7]. So when love will have been so great in sweet goodness that it cannot be extinguished by the tribulations which discipline applies, then knowledge will be useful, by which a man comes to know even about himself what he himself has deserved, and what has been given him by God, through which he may know that he can do what he did not know he could, and through himself could not at all.

**3.** But that he does not say "Give me," but *Teach me*: how is sweetness taught, if it is not given? For many know what does not delight them, and of the things of which they have knowledge they do not have sweetness. For sweetness cannot be learned unless it delights. Likewise discipline, which signifies corrective tribulation, is learned by being received; that is, not by hearing or reading or thinking, but by experiencing. But knowledge, which he put third among the things he says, *Teach me,* is given by teaching. For what else is it to teach than to give knowledge? And these two are so connected to each other that the one cannot be without the other. For no one is taught unless he learns, and no one learns unless he is taught. And therefore, if a pupil is not capable of the things said by his teacher, the teacher cannot say, "I taught him, but he did not learn"; but he can say, "I told him what was to be told, but he did not learn; because he did not grasp, did not comprehend, did not understand." For surely he too would have learned, if the other had taught. And therefore God, when he wills to teach, first gives understanding, without which a man cannot learn the things that pertain to divine teaching; whence this man too a little later says, *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments*[^8]. So a man, when he desires to teach someone, can say those things which the Lord said to his disciples after he rose from the dead; but he cannot do what he did. For the Gospel says, *Then he opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures, and said to them*[^9]. What he said to them is read there; but they grasped what he said because he opened that by which it might be grasped. God therefore teaches sweetness by breathing in delight, teaches discipline by tempering tribulation, teaches knowledge by instilling cognition. So, since some things are those which we learn only that we may know them, but others that we may also do them, when God teaches these, he so teaches that we may know the things to be known, by opening the truth; and he so teaches that we may do the things to be done, by breathing in sweetness. For it is not in vain that it is said to him, *Teach me to do your will*[^10]. So, he says, *teach me that I may do,* not only that I may know. For those very right deeds are our fruit, which we render to our husbandman; but Scripture says, *The Lord will give sweetness, and our land will give its fruit*[^11]. And what is this land, except that of which it is said to the very one who gives sweetness: *My soul is like a land without water before you*[^12]?

**4.** But that, when he had said, *Teach me sweetness, and discipline, and knowledge,* he added and said, *For I have believed your commandments;* it can not without reason be asked why he did not say "I obeyed," but "I believed." For commandments are one thing, promises another. We undertake to do the commandments, that we may deserve to receive the promises. We believe the promises, then; we obey the commandments. What, therefore, is *I have believed your commandments,* except: I believed that you commanded them, not some man, although they were administered to men through men? Because, then, I believed that the commandments are yours, let that very faith of mine, by which I believed it, obtain from you the grace by which I may do what you commanded. For if a man enjoined these things on me from without, would he, that I might also do what he enjoined, help from within? Teach me sweetness, then, by breathing in love; teach me discipline, by giving patience; teach me knowledge, by enlightening understanding: *For I have believed your commandments.* I believed that you commanded them, you who are God, and who give to man wherewith you may make him do what you command.

**5.** [v. 67.] *Before I was humbled,* he says, *I went astray; therefore your word*—or, as others have more expressly, *therefore your utterance I have kept;* surely, lest I be humbled again. This is better referred to that humiliation which came about in Adam, in whom every human creature was as it were corrupted in the root, and, because it was unwilling to be subject to truth, was made subject to vanity. And it profited the vessels of mercy to experience this, that, pride being cast down, obedience might be loved, and misery, never to return, might perish.

**6.** [v. 68.] *You are sweet, O Lord;* or, as more have, *You yourself are sweet, O Lord;* some also, *You yourself are sweet,* or, *You yourself are good,* as we treated of this word above. *And in your sweetness teach me your statutes.* He truly wishes to do the statutes of God, when he wishes to learn them in his sweetness from the very one to whom he said, *You yourself are sweet, O Lord.*

**7.** [v. 69.] Finally there follows: *The iniquity of the proud has been multiplied over me;* of those, namely, to whom it did not profit that, after it went astray, human nature was humbled. *But I will search out your commandments with my whole heart.* However greatly, he says, iniquity abounds, love will not grow cold in me. As if he says this who learns the statutes of God in his sweetness. For the more sweet are the things he commands who helps, the more the one who loves searches them out, that, known, he may do them, and by doing may know them; for they are more perfectly known when they are done.

**8.** [v. 70.] *Their heart has curdled like milk.* Whose, except the proud, whose iniquity multiplied over him he spoke of? He wishes their heart to be understood as hardened by this word and in this place. For it is said also in a good sense, as in the sixty-seventh psalm it is said, *The curdled mountain, the rich mountain*[^13]; and it is understood as full of grace, for some rendered that too *curdled.* But see what he sets against the hardness of their heart: *But I,* he says, *have meditated on your law.* What law? Surely the most just and most merciful: whence it is said to him, *And by your law have mercy on me.* He resists the proud, that they may be hardened; but to the humble he gives grace, that they may love obedience, and receive excellence. For by meditation on this law, voluntary humility is preserved, that the penal humility may be escaped, of which he soon speaks.

**9.** [v. 71.] *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your statutes.* He had already said something near to this above: *Before I was humbled, I went astray; therefore your utterance I have kept.* Indeed by the very fruit he shows that it was good for him to be humbled; but there he gave also the cause, that penal humility had been preceded by his sinning. But that there he says, *Therefore your utterance I have kept;* while here, *That I may learn your statutes:* he seems to me to have indicated sufficiently that to know these things is to keep them, and to keep them is to know them. For Christ too did not fail to know what he reproved; and yet he reproved sin, though it is said of him that *he knew no sin*[^14]. He knew it, then, by a certain knowledge, and again by a certain ignorance did not know it. So too many learn the statutes of God, and do not learn them. For they know them by a certain knowledge; and again by a certain ignorance, since they do not do them, they do not know them. In that way, then, he is to be understood here to have said, *That I may learn your statutes;* by that knowledge by which they are done.

**10.** [v. 72.] But because this does not come about except through love, where the one who does it has delight—on account of which it was said, *In your sweetness teach me your statutes*—the following verse shows, where he says: *The law of your mouth is good to me, above thousands of gold and silver;* so that love may love the law of God more than cupidity loves thousands of gold and silver.
