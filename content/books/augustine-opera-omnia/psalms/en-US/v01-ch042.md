# On the Same Psalm 33 (32)

**1.** [v. 1] This psalm admonishes us to exult in the Lord. It is inscribed: *For David himself.* Those, then, who belong to the most sacred seed of David, let them hear their own voice, and speak their own voice, and exult in the Lord. It begins thus: *Exult, you righteous, in the Lord.* Let the unrighteous exult in this world: when the world is ended, the exultation of the unrighteous is ended. But let the righteous exult in the Lord: for since the Lord abides, the exultation of the righteous will abide. And to exult in the Lord befits us in this way: that we praise him who alone has nothing in him to displease us, while no one has so many things that displease unbelievers. And the precept is brief: He pleases God to whom God is pleasing. Do not think this a slight matter, dearly beloved. For you see how many argue against God, how many are displeased by his works. For when he wills to do something against the will of men—because he is the Lord, and knows what he does, and attends not so much to our will as to our advantage—those who would rather have their own will fulfilled than God's wish to bend God to their will, not to correct their own will to God. To such men, unbelieving, ungodly, unjust—it grieves me to say it, but I will say it, for you know how truly I speak—a pantomime more easily pleases than God.

**2.** Therefore, when he had said: *Exult, you righteous, in the Lord;* since we cannot exult in him except by praising him, and when we praise him we please him the more, the more he himself has been pleasing to us; *Praise,* he says, *befits the upright.* Who are the upright? Those who direct their heart according to the will of God; and if human frailty disturbs them, the divine equity consoles them. For although with a mortal heart they may privately will something that for the moment suits their own cause or business or present need, when they understand and recognize that God wills otherwise, they prefer the will of the better to their own will, and the will of the Almighty to the will of the weak, and the will of God to the will of man. For as far as God is distant from man, so far is the will of God from the will of man. Hence Christ, bearing manhood, and setting forth a rule for us, teaching us to live and granting us to live, showed a certain private will of man, in which he figured his own and ours, since he is our head, and to him, as you know, we as members surely belong: *Father,* he says, *if it can be, let this cup pass from me.* This was the human will, willing something proper and as it were private. But because he willed man to be upright of heart, so that whatever in him was somewhat bent he might direct toward him who is always upright; *Yet not what I will,* he says, *but what you will, Father*[^1]. But what evil could Christ will? What, finally, could he will other than the Father? Of whom the divinity is one, the will cannot be unlike. But from the person of man, transfiguring his own into himself—those whom he transfigured into himself when he said: *I was hungry, and you gave me to eat*[^2]; those whom he transfigured into himself when, to Saul raging and persecuting the saints, he cried from above, though no one was touching him: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^3]—he showed a certain will proper to man; he showed you, and corrected you. Behold, he says, see yourself in me; because you can will something proper, so that God wills otherwise. This is granted to human frailty, granted to human weakness; to will something proper, it is hard that it should not befall you: but at once think who is above you; him above you, you below him; him the Creator, you the creature; him the Lord, you the servant; him the Almighty, you the weak; correcting yourself, and subjecting yourself to his will, and saying: *Yet not what I will, but what you will, Father*: how are you disjoined from God, who now will what God wills? You will be upright, then, and praise will befit you; for *praise befits the upright.*

**3.** But if you are bent, you praise God when it is well with you, you blaspheme when it is ill with you: yet this ill, if it is just, is not ill; and it is just, since it is done by him who can do nothing unjustly: and you will be a foolish child in your father's house, loving your father if he flatters you, and hating him when he scourges you; as though both in flattering and in scourging he were not preparing an inheritance. But see how praise befits the upright; hear the voice of the upright man praising, from another psalm: *I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise shall always be in my mouth*[^4]. What is, *at all times;* this is, *always*: and what is, *I will bless;* this is, *his praise shall be in my mouth.* At all times and always, whether in prosperity or in adversity. For if in prosperity and not in adversity, how at all times, how always? And we have heard many such voices from many: when some happiness comes to them, they exult, they rejoice, they sing to God, they praise God; they are not to be condemned, rather one should rejoice for them, since many do not do even that much. But those who have begun to praise God from the side of prosperity are to be taught to recognize their father even when he scourges, and not to murmur against the hand of the one who corrects; lest, remaining always bent, they deserve to be disinherited; so that, now made upright (what is, made upright? that nothing displease them which God has done), they may be able even in adversity to praise God, and to say: The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so has it been done: blessed be the name of the Lord[^5]. Praise befits such upright ones, who will not first praise and afterward find fault.

**4.** Therefore, you righteous, you upright, exult in the Lord: for praise befits you. Let no one say: Who am I, righteous, or when am I righteous? Do not cast yourselves down and despair of yourselves. You are men, you were made to the image of God: he who made you men, for you was also made man: that you might be adopted as many sons to an everlasting inheritance, the blood of the Only-begotten was poured out for you. If you have become cheap to yourselves through earthly frailty, weigh yourselves by your price: think worthily what you eat, what you drink, to what you subscribe your Amen. Do we admonish you in this so that you should be proud, and dare to claim some perfection for yourselves? But neither, again, ought you to think yourselves exiled from all righteousness. For I do not wish to ask you about your righteousness; for perhaps none of you would dare to answer me: I am righteous: but I ask you about your faith. As none of you dares to say: I am righteous; so no one dares to say: I am not faithful. I do not yet ask how you live, but I ask what you believe. You will answer that you believe in Christ. Have you not heard the Apostle: *The righteous lives by faith*[^6]? Your faith is your righteousness: for surely if you believe, you take care; and if you take care, you strive; and your striving God knows, and he inspects your will, and considers your struggle with the flesh, and exhorts you to fight, and helps you to conquer, and awaits you as you contend, and lifts you up when you fail, and crowns you when you conquer. Therefore: *Exult, you righteous, in the Lord*: I might have said: Exult, you faithful, in the Lord, for the righteous lives by faith. *Praise befits the upright.* Learn to give thanks to God both in prosperity and in tribulations. Learn to have in your heart what every man has on his tongue: What God wills. The very speech of the people is often a saving doctrine. Who does not say daily: Let God do what he wills? And he will be upright among those who exult in the Lord, and whom praise befits: such as the psalm addresses next, saying: *Confess to the Lord with the harp, sing to him with the psaltery of ten strings.* For this is what we were even now singing, expressing it with harmonious mouth, teaching your hearts.

**5.** [v. 2] Did not the institution of these Vigils in the name of Christ bring it about that harps were driven from this place? And behold, the very harps are commanded to sound: *Confess,* it says, *to the Lord with the harp, sing to him with the psaltery of ten strings.* Let no one turn his heart to the instruments of the theater. What is commanded him, he has within himself, as is said elsewhere: *In me, O God, are the vows of praise which I will render to you*[^7]. Those who were present before remember, when we distinguished, as we could, in our discourse, what difference there is between the psaltery and the harp, and we tried meanwhile to bring it to the understanding of all: but how far we succeeded, let those who heard judge. And now we do not unfittingly repeat it, so that in this difference of two musical instruments we may find the difference of human deeds signified by them, but to be fulfilled by our life. The harp is that hollow wood, like a drum with a sounding-board behind it, on which the strings rest, so that when struck they may resound: I do not mean the plectrum with which they are struck, but I said that hollow wood over which they lie, on which they as it were lean, so that from it, when struck, being made to tremble, and receiving sound from that hollow, they may be rendered the more tuneful: this wood, then, the harp has in its lower part, the psaltery in its upper. This is the distinction. Now we are commanded to confess with the harp, and to sing with the psaltery of ten strings. He did not say with the harp of ten strings, neither in this psalm, nor, if I am not mistaken, anywhere. Let our sons the readers read and consider it better and more at leisure: yet, as far as I seem to remember, in many places we find a psaltery of ten strings, but a harp of ten strings I have nowhere found in my reading. Remember that the harp has below the part with which it sounds, the psaltery above. From the lower life, that is, the earthly, we have prosperity and adversity, that we may praise God in both, so that his praise may always be in our mouth, and we may bless the Lord at all times. For there is a certain earthly prosperity, there is a certain earthly adversity: in both God is to be praised, that we may play the harp. What is earthly prosperity? When we are sound according to the flesh, when all the things by which we live abound, when our well-being is preserved, when the fruits come forth abundantly, when he makes his sun rise on the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust[^8]. All these things avail for the earthly life. Whoever does not praise God from these is ungrateful. Are they, because earthly, therefore not God's? Or is another to be thought to give them, because they are given also to the evil? For manifold is the mercy of God, patient, long-suffering. From this he shows the more what he keeps for the good, when he shows how much he bestows even on the evil. But adversities too, from the lower part, from the frailty of the human race, in pains, in sicknesses, in pressures, in tribulations, in temptations: everywhere let him who plays the harp praise God. Let him not attend to the fact that they are lower things, but that they cannot be ruled and governed except by that Wisdom which *reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^9]. For he does not rule heavenly things and abandon earthly ones: or is it not said to him: *Where shall I go from your spirit, and from your face where shall I flee? If I ascend into heaven, you are there; if I descend into hell, you are present*[^10]? Where, then, is he absent who is nowhere absent? Therefore confess to the Lord with the harp. Whether some earthly thing abounds to you, give thanks to him who gave it; or whether something is lacking to you, or perhaps is taken from you to your loss, play the harp untroubled. For he who gave is not taken from you, although what he gave may be taken from you. Even so, I say, play the harp untroubled; sure in your God, touch the strings in your heart, and say, as it were on the harp sounding well in its lower part: The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so has it been done: blessed be the name of the Lord.

**6.** But now, when you attend to the higher gifts of God—what he has conferred on you in precepts, with what heavenly teaching he has imbued you, what he has enjoined upon you from above out of that fountain of his truth—turn also to the psaltery, sing to the Lord on the psaltery of ten strings. For the precepts of the law are ten: in the ten precepts of the Law you have the psaltery. It is a perfect thing. There you have the love of God in three, and the love of neighbor in seven. And surely you know, the Lord saying, that *on these two commandments depend the whole Law and the Prophets*[^11]. God says to you from above that *the Lord your God is one God*: you have one string. *You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain*: you have another string. *Observe the sabbath day,* not carnally, not with Jewish indulgences, where they misuse leisure for wickedness. For it would surely be better to dig all day than to dance all day. But, thinking of the rest in your God and doing all things for the sake of that rest, abstain from servile work. For everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin[^12]: and would that it were of a man, and not of sin! These three pertain to the love of God, whose unity, truth, and delight you should ponder. For there is a certain delight in the Lord, where is the true sabbath, the true rest. Hence it is said: *Delight in the Lord, and he will give you the petitions of your heart*[^13]. For who so delights as he who made all things that delight? In these three is the love of God; in the other seven, the love of neighbor: do not do to another what you do not wish to suffer. *Honor your father and mother*: for you wish to be honored by your sons. *You shall not commit adultery*: because neither do you wish your wife to be committed adultery with after you. *You shall not kill*: because you too do not wish to be killed. *You shall not steal*: because you do not wish to suffer theft. *You shall not bear false witness*: because you hate one who bears false witness against you. *You shall not covet your neighbor's wife*: because you too do not wish yours to be coveted by another. *You shall not covet anything that is your neighbor's*[^14]: because if anyone covets yours, it displeases you. Turn the tongue upon yourself, when one displeases you who harms you. All these are the precepts of God, given by the gift of wisdom; they sound from above. Touch the psaltery, fulfill the Law, which the Lord your God came not to abolish but to fulfill[^15]. For you will fulfill by love what you could not by fear. For he who through fearing does not do evil would rather do it, if it were allowed. And so, even if the opportunity is not given, the will is held guilty. I do not do it, he says. Why? Because I fear. You do not yet love righteousness, you are still a slave: be a son. But from a good slave is made a good son. Meanwhile, through fearing, do not do it; you will learn, by loving, not to do it. For there is a certain beauty of righteousness. Let punishment deter you. Righteousness has its own form, it seeks eyes, it kindles its lovers. For it the Martyrs, trampling the world, poured out their blood. What did they love, when they renounced all those things? Were they then not lovers? Or do we tell you this so that you should not love? He who does not love has grown cold, has grown stiff. Let it be loved, but that beauty which seeks the eyes of the heart. Let it be loved, but that beauty which, when righteousness is praised, sets minds on fire. They utter words, they break forth in voices, on every side they say: Well done, Excellent. What did they see? They saw righteousness, in which even a bent old man is beautiful. For if a righteous old man comes forth, there is nothing in his body to be loved, and yet he is loved by all. There it is loved, where it is not seen; rather, there it is loved, where it is seen with the heart. Let it delight you, then, and ask the Lord that it may delight you. For the Lord will give sweetness, and our earth will yield its fruit[^16]: that through love you may fulfill what through fear is hard to fulfill. What do I say, is hard? The mind cannot yet do it: it would rather that what is commanded did not exist, if it is not led to do it by love, but constrained by fear. Do not steal, fear the fires of hell: it would rather there were no fires of hell into which it might be cast. When does it begin to love righteousness, except when it would rather that thefts did not exist, even if there were no fires of hell into which thieves might be cast? This is to love righteousness.

**7.** And what is righteousness itself like? Who paints it? What beauty has the Wisdom of God? Through it all things are beautiful that please the eyes: to behold and embrace it, hearts must be cleansed. We profess ourselves lovers of it: it composes us, that we may not displease it. And when men reprove us in those matters in which we please her whom we love, how lightly we regard our reprovers, how we despise them and count them as nothing at all! The slippery and damnable lovers of women, when their beloveds compose them according to their own eyes, if they please these women, do not care for those whom they displease, supposing it enough for them that they please the eyes of those to whom they are devoted: and often they displease grave men—rather, they always displease grave men, and are reproved by the better judgment. You are not well shorn, says the grave man to the lascivious youth, it does not become you to go about with such curls. But the youth knows that those locks please some woman or other: he hates you who reprove him with true judgment, and keeps in himself what pleases by a perverse counsel. He thinks you an enemy, because you take away his unseemliness. He flees your eyes, and does not at all care by what rule of righteousness he is reproved. If, then, those men do not care for truthful reprovers, so that they may be beautiful to the deceitful; ought not we, in those matters in which we please the Wisdom of God, to care nothing for unjust mockers, who do not have eyes by which to see what we love? Pondering these things, you upright of heart: *Confess to the Lord with the harp, sing to him with the psaltery of ten strings.*

**8.** [v. 3] *Sing to him a new song.* Put off the old self: you know the new song. The new man, the New Testament, the new song. The new song does not pertain to old men: none learn it but new men, renewed by grace from oldness, and now belonging to the New Testament, which is the kingdom of heaven. For it all our love sighs, and sings a new song. Let it sing a new song, not with the tongue, but with the life. *Sing to him a new song: sing to him well.* Each one asks how he may sing to God. Sing to him, but do not sing badly. He does not wish his ears to be offended. Sing well, brother. If, when you are told to sing so as to please some good musical listener, you tremble to sing without some instruction in the art of music, lest you displease the artist—because what an unskilled man does not notice in you, the artist reproves—who would presume to sing well to God, who so judges of the singer, so examines all things, so hears? When can you offer so refined an art of singing that you displease in nothing such perfect ears? Behold, he gives you as it were a manner of singing: do not seek out words, as if you could set forth what God delights in. Sing *in jubilation*: for this is to sing well to God, to sing in jubilation. What is it to sing in jubilation? To understand, and not be able to set forth in words, what is sung in the heart. For those who sing, whether at the harvest, or in the vineyard, or in some fervent labor, when they have begun to exult in gladness in the words of their songs, as it were filled with so great a gladness that they cannot set it forth in words, turn from the syllables of words and pass into a sound of jubilation. The jubilus is a certain sound signifying that the heart is in labor over what it cannot utter. And whom does this jubilation befit, except the ineffable God? For he is ineffable whom you cannot speak: and if you cannot speak him, and yet ought not to be silent, what remains but that you jubilate; that the heart may rejoice without words, and the boundless breadth of joys have no limit of syllables? *Sing to him well in jubilation.*

**9.** [v. 4] *For the word of the Lord is upright, and all his works are done in faith.* Even in that wherein he is displeasing to the not-upright, he is upright. *And all his works are done in faith.* Let your works be done in faith, since the righteous lives by faith, and faith works through love: let your works be done in faith, since by believing in God you are made faithful. How can God's works be done in faith, as though God too lived by faith? We find God also faithful, and this is not said in our own words; hear the Apostle: *God is faithful,* he says, *who will not let you be tempted beyond what you are able to bear, but with the temptation will also make a way out, that you may be able to endure*[^17]. You have heard that God is faithful; hear also elsewhere: *If we endure, we shall also reign with him; if we deny him, he also will deny us; if we do not believe, he remains faithful, he cannot deny himself*[^18]. We have, then, a faithful God too: let us rather distinguish the faithful God from the faithful man. The faithful man is one who believes God who promises; the faithful God is one who renders to man what he promised. Let us hold him a most faithful debtor, since we hold him a most merciful promiser. For we did not lend him anything, that we should hold him our debtor; since from him we have whatever we offer him, and from him is whatever good we are. All the good things in which we rejoice are from him. For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor; or who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid him? For from him, and through him, and in him are all things[^19]. So we have not given him anything; and yet we hold him our debtor. From where, debtor? Because he is the promiser. We do not say to God: Lord, render what you received; but: Render what you promised. *For the word of the Lord is upright.* What is, *the word of the Lord is upright?* He does not deceive you; do not you deceive him: rather, do not deceive yourself. For who deceives him who knows all things? But iniquity has lied to itself[^20]. *For the word of the Lord is upright, and all his works are done in faith.*

**10.** [v. 5] *He loves mercy and judgment.* Do these things, for he does them. Attend to that mercy and judgment. The time of mercy is now, the time of judgment will be afterward. From where is the time of mercy? He now calls those turned away, he grants pardon to the converted: he is patient over sinners, until they be converted: whenever they have been converted, he forgets the past, he promises the future: he exhorts the slothful, consoles the afflicted, teaches the eager, helps those who contend: he deserts no one who labors and cries out to him: he grants whence he may be offered sacrifice, he himself bestows whence he may be appeased. Let the great time of mercy not pass, brothers, let it not pass us by. Judgment is to come: there will then be repentance, but now fruitless. *They will say within themselves, repenting and groaning for anguish of spirit*—surely these things are written in the book of Wisdom: *What did pride profit us, and what did the boasting of riches confer on us? All those things have passed away like a shadow*[^21]. Let us now say: All things pass away like a shadow. Let us now fruitfully say: They pass away: lest then we say fruitlessly: *They have passed away.* This, then, is the time of mercy; there will be also a time of judgment.

**11.** But do not suppose, brothers, that these can in any way be separated from each other in God. For they sometimes seem contrary to each other, so that he who is merciful does not keep judgment, and he who holds fast to judgment forgets mercy. God is almighty, and neither in mercy does he lose judgment, nor in judgment mercy. For he has mercy—he considers his own image, our frailty, our error, our blindness—and he calls: and to those converted to him he grants pardon of sins, to those not converted he does not grant it. Is he merciful to the unjust? Has he then also lost judgment, or ought he not to judge between the converted and the not converted? Or does it seem just to you that the converted and the not converted should be held alike; that in the same way the confessing and the lying, the humble and the proud, should be received? So he has judgment even in mercy itself. Again, in that judgment he will have also mercy, toward those, namely, to whom he is to say: *I was hungry, and you gave me to eat*[^22]. For it is said in a certain apostolic Epistle: *For judgment is without mercy to him who has not shown mercy*[^23]. *Blessed,* he says, *are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy*[^24]. So in that judgment there will be mercy too, but not without judgment. For if it will be mercy not to just anyone, but to him who has shown mercy beforehand; then that mercy too will be just, because it will not be confounded. It is surely mercy that sins be forgiven, it is mercy that eternal life be granted: see there the judgment: *Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you; give, and it shall be given you*[^25]. Surely, *it shall be given you,* and *it shall be forgiven you,* is mercy. If judgment had departed from this, he would not say: *With what measure you measure, it shall be measured back to you*[^26].

**12.** You have heard how God shows mercy and judgment; show you too mercy and judgment. Or do these perhaps pertain to God, and not pertain to man? If they did not pertain to man, the Lord would not say to the Pharisees: *You have left aside the weightier matters of the Law, mercy and judgment*[^27]. So mercy and judgment pertain to you. Do not think that mercy pertains to you, but judgment does not pertain to you. Sometimes you hear a case between two men, of whom one is rich and the other poor; and it happens that the poor man has the bad case, the rich the good. Now, if you are not instructed in the kingdom of God, you seem to yourself to do well if, as it were pitying the poor man, you hide and cover over his iniquity, and wish to justify him, so that he may seem to have the good case: and if you are reproved for having judged badly, you answer as it were out of mercy: I know, I too am aware; but he was poor, mercy was owed him. How have you held to mercy, and lost judgment? And how, you say, if I held judgment, should I not lose mercy? I would pronounce against the poor man, who would have nothing wherewith to pay; or if he had it, when he had paid, he would not find whereof to live afterward. Your God says to you: *You shall not be partial to the poor man in judgment*[^28]. For the rest, it is easy that we be admonished not to be partial to the rich; this everyone sees, and would that everyone did so! It is in the other thing that one is deceived, like the man who wishes to please God if he is partial to the poor man in judgment, and says to God: I favored the poor man. Rather, you should hold both, mercy and judgment. First, what mercy did you show to the man whose iniquity you favored? Behold, you spared his purse, you struck his heart: that poor man remained unjust; and the more unjust, the more he saw you, as it were a just man, favoring his iniquity. He departed from you unjustly helped, he remained for God to be justly condemned. What mercy did you offer him whom you made unjust? Behold, you are found crueler rather than more merciful. What, then, you say, should I do? You should judge first according to the case, you should rebuke the poor man, you should bend the rich man. The place of judging is one, the place of petitioning is another. When that rich man saw that you held to justice, that you did not raise up the neck of the unjust poor man, but rebuked him justly according to the desert of his sin, would he not be bent to mercy at your petition, who had been made glad by your judging? Although, then, brothers, much of the psalm remains, we must nevertheless take counsel for the strength both of soul and of body, because of the variety of the hearers: for even when we are refreshed from the same wheat, as it were many flavors are made for us, to remove our distaste: let these things suffice you.
