# On the Same Psalm 118 (18)

**1.** [v. 73.] The Lord Jesus, in this psalm, through the Prophet, asked, as it were for himself, that understanding be given by God to his body, which is the Church, for learning the commandments of God. For with him the life of his body, that is, of his people, is hidden in God; and he himself, in that body of his, suffers want, and begs what is necessary for his members. *Your hands,* he says, *have made me, and molded me; give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments.* Because you, he says, formed it, do you reform it; that there may come about in the body of Christ what the Apostle says: *Be reformed in newness of your sense*[^1].

**2.** [v. 74.] *Those who fear you,* he says, *will see me, and be glad;* or, as other manuscripts have, *will rejoice, for I have hoped in your words;* that is, in the things you promised, that they may be sons of the promise, the seed of Abraham, in whom all nations are blessed. But who are they who fear God, and whom they will see and be glad, because he has hoped in the words of God? If it is the body of Christ, that is, the Church, whose voice this is through Christ, or in it and about it, as if of himself this voice of Christ is; surely are they not themselves among those who fear God? Who, then, is he whom they see, and rejoice? Or does the people itself see itself, and rejoice, and is it thus said, *Those who fear you will see me, and be glad, for I have hoped in your words;* or as others more carefully expressed, *I have hoped beyond hope:* as if he said, *Those who fear you will see* your Church, *and be glad, because I have hoped beyond hope in your words;* since they themselves are the Church who see the Church and rejoice? But why did he not say, "those who fear you see me, and rejoice," but *fear you,* a verb of the present time he set down; while *will see* and *will be glad* are verbs of the future time? Is it because in the present time there is fear, as long as human life on earth is temptation[^2]; but the gladness which he wished to be understood here will then be, when the righteous will shine in the kingdom of their Father like the sun[^3]? For hence too is that read in another psalm: *How great is the multitude of your sweetness, O Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you*[^4]! Now, then, as long as they fear, they do not yet see; but *they will see,* and *will be glad:* because there too follows, *But you have perfected it for those who hope in you*[^5]; and here, *because I have hoped,* or, *hoped beyond hope, in your words:* so that in the word so composed, and expressed by the care of a more diligent interpreter, we may understand also that *God is able to do above what we ask and understand*[^6]; so that, because the things *above what we ask and understand* exist, it is too little to hope for them, but we ought to hope beyond hope.

**3.** [vv. 75-76.] Because, then, the Church which is in this life still fears, and does not yet see itself in that kingdom where its joy will be secure, but labors amid perilous temptations in this world, where it hears, *Whoever thinks he stands, let him take heed lest he fall*[^7]; considering the misery of this mortality, in which a heavy yoke lies upon the sons of Adam, from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all[^8], so that, on account of the flesh lusting against the spirit[^9], even the regenerate are compelled to groan under its heaviness: considering this, then, *I have known,* he says, *O Lord, that your judgments are righteousness, and in truth you have humbled me. Let your mercy come, and console me according to your utterance to your servant.* Mercy and truth are so commended by the divine word that, although they are found in many places, especially in the Psalms, it is even read in a certain place: *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^10]. And here indeed he first set truth, by which we have been humbled into death, he judging whose judgments are righteousness; then mercy, by which we are restored to life, he promising whose benefit is grace. Therefore he says, *according to your utterance to your servant;* that is, according to what you promised to your servant. So whether the regeneration by which here we are adopted into the sons of God, or faith and hope and love, which three are built up in us, although they come from the mercy of God; yet in this toilsome and stormy life they are consolations of the wretched, not joys of the blessed: therefore it is said, *Let your mercy come, that it may console me.*

**4.** [v. 77.] But because after these things, and through these things, those other things too are to come; therefore there follows: *Let your mercies come to me, and I will live.* For then I will truly live, when I can fear nothing, lest I die. For life itself, even without any addition, is so called, and is understood as none other than eternal and blessed life, as though it alone is to be called life, in comparison with which this which we lead is rather to be called death than life: as is that in the Gospel, *If you wish to come to life, keep the commandments*[^11]. Did he add "eternal," or "blessed"? Likewise, when he was speaking of the resurrection of the flesh: *Those who have done well,* he says, *to the resurrection of life*[^12]: neither here does he say "eternal" or "blessed." So too here: *Let your mercies come to me,* he says, *and I will live:* neither here does he say "I will live forever," or "I will live blessedly"; as if to live were nothing else than to live without any end and without any misery. But by what merit is this? *Because your law,* he says, *is my meditation.* Unless this meditation were in faith that works through love, no one could ever, on account of it, come to that life. This I thought ought to be said, lest anyone, when he has committed the whole law to memory, and sung it by very frequent recollection, not being silent about what it commands, yet not living as it commands, should think that he has done what he reads—*Because your law is my meditation*—and from this should suppose that he will obtain what in the words above, on account of this merit, he asked, where it is said, *Let your mercies come upon me, and I will live.* This meditation is the thought of one who loves, and so of one who loves that the love of this his meditation does not grow cold, however great the abundance of another's iniquity heaped up against it.

**5.** [v. 78.] Finally there follows, and he says: *Let the proud be put to shame, because they have unjustly worked iniquity against me; but I will exercise myself in your commandments.* Behold what he says: the meditation of the law of God, or rather, the meditation is the law of God.

**6.** [v. 79.] *Let those turn to me,* he says, *who fear you, and who know your testimonies.* In some manuscripts, both Greek and Latin, we find *Let them turn to me;* which I judge to be worth just as much as if it were said, *to me.* But who is this who says this? For no man will dare to say this, or if he says it, he is not to be listened to. Surely, then, it is he who also above interposed the property of his own voice, saying, *I am a sharer with all who fear you*[^13]. Because he was made a sharer of our mortality, that we too might be made sharers of his divinity; we sharers of one unto life, but he a sharer of many unto death. For it is he to whom those who fear God turn, and who know the testimonies of God, foretold so long before about him through the Prophets, shown shortly before by miracles in his presence.

**7.** [v. 80.] *Let my heart,* he says, *become spotless in your statutes, that I may not be put to shame.* He returns to the voice of his body, that is, of his holy people, and prays that his heart now become spotless—that is, the heart of his members—in the statutes of God, not in their own strength: for he asked this, he did not presume. But that he added, *That I may not be put to shame;* something like this is found also in the first verses of this psalm, where he said, *O that my ways may be directed to keeping your statutes: then I shall not be put to shame, when I look into all your commandments*[^14]. What there, because he said *O that,* he signified by one word of wishing, here he expressed in plainer words of praying, by saying, *Let my heart become spotless;* so that neither in that nor in this sentence, which are both one, may be found the boldness of one trusting in free choice against grace. But what he there says, *Then I shall not be put to shame;* this he says here, *That I may not be put to shame.* The heart of the members and of the body of Christ becomes spotless, then, by the grace of God through the very Head of the body, that is, through Jesus Christ our Lord; through the washing of regeneration, where all our past sins are abolished; through the help of the spirit, by which we lust against the flesh, that we may not be overcome in our struggle; through the effect of the Lord's prayer, in which we say, *Forgive us our debts*[^15]. So, regeneration having been given to us, the conflict aided, prayer poured out, our heart becomes spotless, that we may not be put to shame: because this too pertains to the statutes of God, when among his other precepts it is commanded, *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given you*[^16].
