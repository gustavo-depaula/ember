# On the Same Psalm 118 (24)

**1.** [v. 119.] We inquire, if by God's bounty we can find it, how is to be understood what was said in this great psalm: *Transgressors,* or rather, *those transgressing;* for the Greek says the latter, not the former; we inquire, then, how is to be understood: *Those transgressing I have accounted all the sinners of the earth;* on account of what the Apostle says: *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression.* But he said this when he was distinguishing the promise from the law. For, that a fuller sense may be gathered from what precedes: *For not through the law,* he says, *was the promise to Abraham or to his seed, that he should be heir of the world, but through the righteousness of faith. For if those who are heirs are by the law, faith is made void, and the promise is annulled: for the law works wrath; for where there is no law, neither is there transgression. Therefore it is of faith, that according to grace the promise may be sure to all the seed; not only to that which is of the law, but also to that which is of the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all*[^1]. Why does the Apostle say this, except to show that the law, without the grace of the promise, not only does not take away, but even increases sin? Whence that too is: *The law entered in, that the offense might abound*[^2]. But because all things are forgiven through grace, not only those committed without the law, but also those committed under the law, therefore there he went on and added: *But where the offense abounded, grace superabounded*[^3]. So the Apostle does not account all sinners transgressors, but accounts those only transgressors who transgress the law. *For where there is no law,* he says, *neither is there transgression.* And through this, according to the Apostle, every transgressor indeed is a sinner, because he sins under the law, but not every sinner is a transgressor, because some sin without law; *but where there is no law, neither is there transgression.* Further, if no one sinned without law, that same apostle would not say: *Whoever have sinned without law will perish without law.* But according to this psalm, if all the sinners of the earth are transgressors, surely there is no sin without transgression: but there is no transgression without law; therefore there is no sin except under the law. He, then, who says, *Those transgressing I have accounted all the sinners of the earth,* wishes none at all to be understood as sinners except those who have transgressed the law; and on account of this he is opposed to him who said, *Whoever have sinned without law will perish without law.* For according to the latter there are some sinners, although they are not transgressing, that is, who *have sinned without law: For where there is no law, neither is there transgression:* but according to the former there is no sinner without transgression; because he accounts all the sinners of the earth transgressors. According to the former, then, no one has sinned without law; *for where there is no law, neither is there transgression.* Or shall we perhaps say that it is true indeed that there is no transgression where there is no law, but not true that some have sinned without law; or true indeed that some have sinned without law, but not true that, where there is no law, there can be no transgression? But the Apostle said both: both, then, are true, because the Truth said both through the Apostle. How, then, will it be true what in this psalm the same Truth without doubt said: *Those transgressing I have accounted all the sinners of the earth?* For it is answered us: Who, then, are those who, according to the Apostle, have sinned without law? For not even any of them is to be accounted transgressing, since, according to the same apostle, there is no transgression where there is no law.

**2.** But surely, when the Apostle said, *Whoever have sinned without law will perish without law,* he was dealing with that law which God gave through Moses his servant to his people Israel. This the very words that surround it show. For he was discoursing of Jews and Greeks, that is, Gentiles, pertaining not to the circumcision, but to the uncircumcision; therefore calling them without law, because they had not received the law which the Jews gloried that they had received: whence he says to them, *But if you are called a Jew, and rest in the law, and glory in God*[^4]. Finally, whence he came to this sentence is to be observed, that he should say: *Whoever have sinned without law will perish without law. Wrath,* he says, *and indignation, tribulation and anguish upon every soul of man who works evil, the Jew first and the Greek; but glory and honor and peace to everyone who works good, to the Jew first and to the Greek: for there is no respect of persons with God.* To these he added what is now in question, and says: *For whoever have sinned without law will perish without law; and whoever have sinned under law will be judged by the law*[^5]. Wishing the latter to be understood as Jews, but the former as Greeks, since he was dealing with these; showing both to be under sin, that both may confess that they need grace: on account of which he says, *For there is no distinction: for all have sinned, and need the glory of God; being justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus*[^6]. Whom, then, does he say all have sinned, except Jews and Greeks, of whom he had said, *For there is no distinction?* For of these too, a little before, *For we have charged,* he says, *that Jews and Greeks are all under sin*[^7]. And through this, *whoever have sinned without law*—that, namely, of which the Jews gloried—*will perish without law; and whoever have sinned under law*—that is, the Jews themselves—*will be judged by the law.* Nor will they therefore not perish, unless they believe in him who came to seek what had perished[^8].

**3.** Indeed some Catholic commentators too, by attending too little, understood these words of the Apostle otherwise than the matter stands, so as to say that those perish who have sinned without law; but that those who have sinned under law are only judged, not perish: as though they are believed to be purified by transitory penalties, like that one of whom it was said, *But he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire*[^9]. But this is rightly understood by the merit of the foundation, of which the Apostle was speaking, so that he should say this. For above he had said: *As a wise architect I have laid a foundation; another builds upon it: but let each one see how he builds upon it. For no one can lay another foundation besides that which has been laid, which is Christ Jesus*[^10]: and the rest, up to that place where he said that he is saved through fire who builds upon this foundation not gold, silver, precious stones, but wood, hay, stubble; yet does not reject so as not to receive, nor forsake the received foundation; and prefers it to all his carnal delights, by which he is captivated and to which he succumbs, when it comes to this point, that either those are forsaken, or Christ; where, if Christ is not preferred, he is not his foundation. For the foundation is preferred to all the later parts of the structure. Nor do I think that those who held that they do not perish of whom it was said, *They will be judged by the law,* considered anything except that they have Christ as their foundation. They attended too little, then, to what we have shown; and Scripture itself cries that the Apostle says this of the Jews, who are without the foundation, Christ. But what Christian would say that a Jew does not perish, if he does not believe in Christ, but is only judged: when Christ himself testifies that he was sent to that same nation, on account of the sheep that had perished from it; and says that it will be more tolerable for the Sodomites in the day of judgment—who surely perished without law—than for the Jewish city which did not believe in him, doing such wonders by so great power[^11]?

**4.** If, then, the Apostle, according to the law which God gave through Moses to the people of Israel, but did not give to the other nations, said that the other nations are without law; what are we to understand to have been said in this psalm, *I have esteemed transgressors all the sinners of the earth,* unless we understand some law not given through Moses, according to which the sinners of the other nations are transgressors? *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression.* What is this law, unless perhaps that of which the same apostle says: *The Gentiles who do not have the law do by nature the things of the law; these, not having the law, are a law to themselves*[^12]? According to this, then, that he says, *not having the law,* they sinned without law, and will perish without law: but according to that which he says, *they are a law to themselves,* not without reason are all the sinners of the earth esteemed transgressors. For there is no one who does an injury to another, except one who would be unwilling that it be done to himself: and in this he transgresses the law of nature, which he is not permitted to be ignorant of, while he is unwilling to suffer what he does. But was this natural law not in the people of Israel? It plainly was, since they too were men: but they would be without the natural law, if they could be outside the nature of the human race. Much more, then, were they made transgressors by the divine law, by which that natural law was either restored, or increased, or strengthened.

**5.** But now, if among all the sinners of the earth even little ones are not incongruously accounted, on account of the bonds of original sin, they too, in the likeness of Adam's transgression[^13], are shown to pertain to that transgression which, the law having been given in paradise, was first committed: and through this, rightly, with absolutely none excepted, all the sinners of the earth are esteemed transgressors. But *all have sinned, and need the glory of God*[^14]. The grace of the Savior, therefore, finds all transgressing, some more, some less. For the greater the knowledge of the law in anyone, the less the excuse of sin: the less the excuse of sin, the more manifest the transgression. It remained, then, that to all not their own righteousness, but that of God, that is, granted by God, should come to aid. Whence the Apostle says: *Through the law is the knowledge of sin.* Not, then, the removal, but the *knowledge. But now, without law,* he says, *the righteousness of God has been manifested, witnessed by the Law and the Prophets*[^15]. Accordingly this man too added: *Therefore,* he says, *I have loved your testimonies.* As if he said: Since the law, whether given in paradise, or naturally implanted, or promulgated in letters, made all the sinners of the earth transgressors; *Therefore I have loved your testimonies,* which are in your law, concerning your grace; that there may not be in me my own righteousness, but yours. For the law profits toward this, that it may send to grace. For not only by what it witnesses to the righteousness of God, which is without law, to be manifested; but by this very thing too, that it makes transgressors, so that even the letter kills, it compels by fear to flee to the life-giving Spirit, through whom the whole sum of sins may be blotted out, and the love of right deeds breathed in: *Therefore,* he says, *I have loved your testimonies.* Some manuscripts have *always,* some do not. But if it is there, *always* is so to be taken that it be understood: As long as one lives here. For here testimonies are necessary, from the Law and the Prophets, witnessing to the righteousness of God by which we are justified freely: here our testimonies too are necessary, for which the martyrs ended this life that is led here.

**6.** [v. 120.] Having come to know, then, the grace of God, which alone sets free from the transgression which is committed by the knowledge of the law, praying he says: *Pierce my flesh with nails by your fear.* For thus some of ours more expressly rendered what in Greek could be said in one word. This others wished to say *pierce,* and did not add *with nails;* and so, while they wish to render one Latin word in interpreting one Greek word, they unfolded the sense less; since in that which is *pierce,* nails do not sound; but the Greek word cannot be understood without nails, nor can it be said in Latin except in two words, as it was said, *pierce with nails.* Where what does he wish to be understood, except what the Apostle says: *But far be it from me to glory, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ; through whom the world is crucified to me, and I to the world*[^16]? And again: *With Christ,* he says, *I am nailed to the cross; yet I live, now not I, but Christ lives in me*[^17]. What is this but: There is not in me my own righteousness, which is of the law, in which I was made a transgressor; but the righteousness of God, that is, which is to me from God, not from me? For thus in me lives, not I, but Christ, *who was made for us wisdom from God, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption: that, as it is written, He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^18]. Likewise he says: *But those who are Jesus Christ's have crucified the flesh with its passions and lusts*[^19]. Although it is here said that they themselves *have crucified their flesh,* yet in this psalm God is asked to do it, to whom it is said, *Pierce my flesh with nails by your fear;* so that we may understand that even what we do rightly is to be attributed to the grace of God, who works in us both to will and to work, for his good pleasure[^20].

**7.** But what does it mean, that, when he had said, *Pierce my flesh with nails by your fear,* he added, *for I have feared your judgments?* What is, *Pierce by your fear; for I have feared?* If he had already feared, or was fearing, why did he still pray that God would crucify his flesh by his fear? Or did he wish fear to be added to him, that he might fear as much as would suffice for crucifying his flesh—that is, his carnal lusts and affections: as if saying, "Perfect in me your fear; for I have feared your judgments"? But there is here another, deeper sense, as far as God grants, to be dug out by searching the bosom of this Scripture. *Pierce,* he says, *my flesh with nails by your fear; for I have feared your judgments:* that is, by your chaste fear, which abides for ever and ever[^21], let my carnal desires be pressed down; *for I have feared your judgments,* when the law threatened me a penalty, which could not give me righteousness. But this fear, by which a penalty is dreaded, perfect love casts out[^22]; which makes men free not by fear of punishment, but by delight in righteousness. For that fear, by which righteousness is not loved, but punishment feared, is servile, because it is carnal; and therefore it does not crucify the flesh. For the will to sin lives, which then appears in the deed, when impunity is hoped for. But when a penalty is believed to follow, it lives in hiding: yet it lives. For it would prefer that it were permitted, and grieves that what the law forbids is not permitted; because it does not spiritually delight in its good, but carnally dreads the evil it threatens. But by chaste fear, that very love which casts out this fear fears to sin, even if impunity should follow; because it does not judge that impunity will follow, when, by love of righteousness, it accounts the sin itself the penalty. By such fear the flesh is crucified; since carnal delights, which by the letter of the law are forbidden rather than avoided, are overcome by delight in spiritual goods, and are destroyed, the same victory growing to perfection. *Pierce,* then, *my flesh with nails by your fear,* he says, *for I have feared your judgments:* that is, Give me chaste fear, to the asking of which that fear of the law, as a tutor, led me; by which fear *I have feared your judgments.*
