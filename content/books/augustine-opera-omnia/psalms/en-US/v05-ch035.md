# On the Same Psalm 118 (10)

**1.** [v. 33.] In this great psalm there follows what we must, with the Lord helping, consider and treat: *Set a law for me, O Lord, the way of your justifications, and I will seek it out always.* The Apostle says: *For the just a law is not set, but for the unjust and unsubmissive,* and the rest, which there concluding he says: *And if there is anything else contrary to sound doctrine, which is according to the Gospel of the glory of the blessed God, which was entrusted to me*[^1]. Was this one, then, such, who says: *Set a law for me, O Lord,* as those for whom blessed Paul says a law is set? Far from it. For if he were such, he would not have said above: *The way of your commandments I ran, when you enlarged my heart.* What is it, then, that he prays that a law be set for him by the Lord, if it is not set for the just? Or in that manner it is not set for the just, in which it was set for the contumacious people, on tablets of stone, not on the carnal tablets of the heart; according to the old testament from Mount Sinai, which begets unto slavery, not according to the new testament, of which it is written through the prophet Jeremiah: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will consummate for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new testament, not according to the testament which I arranged for their fathers in the day when I took their hand, to lead them out of the land of Egypt: because they did not remain in my testament; and I neglected them, says the Lord. Because this is the testament which I will establish for the house of Israel: After those days, says the Lord, I will give my laws in their minds, and in their hearts I will write them*[^2]? Behold how this one wishes a law to be set for him by the Lord; not as for the unjust and unsubmissive, pertaining to the old testament, it was set on tablets of stone; but as for the holy sons of the free, that is, the supernal Jerusalem, the sons of promise, the sons of eternal inheritance, by the Holy Spirit as it were the finger of God it is given in the mind, and written in the hearts: not which they hold by memory, and neglect by life; but which they may know by understanding, do by loving, in the breadth of love, not in the narrows of fear. For he who does the work of the law by the fear of punishment, not by the love of righteousness, surely does it unwillingly. But what he does unwillingly, if it could be done, he would surely prefer not to be commanded: and through this he is not a friend of the law which he would wish not to be, but rather an enemy; nor is he cleansed by the work, who is unclean in will. Such a one cannot say what this one said in the earlier verses: *The way of your commandments I ran, when you enlarged my heart;* because that enlargement signifies love, which according to the Apostle is the fullness of the law.

**2.** Why, then, does this one still ask that a law be set for him; which surely, if it had not been set for him, he would not have run the way of the commandments of God in the breadth of heart? But because he speaks as one making progress, and knows it to be the gift of God that he makes progress; what else does he ask, when he asks a law to be set for him, except that in it he may more and more make progress? Just as, if you hold a full cup, and begin to give to one who thirsts; he both drinks by drinking, and asks by desiring. But to those unjust and unsubmissive for whom the law is set on tablets of stone, it makes them guilty of transgression, not sons of promise. But even he who remembers it, and does not love it, is in that manner guilty; because its memory is to him as it were a stone written, not by which he is adorned, but by which he is pressed; a weight of burden, not a title of honor. But this law this one called the way of the justifications of God; nor is there another way of his commandments, than that which he had already said he ran, when his heart was enlarged. Which therefore he also calls a path, because narrow is the way that leads to life; and since it is narrow, it is not run except with heart enlarged. Finally, when he had said: *Set a law for me, O Lord, the way of your justifications;* he added, *and I will seek it out always.* For what does he seek out which he has, except because he both has it by doing, and seeks it out by making progress?

**3.** But what is it, *always?* Whether there will be no end of seeking out; as it was said: *His praise is always in my mouth*[^3]; because there will be no end of praising: for we will not not praise God, when we have arrived at his eternal kingdom, since we read: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house, for ages of ages they will praise you*[^4]? Or, *always,* was it said, as long as one lives here, because so long one makes progress; but after this life he who here made good progress, there is perfected? As it was said of certain women, *always learning*: but they badly, because going on he added: *And never arriving at the knowledge of the truth*[^5]. But he who here always makes progress for the better, arrives by making progress where he no longer makes progress, because perfected he stands without end. Nor yet of those was it so said, *always learning,* that they persevere after death in learning vain and unfruitful things; since to such teachings have succeeded not studies, but everlasting torments. Here, then, the law of God is sought out, as long as in it progress is made, both by knowing and by loving; but there its fullness abides for enjoying, it does not remain for seeking. So too that was said: *Seek his face always*[^6]. Where *always,* except here? For we shall not there too seek the face of God, where we shall see face to face. Or if it is rightly said that what is loved without weariness is sought out, and this is done lest it be lost; we shall always altogether without end seek the law of God, that is, the truth of God: for in this very psalm it is said: *And your law is truth.* It is sought now, that it may be held; then it will be held, lest it be forsaken: as it was said of the Spirit of God that he searches all things, even the depths of God; not certainly that he may find what he does not know, but because he leaves nothing at all that he does not know.

**4.** [v. 34.] And so the grace of God is especially commended to us, when he asks a law to be set for him by the Lord, who surely already knew the law according to the letter. But because the letter kills, but the spirit gives life; he prays that through the Spirit he may do what through the letter he knew; lest, through the knowledge of a commandment not observed, the crime of transgression too accrue to him. Although even that the law be known in the way it ought to be known, that is, that it be understood what it means, why it was set for those who were not going to keep it, what usefulness even this very thing has, that *the law entered in, that the offense might abound*[^7], no one comprehends, unless he has received understanding from the Lord: whence this one adds and says: *Give me understanding, and I will scrutinize your law, and keep it with my whole heart.* For when anyone has scrutinized the law, and arrived at its heights, on which the whole hangs; surely he ought to love God with his whole heart, with his whole soul, with his whole mind; and his neighbor as himself. For on these two commandments the whole Law hangs, and the Prophets. This he seems to have promised, when he said: *And I will keep it with my whole heart.*

**5.** [v. 35.] But because this too is of less avail by one's own powers, unless he is helped by him who commands, that he may do what he commands: *Lead me,* he says, *on the path of your commandments, because I willed it.* My will is too little for me, unless in that which I willed you yourself lead me. And certainly that is the path; that is, the way of the commandments of God, which he had already said he ran, his heart being enlarged by him. Which therefore he also calls a path, because the way that leads to life is narrow; and since it is narrow, it is not run except with heart enlarged.

**6.** [v. 36.] But since he still makes progress, still runs; and therefore he seeks divine help by which he may be led, because it is neither of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy; finally, because the very willing too God works in us, for the will is prepared by the Lord; there follows and he says: *Incline my heart into your testimonies, and not into avarice.* What is it to have the heart inclined to something, except to will this? And he both willed it, and prays that he may will it. He willed it, when he says: *Lead me on the path of your commandments, because I willed it*: but he prays that he may will it, when he says: *Incline my heart into your testimonies, and not into avarice.* This, then, he prays, that in the very will he may make progress. But what are the testimonies of God, except those by which he himself attests something to himself? For by testimonies something is proved; and through this the justifications of God and the commandments of God are proved by the testimonies of God; and whatever God wishes to persuade us, he persuades by his testimonies: into which this one asks his heart to be inclined, and not into avarice. For by his testimonies God deals with us, that we worship him freely; which avarice, the root of all evils, impedes. By such a Greek word he here names it, from which can be understood general avarice, by which each one seeks more than is enough: for pleon in Latin is "more"; hexis is "habit," from that which is "to have." Therefore from "having more" pleonexia took its name, which some Latin translators in this place rendered *emolument,* but some *usefulness;* but better those who rendered *avarice.* But the Apostle says: *For the root of all evils is avarice*[^8]. But in the Greek, whence the very words were translated into our language, there is not read in the Apostle pleonexia, which is in this place of this psalm; but philargyria, by which word is signified the love of money. But the Apostle is to be understood by this name to have signified the genus through the species, that is, through the love of money the universal and general avarice, which is truly the root of all evils. For the first men themselves would not have been deceived and cast down through the serpent, unless they had wished to have more than they had received, and to be more than they had been made. For this he had promised, saying: *You will be as gods*[^9]. Therefore by these things they were subverted. For wishing to have more than they had received, they lost too what they had received. A trace of which truth, which is everywhere dispersed, has been caught even in forensic law, by which it is established that by demanding more one loses the case: that is, that he who has demanded more than is owed to him, loses too what was owed to him. But all avarice is circumcised from us, if God is worshiped freely. To which contest the enemy himself, in the agony of the temptation, provokes holy Job, when he says of him: *Does Job worship God for nothing?*[^10] For the Devil supposed that in worshiping God the just man had his heart inclined into avarice, and for the sake of emolument or usefulness of the temporal things with which the Lord had enriched him, served him as it were a hireling for such a reward: but how freely he worshiped God, being tempted, appeared. If, then, we have not the heart inclined into avarice, we worship God only for the sake of God, that his worship itself may be its reward. Let us love him in himself, let us love him in us, let us love him in our neighbors whom we love as ourselves, whether they have him, or that they may have him. Which, because it is conferred on us by him giving, therefore it is said to him: *Incline my heart into your testimonies, and not into avarice.* But the things that follow are to be treated in another sermon.
