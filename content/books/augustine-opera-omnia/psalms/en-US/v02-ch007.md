# Exposition of Psalm 36 (2)

**1.** To speak to your Charity about this psalm we were both bidden, and we had to obey. For the Lord willed, because of the excess of rain, to delay our departure; and it was commanded us that our tongue should not here be idle toward you, since you are always the business of our heart, as we are of yours. We had already commended the will of God in this psalm, what he wishes to teach us, what to admonish, against what to be cautious, and what to endure, and what to hope. For two kinds of men, of the just and of the wicked, are mingled in this earth and in this life. Each of these kinds has its own intentions of heart. The kind of the just strives toward the heights through humility: the kind of the wicked weighs down toward the depths through exaltation. For the one depresses itself that it may rise, the other lifts itself up that it may fall. From this it comes that one kind endures, the other is endured; and it is the purpose of the just to win even the wicked themselves to eternal life, but the purpose of the wicked to repay evil for good, and, if it can be done, to deprive even of temporal life those who wish them eternal life. For the unjust bears with difficulty the just, and the just the unjust: they are a burden to each other. No one doubts that these two are a burden to each other, but with diverse intentions. For the just is a burden to the unjust for this reason, that he does not wish him to be unjust, but to become just, and he both desires this with prayers and strives for it with deeds: but the unjust so hates the just that he does not wish him to exist, not that he wishes him to be good. For the more good he is, the more is he a burden to that man's iniquity. And he labors indeed, if it can be done, to make him unjust; but if he cannot, to take him out of the way, and to remove him from his weariness and trouble. But even if he has made him unjust, he will nonetheless be a burden to him. For not only is the just a burden to the unjust, but even two unjust men scarcely endure each other: and when they seem to love each other, they owe each other complicity, not friendship. But they then agree with each other when they conspire to the destruction of the just, not because they love each other, but because they hate together the one who was to be loved. Against this kind of men our Lord God enjoins on us tolerance and that affection of love which we know in the Gospel, the Lord commanding us and saying, *Love your enemies, and do good to those who hate you*[^1]. As also the Apostle: *Be not conquered by evil, but conquer evil with good*[^2]. Contend with the evil man, but out of goodness. For this is the true contention, or rather the wholesome struggle, that one be good against the evil, not that there be two evil men.

**2.** [vv. 12-13] Therefore look to the psalm. The first parts have already been treated, these follow. *The sinner shall watch the just, and shall gnash at him with his teeth: but the Lord shall deride him*[^3]. Whom? The sinner, of course, gnashing at the just with his teeth. But why *shall the Lord deride him? Because he foresees that his day will come.* He seems harsh, when he threatens the just, not knowing the hour of his own tomorrow: but the Lord sees, and foresees his day. What day? In which he will repay to each according to his works. For he treasures up for himself wrath in the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God[^4]. But the Lord foresees, while you do not foresee; he who foresees has shown you. You were ignorant of the day of the unjust, on which he is to pay the penalty: but he who knows has not hidden it from you. It is no small part of knowledge to be joined to one who knows. He has the eyes of knowing, do you have those of believing. What God sees, believe you. For the day of the unjust will come, which God foresees. What day? Of each one's vengeance. For it is necessary that vengeance be taken on the ungodly, vengeance be taken on the unjust, whether he convert himself, or not convert himself. For if he converts himself, this very thing is avenged in him; that the iniquity has perished. Did not the Lord deride, foreseeing the days of two unjust men, both Judas the betrayer and Saul the persecutor? Of the one he foresaw the day for punishment, of the other for justice. In both vengeance was taken: the one was appointed to the fires of hell, the other was laid low by a heavenly voice. Therefore you too, whatever unjust man you endure, through the eyes of faith foresee with God his days; and when you see him raging against you, say to yourself: He, either corrected, will be with me; or persevering, will not be with me.

**3.** [vv. 14-16] What then? Does the injustice of the unjust harm you, and not harm him? How can it be that his iniquity, which through his indignation and hatred goes forth to injure you, does not first lay waste himself within, before it assails you outside? Adversity presses your body, but iniquity rots his soul. For whatever he brings forth against you, returns upon him. For his persecution makes you purified, him guilty. Whom, then, does it harm more? Behold, by raging he has despoiled you: who is struck with the heavier loss, he who loses money, or he who loses faith? They know how to grieve over those losses who have the inner eye. For to many gold gleams, faith does not gleam. They have eyes by which they may see gold: by which they may see faith, they do not have. For if they had and saw it, they would surely love it more: and yet when faith is broken to them, they cry out, they make a grievance, and say: O faith! where is faith? You love it that you may exact it; love it that you may display it. Therefore, because all who persecute the just are struck with a heavier loss, and are afflicted with a heavier destruction, when in them the very soul is laid waste; there follows, and the psalm shows this: *The sinners have drawn the sword, they have bent their bow: to cast down the needy and poor, to slay the upright of heart. Let their sword enter into their own heart*[^5]. It is easy that his sword, that is, his blade, reach your body, as the sword of the persecutors reached the bodies of the martyrs; but, the body struck, the heart remained unhurt: but the heart of the one who drew the sword against the body of the just plainly did not remain unhurt. This psalm testifies to it. He did not say, "Let their sword enter into their body": but, *Let their sword enter into their own heart.* They wished to kill in the body; let them die in the soul. For those whose bodies they wished to slay the Lord made secure, saying to them, *Do not fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul*[^6]. But what is it, to rage with the sword, and not be able to kill anything but the body of the enemy, and to be able to kill one's own soul? They are foolish, they rage against themselves, they are mad, they do not see themselves: as if someone wished to drive iron through his own body, that he might cut the tunic of another. You attend to where you have arrived, and do not attend to what you have pierced through: you have cut his garment, your own flesh. It is established, then, that what the wicked harm themselves, and that they do themselves more harm, than what they seem to do to those whom they hate. *Let their sword,* then, *enter into their own heart.* It is the sentence of the Lord; it cannot be otherwise. *And let their bow be broken.* What is *let their bow be broken*? Let their treacheries be frustrated. For above he had said, *The sinners have drawn the sword, they have bent their bow.* By the drawing of the sword he wished open assault to be understood: by the bending of the bow he wished hidden treacheries to be understood. Behold, his sword destroys him, and the contrivance of his treacheries is frustrated. What is "is frustrated"? It harms the just in nothing. How, then, did it harm him in nothing—him, for example, whom he so despoiled, whom by taking away his goods he reduced to straits? He has what to sing: *Better is a little to the just than the many riches of sinners*[^7].

**4.** [v. 17] But the wicked are powerful, and do many things, and the effecting of things is at their disposal; speed of acting, obedience follows their command. Is it always so? *Because the arms of the sinners shall be broken*[^8]. Their arms, then, their power. What will he do in hell? Or what that rich man does, who among the living feasted, among the dead was tormented? *The arms,* then, *of the sinners shall be broken. But the Lord confirms the just.* How does he confirm them? what does he say to them? What is said in another psalm: *Endure the Lord, act manfully, and let your heart be strengthened, and endure the Lord*[^9]. What is this: *Endure the Lord*? You labor for a time, you will not labor forever: brief is your trouble, eternal will be your blessedness: for a little you grieve, without end you will rejoice. But amid your troubles you begin to slip? There is set before you the example also of the passions of Christ. See what he endured for you, who had no reason to endure it. However much you suffer, you will not come to those insults, to those scourges, to that ignominious garment, to that crown of thorns, to that cross, finally, you will not come, because it has now even been removed from the punishment of the human race. For when, under the ancients, the wicked were crucified, now no one is crucified. It has been honored, and ended. It has ended as a punishment, it remains in glory. From the places of execution it has made a passage to the foreheads of emperors. He who gave so great honor to his punishments, what does he keep for his faithful? By these things, then, by these words, by these addresses, by such an example *the Lord confirms the just.* Let the sinners rage as much as they wish, and as much as they are permitted: *the Lord confirms the just.* Whatever happens to the just, let him impute it to the divine will, not to the power of the enemy. He can rage: to strike, if God is unwilling, he cannot. And if God wills that he strike, he knows how to receive his own: *For whom the Lord loves, he chastises: but he scourges every son whom he receives*[^10]. Why, then, does the unjust applaud himself, because my Father has made him a scourge for me? He takes him up for service, he trains me for an inheritance. Nor ought we to attend to how much he permits to the unjust, but how much he keeps for the just.

**5.** But we ought to wish even for those through whom we are scourged, that they may be converted, and be scourged. For so he was training his faithful, who had made for himself a scourge of Saul, but afterward converted Saul too. And when the Lord said to holy Ananias, by whom Saul was baptized, that Saul was to be received, because he was a vessel of election; Ananias answered, fearing and shuddering at the heard report of Saul the persecutor: *Lord, I have heard,* he said, *of this man, how great persecutions he has made against your saints in Jerusalem, and now, having received letters, he goes, that wherever he finds those calling on your name, he may drag and bind them, and bring them to the high priests.* And, on the contrary, the Lord: *Let be,* he said; *I will show him what he must suffer for my name*[^11]. I will repay him, he says, I will avenge myself on him; and he will suffer for my name, who rages against my name. I train, or have trained, others through him; I will train him too through others. This was done, and we know how much Saul endured, far more than he had done, as a greedy collector he received back with interest what he had given.

**6.** But see whether in him was fulfilled what the psalm now says: *But the Lord confirms the just. Not only,* says the same Paul, when he was suffering many evils, *but we even glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation works patience, patience trial, trial hope, but hope does not confound, because the love of God is poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us*[^12]. Well; plainly now just, now confirmed. As, then, those who assailed him, now confirmed, harmed him in nothing, so neither did he harm those whom he persecuted. *But the Lord,* he says, *confirms the just.* Hear other voices of the confirmed just one. *Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Tribulation, or distress, or famine, or nakedness, or persecution?*[^13] How did he cling, who by such things was not separated? *But the Lord confirms the just.* Certain prophets had come down from Jerusalem, and, filled with the Holy Spirit, prophesied to this same Paul that he would suffer many things in Jerusalem: so that one of them, Agabus by name, loosing his belt, bound himself, as is wont to be done, that by these signs of things the prophet might show the future, saying, *As you see me bound, so must this man be bound in Jerusalem.* But the brothers, when Saul, now Paul, was admonished by the response, began to deter him from committing himself to such great perils, and by warning and asking to call him back lest he go to Jerusalem. But he, who was now in the number of those of whom it was said, *But the Lord confirms the just*, said, *Why do you break my heart?* I do not make my soul precious to myself. For he had already said to those whom he had brought forth in the Gospel, *And I myself will be spent for your souls*[^14]. *For I,* he said, *am ready not only to be bound, but even to die for the name of the Lord Jesus Christ*[^15].

**7.** [v. 18] *The Lord,* then, *confirms the just.* How does he confirm them? *The Lord knows the ways of the spotless*[^16]. When they suffer evils, they are believed by the ignorant to walk evil ways, by those who do not know how to see the ways of the spotless. He who knows them knows by what straight way he leads his meek ones. Whence he said in another psalm, *He will direct the meek in judgment, he will teach the gentle his ways*[^17]. How do you think men detested the poor man full of sores lying before the rich man's gate as they passed by? How perhaps they spat on him with nostrils stopped? But the Lord knew to keep paradise for him. And how did they wish for themselves the life of the one who was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly every day? But the Lord, who foresaw his days, knew his future torments, and torments without end. Therefore, *The Lord knows the ways of the spotless.*

**8.** *And their inheritance shall be forever*[^18]. This we hold in faith: is the Lord in faith? The Lord knows those things in so great a manifestation as we cannot tell, even when we shall be made equal to the angels. For not so manifest to us will be the things that will be manifest, as they are manifest to him who cannot even be changed. Yet of us too what was said? *Beloved, now we are children of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be: we know that when he appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^19]. There is kept for us, then, an utterly sweet spectacle, I know not what: and even if it can be thought in some part in an enigma and through a mirror, yet in no way can the beauty of that sweetness be told, which God keeps for those who fear him, and perfects for those who hope in him[^20]. For this our hearts are prepared in all the tribulations and temptations of this life. Do not wonder that you are prepared in labors: you are prepared for something great. Whence that voice of the confirmed just one: *The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory which shall be revealed in us*[^21]. What will our future glory be, but to be made equal to the angels, and to see God? How much does he confer on a blind man, who heals his eyes for him, that he may see this light? When he has been made well, he does not find what worthy thing to repay his healer: for however much he gives him, what will he give such as that which the other granted? Suppose he gives the most—he will give gold, and much gold he will give: the other granted light. That he may know that he gives nothing, let him see in darkness what he gives. What, then, shall we give to that physician, who heals our inner eyes, to see a certain eternal light, which is he himself? what shall we give him? Let us seek, let us find, if we can; and in the straits of our seeking let us cry out, *What shall I render to the Lord for all the things that he has rendered to me?* And what does he find? *I will take the cup of salvation, and call upon the name of the Lord*[^22]. *Can you,* he says, *drink the cup which I am to drink?*[^23] Hence to Peter: *Do you love me? Feed my sheep*[^24]: for whom he might drink the cup of the Lord. *But the Lord confirms the just. The Lord knows the ways of the spotless, and their inheritance shall be forever.*

**9.** [v. 19] *They shall not be confounded in the evil time*[^25]. What is *They shall not be confounded in the evil time*? In the day of tribulation, in the day of straits they shall not be confounded: as one is confounded whom hope deceives. Who is it that is confounded? He who says: I have not found what I hoped for. Not undeservedly: for you hoped in yourself, or you hoped in a man, a friend: but cursed is he who places his hope in man. You are confounded, because hope deceived you, hope placed in a lie deceived you: for every man is a liar[^26]. But if you place your hope in your God, you will not be confounded; because he in whom you placed your hope cannot be deceived. Whence also that confirmed just man whom I recalled a little before, set in the evil time, in the day of tribulation, because he was not confounded, what does he say? *We glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation works patience, patience trial, trial hope, but hope does not confound.* Whence does hope not confound? Because it is placed in God. Therefore it follows: *Because the love of God is poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^27]. The Holy Spirit has already been given to us; how does he deceive us, whose pledge we hold such? *They shall not be confounded in the evil time: and in the days of famine they shall be satisfied.* For there is here a certain satisfying of them. For the days of this life are days of famine: others hungering, they are satisfied. For whence would that man glory, saying, *We glory in tribulations,* if within he suffered want? Straits were seen outside, but within there was breadth.

**10.** [v. 20] But what does the evil man do when he begins to be in tribulation? Outside he has nothing, all has been taken away; in his conscience there is no consolation: there is nowhere to go out, because things are hard; there is nowhere to go in, because things are evil. Deservedly there comes to him what follows: *Because the sinners shall perish*[^28]. For those who have nowhere a place, how shall they not perish? Consolation is not in external things, is not in internal things. For those things are outside us, from which there is no consolation. And all who do not have God serve money, friendships, glory, the resources of the world, and whatever bodily goods there are; they cannot be consoled inwardly, as that man was consoled who was full of inner fatness, and from that very fatness belched forth: *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away, as it pleased the Lord, so has it been done; blessed be the name of the Lord*[^29]. Therefore for those sinners there is no place in the things that are outside, because there they suffer tribulations: their conscience does not console them; it is not well with them in their own company, because it cannot be well with an evil man. But whoever is evil, it is ill with him in his own company: he must be tormented by himself as his own torment. For he himself is his own punishment, whom his own conscience torments. He flees from an enemy wherever he can; from himself, where will he flee?

**11.** Thus a certain man from the party of Donatus had come to us, accused and excommunicated by his own people, seeking here what he had lost there. But because he could not be received except in the rank in which he ought: for he did not forsake that party as one in good standing among them, so that it might appear that he had acted not by necessity, but by choice: because, then, he could not there have what he sought—but he sought a vain exaltation and a false honor—and because here he did not find what he had lost there, he too perished. He groaned, wounded, and was not consoled: for in his conscience were horrible silent stings. We tried to console him from the word of God: but he was not of the wise ants, which gather for themselves in summer whence to live in winter. For when things are tranquil, then a man ought to gather for himself the word of God, and store it in the inmost recesses of his heart, just as the ant hides in its cavernous chambers the labors of summer. For during summer there is leisure to do this: but winter comes, that is, tribulation comes upon him; and unless he find within what to eat, he must perish of hunger. This man, then, had not gathered for himself the word of God; winter came upon him, he did not find here what he sought, he could be consoled only from there, but from the word of God in no way. Within himself he had nothing, outside he did not find what he sought: he burned with the torches of indignations and griefs, his mind was violently agitated, and so long secretly, until it even broke forth into certain groans, so that it sounded among the brothers, and he did not know he was heard. We saw, and grieved vehemently, God knows, at so great a punishment of the soul, so great crosses, so great hells, so great torments. What more? Impatient of the humble place, which, if he were wise, could be for him a wholesome place, he showed himself to be such that he was even cast out. Nor from this ought we to despair, brothers, of others, who perhaps have chosen the truth, and have not followed necessity. For so far is it from being right to despair of the rest, that I do not despair even of this man, as long as he lives. For of no living man is one to despair. This, then, brothers, on this occasion your Charity ought to know, lest perhaps anyone tell you otherwise. For their subdeacon, who, when no charge was being stirred against him there, chose the catholic peace and unity, and, leaving them, came; came truly as one choosing what is good, not as one repudiated by the wicked: so he was received, that we rejoice in his conversion, and commend him to your prayers. For God is powerful, who can make him better and better. But of no one is anything to be pronounced in either direction, whether for good or for evil. For as long as one lives here, tomorrow's day is always unknown. *They shall not be confounded in the evil time, and in the days of famine they shall be satisfied: because the sinners shall perish.*

**12.** *But the enemies of the Lord, as soon as they shall be glorified and exalted, failing like smoke they shall fail*[^30]. From the very likeness recognize the thing which he has insinuated. Smoke, breaking forth from the place of fire, is lifted up on high, and by that very rising swells into a great ball: but the larger that ball is, the more empty it becomes; for, from that greatness not founded and made solid, but hanging and inflated, it goes into the breezes and dissolves, so that you may see that its very greatness was a harm to it. For the more it is raised up, the more extended, the more diffused on every side into a larger compass, the thinner it becomes, and failing, and not appearing. *But the enemies of the Lord, as soon as they shall be glorified and exalted, failing like smoke they shall fail.* Of such it was said, *As Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so these too withstand the truth: men corrupt in mind, reprobate concerning the faith.* But whence do they withstand the truth, except by the inflation of their swelling, going into the winds, lifting themselves up as though just and great? But what does he say of them? As of smoke: *But they shall proceed no further; for their madness shall be manifest to all, as that of those too was*[^31]. *But the enemies of the Lord, as soon as they shall be glorified and exalted, failing like smoke they shall fail.*

**13.** [v. 21] *The sinner borrows, and will not repay*[^32]. He receives, and will not return. What will he not return? Thanksgiving. For what does God wish from you, or what does God require, except what profits you? And how much has the sinner received, for which he will not repay? He has received that he should exist, he has received that he should be a human being, and that there should be much difference between him and the beast; he has received the form of a body, he has received in the body the distinction of the senses, eyes for seeing, ears for hearing, nostrils for smelling, the palate for tasting, hands for handling, feet for walking, and the very health of the body. But still these things we have in common with the beast: he has received also more, that is, a mind that can understand, that can grasp the truth, that can distinguish the just from the unjust, that can search out, desire the Creator, praise and cleave to him. All these things the sinner too has received, but by not living well he does not return what he owes. Therefore *the sinner borrows, and will not repay,* will not return to him from whom he received, will not give thanks: rather indeed he will repay evil for good, blasphemies, murmuring against God, indignation. Therefore he *borrows, and will not repay: but the just shows mercy, and lends.* The one, then, has nothing, this one has. See the want, see the riches. The one receives, and will not repay: this one shows mercy, and lends; it abounds for him. What if he is poor? Even so he is rich. Do you only direct godly eyes to his riches. For you look at the empty chest, you do not look at the conscience full of God. Outwardly he does not have means, but inwardly he has love. Out of love how much he disburses, and is not exhausted! For if outside he has means, that very love gives, but out of what it has: but if it does not find outside what to give, it gives goodwill, it furnishes counsel, if it can; it furnishes help, if it can: at the last, if it can aid neither by counsel nor by help, it aids at least by wish, or it prays for the afflicted, and perhaps he himself is heard more than the one who offers bread. He always has whence to give, whose breast is full of love. This is the love, which is also called good will. God requires no more from you than what within he gave you. For good will cannot be idle. For one not having good will, even if money be left over for you, does not hold it out to the poor: the poor themselves furnish to one another out of good will, they are not unfruitful to one another. You see a blind man led by one who sees: because he had no coins to give the needy, he lent his eyes to one who did not have them. Whence did it come about that he should lend his members to one who does not have them, except because within was good will, the treasure of the poor? In which treasure there is most sweet rest, and true security. To lose it neither is a thief admitted, nor is shipwreck feared. He keeps with himself what within he has, he escapes naked, and is full. *The just,* then, *shows mercy, and lends.*

**14.** [v. 22] *For those who bless him shall possess the earth by inheritance*[^33]: like that just one, truly the only just one and the one who justifies, who was both poor here, and brought great riches, by which to make rich those whom he found poor. For it is he who by the Holy Spirit enriched the hearts of the poor, and the souls emptied by confessing sins he filled with the wealth of justice; who could make a fisherman rich, by leaving his nets, despising what he had, drawing in what he did not have. For the weak things of the world God chose, that he might confound the strong[^34]. And not from an orator did he win a fisherman, but from a fisherman he gained an orator, from a fisherman he gained a senator, from a fisherman he gained an emperor. *For those who bless him shall possess the earth by inheritance*: they will be his coheirs, in that land of the living, of which it is said in another psalm, *You are my hope, my portion in the land of the living*[^35]. You are my very portion, he says to God, and he did not hesitate to make God his portion. *They shall possess the earth by inheritance. But those who curse him shall perish.* But that those who bless may bless, was granted to them. For we have come to those who curse, and they have been made blessing ones: and now thus have those who cursed him perished, when by his gift they were made to bless him whom by their own evil they cursed, but by his own good they bless.

**15.** [v. 23] See what follows: *By the Lord shall the steps of a man be directed, and he shall will his way*[^36]. That a man himself may will the way of the Lord, by the Lord himself are his steps directed. For if the Lord did not direct the steps of a man, they were so crooked that they would always go through crooked things, and by following bent paths could not return. But he came, and called, and redeemed, and shed his blood: this price he gave, these good things he did, and evil things he suffered. Attend to what he did; he is God: attend to what he suffered; he is man. Who is this God-man? If you, O man, had not forsaken God, God would not have become man for you. For it was too little for you, for his recompense or for his gift, that he made you also a man, unless a man too were made for you? For it is he who directed our steps, that we might will his way. *By the Lord shall the steps of a man be directed, and he shall will his way.*

**16.** Now, when you follow the way of Christ, do not promise yourself the prosperities of the world. He walked through hard things, but promised great things. Follow. Do not attend only to where you are going, but also to where you will arrive. You will endure hard temporal things, but you will come to glad eternal things. If you wish to bear the labor, attend to the reward. For even a laborer in the vineyard would fail, unless he attended to what he would receive. But when you attend to what you will receive, all that you suffer will be cheap to you, nor will you judge them worthy in exchange for which you receive that. You will marvel that so much is given for so little labor. For surely, brothers, for eternal rest eternal labor was to be undergone; and, about to receive eternal happiness, you would have to sustain eternal sufferings: but if you sustained eternal labor, when would you come to eternal happiness? So it comes about that, necessarily, your tribulation is temporal, and, it being finished, you come to infinite happiness. But yet, brothers, the tribulation could be long for eternal happiness. For example, that since our happiness will have no end, our misery and our labor and our tribulations should be long-lasting. For even if they were of a thousand years, weigh a thousand years against eternity: what do you weigh, however great a finite amount, with the infinite? Ten thousand years, ten times a hundred thousand, if it must be said, and thousands of thousands, which have an end, cannot be compared with eternity. To this is added, that God willed not only your labor to be temporal, but also brief. The whole life of man is of few days, even if glad things were not mingled with hard ones, which are surely more and longer than the hard; and therefore the hard things are briefer and fewer, that we may be able to endure. If, then, through his whole life a man were in labors and in hardships, in griefs, in torments, in prison, in blows, in hunger and thirst all his days, all hours, through his whole life even to the age of old age, few days are the whole life of man: this labor accomplished, the eternal kingdom will come, happiness without end will come, equality with the angels will come, the inheritance of Christ will come, Christ the coheir will come. For how great a labor how great a reward we receive! Veteran men who labor in military service, and are engaged amid wounds for so many years, begin to serve from youth, go out old; and that they may have the few quiet days of their old age, when that very age begins to weigh on them whom wars do not weigh down, how many hard things they endure; what journeys, what cold, what suns, what necessities, what wounds, what perils! And they do not attend, in suffering all these things, to anything but those few quiet days of old age, to which they do not know whether they will come. Therefore *by the Lord shall the steps of a man be directed, and he shall will his way.* Hence I had begun to say: if you wish the way of Christ, and are truly a Christian—for he is a Christian who does not spurn the way of Christ, but wishes to follow the way of Christ through his sufferings—do not wish to go by any other way than that by which he himself went. It seems hard, but it is the safe way: another perhaps has delights, but is full of robbers. *And he shall will his way.*

**17.** [v. 24] *When he falls, he shall not be troubled, for the Lord confirms his hand*[^37]. Behold what it is, to will the way of Christ. Let it happen to him that he suffer some tribulation, some dishonor, some reproach, some affliction, some losses, and whatever other things abound in this life for the human race: he sets before himself his Lord, how many kinds of temptations he suffered: and *he shall not be troubled when he falls, because the Lord confirms his hand;* because he suffered first. For what will you fear, O man, whose steps have been directed, that you should will the way of the Lord? What will you fear? Griefs? Christ was scourged. Will you fear reproaches? He heard, *You have a demon,* who cast out demons. Perhaps the faction and conspiracy of the wicked? It was conspired against him. Perhaps you cannot show your conscience good in some accusation, and you suffer violence in that false witnesses are heard against you. Against him first they spoke false witness, not only before his death, but even after the resurrection. False witnesses were brought in, that he might be condemned by the judges: false witnesses, the guards, came to the tomb. He rose again with so great a miracle; the earth, shaken, betrayed the Lord rising. There too was earth guarding earth; but the harder earth could not be changed. It announced true things, but was seduced by a false reward. For those guards told the Jews what they had seen, and what had happened: they received money, and it was said to them, *Say that, while you slept, his disciples came, and took him away*[^38]. Behold false witnesses, and against him rising. But how great the blindness in false witnesses, how great the blindness, brothers! For this false witnesses are wont to suffer, that they be blinded, and speak against themselves, not knowing whence it may appear that they are false witnesses. For what did they say against themselves? While we slept, *his disciples came, and took him away.* What is this? Who is it that gives testimony? He who was sleeping. Such men, telling this, I would not believe, not even if they told me their dreams. Foolish madness! if you were awake, why did you permit it? if you were asleep, how did you know?

**18.** So too these sons of theirs, as you remember, and it is not to be passed over on this occasion. For the more we seek their salvation, the more ought we to recall their vanity. Behold, the body of Christ suffers false witnesses, the body endures what went before in the head. It is no wonder, and even now there are not lacking to the body of Christ, spread through the whole world, those who say: Offspring of betrayers. You speak false witness. There I convict you of being a false witness, by following out a few words. You say to me: You are a betrayer. I say to you: You are a liar. But you nowhere and never prove my betrayal: I prove your lie here, in these very words of yours, now. Surely there you said that we sharpen our swords: I recite the deeds of your Circumcellions. Surely there you said that you let pass the things taken away: I recite the Acts, where you made a procuration for exacting them. Surely there you said: We alone offer the Gospels: I recite so many orders of judges, by which you persecuted those divided from you: I recite the Petition to the Apostate emperor, to whom you said that justice alone has a place with him. Or does perhaps the apostasy of Julian seem to you a part of the Gospel? Behold, I hold you a liar. What have you said about me that ought to be believed? Even if I did not find whence to show that you speak falsely, it is enough that I show you to be a liar. What do you say? Such as you are, such are the rest too. For deservedly you sent such words to all: you wished to abound in the fellowship of liars, lest you alone should blush for the lie.

**19.** But, he says, let the judgment of our fathers against Caecilian stand. Why will it stand? Because bishops judged. Let that too stand against you which the Maximianists judged. For first—what I believe you know—bishops agreeing with Maximian, while he was still his deacon, came to Carthage, as the Tractatoria has it, which they even attached to the Acts, when they were litigating about a house with the procurator of that man who lets pass the things taken away. So first they sent a Tractatoria about him, complaining that he was unwilling to come out to them: for of this above all they complained. See how God repaid them what they said about Caecilian. A wonderful likeness: God willed, after so many years, to throw back in their face what was done, so that they should find no way at all to dissemble or escape. They would say that they had forgotten what was done before; God does not allow them to forget: and would that it might avail them for salvation! For God did this in his mercy, if they consider what was done. Set, then, before your eyes, brothers, the unity of the whole world at that time, from which these men divided themselves against Caecilian: set now also the party of Donatus, from which the Maximianists divided themselves against Primian. What those did then to Caecilian, this these now did to Primian. Therefore the Maximianists call themselves even more truthful than the Donatists, because they truly imitated the deeds of their elders. For they raised up Maximian against Primian, just as those raised up Majorinus against Caecilian; and these complained about Primian, just as those about Caecilian. For if you remember, those said this, that Caecilian was unwilling to come out to them, mindful of his conscience; but he knew their faction: so these too complain that Primian was unwilling to come out to them. Why is it granted to Primian to know the faction of the Maximianists, and not granted to Caecilian to know the faction of the Donatists? Maximian was not yet ordained, charges were being spoken about Primian: bishops came, they wished that he come out to them: he did not go out, as their Tractatoria, inserted into the Acts, shows. He did not go out: I do not disapprove, rather I even praise it. If you saw some faction, you ought not to proceed to factious men, but to keep your cause for a better judgment of your party. For a great part of Donatus's party remained, where Primian could clear himself: therefore he was unwilling to go out to those who had already conspired in a faction. You see how we praise your counsel against the Maximianists; attend well also to the cause of Caecilian: you do not wish to as a brother; judge as a stranger. You were unwilling to go out, saying to yourself what? These have conspired in a faction against my safety, they are corrupted against me: if I commit myself to them, I prejudge my own cause: I will not go out to them, let my cause be kept for better and weightier authorities. Good counsel. What if Caecilian said this? Although you will labor to show what other things Lucilla corrupted those people with against you, and perhaps you will not find it; which he then knew so well that afterward it was opened up in the Acts. But you saw something hidden, I know not what; something to be feared was reported to you. I grant this caution to your fear: you did rightly, not to proceed to such men; for there were others who could judge concerning you. Now attend to Caecilian: you kept Numidia for yourself, he the whole world. But if you wish the sentences of the Donatists then to avail against him, let the sentences of the Maximianists now avail against you: bishops condemned him; bishops condemned you too. Why did you afterward plead your cause, and prevail there over the Maximianists; just as he afterward pleaded his cause, and prevailed there over the Donatists? What, then, was done then, seems to be rolled back before our eyes in a wonderful and manifest example, that the Maximianists complain of such things about Primian as all these complained about Caecilian. It is wonderful, brothers, how I am moved, how I give thanks to God: because truly the mercy of God shaped an example to enlighten these men, if they are wise. Accordingly, if you please, a little, brothers, because God has put this too into our hands, hear the council of the Maximianists. (And as he treated it, he read out the council of the Maximianists.)

**20.** *To the most holy brothers and colleagues throughout all Africa.*—Their whole unity is throughout Africa. But here the Catholic Church is with them: in other parts of the world, however, they are not with the Catholic Church. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*To the most holy brothers and colleagues established throughout all Africa, that is, throughout the Proconsular province, Numidia, Mauretania, Byzacena, and Tripoli; but also to the presbyters and deacons, and to all the peoples serving with us in the truth of the Gospel: Victorinus, Fortunatus, Victorianus, Migginus, Saturninus, Constantius, Candorius, Innocentius, Cresconius, Florentius, Salvius, another Salvius, Donatus, Geminius, Praetextatus.*—(And as he read it, he said:) This is the bishop of Assuras, whom they afterward received: the one who pronounced sentence against himself, they afterward received. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Maximianus, Theodorus, Anastasius, Donatianus, Donatus, another Donatus, Pomponius, Pancratius, Ianuarius, Secundinus, Pascasius, Cresconius, Rogatianus, another Maximianus, Benenatus, Gaianus, Victorinus, Guntasius, Quintasius, Felicianus.*—(And as he read it, he said:) This is the bishop of Musti, who is still alive; but perhaps it is another from elsewhere. Afterward, subscribing, they give also the places from which each one was. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Salvius, Migginus, Proculus, Latinus, and the rest of us who were at the council at Cabarsussi, eternal salvation in the Lord. Let no one be ignorant, most beloved brothers, that it belongs to the priests of God, not by the impulse of their own will, but of the divine law, both to pronounce sentence against the guilty, and rightly and deservedly to remove from them a sentence inflicted on the innocent. For he will lie under no light peril, whoever either spares the guilty, or attempts to undo the innocent: especially since it is written, " You shall not slay the innocent and the just, and you shall not, by acquittal, acquit the guilty." Admonished, therefore, by this edict of the law, it was necessary for us to hear and discuss the cause of Primian, whom the holy people of the Church of Carthage had obtained as bishop into the fold of God, the elders of that same Church demanding it by letters; to the end that, all things being explained, we might either, as was to be desired, clear him as innocent; or surely show him as guilty to be condemned by his own merits. For most desirable was it to us, that the holy people of the Church of Carthage should rejoice to be exalted by such a bishop as would be held holy in all things and reprehensible in nothing. For this reason indeed the priest of the Lord ought to be such, that what the people could not avail for themselves before God, he himself may deserve to obtain for the people what he asks: because it is written, " If the people sin, the priest shall pray for them; but if the priest sin, who shall pray for him?"*—(And as he read it, treating it, he said:) The apostles too wrote to the peoples that they should pray for them, and the apostles, praying, said, *Forgive us our debts*[^39]; and the apostle John said, *We have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just, and he himself is the propitiation for our sins*[^40]. But that was written about that priest whom these men do not understand, that the people might be admonished in the prophecy that they ought to recognize such a priest, for whom no one could pray. But who is he for whom no one prays, except the one who intercedes for all? Because, then, the priesthood was then Levitical, where the priest entered into the holies, and offered victims for the people; but it had an image, not the truth, of a certain future priest; for then the priests themselves too were sinners, like other men: God, wishing through prophecy to admonish the people that such a priest was already to be desired, who would intercede for all, and for whom no one would pray, designating such a one, admonished and said, *If the people sin, the priest shall pray for them; but if the priest sin, who shall pray for him?* And so, O people, choose such a priest, for whom you are not compelled to pray, but of whose prayer for you you can be secure. He is our Lord Jesus Christ, the one priest, the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^41]. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*The scandals of Primian, then, and his singular wickedness, so provoked the heavenly judgment against him, that it was necessary utterly to cut off the author of these crimes: who, recently ordained,—*(And as he read it, treating it, he said:) Now, now his crimes are spoken. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*impelling the presbyters of the aforesaid people to the conspiracy of an impious plot, demanded of them, as by a kind of precarious right, that they should, without hesitation, promise to lend their consent to condemning four deacons, excellent men, approved by singular merits, namely Maximian, Rogatian, Donatus, and likewise Salgamius.*—(And as he read it, treating it, he said:) Among these four was that author of the schism, cutting a piece from a piece, and not grieving that he himself was cut off from the whole. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Stupefied at his evil presumption, when they had refused the matter by silence, he did not hesitate to fulfill by himself the crime he had devised, to such a degree that he thought a sentence should be brought forth against the deacon Maximian, a man, as is known to all, innocent, without cause, without accuser, without witness, absent and lying in bed.*—(And as he read it, treating it, he said:) See the crime! (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Who already before would condemn clerics with no unlike fury. For when he was joining incestuous persons, against the law and the decrees of all the priests, to holy communion, and when, with the greatest part of the people opposing, he was approached even by letters of the most noble elders, that he should himself correct what he had committed, possessed by his own rashness he disdained to amend it. Moved, therefore, by these things, the elders of the aforesaid Church sent letters and legates to the whole assembly, by which not without tears they besought that we should come to them the more fervently, in order that, the balance being weighed, the charges being explored, the reputation of the Church might be cleared. When, accordingly, we came according to the letters of the aforementioned, he, growing hot with his own characteristic disposition, utterly declined our coming.*—(And as he read it, treating it, he said:) You know what was charged against him, that the party of Donatus has now been made incestuous. This was in their rule: Such as those were with whom there had been communion, such all became, and the whole mass. And so, if these men speak the truth, the whole party of Donatus is now incestuous. Let the Numidians plainly come out, and say: It does not pertain to us if you have admitted to your communion those incestuous men, whoever they are: could it harm us, set so far away? If, then, you who are in Numidia do not wish what is done at Carthage to harm you; could what is done in Africa harm the whole world? Always whence they defend themselves, thence they accuse themselves, and excuse us. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*He utterly declined our coming.* (And as he read it, he said:) Which they complained about Caecilian. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Who, everywhere kicking with rebellious spirit, remained in evil, so that, having hired a multitude of abandoned men—*(And as he read it, he said:) Now this is more. This they did not say about Caecilian: see what things. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*and having procured officers, they besieged the doors of the basilicas,—*(And as he read it, he said:) Lest the bishops should enter. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*who forbade us the faculty of entering and performing the solemnities. Whether it befits a bishop to do this, whether it is lawful for Christians to commit it, whether the Gospels attest this, let whoever is a lover or asserter of the truth prove or judge. For this a brother once our own inflicted on us, which a stranger would never do.*—(And as he read it, he said:) What more? They say many things and condemn the man: but let us now read the condemnation itself. (And as he treated it, he read out:)—*We have decreed, all the priests of God, the Holy Spirit being present, this same Primian: first, that he subrogated other bishops over living bishops; that he mingled incestuous persons with the communion of the saints; that he attempted to constrain presbyters to enter a conspiracy; that he had the presbyter Fortunatus thrown into a sewer, when he had succored the sick with baptism; that he refused communion to the presbyter Demetrius, to compel him to disown his son; that the same presbyter was rebuked, because he had received bishops in hospitality; that the aforesaid Primian sent a multitude to overthrow the houses of Christians; that bishops together with clerics were besieged, and afterward stoned by his satellites; that the elders were beaten in the basilica, because they took it ill that the Claudianists were admitted to communion; that he thought innocent clerics were to be condemned; that he was unwilling to present himself to us to be heard, when he barred with a multitude and with officials the doors of the basilicas, lest we should enter; that he injuriously rejected the legates sent by us to him; that he usurped many places, first by force, then by judicial authority.*—(And as he read it, he said:) A passer-over of the things taken away: though the apostle Paul says, *Dare any of you, having a matter against another, be judged before the unjust, and not before the saints?*[^42] See what a crime they charged against him, that he was unwilling to plead about the places before bishops, but before a judge. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*Besides his other unlawful deeds, which for the honor of our pen we have passed over in silence, he is condemned in perpetuity from the priestly assembly: lest, he being soothed, the Church of God be stained either with contagion or with some crime. This same thing the apostle Paul exhorts and admonishes: " Now we command you, brothers, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you withdraw from every brother walking disorderly." And so, not unmindful of the purity of the Church, we have thought it conducive to admonish all the holy fellow priests, and all clerics, and all peoples who remember themselves to be Christians, by this our Tractatoria, that all should, with diligent care, shun his communion, as of one condemned. For he himself will render account of his own destruction, who shall attempt, by not hearing this our decree, to violate it. It has pleased us indeed, and the Holy Spirit, that a time be reserved for the slow to convert, on this condition, that whoever of the fellow priests or clerics, unmindful of their own salvation, shall, from the day of the condemnation of the aforesaid Primian, that is, from the eighth day before the Kalends of July to the eighth day before the Kalends of January, have less withdrawn from the communion of the condemned Primian, be constrained by such a sentence. The laity also, unless they shall, from the aforesaid day of his condemnation, within the day of the coming Easter, have separated themselves from his fellowship, that no one can, except through penance, if indeed they remember themselves, be reformed to the Church. I, Victorinus, bishop of Munatiana, have subscribed. I, Fortunatus, bishop of Dionysiana, have subscribed. I, Victorianus, bishop of Carcabiana, have subscribed. I, Florentius of Adrumetum, bishop, have subscribed. I, Migginus of Elephantaria, bishop, have subscribed. I, Innocentius, bishop of Thebalta, have subscribed. I, Miggin, on behalf of my colleague Salvius, bishop of Membressa, have subscribed. I, Salvius, bishop of Ausafa, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Sabrata, have subscribed. I, Gemelius of Tanabae, bishop, have subscribed.*—(And as he read it, he said:) From among those very condemners subscribed also Praetextatus of Assuras, and Felicianus of Musti. (And as he treated it, he read out the following:)—*I, Praetextatus, bishop of Assuras, have subscribed. I, Maximianus, bishop of Stabata, have subscribed. I, Datianus, bishop of Camiceta, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Fisciana, have subscribed. I, Theodorus, bishop of Usula, have subscribed. I, Victorianus, by order of my colleague bishop Agnosius, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Cebresuta, have subscribed. I, Natalicus, bishop of Thelae, have subscribed. I, Pomponius, bishop of Macriana, have subscribed. I, Pancratius, bishop of Baliana, have subscribed. I, Ianuarius, bishop of Aquae, have subscribed. I, Secundus, bishop of Iacondiana, have subscribed. I, Pascasius of Vicus Augusti, bishop, have subscribed. I, Creso, bishop of Coniustiacum, have subscribed. I, Rogatianus, bishop, have subscribed. I, Maximianus, bishop of Erummina, have subscribed. I, Benenatus, bishop of Tuguta, have subscribed. I, Ritanus, bishop, have subscribed. I, Gaianus, bishop of Tiguala, have subscribed. I, Victorinus, bishop of Leptis Magna, have subscribed. I, Guntasius, bishop of Benefa, have subscribed. I, Quintasius, bishop of Capsa, have subscribed. I, Felicianus, bishop of Musti, have subscribed. I, Victorianus, by delegation of bishop Migginus, have subscribed. I, Miggius, bishop, have subscribed. I, Latinus, bishop of Mugia, have subscribed. I, Proculus, bishop of Girba, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Sabrata, on behalf of my brother and colleague Marratius, have subscribed. I, Proculus of Girba, on behalf of my colleague Gallio, have subscribed. I, Secundianus, bishop of Prisiana, have subscribed. I, Helpidius, bishop of Tusdrita, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Samurdata, have subscribed. I, Getulicus, bishop of Victoriana, have subscribed. I, Annibonius, bishop of Robaute, have subscribed. Likewise I, Annibonius, asked by my colleague the bishop of Augendiara, have subscribed. I, Tertullus, bishop of Abita, have subscribed. I, Primulianus, bishop, have subscribed. I, Secundinus, bishop of Arusiana, have subscribed. I, Maximus, bishop of Pittana, have subscribed. I, Crescentianus, bishop of Murra, have subscribed. I, Donatus, bishop of Belma, have subscribed. I, Perseverantius, bishop of Teberte, have subscribed. I, Faustinus, bishop of Bina, have subscribed. I, Victor, bishop of Altiburita, have subscribed.* [*All, fifty-three in number.*] (And as he read it, treating it, he said:)

**21.** Deign to attend a little. This is your condemnation. We say to him: What do you wish? Should it have weight, or not have weight? I favor you: I say plainly that all these spoke false things against you; and hear why I would believe it: Because before other judges you obtained your cause, and these were condemned. If, then, for this reason I believe you innocent, because, not going out to the factious, you showed your innocence elsewhere, so that those who condemned you deserved condemnation; deign to accept Caecilian as innocent, who was unwilling to go out to your elders, and so kept his cause for the whole world, just as you kept yours for the council of the Numidians. If the see of Bagai made you innocent, how much more shall the Apostolic See make him so? Or do you wish those to avail who first condemned? If they avail; they avail against you. For those neither availed nor will avail against Caecilian: yet attend to what the judges decide against you.

**22.** Now here they dare to say: But we who afterward condemned the Maximianists were more in number. Let your sentence, then, avail against Felician, and theirs will avail against Caecilian. When they held a council at Bagai, they condemned Felician too: now Felician is within: either a guilty man was received, or an innocent man was condemned. If, then, you receive a guilty man for the peace of Donatus; yield to all the nations for the peace of Christ: but if through your error an innocent man was condemned; could three hundred and ten err in condemning Felician, and could seventy not err in condemning Caecilian? What, then, do you say? When you hear it said to you, "The Maximianists condemned you first," you run back, and say: But we were more in number who condemned the Maximianists. To both you are quickly answered, that those elders of yours too first condemned Caecilian. If the first avail, let the Primianists yield to the council of the Maximianists: if the more in number avail, let the Donatists yield to the whole world: nothing, I judge, is more just. The Maximianists are few: but they are first. The guilty does not make another guilty. If you think this, how could you, condemned, condemn? because among the condemners he himself too was inscribed, nor did they keep a place for one pleading his cause. But otherwise with Caecilian: a place was kept for him as a man pleading his cause, as the very sentence has it; because he was not received into communion except cleared. But this man is found here condemned by the judges, there among the judges condemning. But suppose this was the equity of the council of Bagai: we grant the whole, plainly. The Maximianists condemned you wrongly: those elders of yours condemned Caecilian wrongly too. You cleared yourself at Bagai: he cleared himself in the overseas judgment. To this judgment the whole world consented. What will you say? We are more in number than the Maximianists. Be more in number: let us, then, deal with number: see how great the difference is. The Maximianists condemned you absent, when you were unwilling to go out to them: this indeed is alike, for so those too condemned Caecilian absent, when he had avoided their faction: but you in turn had sentences pronounced against them absent in the council of Bagai: whereas Caecilian, present, was cleared with his adversary present. Then another great difference: the Numidian judges before whom you might clear yourself, you yourself approached, you appointed; the Maximianists did not ask for them: but before those judges Donatus was conquered by Caecilian, whom the party of Donatus had demanded as judges. The Maximianists now answer you, and rightly say: We first came to you from your province, bishops of the diocese belonging to you; and we wished to hear your cause: you despised us, you did not go out to us. If you feared our judgment, let us at least choose judges together, do not go to whomever you wished. See how great the difference. But then the Donatists themselves by their letters petitioned the Emperor, that he should appoint judges: they disapproved those before whom they were conquered, whom, before they were conquered, they had petitioned for: others were given at their request, and there they were conquered: they appealed to the Emperor, and there they were conquered. The Maximianist was conquered once, absent, and is silent: and the Donatist, conquered three times, present, is not silent?

**23.** But you contend about number with the Maximianists. As I said, I favor you. Three hundred and ten are more than a hundred, or however many there were who condemned Primian from the party of Maximian: do you think that thousands of bishops throughout the whole world who condemned Donatus from the party of Caecilian have no weight with you? But you will say to me: Did thousands of bishops from the whole world condemn the Donatists? Excellently, they did not condemn them. Why did they not condemn them? Because they were not present at the judgment. If they were not present at the judgment, for this reason they did not condemn them, because they utterly did not know that cause. From the innocent why did you separate yourself? There comes here to you from the whole world one baptized, whom you wish to rebaptize: and you, now exercising a deadly ministry, and wishing to repeat what is given once and is not lost, he approaches with a loud voice and groaning, and says to you: What is it that you wish to do? to rebaptize me? says some man of Mesopotamia, some Syrian, some man of Pontus, or one set further off. You answer: Because you do not have baptism. Why? Read the Epistles of the Apostle written to me. There comes some man from Galatia, from Pontus, there comes some man from Philadelphia, to which Churches John wrote; there comes one from Colossae, there comes one from Philippi, from Thessalonica: Do I not have baptism, I to whom the Apostle wrote, through whom you have it? Do you dare to read my epistle, you who detest my peace?
