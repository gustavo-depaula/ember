# On the Same Psalm 118 (19)

**1.** [v. 81.] With the Lord's help, we have undertaken to consider and expound this part of this great psalm, where he says: *My soul has fainted for your salvation, and in your word I have hoped.* Not every fainting is to be thought either of fault or of penalty: there is also a praiseworthy or desirable fainting. For since these two are contrary to each other, to advance and to faint, advance is more usually taken in a good sense, fainting in a bad, when there is not added or understood "in what" one advances or faints; but when it is added, to advance can be evil, and to faint good. For the Apostle plainly said, *Avoid profane novelties of words: for they will advance much toward impiety*[^1]; he says too of certain men, *They will advance to the worse*[^2]. So too the fainting from good to evil is evil, the fainting from evil to good is good. For by a good fainting it is said, *My soul longs and faints for the courts of the Lord*[^3]. So too here he does not say "It has fainted from your salvation," but *It has fainted for your salvation,* that is, toward your salvation, *my soul.* Good, then, is this fainting: for it indicates desire of the good, not indeed yet attained, but most eagerly and vehemently longed for. But who says this, except the chosen race, the royal priesthood, the holy nation, the people of acquisition[^4], from the origin of the human race to the end of this age, in those who in their own time have lived here, are living, will live, desiring Christ? Witness the most holy old man Simeon, who, when he had received him as an infant in his hands, said: *Now, O Lord, you dismiss your servant, according to your word, in peace; for my eyes have seen your salvation*[^5]. For he had received a divine answer that he would not taste death unless he had seen the Christ of the Lord. But such as the desire was in this old man, such it is to be believed to have been in all the saints of earlier times. Hence the Lord himself says to the disciples: *Many prophets and kings desired to see the things you see, and did not see them; and to hear the things you hear, and did not hear them*[^6]: so that their voice too may be recognized in this place: *My soul has fainted for your salvation.* Neither then, therefore, did this desire of the saints rest, nor now does it rest in the body of Christ which is the Church, until the end of the age, until there comes *the Desired of all nations,* as is promised through the Prophet[^7]. On account of which the Apostle says: *There is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will render to me on that day; and not to me only, but also to all who love his manifestation*[^8]. This desire, then, of which we now treat, rises up from the love of his manifestation; of which likewise he says: *When Christ your life shall appear, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^9]. The first times of the Church, then, before the Virgin's childbearing, had saints who desired the coming of his incarnation; but these times, since he ascended into heaven, have saints who desire his manifestation for the judging of the living and the dead. Nor did this desire of the Church rest at all from the beginning to the end of the age, except as long as he dwelt here in the flesh with the disciples; so that the voice of the whole body of Christ, groaning in this life, may fittingly be understood: *My soul has fainted for your salvation, and in your word I have hoped;* that is, in your promise; which hope makes us await with patience what is not seen by believers. Here too the Greek has that word which some of our interpreters preferred to render *I have hoped beyond hope;* because beyond doubt it will be more than can be said.

**2.** [v. 82.] *My eyes have fainted,* he says, *for your utterance, saying: When will you console me?* Behold again, in the eyes—but surely the inner ones—that praiseworthy and happy fainting, coming not from weakness of mind, but from strength of desire for God's promise: for he says this, *for your utterance.* But how do such eyes say, *When will you console me?* except when with such intent and expectation one prays and groans? For it is the tongue that is wont to speak, not the eyes; but the desire of prayer is in a way the voice of the eyes. But in that he says, *When will you console me?* he shows that he is, as it were, enduring delay. Hence too is that, *And you, O Lord, how long*[^10]? Which either comes about that the deferred gladness may come more sweet, or this is the sense of those who desire, that a span of time, even one that is short for the one giving help, is long for the one who loves. But the Lord knows what he does and when, who arranges all things in measure and number and weight[^11].

**3.** [v. 83.] But when spiritual desires burn, carnal desires without doubt grow cold: on account of which there follows: *For I have become like a wineskin in the frost; I have not forgotten your statutes.* Surely by the wineskin he wishes the flesh of this death to be understood, but by the frost the heavenly benefit, by which the lusts of the flesh grow numb, as though restrained by cold; and from this it comes about that the statutes of God do not slip from the memory, while nothing else is thought about; since what the Apostle says comes to pass: *Make no provision for the flesh in its lusts*[^12]. Therefore when he had said, *For I have become like a wineskin in the frost,* he added, *I have not forgotten your statutes;* that is, I have not forgotten precisely because I have become such. For the heat of cupidity grew numb, that the memory of love might glow.

**4.** [v. 84.] *How many are the days of your servant? When will you execute judgment on those who persecute me?* In the Apocalypse this is the voice of the martyrs, and patience is enjoined on them until the number of their brethren is fulfilled[^13]. The body of Christ, then, asks about its own days, which are to be in this age. And lest anyone should think that the Church will not be here before the end of the age comes, and that some time will be in this age in which the Church is no longer on earth, for that reason, when he had asked about his days, he added also about the judgment, plainly showing that, up to the judgment in which the vengeance on his persecutors is to come, the Church itself too will be on earth. But if anyone is troubled why he asks what, when the disciples asked it, the Master answered, *It is not for you to know the times which the Father has set in his own power*[^14]; why should we not believe that in this place of the psalm it was prophesied that they would ask this very thing, and that the voice of the Church, foretold here so long before, was fulfilled by their question?

**5.** [v. 85.] But what follows: *The unjust have told me delights; but not as your law, O Lord:* our interpreters chose so to render what the Greeks call by that name, which can in no way be expressed by a single Latin word, so that some said *delights,* some *fabulations;* so that not without reason it is taken that these are indeed exercises, but in discourse with a certain delight. Such things, in their various sects and professions, both secular literature has, and that of the Jews which is called Deuterosis, containing, besides the canon of the divine Scriptures, thousands of fables; the empty and erring talkativeness of heretics has them too. All these he wished to be understood as the unjust, by whom he says these things were told him—that is, delightful exercises in words: *But not,* he says, *as your law, O Lord;* because in it truth, not words, delights me.

**6.** [v. 86.] Finally he adds: *All your commandments are truth: they have persecuted me unjustly, help me.* And the whole sense hangs on what is above: *How many are the days of your servant? When will you execute judgment on those who persecute me?* For, that they might persecute me, they told me the delights of their discourses; but I set before them your law, which delighted me the more for this reason, that *all your commandments are truth;* not, as vanity abounds in their discourses. And through this *they have persecuted me unjustly,* because in me they persecuted nothing but the truth. Therefore *help me,* that I may strive for the truth even to death; because this too is your commandment, and therefore this too is truth.

**7.** [v. 87.] When the Church was doing this, it suffered what he added: *They have almost made an end of me on the earth;* with much slaughter of the martyrs, namely, while they confess and preach the truth. But because it was not said in vain, *Help me; But I,* he says, *have not forsaken your commandments.*

**8.** [v. 88.] And that he might be able to persevere to the end: *According to your mercy,* he says, *give me life, and I will keep the testimonies of your mouth;* which the Greek has as *martyria.* This ought not to be passed over in silence, on account of the most sweet name of the martyrs, who without doubt, when so great a cruelty of persecutors was raging that the Church was almost made an end of on the earth, would in no way have kept the martyria of God, unless there had come about for them what is here prayed: *According to your mercy give me life.* For they were given life, lest, by loving life, they should deny life, and, by denying life, should lose life: and so those who were unwilling to forsake the truth for the sake of life, by dying for the truth, lived.
