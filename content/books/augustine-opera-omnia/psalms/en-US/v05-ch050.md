# On the Same Psalm 118 (25)

**1.** [v. 121.] We now undertake to consider and treat these verses of this great psalm: *I have done judgment and righteousness; do not hand me over to those who harm me.* It is no wonder that he did judgment and righteousness, who above had begged that his flesh—that is, his carnal lusts, which are wont to hinder our judgment from being right—be pierced with nails by the fear of God, the chaste fear, surely. But although, whether right or wrong, "judgment" is named in the usage of our speech—whence in the Gospel it is said to men, *Do not judge by appearances, but judge a right judgment*[^1]—yet in this place "judgment" is so set down as though, if it has not been right, it ought not to be called judgment; otherwise it would not suffice to say *I have done judgment,* but it would be said, "I have done right judgment." In this manner the Lord Jesus spoke, where he says: *You have left the weightier matters of the Law: judgment, and mercy, and faith*[^2]. For here too judgment is so set down as though it were not judgment if it is perverse. And in many places of the divine Scriptures it is so set down, as that is: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^3]. And that in Isaiah: *I waited that he should do judgment; but he did iniquity*[^4]. He does not say: I waited that he should do just judgment, but he did unjust judgment; but as though it is therefore already judgment, because it is just; and is not judgment, what is unjust. But righteousness is not wont to be called good righteousness, or bad righteousness, as judgment is sometimes called good or bad; but by this very fact it is already good, because it is righteousness. So, then, custom of speaking holds that both good judgment and bad judgment are said; just as it holds that both a good judge and a bad judge are said: but good righteousness or bad righteousness is not said, just as a good righteous man and a bad righteous man are not said, because anyone is at once good if he is righteous. Righteousness, then, is a great and especially praiseworthy virtue of the mind, of which there is now no need to discourse more copiously. But judgment, when the more discriminating reckoning of speech sets it only in a good sense, is the operation of this virtue. For he who has righteousness judges rightly: indeed, according to this manner of speech, he who has righteousness judges; because he does not even judge, if he does not judge rightly. And by the name of righteousness in this place, not the virtue itself, but its work is signified. For who works righteousness in man, except him who justifies the ungodly—that is, by his grace makes the righteous out of the ungodly? Whence the Apostle says, *Being justified freely by his grace*[^5]. He does righteousness, then—that is, the work of righteousness—who has in himself righteousness, that is, the work of grace.

**2.** *I have done,* he says, *judgment and righteousness: do not hand me over to those who harm me;* that is: I have done just judgment; do not hand me over to those who for that reason persecute me. For some manuscripts too have: *Do not hand me over to those who persecute me;* for what is said in Greek some interpreted *those who harm,* some *those who persecute,* some *those who slander.* But I marvel that in all the manuscripts I could have at hand I have nowhere read *those who oppose;* when, without controversy, what in Greek is the term is in Latin called adversary. Praying, then, that he not be handed over by the Lord to those who oppose him, what does he pray, except what we pray when we say, *Lead us not into temptation*[^6]? For it is the adversary of whom the Apostle says, *Lest perhaps he who tempts has tempted you*[^7]. God hands over the one he forsakes. For that one does not deceive him whom this One does not forsake, granting in his will strength to the beauty of man: but from him who had said in his abundance, *I shall not be moved for ever*[^8], he turned away his face, and he was made troubled, and shown to himself. Whoever, therefore, has his flesh crucified by the chaste fear of God, and, corrupted by no carnal enticement, does judgment and the work of righteousness, ought to pray that he not be handed over to those who oppose him; that is, that, by fearing to suffer evils, he not yield to his persecutors toward doing evils. For from him from whom he receives the victory over concupiscence, lest he be dragged off by pleasure, from him also the strength of patience, lest he be broken by pain: since of him of whom it is said, *The Lord will give sweetness*[^9], of him also it is said, *For from him is my patience*[^10].

**3.** [v. 122.] Finally there follows: *Receive your servant unto good; let not the proud slander me.* They drive me to fall into evil; do you receive me unto good. But those who interpreted *Let them not slander me* followed the Greek expression, less usual to the Latin tongue. Or perhaps it has its force when it is said, *Let them not slander me,* which it would have if it were said, Let them not take me by slandering?

**4.** [v. 123.] But many slanders of the proud can be understood, by whom Christian humility is despised; but that one is the greatest, if in this place the proud are taken to be men, that they slander us as worshiping one who is dead. For Christian humility itself is conveyed by the death of Christ, and divinely commended. But this slander is common to both kinds of unbelievers, that is, to Jews and Gentiles. Heretics too have their slanders, proper to each several heresy: schismatics too have them, all of whom pride has cut off from the structure of the members of Christ. But how great and of what kind is the slander of the devil himself, by which he slandered the righteous man, saying, *Does Job worship the Lord for nothing*[^11]? The slanders of all these proud ones, like the venoms of serpents, are overcome when, with most vigilant and most diligent piety, Christ crucified is regarded. For which, to be prefigured, Moses, with God's mercy and command, lifted up in the desert the likeness of a serpent on wood, that the likeness of sinful flesh to be crucified might be prefigured in Christ[^12]. Gazing on this saving cross, we drive out all the poison of the slandering proud: which this man too, as it were gazing very intently, says: *My eyes have fainted for your salvation, and for the utterance of your righteousness.* For God made Christ himself, on account of the likeness of sinful flesh, to be sin for us, that we might be the righteousness of God in him[^13]. For the utterance, then, of this righteousness of God he says that his eyes have fainted, gazing ardently and thirstily, while, mindful of human weakness, he longs for the divine grace in Christ.

**5.** [v. 124.] On account of which there follows: *Deal with your servant according to your mercy;* not, surely, according to my righteousness. *And teach me,* he says, *your statutes:* those, without doubt, by which God makes the righteous, not they themselves.

**6.** [v. 125.] *I am your servant.* For it did not go well with me, when I wished to be my own free man, not your servant. *Give me understanding, and I shall know your testimonies.* This petition is never to be left off. For it does not suffice to have received understanding, and to have learned the testimonies of God, unless it is always received, and in a way always drunk from the fountain of eternal light. For the testimonies of God, the more understanding anyone becomes, the more and more they are known.

**7.** [v. 126.] *It is time,* he says, *for the Lord to act.* For more manuscripts have this; not, as some, *O Lord.* What time, then, or what acting did he wish to be understood *for the Lord?* That, indeed, which a little before he had said: *Deal with your servant according to your mercy;* this is the time for the Lord to act. But what is it, except the grace which was revealed in Christ in his own time? Of which time the Apostle says: *But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son*[^14]. On account of which elsewhere too, adding a prophetic testimony, where he said, *In an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you*[^15], *Behold, now,* he says, *is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation*[^16]. But what is it that, as though wishing to show that it is time for the Lord to act, he immediately added: *They have scattered your law;* as though it were time for the Lord to act because the proud scattered his law—they who, ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the righteousness of God[^17]? For what is, *They have scattered your law,* except, By the iniquity of transgression, they did not keep its integrity? It was fitting, then, that to the proud, presuming on the freedom of their own choice, a law should be given, by transgressing which whoever were pricked might be humbled, and now run, not through the law, but through faith, to the grace that comes to aid. The law being scattered, then, it was time that, through the only-begotten Son of God, mercy should be sent. For the law entered in, that the offense might abound: by which offense the law was scattered; and at the now opportune time Christ came, that, where the offense had abounded, grace might superabound[^18].

**8.** [v. 127.] *Therefore,* he says, *I have loved your commandments above gold and topaz.* Grace brings it about that the commandments of God are fulfilled by love, which by fear they could not be. For by the grace of God *love is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^19]. On account of which the Lord himself too says, *I have not come to destroy the law, but to fulfill it*[^20]; and the same apostle, *Love is the fulfilling of the law*[^21]. Therefore, *above gold and topaz.* For this too is read in another psalm: *Above gold and much precious stone*[^22]. For they say that topaz is a much precious stone. But in the old testament, not understanding the grace lying hidden, as if by an interposed veil—which was signified when they could not gaze on the face of Moses—they tried, for the sake of an earthly and carnal reward, to do the commandments of God, and did not; because they loved not the commandments themselves, but something else. Whence those were not the works of the willing, but rather the burdens of the unwilling. But when the commandments themselves are loved above gold and much precious stone, every earthly reward is cheap in comparison with the commandments themselves; nor are any other goods of man at all comparable to these goods by which man himself is made good.

**9.** [v. 128.] *Therefore,* he says, *I was directed to all your commandments.* Surely I was directed, because I loved; and I clung by love to those right things, that I too might become right. Now what he adds was consequent: *Every,* he says, *unjust way I have hated.* For whence could it come about that one loving the right way did not hate the unjust way? For just as, if he loved gold and a precious stone, he would surely hate whatever could bring him loss of such things: so, because he loved the commandments of God, he hated the way of iniquity, as some most monstrous rock on a sea voyage, where shipwreck of such precious things must be suffered. That this may not happen, he sails far from it, who voyages on the wood of the cross with the merchandise of the divine commandments.
