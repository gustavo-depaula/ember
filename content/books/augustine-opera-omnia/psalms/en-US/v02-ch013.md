# Exposition of Psalm 41

**1.** [v. 2] It is long since our soul has desired to rejoice with you in the word of God, and to greet you in him who is our help and our salvation. What, then, the Lord gives, hear through us, and in him exult with us in his word, and in his truth and in his love. For we have taken up a psalm, of which we are to speak to you, suited to your desire. For the psalm itself began from a certain holy desire, and he who sings thus says: *As the deer desires for the fountains of waters, so my soul desires for you, O God*[^1]. Who, then, is it that says this? If we will, it is we. And why do you seek outside who it may be, when it is in your power to be what you seek? Yet it is not one man, but it is one body: but the body of Christ is the Church. Nor is such a desire found in all who enter the Church: but yet whoever have tasted the sweetness of the Lord, and recognize in this Canticle what is savory to them, let them not think themselves alone; but let them believe that such seeds are scattered in the Lord's field, throughout the whole world, and that this is the voice of a certain Christian unity: *As the deer desires for the fountains of waters, so my soul desires for you, O God.* And indeed it is not badly understood to be the voice of those who, being catechumens, hasten to the grace of the holy laver. Whence also this psalm is solemnly sung, that they may so desire the fountain of the remission of sins, *as the deer desires for the fountains of waters.* Let this be so, and let this understanding have its place in the Church, both true and solemn. Yet, brothers, it seems to me that even in Baptism such a desire is not yet satisfied for the faithful: but perhaps, if they know where they are pilgrims, and whither they must pass over, they are even more ardently inflamed.

**2.** [v. 1] In short, its title is: *To the end, for understanding, to the sons of Korah, a psalm*[^2]. We find the sons of Korah in other titles of the Psalms too, and we remember to have already treated, and spoken what this name means: yet now this title is to be recalled, in such a way that what we have already said does not, as it were, prejudge us, so that we should not say it again: for not everywhere we said it were all present. Korah was perhaps some man, as he was, and had sons who were called the sons of Korah: yet let us search the secret of the sacrament, that this name may bring forth the mystery with which it is pregnant. For it is a matter of great sacrament, that Christians be called the sons of Korah. Whence the sons of Korah? Sons of the bridegroom, sons of Christ. For they were called Christians, sons of the bridegroom. Why, then, is Christ Korah? Because Korah is interpreted "Bald." This is much more remote. I was asking why Christ is Korah: I ask more intently why Christ seems to pertain to "Bald-place." Does it not already occur that he was crucified in the place of Calvary? It occurs altogether. Therefore the sons of the bridegroom, sons of his passion, sons redeemed by his blood, sons of his cross, bearing on the forehead what the enemies fixed in the place of Calvary, are called the sons of Korah: to them this psalm is sung for understanding. Let us, then, be roused by understanding; and if it is sung to us, let us understand. What are we to understand? for what understanding is this psalm sung? I dare to say: for the invisible things of him, from the creation of the world, through the things that are made, are understood and seen[^3]. Come, brothers, take my avidity, share this desire with me: together let us love, together let us burn in this thirst, together let us run to the fountain of understanding. Let us desire, then, like the deer, the fountain; except that fountain which those to be baptized desire on account of the remission of sins, let us, already baptized, desire that fountain of which another Scripture says: *For with you is the fountain of life.* For he himself is both fountain and light: since *in your light we shall see light.* If he is both fountain and light; rightly he is also understanding, because he both satisfies the soul avid to know; and everyone who understands is illumined by a certain light, not corporeal, not carnal, not exterior, but interior. There is, then, brothers, a certain light within, which those do not have who do not understand. Whence the Apostle now addresses those who desire this fountain of life, and crop something from it, beseeching, and saying: *That you now no longer walk as the Gentiles also walk in the vanity of their mind, darkened in understanding, alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them, on account of the blindness of their heart*[^4]. If, then, those are darkened in understanding, that is, because they do not understand, they are darkened; therefore those who understand are illumined. Run to the fountains, desire the fountains of waters. With God is the fountain of life and an inexhaustible fountain: in his light is an undarkenable light. Desire this light, a certain fountain, a certain light such as your eyes do not know; to seeing which light the inner eye is prepared, to drinking which fountain the inner thirst burns. Run to the fountain, desire the fountain: but do not just anyhow, do not run like just any animal; run like the deer. What is, like the deer? Let there be no slowness in running, run nimbly, nimbly desire the fountain. For we find a mark of swiftness in the deer.

**3.** [v. 2] But perhaps Scripture wished us to consider not this alone in the deer, but also something else. Hear what else is in the deer. It kills serpents, and after the killing of the serpents it burns with a greater thirst, the serpents being slain it runs the more keenly to the fountains. The serpents are your vices: consume the serpents of iniquity, then you will the more desire the fountain of truth. Greed perhaps hisses something dark in you, and hisses against the word of God, hisses against the precept of God: and because it is said to you, Despise something, that you may not do iniquity; if you prefer to do iniquity, rather than despise some temporal advantage, you choose to be bitten by the serpent, rather than to slay the serpent. When, therefore, you still favor your vice, your cupidity, your greed, your serpent; when do I find in you such a desire, by which you may run to the fountain of waters? when do you covet the fountain of wisdom, while you still labor in the poison of malice? Kill in yourself whatever is contrary to truth: and when you see yourself as it were free from perverse cupidities, do not remain as though there were nothing to desire. For there is something to which you may lift yourself; if you have already wrought in yourself, so that there be no hinderer against you. For perhaps you will now say to me, if you are a deer: God knows that I am no longer greedy, that I no longer covet anyone's property, that I do not burn with the cupidity of adultery, that I do not pine away with the hatred and envy of anyone, and other such things; you will say: I do not have these things: and you seek perhaps whence you may be delighted. Desire whence you may be delighted; desire *for the fountains of waters*: God has whence to refresh you, and to fill you coming to him, and thirsting after the killing of the serpents, like a swift deer.

**4.** There is another thing which you may note in the deer. It is handed down of the deer, and by some they have even been seen; for such a thing would not be written of them, unless it were first seen: it is said, then, that the deer, whether when they walk in their column, or when, swimming, they make for other parts of the lands, place the burdens of their heads upon one another in turn, so that one goes before, and those follow who place their heads upon him, and above them others following, and then others, until the column ends: but that one who bore the weight of the head in the first place, wearied, returns to the rear, that another may succeed him, to bear what he was bearing, and that he may refresh his weariness, his head being laid, as the others too were laying theirs: so by bearing in turn what is heavy, they both accomplish the way, and do not desert one another. Does not the Apostle address certain deer, saying: *Bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^5]?

**5.** [v. 3] Such a deer, then, set in faith, not yet seeing what he believes, desiring to understand what he loves, suffers also adversaries not deer, darkened in understanding, set in inner darkness, blinded by the cupidity of vices; moreover insulting and saying to the man who believes, and who does not show what he believes: *Where is your God?*[^6] What, then, this deer did against these words, let us hear, that we too, if we can, may do it. First he expressed his thirst: *As the deer,* he says, *desires for the fountains of waters, so my soul desires for you, O God.* What if the deer desires for the fountains of waters for the sake of washing? Whether, then, for the sake of drinking or of washing, we do not know. Hear what follows, and do not ask: *My soul has thirsted for the living God.* What I say: *As the deer desires for the fountains of waters, so my soul desires for you, O God,* this I say: *My soul has thirsted for the living God.* For what did it thirst? *When shall I come and appear before the face of God?* This is what I thirst, to come and appear. I thirst in pilgrimage, I thirst on the way: I shall be satisfied at the arrival. But *when shall I come?* And what is quick to God, is slow to desire. *When shall I come and appear before the face of God?* From that desire is also this, from which it is cried elsewhere: *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this I will seek, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life.* Why this? *That I may contemplate,* he says, *the delight of the Lord*[^7]. *When shall I come and appear before the face of the Lord?*

**6.** [v. 4] Meanwhile, while I meditate, while I run, while I am on the way, before I come, before I appear: *My tears have been bread to me day and night, while it is said to me daily: Where is your God?*[^8] *They have been to me,* he says, not bitterness, but *bread.* Sweet to me were those very tears: thirsting for that fountain, because I could not yet drink, the more avidly I ate my tears. For he did not say: My tears have been made my drink, lest he should seem to have desired them as the fountains of waters: but, that thirst being preserved by which I burn, by which I am snatched to the fountains of waters, my tears have been made my bread, while I am deferred. And surely, eating his tears, without doubt he thirsts the more for the fountains. For day and night my tears have been made my bread. That food which is called bread, men eat by day, by night they sleep: but the bread of tears is eaten both by day and by night: whether you take the whole time, day and night; or understand the day for the prosperity of this world, but the night for the adversity of this world. Whether, he says, in the prosperous things of the world, or in the adverse things of the world, I pour out the tears of my desire, I do not abandon the avidity of my desire: and when it is well in the world, it is ill with me, before I shall appear before the face of God. Why, then, do you compel me to rejoice, as it were, if some prosperity of this world has smiled? is it not deceitful? is it not fluid, falling, mortal? is it not temporal, flitting, transitory? does it not have more of deception than of delight? Why, then, should my tears not be made my bread in it too? Since even when the felicity of the world shines round about, as long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims away from the Lord; and *it is said to me daily: Where is your God?* Because if a pagan says this to me, I cannot say to him too, *Where is your God?* For he points to his god with his finger. For he stretches out his finger to some stone, and says: Behold, this is my god. *Where is your God?* When I have derided the stone, and he who pointed it out has blushed, he lifts his eye from the stone, looks up at heaven, and perhaps, pointing his finger to the sun, again says: Behold, this is my god. *Where is your God?* He found what he might show to the eyes of flesh: I, however, not as though I had not whom I might show, but he has not eyes with which I might show him. For he could show to the eyes of my body his god the sun: with what eyes shall I show the creator of the sun?

**7.** Yet, by hearing daily, *Where is your God?* and being fed on my daily tears, day and night I meditated on what I heard: *Where is your God?* I too myself sought my God, that if I could, not only might I believe, but also might see something. For I see what my God has made, but I do not see my God himself who made these things. But since, like a deer, I desire for the fountains of waters, and with him is the fountain of life, and the psalm was written for understanding to the sons of Korah, and the invisible things of God are seen, understood through the things that are made; what shall I do, that I may find my God? I will consider the earth: the earth was made. There is great beauty of the lands; but it has a craftsman. Great are the wonders of seeds and of the things that beget; but all these have a creator. I look at the greatness of the sea poured round, I am astonished, I marvel; I seek the craftsman: I look up at the heaven and the beauty of the stars; I admire the splendor of the sun, sufficient to bring forth the day, the moon consoling the darkness of night. These things are wonderful, these are to be praised, or even to be marveled at; for these are not earthly, but already heavenly. Not yet does my thirst stand there: I marvel at these, I praise these; but I thirst for him who made these. I return to myself, and I scrutinize who I myself am, who seek such things: I find that I have a body and a soul; one which I rule, the other by which I am ruled; the body to serve, the soul to command. I discern that the soul is something better than the body, and I see that the inquirer into such things is not the body, but the soul: and yet all these things which I surveyed, I recognize that I surveyed them through the body. I praised the earth, I knew it by the eyes; I praised the sea, I knew it by the eyes; the heaven, the stars, the sun and moon I praised, I knew them by the eyes. The eyes are members of the flesh, they are windows of the mind: he is within who sees through these; when by some thought he is absent, they open in vain. My God who made these things, which I see with the eyes, is not to be sought with these eyes. Let the mind itself also behold something through itself: whether there is something which I perceive not through the eyes, as colors and light; not through the ears, as song and sound; not through the nostrils, as the sweetness of odors; not through the palate and tongue, as taste; not through the whole body, as hardness and softness, rigor and heat, roughness and smoothness which I handle; but whether there is something within which I may see. What is, see within? That which is neither color, nor sound, nor odor, nor taste, nor heat, nor cold, nor hardness, nor softness. Let it then be told me, what color wisdom has. When we think of justice, and within, in the very thought, enjoy its beauty, what sounds to the ears? what rises like a vapor to the nostrils? what is brought to the mouth, what is handled and delights? And it is both within, and beautiful, and praised, and seen: and if these eyes are in darkness, the mind enjoys its light. What was that which Tobit saw, when, seeing, the blind man gave counsel of life to his son? There is, then, something which the mind itself, the ruler, governor, dweller of the body, sees; which it perceives not through the eyes of the body, not through the ears, not through the nostrils, not through the palate, not through the touch of the body, but through itself: and surely better what is through itself, than what is through its servant. For it sees itself through itself, and the mind itself, that it may know itself, sees itself. Nor surely, that it may see itself, does it seek the aid of bodily eyes: rather indeed from all the senses of the body, as from things hindering and clamoring, it withdraws itself to itself, that it may see itself in itself, that it may know itself in itself. But is the God of the mind anything such, as the mind is? God indeed cannot be seen except by the mind, yet not in such a way as the mind can be seen. For the mind seeks something which is God, of which let not those insult who say: *Where is your God?* It seeks a certain unchangeable truth, a substance without defect. The mind itself is not such: it fails, advances; knows, is ignorant; remembers, forgets; now wills that, now does not will it. This changeableness does not fall upon God. If I say: God is changeable, those will insult me who say: *Where is your God?*

**8.** [v. 5] Seeking, then, my God in visible and bodily things, and not finding him; seeking his substance in myself, as though he were something such as I am, and not finding this either; I perceive that my God is something above the soul. Therefore, that I might touch him: *These things I meditated, and I poured out my soul above myself*[^9]. When would my soul attain what is sought above my soul, unless my soul were poured out above itself? For if it remained in itself, it would see nothing other than itself: and when it saw itself, it would surely not see its God. Let my insulters now say: *Where is your God?* let them say it: I, as long as I do not see, as long as I am deferred, eat day and night my tears. Let them still say: *Where is your God?* I seek my God in every body, whether earthly or heavenly, and find him not: I seek his substance in my soul, and find it not: yet I meditated the inquiry into my God, and, through the things that are made, desiring to behold the invisible things of my God understood, *I poured out my soul above myself;* and there now remains none whom I may touch, except my God. For there is the house of my God, above my soul: there he dwells, thence he beholds me, thence he created me, thence he governs me, thence he counsels me, thence he rouses me, thence he calls me, thence he directs me, thence he leads me, thence he leads me through.

**9.** For he who has his highest house in secret, has also a tabernacle on earth. His tabernacle on earth is his Church, still on pilgrimage. But here he is to be sought, because in the tabernacle is found the way by which one comes to the house. For indeed, when I poured out my soul above myself to attain my God, why did I do this? *Because I will enter into the place of the tabernacle*[^10]. For outside the place of the tabernacle I shall wander, seeking my God. *Because I will enter into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God.* Into the place of the tabernacle I will enter, of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God. For now I marvel at many things in the tabernacle. Behold how many things I marvel at in the tabernacle! For the tabernacle of God on earth is the faithful men: I marvel in them at the obedience of their very members; because sin does not reign in them so as to obey its desires, nor do they present their members as arms of iniquity to sin, but present them to the living God in good works: I marvel that the bodily members serve the soul serving God. I regard also the soul itself obeying God, distributing the works of its action, bridling lusts, driving out ignorance, stretching itself to bear all hard and harsh things, expending justice and love on others. I marvel also at these virtues in the soul: but still I walk in the place of the tabernacle. I pass over these too: and although the tabernacle is wonderful, I am astonished when I come even to the house of God. Of which house he says in another psalm, when he had set himself a certain hard and difficult question, why in this earth it is for the most part well with the evil, and ill with the good, and says: *I undertook to know this, it is labor before me, until I enter into the sanctuary of God, and understand in the last things*[^11]. For there is the fountain of understanding, in the sanctuary of God, in the house of God. There he understood in the last things, and solved the question about the felicity of the iniquitous, and the labor of the just. How did he solve it? Because the evil, when they are here deferred, are kept for punishments without end; and the good, when they here labor, are exercised, that in the end they may obtain the inheritance. And this he knew in the sanctuary of God, he understood in the last things. Ascending the tabernacle, he came to the house of God. Yet, while he marvels at the members of the tabernacle, he was so led to the house of God, by following a certain sweetness, an interior and hidden delight, I know not what, as though from the house of God some instrument sounded sweetly: and while he walked in the tabernacle, hearing a certain interior sound, led by sweetness, following what sounded, withdrawing himself from all the din of flesh and blood, he came even to the house of God. For he so recalls his way and his leading, as though we said to him: You marvel at the tabernacle on this earth; how did you come to the secret of the house of God? *In the voice,* he says, *of exultation and confession, the sound of one celebrating a festivity.* When men celebrate festivities here, even of their luxury, they have the custom of setting up instruments before their houses, or of placing musicians, or any music serving and enticing to luxury. And when these things are heard, what do we who pass by say? What is going on here? And it is answered us, that there are certain feasts. They are celebrating, he says, a birthday, there are nuptials here; so that those songs may not seem foolish, but luxury be excused by festivity. In the house of God there is an everlasting festivity. For not something is celebrated there and passes away. The everlasting festival, the choir of angels: the present countenance of God, joy without failing. This festal day is such that it is neither opened by a beginning, nor closed by an end. From that eternal and perpetual festivity there sounds something tuneful and sweet to the ears of the heart: but only if the world does not make a din. To one walking in this tabernacle, and considering the wonders of God in the redemption of the faithful, the sound of that festivity soothes the ear, and snatches the deer to the fountains of waters.

**10.** [v. 6] But because, brothers, as long as we are in this body, we are pilgrims away from the Lord, and the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that thinks of many things: even if, somehow, the mists being dispersed, by walking through desire, we come at times to this sound, so that, by straining, we grasp something of that house of God; yet by a certain weight of our weakness we fall back to our accustomed things, and slip down to these usual things. And as there we had found whence we might rejoice, so here there will not be lacking what we may groan over. For indeed this deer, eating day and night his tears, snatched by desire to the fountains of waters, namely the interior sweetness of God, pouring out his soul above himself, that he might touch what is above his soul, walking into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God, and led by the pleasantness of an interior and intelligible sound, so as to despise all exterior things, and be snatched into interior things; yet is still a man, still here he groans, still he carries fragile flesh, still amid the scandals of this world he is imperiled. He looked back, then, to himself, as it were coming from there, and said to himself, set amid these sadnesses, and comparing these things with those which he had entered to see, and after seeing which he had gone out: *Why,* he says, *are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?*[^12] Behold, we have now been gladdened by a certain interior sweetness, behold, with the edge of the mind we have been able to behold something unchangeable, even if cursorily and snatchingly: why do you still trouble me, why are you still sad? For you do not doubt about your God. For you do not lack what you may say to yourself, against those who say: *Where is your God?* I have now perceived something unchangeable, why do you still trouble me? *Hope in God.* And as if his soul answered him in silence: Why do I trouble you, except because I am not yet there, where that sweet thing is, by which I was so snatched as it were in passing? Do I now drink of that fountain, fearing nothing? do I now dread no scandal? am I now secure about all lusts as though subdued and conquered? Does not the devil my enemy keep watch against me? does he not daily stretch out for me the snares of deception? Do you not wish that I trouble you, set in the world, and still a pilgrim away from the house of my God? But: *Hope in God,* he answers his troubling soul, and as it were rendering the reason of its perturbation, on account of the evils with which this world abounds. Meanwhile, dwell in hope. For hope that is seen is not hope: but if we hope for what we do not see, by patience we await it[^13].

**11.** [v. 7] *Hope in God.* Why *Hope*? *Because I will confess to him.* What will you confess to him? *The salvation of my countenance, my God*[^14]. From myself there cannot be salvation to me; this I will say, this I will confess: *The salvation of my countenance is my God.* For indeed, that he may fear in these things which he somehow knows as understood, he looked back again solicitous lest the enemy creep up: he does not yet say: I am safe on every side. For indeed, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, in ourselves we groan, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body. That salvation being made perfect in us, we shall be living in the house of God without end, and without end praising him to whom it was said: *Blessed are they who dwell in your house, forever and ever they shall praise you*[^15]. This is not yet, because the salvation which is promised is not yet: but I confess to my God in hope, and say to him: *The salvation of my countenance, my God.* For in hope we have been saved: but hope that is seen is not hope. Persevere, then, that you may arrive; persevere until salvation comes. Hear your very God speaking to you from within: *Endure the Lord, act manfully, and let your heart be strengthened, and endure the Lord*[^16]; since *he who perseveres to the end, he shall be saved*[^17]. *Why,* then, *are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me? Hope in God, because I will confess to him.* This is my confession, *The salvation of my countenance, my God.*

**12.** *Unto myself my soul has been troubled*[^18]. Is it troubled unto God? Unto me it has been troubled. By the unchangeable it was refreshed, by the changeable it was perturbed. I know that the justice of my God abides: whether mine abides, I do not know. For the Apostle terrifies me, saying: *He who thinks he stands, let him see lest he fall*[^19]. Therefore, because there is no firmness in me for myself, nor is there hope to me from myself: *unto myself my soul has been troubled.* Do you wish it not to be troubled? Let it not remain in yourself, and say: *To you, O Lord, I have lifted up my soul*[^20]. Hear this more plainly. Do not hope from yourself, but from your God. For if you hope from yourself, your soul is troubled unto you; because it has not yet found whence it may be secure about you. Therefore, since unto me my soul has been troubled, what remains but humility, that the soul may not presume of itself? What remains, but that it make itself altogether least, that it humble itself, that it may deserve to be exalted? Let it attribute nothing to itself, that to it may be attributed by him what is useful. Therefore, because unto me my soul has been troubled, and pride makes this perturbation: *Therefore I have remembered you, O Lord, from the land of Jordan and Hermon, from the little mountain*[^21]. Whence have I remembered you? From the little mountain, and from the land of Jordan. Perhaps from Baptism, where is the remission of sins. For indeed no one runs to the remission of sins, except one who is displeasing to himself; no one runs to the remission of sins, except one who confesses himself a sinner; no one confesses himself a sinner, except by humbling himself to God. Therefore *from the land of Jordan I have remembered you, and from the little mountain*: not from the great mountain; that from the little mountain you may make me great: *because he who exalts himself shall be humbled; and he who humbles himself shall be exalted*[^22]. But if you seek also the interpretations of the names, Jordan is "Their descent." Descend, then, that you may be lifted up; do not be lifted up, lest you be dashed down: *And from Hermon, the little mountain.* Hermon is interpreted "Anathematizing." Anathematize yourself, by being displeasing to yourself: for you will be pleasing to God, if you are pleasing to yourself. Therefore, because God grants us all good things, because he himself is good, not because we are worthy; because he is merciful, not because we have deserved it in anything; *from the land of Jordan and Hermon I have remembered God.* And because he is humbly remembered, exalted he will deserve to enjoy: because he is not exalted in himself who glories in the Lord.

**13.** [v. 8] *Deep calls to deep, in the voice of your cataracts*[^23]. I can perhaps finish the psalm, aided by your zeal, whose fervor I see. But about your labor I do not much care, that you hear it; when you see even me who speak so sweating in these labors. Looking at me laboring, surely you labor together: for I do not labor for myself, but for you. Therefore hear; because I see you willing. *Deep calls to deep, in the voice of your cataracts*: he said to God who remembered him from the land of Jordan and Hermon: this he said in wonder: *Deep calls to deep, in the voice of your cataracts.* What deep, that calls what deep? Truly, because this understanding is a deep. For a deep is a certain impenetrable, incomprehensible depth: and it is especially wont to be said of a multitude of waters. For there is the height, there the depth: which cannot be penetrated to the bottom. In short, in a certain place it was said: *Your judgments are a great deep*[^24]: Scripture wishing to commend this, that the judgments of God are not comprehended. What deep, then, calls what deep? If a deep is a depth, do we suppose that the heart of man is not a deep? For what is deeper than this deep? Men can speak, can be seen through the working of the members, be heard in speech: but whose thought is penetrated, whose heart is inspected? What he is doing within, what he can do within, what he is transacting within, what he is arranging within, what he wills within, what he does not will within, who shall comprehend? I think it is not absurd to understand man as the deep, of whom it is said elsewhere: *Man shall approach, and a deep heart, and God shall be exalted*[^25]. If, then, man is a deep, how does deep call to deep? Does man call to man? does he call as God is called? No. But he calls, it is said, calls to himself. For it was said of someone: He calls upon death: that is, he so lives that he calls death to himself. For there is no man who makes a prayer, and calls upon death: but by living badly men call upon death. *Deep calls to deep,* man to man. So wisdom is learned, so faith is learned, when deep calls to deep. The holy preachers of the word of God call to the deep. Are they not themselves also a deep? That you may know that they too are a deep, the Apostle says: *It is a small thing that I be judged by you, or by a human day.* But how great a deep, hear further: *For neither do I judge myself*[^26]. Do you believe so great a depth to be in man, which lies hidden from the very man in whom it is? How great a depth of weakness lay hidden in Peter, when he did not know what was being transacted within him, and rashly promised that he would die with the Lord or for the Lord! how great a deep he was! Which deep, yet, was bare to the eyes of God. For Christ foretold to him this, which he himself was ignorant of in himself. Therefore every man, though holy, though just, though advancing in many things, is a deep, and calls to a deep, when he preaches to man something of faith, something of truth, for the sake of eternal life. But then is the deep useful to the deep that is called, when it is done in the voice of your cataracts. *Deep calls to deep,* man gains man: yet not in his own voice, but *in the voice of your cataracts.*

**14.** Receive another understanding: *Deep calls to deep, in the voice of your cataracts*[^27]. I who tremble, when unto me my soul has been troubled, I feared vehemently at your judgments: for *your judgments are a great deep*[^28], and, *deep calls to deep*. For under this mortal flesh, laborious, sinful, full of troubles and scandals, subject to lusts, there is a certain damnation from your judgment: because you said to the sinner, *You shall die the death,* and, *In the sweat of your countenance you shall eat your bread*[^29]. This is the first deep of your judgment. But if men live badly here, *deep calls to deep*: because they pass from punishment to punishment, and from darkness to darkness, and from depth to depth, and from torment to torment, and from the ardor of cupidity into the flames of hell. Therefore this man perhaps feared this, when he said: *Unto myself my soul has been troubled: therefore I have remembered you, O Lord, from the land of Jordan and Hermon.* I ought to be humble. For I shuddered at your judgments, I feared vehemently your judgments: therefore *unto me my soul has been troubled.* And what judgments of yours did I fear? Are these judgments of yours small? They are great, they are hard, they are troublesome: but would that they were the only ones! *Deep calls to deep, in the voice of your cataracts.* Because you threaten, you say that even after those labors there remains another damnation: *In the voice of your cataracts, deep calls to deep.* Whither, then, shall I go from your face, and whither shall I flee from your spirit; if deep calls to deep, if after these labors more grievous ones are feared?

**15.** *All your suspensions and your waves have come in over me*[^30]. The waves in the things I feel, the suspensions in the things you threaten. My every suffering is your wave: my every threat is your suspension. In the waves this deep calls, in the suspensions it calls the other deep. In that I labor, all are your waves: in that you threaten more grievously, all your suspensions have come in over me. For he who threatens does not press, but suspends. But because you free, I have said this to my soul: *Hope in God, because I will yet confess to him*; he speaks the confession itself: he repeats the confirmation of hope: *the salvation of my countenance, my God.* Because evils are the more frequent, the sweeter will be your mercy.

**16.** [v. 9] Therefore it follows: *In the day the Lord has commanded his mercy, and by night he will declare it*[^31]. No one has leisure to hear in tribulation. Attend when it is well with you; hear when it is well with you: learn, when you are tranquil, the discipline of wisdom, and gather the word of God like food. For when anyone is in tribulation, what he secure heard ought to profit him. For in prosperous things God commands you his mercy, if you serve him faithfully; because he frees you from tribulation; but he does not declare to you that very mercy, which he commanded you by day, except by night. When tribulation itself comes, then he does not desert you with aid: he shows you that it was true what he commanded you by day. For it is written in a certain place: *Beautiful is the mercy of the Lord in the time of tribulation, like clouds of rain in the time of drought*[^32]. *In the day the Lord has commanded his mercy, and by night he will declare it.* He does not show you that he comes to your aid, unless tribulation comes to you, from which you may be delivered by him who promised you by day. Therefore we are admonished to imitate the ant. For as the prosperity of the world is signified by day, the adversity of the world is signified by night: so in another way the prosperity of the world is signified by summer, the adversity of the world is signified by winter. And what does the ant do? In summer it gathers what may profit it in winter. Therefore when it is summer, when it is well with you, when you are tranquil, hear the word of the Lord. For whence can it come about that in this storm of this world you cross this whole sea without tribulation? whence can it come about? to whom of men has this befallen? If it has befallen anyone, that very tranquillity is the more to be feared. *In the day the Lord has commanded his mercy, and by night he will declare it.*

**17.** [vv. 9-10] What, then, shall you do in this pilgrimage? what shall you do? *With me is a prayer to the God of my life*[^33]. This here I do, a deer thirsting and desiring for the fountains of waters, recalling the sweetness of that voice by which I was led through the tabernacle even to the house of God: as long as this body which is corrupted weighs down the soul: *With me is a prayer to the God of my life.* For not, that I may supplicate God, am I to buy from overseas places; nor, that my God may hear me, shall I sail, that I may bring frankincense and spices from afar, or lead a calf or a ram from the herd: *With me is a prayer to the God of my life.* Within I have a victim which I may immolate, within I have incense which I may set on, within I have a sacrifice by which I may bend my God: *A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit*[^34]. What sacrifice of a troubled spirit I have within, hear: *I will say to God: You are my upholder, why have you forgotten me?* For I so labor here, as though you had forgotten me. But you exercise me: and I know that you defer to me, you do not take away from me, what you promised: but yet, *why have you forgotten me?* As though from our voice our head too cried: *God, my God, why have you forsaken me?*[^35] *I will say to God: You are my upholder, why have you forgotten me?*

**18.** [v. 11] *Why have you cast me off?*[^36] from that height of the fountain of understanding of unchangeable truth, *Why have you cast me off?* Why, by the heaviness and weight of my iniquity, gaping now toward there, am I cast down to these things? This voice says in another place: *I said in my ecstasy,* where it saw something great, I know not what, by an excess of mind: *I said in my ecstasy: I am cast away from before your eyes*[^37]. For it compared these things in which it was with those to which it had been raised, and saw itself cast far away from before the eyes of God, as here too: *Why have you cast me off, and why do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me, while he breaks my bones,* that tempter the devil, with scandals everywhere increasing, by whose abundance the love of many grows cold? When we see the strong of the Church for the most part yield to scandals, does not the body of Christ say: The enemy *breaks my bones*? For the bones are the strong, and sometimes the strong themselves yield to temptations. When anyone considers these things from the body of Christ, from the voice of the body of Christ, does he not cry out: *Why have you cast me off, and why do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me, while he breaks my bones?* Not only my flesh, but even my bones: so that in those in whom some strength was thought to be, you see them yield in temptations, that the rest, the weak, may despair, when they see the strong succumb. How great these perils, my brothers!

**19.** [vv. 11-12] *They have reproached me who trouble me*[^38]. Again that voice: *While they say to me daily: Where is your God?* And especially these things they say in the temptations of the Church: *Where is your God?* How much the martyrs heard this, strong and patient for the name of Christ, how much it was said to them: Where is your God? Let him free you, if he can. For men saw their torments from without, the crowns within they did not see. *They have reproached me who trouble me, while they say to me daily: Where is your God?* And I, on account of these things, since unto me my soul has been troubled, what else shall I say to it than that: *Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?* And as if answering: Do you not wish that I trouble you, set here amid such great evils? sighing for good things, thirsting and laboring, do you not wish that I trouble you? *Hope in God, because I will yet confess to him.* He speaks the confession itself, he repeats the confirmation of hope: *The salvation of my countenance and my God.*
