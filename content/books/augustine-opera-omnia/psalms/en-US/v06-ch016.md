# Exposition of Psalm 136

**1.** [v. 1.] *Confess to the Lord, for he is good, for his mercy is forever*[^1]. This psalm contains the praise of God, and in all its verses it ends in the same way. Accordingly, although here many things are said in praise of God, yet most of all his mercy is commended, with whose most plain commendation he willed no verse to be closed, by which the Holy Spirit composed the Psalm. But I remember that in the one hundred fifth psalm, which begins similarly—since the manuscript which I was looking at does not have *forever* (in aeternum), but *his mercy is for the age* (in saeculum)—I asked what we ought rather to understand: for in the Greek language it is written what can be interpreted both 'for the age' and 'forever.' But what I there discussed, as I could, it is long to weave over again here too. But in this psalm even that same manuscript does not have *for the age*, which most have; but, *his mercy is forever.* And although, after the judgment by which at the end of the age the living and the dead are to be judged, the just being sent into eternal life, but the wicked into eternal burning[^2], there will not thereafter be those of whom God still has mercy; yet his mercy can rightly be understood to be forever, which he bestows on his holy and faithful ones: not because they will be wretched forever, and therefore will forever need his mercy; but because the very blessedness which he mercifully grants to the wretched, that they may cease to be wretched, and begin to be blessed, will have no end, therefore *his mercy is forever.* For that we will be just from unjust, healthy from weak, alive from dead, immortal from mortal, blessed from wretched, is his mercy. But that we will be so, will be forever: so *his mercy is forever.* Accordingly: *Confess to the Lord*, that is, by confessing praise the Lord; *for he is good.* Nor for this confession are you about to receive anything temporal; *for his mercy is forever*, that is, the benefit which he mercifully bestows on you is eternal. But where it has: *For he is good*, the Greek has *chrestos*: not as in the one hundred fifth psalm; what there is: *For he is good*, the Greek has *agathos*. Therefore some have interpreted that as, For he is sweet. But he is not just somehow good, but most excellently good.

**2.** [vv. 2-3.] Then it follows: *Confess to the God of gods; for his mercy is forever. Confess to the Lord of lords; for his mercy is forever*[^3]. Who, then, are the gods and lords, of which gods and lords he is God and Lord, who is the true God; is rightly often asked. And we find it written in another psalm that men too are called gods, as it is: *God stood in the synagogue of gods; but in the midst he distinguishes the gods*: and a little after: *I said: You are gods and all sons of the Most High; but you will die like men, and fall like one of the princes*[^4]. Which testimony the Lord too mentions in the Gospel, saying: *Is it not written in your Law, that, I said: You are gods? If he called those gods to whom the word of God came, and the Scripture cannot be broken; do you say of him whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world, You blaspheme, because I said: I am the Son of God*[^5]? So not because all are good, but because the word of God came to them, they were called gods. For if it were because all were good, he would not so distinguish them. For when he had said: *God stood in the synagogue of gods;* he did not say: In the midst he distinguishes gods and men, as if showing what difference there is between gods and men: but: *In the midst*, he says, *he distinguishes the gods.* Then it follows: *How long will you judge iniquity*? and the rest: which surely he says not to all but to certain ones, because he says it distinguishing; and yet *in the midst he distinguishes the gods.*

**3.** But it is asked, if men to whom the word of God came are called gods, whether the angels too are to be called gods; since to just and holy men the greatest reward is promised, equality with the angels. And in the Scriptures indeed I do not know whether it can, or can easily, be found, that angels are openly called gods; but when it had been said of the Lord God: *Terrible above all gods*, why he said this, he at once added, as it were by way of exposition: *For the gods of the nations are demons*[^6]. Above such gods he said the Lord is terrible in his saints, whom he made heavens, by whom the demons might be terrified. For so it follows: *But the Lord made the heavens.* So not: Gods without addition; but: The gods of the nations are demons: yet above he says: *Terrible above all gods;* he does not say, above all the gods of the nations, although he wished this to be understood, by adding what follows: *For the gods of the nations are demons.* But this in Hebrew is said not to be so written; but: *The gods of the nations are images.* And if this is true, all the more are the Seventy to be believed to have interpreted by the divine Spirit, by which Spirit those things too were said which are in the Hebrew letters. For by the working of the same Spirit, this too had to be said which was said: *The gods of the nations are demons*: that we might understand that thus too it was set down in the Hebrew: *The gods of the nations are images;* so that the demons which are in the images might rather be signified. For as to the images themselves, which in Greek are called idols, by which name we now use for the Latin, they have eyes and do not see; and the rest which are said of them precisely because they lack all sense: wherefore they cannot even be terrified, because, except for the things that have sense, they surely cannot be terrified. How, then, was it said of the Lord: *He is terrible above all gods, for the gods of the nations are images*, unless by the images are understood the demons, which can be terrified? Whence the apostle too says: *We know that an idol is nothing*[^7]: for this he referred to the earthly matter lacking sense. Likewise, lest anyone should think there is no living and sentient nature which delights in the sacrifices of the nations, he added: *But the things which the nations sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons, and not to God. I do not wish you to become companions of the demons*[^8]. If, then, nowhere in the divine words is it found that the holy angels are called gods, this cause especially occurs to me, lest by that name men should be built up to this, that they should render the ministry or service of religion (which in Greek is called liturgy, or latria) to the holy angels; which they themselves do not wish to be rendered by men, except to that God who is the God both of themselves and of men. Whence much more usefully they are called angels, who in Latin are called messengers; that by a name not of substance, but of office, we may sufficiently understand that they wish us to worship that God whom they announce. So the whole of this question the apostle briefly resolves, where he says: *For even if there are those that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth; as there are many gods and many lords, but for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things, and we in him, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we through him*[^9].

**4.** [v. 4.] So let us confess to the *God of gods, and the Lord of lords; for his mercy is forever. Who alone does great wonders*[^10]. Just as in the last parts of all the verses there is set: *For his mercy is forever;* so at the heads of all, although it is not set, it must be supplied: *Confess.* Which appears more evidently in the Greek. But it would appear in the Latin too, if our interpreters could have rendered the same expression. Which indeed in this verse they could, if they said, *to him doing wonders*: for what we have: *Who did wonders;* the Greek has, *to him doing wonders*; where necessarily *Confess* is supplied. And would that they had added at least the pronoun, so as to say: To him *who does;* or, to him *who did;* or, to him *who established*: because even so it would easily be understood that *Confess* must be supplied. But now it has become so obscure, that one who either has not known how to look at the Greek manuscript, or has neglected it, can think: *Who made the heavens, Who established the earth, Who made the lights, for his mercy is forever*, was said as though for this reason he made these things: *Because his mercy is forever*: when to his mercy belong those whom he frees from misery; but not so that we should understand it to have been of his mercy to make heaven and earth and the lights, since that is of his goodness, who created all things very good[^11]. But he created, that all things might be; this indeed is of his mercy, that he cleanse us from our sins, and free us from misery forever. So then the Psalm addresses us: *Confess to the God of gods, confess to the Lord of lords;* confess to him *who alone does great wonders;* confess to him *who made the heavens in understanding;* confess to him *who established the earth upon the waters;* confess to him *who alone made the great lights.* But why we should confess, he sets at the ends of all the verses: *For his mercy is forever.*

**5.** [vv. 5-10.] But what is: *Who alone does great wonders*? is it because he did many wonders through angels and through men? There are certain great things which God alone does, and he himself sets them out, saying: *Who made the heavens in understanding, Who established the earth upon the waters, Who alone made the great lights*[^12]: therefore here too he added, *alone*, because the other wonders which he is about to tell, he did through men. For when he had said: *Who alone made the great lights;* what those same lights are, he went on and opened: *The sun for power over the day, the moon and the stars for power over the night*[^13]. Then he begins to tell what he did through angels or through men: *Who struck Egypt with their firstborn*[^14]; and the rest. So the whole of creation God made, not surely through some creature, but alone. Of which creation he mentioned certain more excellent parts, from which we might think of the whole, the intelligible heavens and the visible earth: and since there are also visible heavens, by mentioning their lights he warned us to take the whole bodily heaven as made by him.

**6.** Although as to what he says: *Who made the heavens in understanding*, or, as others have interpreted, *in intelligence*, it can be asked whether he meant in this way to signify the intelligible heavens, or that God made the heavens in his own understanding, or in his own intelligence, that is, in his Wisdom, as it is written elsewhere: *You made all things in Wisdom*[^15], in that way intimating the only-begotten Word. But if it is so, that we take God to have made the heavens in his own understanding, why does he say this only of the heavens, when he made all things in that same Wisdom? Or was it to be expressed there only, so that in the rest too it could be understood, though unspoken: so that this is the sense: *Who made the heavens in understanding, Who established the earth upon the waters;* so that here too you supply, *in understanding: Who alone made the great lights, the sun for power over the day, the moon and the stars for power over the night;* surely, *in understanding*? How, then, *alone*, if this *in understanding*, or *in intelligence*, is the same as *in Wisdom*, that is, in the only-begotten Word? Or is it because the Trinity is not three gods, but one God, that it is referred to what God alone made, because he did not make creation through a creature?

**7**. But what is: *Who established the earth upon the waters*[^16]? For it is an obscure question; because the earth seems heavier, so that it is believed not to be carried by the waters, but rather to carry the waters. About which matter, lest we seem contentiously to defend our Scriptures against those who think they have found out these things by sure reasonings; whether it be so, or otherwise, we have what we may understand from close at hand: that the earth which is inhabited by men and contains the land animals, which in another way is called in the Scriptures 'the dry land,' as it is written: *Let the dry land appear, and God called the dry land earth*[^17], is founded upon the waters, in that it stands out above the surrounding waters. For a city said to be coastal, founded upon the sea, does not have the sea beneath it in the way that there are waters beneath the vaults of caves, or beneath ships floating above them; but it is said to be 'upon the sea' because it stands out above the sea lying below. So Pharaoh was said to have gone out *upon the water*: for so the Greek has, which some Latin copies have, *to the water*[^18]; so the Lord sat *upon the well*[^19]: in that both were higher than the river and the well; the one beside the river, the other beside the well.

**8.** But if these sayings signify also something else, which pertains more to us: *God made the heavens in understanding*, his spiritual saints, to whom he granted not only to believe, but also to understand divine things: those who cannot yet do this, and hold the most firm faith alone, are as it were figured below the heavens by the name of earth. And because in the Baptism which they received they stand firm with unshaken belief, therefore it was said: *He established the earth upon the waters.* Likewise, because it is written of the Lord Jesus Christ that in him are *all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden*[^20]; but that these two, that is, wisdom and knowledge, differ somewhat from each other, other words of the Scriptures too testify, especially holy Job speaking, where in a certain way each is defined; for he says: *But he said to man: Behold, devotion is wisdom, but to abstain from evil is knowledge*[^21]: not unfittingly we understand wisdom to be in the knowledge and love of that which always is, and abides unchangeably, which is God. For as to what he says: *Behold, devotion is wisdom*, it was said in Greek *theosebeia*: which, that it may be wholly expressed in Latin, can be said 'worship of God.' But *to abstain from evil*, which he said to be knowledge, what else is it than, in the midst of a crooked and perverse nation, as in the night of this world, to conduct oneself cautiously and prudently[^22]; so that, by abstaining from iniquity, one may not be confounded by the darkness, distinguished by the light of one's proper gift? So when in a certain place the apostle wished to show the harmonious variety of graces in the men of God, he set these two first, saying: *To one indeed is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom;* this I think to be: *The sun for power over the day: To another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit;* this is, *the moon.* Then in a certain way I think the *stars* are mentioned in what is said: *To another faith in the same Spirit, to another the gift of healings in the one Spirit, to another the workings of powers, to another prophecy, to another the discerning of spirits, to another kinds of tongues, to another the interpretation of tongues*[^23]. For there is none of these which is not necessary in the night of this world: when it has passed, these will not be necessary: therefore, *for power over the night.* For he says *for power*, whether *over the day* or *over the night*, so that there might be power to shine by day or by night; which in spiritual gifts is understood thus, because he gave power to become sons of God[^24]. *Who struck Egypt, with their firstborn.* He struck the world too, with the things that are thought foremost in the world.

**9.** [vv. 11-26.] *Who led Israel out of their midst.* He led his saints and faithful too out of the midst of the wicked. *With a mighty hand and a lofty arm.* What is mightier, what is loftier than he of whom it was said: *And the arm of the Lord, to whom has it been revealed*[^25]? *Who divided the Red Sea into divisions.* He divides too, so that one and the same Baptism is for some unto life, for others unto death. *And led Israel through the midst of it.* He leads too his renewed people through the bath of regeneration. *And shook off Pharaoh and his power in the Red Sea.* He swiftly destroys too the sin of his own and its guilt through Baptism. *Who led his people through in the desert.* He leads us through too in the dryness and barrenness of this world, that we may not perish in it. *Who struck great kings, and slew strong kings.* He strikes and slays too from us the diabolical and harmful powers. *Sihon king of the Amorites.* 'Useless shoot' or 'burning Temptation,' which is the interpretation of Sihon; king of the Embittering ones, which is the interpretation of the Amorites. *And Og king of Bashan.* 'Heaping up,' which is the interpretation of Og; and king of Confusion, which is the interpretation of Bashan: for what does the devil heap up, except confusion? *And gave their land for an inheritance, an inheritance for Israel his servant*[^26]. He gives too those whom the devil possessed, as an inheritance to the seed of Abraham, which is Christ. *Because in our humiliation he was mindful of us. And redeemed us from our enemies*[^27]: by the blood of his Only-begotten. *Who gives food to all flesh;* that is, to every race of men, not only of the Israelites, but also of the Gentiles: of which food it is said: *My flesh is truly food*[^28]. *Confess to the God of heaven, for his mercy is forever. Confess to the Lord of lords, for his mercy is forever*[^29]. What he says here: *To the God of heaven*, I believe he wished to say in another way what he had already said above: *To the God of gods*: for what he then subjoined there, this too he repeated here: *Confess to the Lord of lords.* But *even if there are those that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth; as there are many gods and many lords; yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things, and we toward him, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we through him*[^30]; to whom we confess, *for his mercy is forever.*
