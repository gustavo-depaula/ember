# On the Same Psalm 118 (8)

**1.** [v. 21.] The parts of this psalm which follow to be treated admonish us to recall the cause of our misery. For when he had said, *My soul has coveted to desire your justifications in all time;* namely whether in prosperous things, or in adverse, because both in labors and griefs righteousness ought to delight, nor is it so to be loved in tranquil time that in turbulent time it be deserted, but it is to be embraced in all time: he immediately subjoined: *You have rebuked the proud; cursed are those who decline from your commandments.* For the proud decline from the commandments of God. For it is one thing not to fulfill the commandments of God through weakness or ignorance: another to decline from them through pride; as did those who begot us mortally into these evils. For it delighted them: *You will be as gods*[^1]; and so from the commandment of God, which they knew that he had commanded them, and which most easily, with no weakness recalling, impeding, delaying, they could fulfill, through this pride they declined. And behold, this whole hard and unhappy hardship of mortals is in a way a hereditary rebuke of the proud. For when God said: *Adam, where are you?* he did not not know where he was, but was rebuking the proud one; and where he then was, that is, to what misery he had come, he did not desire to know, but, by asking, rebuking, admonished. But see how, when he had said: *You have rebuked the proud,* he did not say: Cursed are those who declined from your commandments; that only that sin of the first men might come to mind: but he said: *Cursed are those who decline.* For it was fitting that all be frightened by that example not to decline from the divine commandments, and, by loving righteousness in all time, in the labor too of this world to recover what we lost in the pleasure of paradise.

**2.** [v. 22.] But since the proud do not lay down their neck even in so great a rebuke, and, although cast down by the punishment of labor and death, are lifted up by the pride of arrogance, imitating the elation of those who fall, mocking the humility of those who rise; the body of Christ prays for them, when it says: *Take from me reproach and contempt, because I have sought out your testimonies.* In Greek the testimonies are called martyria, which word we now use for the Latin: whence we call those who, because of the testimony of Christ, were humbled by various sufferings, and contended even unto death for the truth, not witnesses (testes), which in Latin we surely could, but in Greek martyrs. Since, then, you hear this more familiarly and more sweetly, let us so take these words as if it were said: *Take from me reproach and contempt, because I have sought out your* martyria. When the body of Christ says these things, does it reckon any punishment to hear reproach and contempt from the impious and proud, since rather from there it arrives at the crown? Why, then, does it ask as if some grave and intolerable thing be taken from it, except because, as I said, it prays for these its very enemies, to whom it perceives it is harmful to cast the holy name of Christ as it were a reproach at Christians, and his cross mocked by the Jews and the whole medicine of Christian humility, by which alone that swelling is healed by which, inflated, we fell and, lying, swelled the more, to scorn, the same pride remaining and growing? Let the body of Christ say, then; for now it has learned to love its enemies; let it say to the Lord its God: *Take from me reproach and contempt, because I have sought out your* martyria: that is, the reproach which I hear, and the contempt by which I am for this reason contemned, because I have sought out your martyria, take from me. For my enemies, whom you bid me to love, who more and more die and perish, when they scorn your martyria and find fault with them in me, surely will revive and be found, if they venerate your martyria in me. So it was done; this we see. Behold, the martyrdom of Christ, both among men and in this world, is not only not a reproach, but a great ornament: behold, not only in the sight of the Lord, but even in the sight of men, the death of his saints is now precious; behold, not only are his martyrs not scorned, but even are preferred with great honors. Behold, that younger son who, for the swine which he fed, that is, for the unclean demons which he worshiped, persecuted his portion going before in the very few Christians; now in so many and great peoples of the nations most religiously proclaiming the martyrs to whom he was hurling reproach, and with the greatest praises exalting those whom he scorned, was dead and revived, had perished and was found. For this so great gain of the correction, conversion, and redemption of its enemies, the body of Christ said to God: *Take from me reproach and contempt*: and as if it were asked about what thing reproach, about what thing contempt; it subjoins: *Because I have sought out your* martyria.

**3.** [vv. 23, 24.] Where is now that reproach? where that contempt? They have gone away and passed; and because those who had perished were found, those things perished. But when the Church prayed such things, it was suffering them. *For the princes sat,* he says, *and spoke against me.* Thence the persecution was grave, because those sitting, that is, eminent on the seats of judgment, the princes, decreed it. Refer this to the head itself; you will find that the princes of the Jews sat, seeking counsel how they might destroy Christ. Refer this to his body, that is, the Church; you will find that the kings of the earth devised and commanded how Christians should be nowhere. *For the princes sat, and spoke against me: but your servant was exercised in your justifications.* What kind of exercise this was, you who desire to know, understand what he added: *For both your testimonies are my meditation, and my counsel is your justifications.* Recall what I commended above, that testimonies are martyria. Recall that among the justifications of the Lord none is more difficult and more wonderful than that each one love his enemies. So, then, the body of Christ was exercised, that it both meditated on his martyria, and loved those by whose reproaching and contemning, because of those very martyria, it suffered persecutions. For not for itself, as we have already commended, but rather for them it prayed, saying: *Take from me reproach and contempt. The princes sat,* then, *and spoke against me: but your servant was exercised in your justifications.* In what manner? *For both your testimonies are my meditation, and my counsel is your justifications.* Counsel against counsel: the counsel of the sitting princes was to destroy the martyrs they found; the counsel of the suffering martyrs was to find the enemies who had perished. Those rendered evil for good; these, good for evil. What wonder, then, if those by killing failed, these by dying conquered? What, I say, wonder if the martyrs, with the Gentiles raging, endured temporal death most patiently, and the Gentiles, with the martyrs praying, were able to arrive at eternal life; while the body of Christ is so exercised, that it both meditates on the martyria, and prays good things for the bad persecutors of the martyrs?

[^1]: Gen 3:5
