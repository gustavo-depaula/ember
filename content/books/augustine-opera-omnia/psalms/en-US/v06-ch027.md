# Exposition of Psalm 147 (Part 1: vv. 1-11)

**1.** [v. 1.] We were listening attentively, while the present psalm was sung; and not all of us who were listening also understood. How much more attentively, then, must it now be heard, if, as I hope and desire, with the prayers of all who hear helping, whatever here is perhaps obscure, with God granting, will be revealed; that the hearing may be fruitful, and the hearer not return empty, who was present attentive when he heard? Whence does it begin? It is said to us: *Praise the Lord.* This is said to all nations, not to us alone: and that voice, sounding indeed in individual places from the Readers, the churches hear one by one; yet one voice of God over all does not keep silent, that we should praise him. And as though we asked why we ought to praise God, see what cause he brought: *Praise the Lord*, he says, *because a psalm is good*[^1]. Is this the whole reward of those who praise? Let us praise the Lord. Why? *Because a psalm is good.* I would wish, someone says, to praise the Lord, if he gave me something for this praise. For who praises even a man for nothing? So the praisers of men expect some reward; ought the praiser of God to expect or ask or hope for no reward? A weak man is praised, and something is hoped from him; the Almighty is praised, and there is no reward? Or do I perhaps desire what he cannot give? What does a man desire, which is not in the hand of God? When you praise a man, perhaps you desire that which cannot be provided by him: praise God securely, of whom no one can say that he cannot provide what you could desire. So with the hope of some reward set before us we ought to praise God, yet not one who will give whatever we desire. For he is a Father, and what evil things sons desire, he does not give. Let us praise, and hope, and desire not this or that, but what he judges should be given, whom we praise. For he knows what it is expedient to be given us; but let us attend to what it profits us to receive. The apostle says: *For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know*[^2]. And that same apostle Paul himself hoped it would profit him, if the thorn of his flesh were taken from him, the angel of Satan by whom he was buffeted, as he himself confesses and says: *On account of which I asked the Lord three times, that he would take it from me; and he said to me: My grace is sufficient for you; for strength is made perfect in weakness*[^3]. He desired something; it was not granted to his will, that it might be provided for his health. Here, then, what is set before us? *Praise*, he says, *the Lord.* Why should we praise the Lord? *Because a psalm is good.* The psalm itself is the praise of the Lord. So this he says: Praise the Lord, because it is good to praise the Lord. Let us not so pass over the praising of the Lord. It was said, and passed; it was done, and we fell silent; we praised, and held our peace; we sang, and rested. We go to do perhaps something else that remains, and when other actions meet us, will the divine praise cease in us? Not so; for your tongue praises for an hour, let your life always praise. Thence, then, a psalm is good.

**2.** For a psalm is a song, not any kind, but to the psaltery. But the psaltery is a certain instrument of song, like the lyre, like the cithara, and instruments of this kind, which were invented for singing. He, then, who sings to the psaltery (psallit) does not sing with the voice alone; but, having taken up also a certain instrument, which is called a psaltery, with his hands joining accords with the voice. Do you wish, then, to sing to the psaltery? Let not your voice alone sound the praises of God, but let your works accord with your voice. For when you have sung with the voice, you will at some time be silent: live so as to sing, that you may never be silent. You do business, and think of fraud; you have kept silence from the praise of God: and what is more grave, you have not only kept silence from praise, but have proceeded to blasphemy. For when God is praised because of your good work, by your work you praise God; and when God is blasphemed because of your evil work, by your work you blaspheme God. So for the exhortation of the ears sing with the voice; with the heart do not keep silence, with your life do not be silent. You do not think of fraud in your business, you sing to God. When you eat and drink, sing; not by intermingling the sweetness of sounds suited to the ears, but by eating and drinking modestly and frugally and temperately: because the apostle says this: *Whether you eat, or drink, or whatever you do; do all to the glory of God*[^4]. If, then, you do well in that you eat and drink, and take it for the refreshment of the body and the restoration of the members, giving thanks to him who provided you, mortal and frail, these solaces of supports; both your food and your drink praise God: but if you exceed the due measure of nature by the immoderation of voracity, and gorge yourself with wine; however many praises of God your tongue may sound, your life blasphemes. After food and drink you rest, that you may sleep; do nothing shamefully in bed, do not exceed beyond the license granted in the law of God: let the bed be chaste with your spouse; and if there is a care of propagating children, let there not be the unbridled luxury of lusts; render in your bed the due to your spouse, because you are both members of Christ, both founded by him, both restored by his blood[^5]: doing these things you praise God, nor will your praise be altogether silent. What, when sleep has come? Even when you sleep, let not your bad conscience rouse you from rest; and the innocence of your sleep praises God. If, then, you praise, do not sing with the tongue only, but, having taken up the psaltery of good works; *because a psalm is good.* You praise when you do business, you praise when you take food and drink, you praise when you rest in bed, you praise when you sleep; when do you not praise? The praise of God will be perfected in us, when we have come to that city, when we have been made equal to the angels of God[^6]; when no bodily need at all troubles us, neither hunger, nor thirst interrupts us, heat does not weary us, cold does not constrain us, fever does not cast us down, death does not end us. For that most perfect praise let us exercise ourselves with this praise in good works.

**3.** Therefore when he had said: *Praise the Lord, because a psalm is good: To our God*, he says, *let praise be pleasant*[^7]. How will praise be pleasant to our God? If it praise him by living well. Hear that then it will be pleasant to him. In another place it is said: *Praise is not comely in the mouth of a sinner*[^8]. If, then, in the mouth of a sinner praise is not comely, it is not pleasant; for this is pleasant, which is comely. Do you wish, then, that to your God praise be pleasant? Do not drown out your good song with bad morals. *To our God let praise be pleasant.* What did he say? You who praise, live well. The praise of the impious offends God. He attends more to how you live, than to what you sound. Surely with him whom you praise you wish to have peace: how do you seek peace with him, when you are out of tune with yourself? How, you will say, am I out of tune with myself? The tongue sounds one thing, the life shows another. *To our God let praise be pleasant.* For praise can be pleasant to a man, when he hears one praising with neat and sharp sentences and with a sweet voice: but *to our God let praise be pleasant;* whose ears lie open, not to the mouth, but to the heart; not to the tongue, but to the life of the praiser.

**4.** [v. 2.] Who is our God, to whom praise may be pleasant? He grows sweet to us, he commends himself to us: thanks to his condescension. For he deigns to commend himself to us, not as if we were about to provide anything, but rather as about to receive many things from him. How, then, does God commend himself to us? Hear the apostle Paul: *But God commends*, he says, *his love in us*[^9]. How does he *commend* it? Hear; let the apostle himself say it, that it may be compared with the Psalm: *He commends*, he says, *his love in us.* How does he *commend* it? *Because when we were still sinners, Christ died for us*[^10]. What, then, does he keep for his praisers, who so commends himself to sinners? Because, then, the apostle so said that God commended his love to us, that Christ died for the impious; not that the impious might remain, but that by the death of the just one they might be healed from injustice: here what do you hear, when he had said: *To our God let praise be pleasant*? Let us see whether it is that very commendation which the apostle spoke: That Christ died for sinners and for the impious. *Building*, he says, *Jerusalem the Lord, and gathering the dispersions of Israel*[^11]. Behold the Lord building Jerusalem, gathering the dispersions of his own people. For the people of Jerusalem is the people of Israel. There is an eternal Jerusalem in the heavens, where the citizens are even the angels. What, then, is Israel there? If you consider that man, the grandson of Abraham, who was called also Jacob; how do we understand the angels as Israel? If we examine the interpretation of the name, because that Jacob too with changed name was called Israel[^12]; the name Israel fits rather there: and would that we, following, may be Israel. For what is Israel interpreted? Seeing God. So all the citizens of that city, by seeing God rejoice in that great and ample and heavenly city; God himself is a spectacle for them. But we are pilgrims away from that city, expelled by sin, that we might not abide there; and weighed down by mortality, that we might not return there. God looked upon our pilgrimage, and he who builds Jerusalem restored the fallen part. Whence did he restore the fallen part? *Gathering the dispersions of Israel.* For a certain part fell, and became a pilgrim: this pilgrim God mercifully saw, and sought those not seeking him. Whence did he seek us? whom did he send to our captivity? He sent a Redeemer, according to what the apostle says: *God commends his love in us, because when we were still sinners, Christ died for us.* So he sent to our captivity a redeemer, his Son. Carry with you, he says, a money-bag, bring there the price of the captives. For he clothed himself with the mortality of the flesh, and there was the blood by whose shedding we might be redeemed. By that blood he gathered the dispersions of Israel. And if he gathered those once dispersed, how must we be busy that the dispersed now may be gathered? If the dispersed were gathered, that in the hand of the craftsman they might be formed for the building; how are those to be gathered, who by restlessness fell from the hand of the craftsman? *Building Jerusalem the Lord.* Behold whom we praise, behold to whom we owe praise with our whole life. *Building Jerusalem the Lord, and gathering the dispersions of Israel.*

**5.** [v. 3.] How does he gather? what does he do, that he may gather? *Who heals the contrite of heart*[^13]. Behold how the dispersions of Israel are gathered, that the contrite of heart may be healed. Those who do not contrite their heart are not healed. What is, to contrite the heart? Let it be known, dearly beloved; let it be done, that you may be able to be healed. For it has been said in many other places of the Scriptures; and especially in that place, one singing with our voice said: *For if you had wished a sacrifice, I would surely have given it:* he was saying to God: *If you had wished a sacrifice, I would surely have given it; but in holocausts you will not delight*[^14]. What then? Shall we remain without the offering of a sacrifice? Hear what he wishes you to offer. It follows, and he says: *A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God will not spurn*[^15]. So he *heals the contrite of heart*: because he draws near to them to heal them; as it is said in another place: *The Lord is near to those who have crushed their heart*[^16]. Who have crushed their heart? The humble. Who have not crushed their heart? The proud. The contrite will be healed, the exalted will be cast down. For perhaps for this it is cast down, that the contrite may be healed. Let our heart not wish, then, brothers, to be raised up, before it is set right: it is raised up badly, which is not first corrected.

**6.** *Who heals the contrite of heart, and binds up their contritions. He heals*, he says, *the contrite of heart*: so he heals the humbled of heart, he heals those confessing, he heals those punishing themselves, exercising a severe judgment in themselves, that they may be able to be feeling his mercy. Such he heals; but the perfect health of them will be when mortality is past, when this corruptible has put on incorruption, and this mortal has put on immortality[^17]; when there will be nothing which from the stain of the flesh may trouble, not only nothing to which we may consent, but nothing which may be suggested from the flesh. For even now, my brothers, how many unlawful delights touch the soul! Although we do not consent to them, that our members may serve justice, not iniquity; yet even to be delighted by such things, even if you do not consent, is not yet perfect health. You will be healed, then, with a contrite heart you will be healed. Do not blush, contrite the heart; such God heals. But what do I do, you will say, now? *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man; but I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin*[^18]. What do you do? Contrite the heart, confess; come, say what follows: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death*[^19]? For to say this: *Wretched man*, is already to contrite the heart. Let him hope for happiness who confesses unhappiness. Say, then: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death;* that it may be answered you: *The grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^20]. But how will that grace of God free us, of which we have now received a pledge? Hear the same apostle: *The body indeed*, he says, *is dead on account of sin; but the spirit is life on account of justice. If, then, the Spirit of him who raised Jesus Christ from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Jesus Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies, through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^21]. This pledge our spirit received, that we might begin by faith to serve God, and from faith to be called just: *because the just man lives by faith*[^22]. But whatever still fights against us and resists us, is from the mortality of the flesh; and this will be healed. *He will give life*, he says, *even to your mortal bodies, through his Spirit dwelling in you.* For this he gave a pledge, that he might fulfill what he promised. What, then, now in this life, when we are still confessors, not yet possessors, what in this life, what will be done? How will it be cured? *He heals the contrite of heart;* but perfect health will then be, as we have said. Now, then, what? *He binds up their contritions.* He who cures, he says, the contrite of heart, whose perfect health will be in the resurrection of the just; now binds up their contritions.

**7.** What are the bindings of the contritions? As physicians bind up fractures. For sometimes—let your charity understand this too, and it is known to those who have observed it, or heard it from physicians: sometimes things set crookedly and twistedly, that the physicians may correct them, they break, and make a new wound, because the bad health was crooked. So then *the ways of the Lord are right*, says Scripture, *but the crooked of heart will be scandalized in them*[^23]. What is, *crooked of heart*? Twisted of heart, having a twisted heart. He thinks all the things said by God are twisted, he thinks all the things God made are crooked, and all the judgments of God displease him, especially those in which he himself is rebuked; and he sits, and disputes how badly God acts, because he does not act according to his will. A twisted heart—it is too little that it does not correct itself to God; it even wishes to twist God to itself. What, then, does God say from above? You are twisted, I am straight. If you were straight, you would feel my straightness. As, if on a level pavement you set a twisted piece of wood, it would not be settled, it would totter on every side, be agitated on every side; which not the unevenness of the place, but the twistedness of the wood causes: as Scripture says: *How good is the God of Israel to the straight of heart*[^24]! What, then? how is a twisted heart set straight? It is both twisted, and hard: now then, twisted and hard, let it be broken, contrited, that it may be set straight. You cannot set your heart straight: you break, let him set straight. How do you break? how do you contrite? By confessing, by punishing your sins. What else does the beating of the breast signify? Unless perhaps we think our bones sinned something, when we strike the breast. But we signify that we contrite the heart, that it may be set straight by the Lord.

**8.** So *he heals the contrite of heart*, and those having a contrite heart; and the health of that very heart will then be perfect, when the restoration of the body too which was promised will be fulfilled. Now meanwhile what does the physician do? He binds up your contritions, that you may be able to come to the fullest soundness, until that be consolidated which was broken, which was bound up. What are these bindings? Temporal sacraments. The medicinal bindings of our contrition are the meanwhile temporal sacraments, by which we have consolation: and all these things which we speak to you, the very things which sound and pass, whatever is done in the Church temporally, are the bindings of our contritions. For as the physician, when health is perfect, removes the binding; so in that city Jerusalem, when we have been made equal to the angels, do you think we are about to receive there what we receive here[^25]? Or is the Gospel to be recited to us there, that our faith may remain? or is a hand to be laid on us by some superior? All these are bindings of the fracture; when health is perfect they will be removed: but we would not arrive there, unless they were bound up. So *he heals the contrite of heart, and binds up their contritions.*

**9.** [v. 4.] *Who numbers the multitude of the stars, and calls names for them all*[^26]. What great thing is it for God to number the multitude of the stars? This men too have tried: let them see whether they could complete it; yet they would not try, unless they hoped to complete it. Let us leave to them what they could do, and how far they reached: but for God I think it is no great thing to number all the stars. Or does he perhaps count the number over, lest he forget it? Is it a great thing for God to number the stars, for whom the hairs of our heads are numbered[^27]? It is plain, brothers, that God wishes us to understand something in that he says: *Who numbers the multitude of the stars, and calls*, he says, *names for them all.* There are certain stars, lights in the Church consoling our night, all those of whom the apostle says: *Among whom you appear as luminaries in the world. In this*, he says, *crooked and perverse nation, among whom you appear as luminaries in the world, holding the word of life*[^28]. These stars God numbers; all who are to reign with him, all who are to be joined to the body of his Only-begotten he holds numbered, and numbers them. He who is unworthy is not even numbered: and many believed, or many under some shadowed appearance of faith attached themselves to his people; yet he knows what he numbers, and what he winnows. For so great is the loftiness of the Gospel, that there came to pass what was said: *I announced and spoke, they were multiplied above number*[^29]. So there are in the peoples even as it were supernumeraries. What is, supernumeraries? More than are to be there. Within these walls there are more, than are to be in the kingdom of God, in that heavenly Jerusalem; they are above the number. Let each one notice whether he shines in the darkness, whether he is not seduced by the darksome iniquity of the world; if he has not been seduced nor conquered, he will be as a star which God now numbers.

**10.** *And for all of them*, he says, *calling names*: there is the whole reward. We have certain names with God: that God may know our names, this we ought to wish, this to do, this to be busy about, as much as we can, not to rejoice over other things, nor over certain spiritual gifts. Let your charity see: there are many gifts in the Church, as the apostle says: *To one indeed through the Spirit is given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit, to another faith in the same Spirit, to another the gift of healings, to another the discerning of spirits*, that is, that he may distinguish between good and evil spirits; *to another kinds of tongues, to another prophecy*[^30]. How many he said! how many he said! Many who have used such gifts badly will hear at the end: *I do not know you.* And what will they say at the end, who will hear: *I do not know you*? *Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and in your name cast out demons, and in your name do many mighty works*[^31]? In your name all these things. And what will he say to them? *I never knew you; depart from me, you who work iniquity*[^32]. What is it, then, to be now a light of heaven consoling the night, and not to be conquered by the night? *Still*, he says, *a more eminent way I show you.* *If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal.* What a gift it is to speak with the tongues of angels and of men! Yet *if I have not*, he says, *love, I am sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal. If I know*, he says, *all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all prophecy, and all faith, so as to remove mountains* (how great these gifts are!); *but have not*, he says, *love, I am nothing.* What a gift is that of martyrdom, and of the distribution of one's goods! And yet *if I distribute*, he says, *all my goods to the poor, and hand over my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me nothing*[^33]. He, then, who does not have love, even if for a time he has these gifts, they will be taken from him. What he has will be taken from him, because he does not have a certain thing: and this certain thing he does not have, by which the rest may be held and he himself not perish. What is it that the Lord just now said: *He who has, to him it will be added; but he who has not, even what he has will be taken from him*[^34]? So he who does not have, even what he has will be taken from him. He has the grace of possessing, but does not have the love of using: because, then, he does not have that, even what he has will be taken from him. Therefore the disciples, that they might have love—whom he wished to make in heaven stars walking the eminent way, he *who numbers the multitude of the stars, and calls names for all of them;* when the disciples returned from where they had been sent, exulted, and said: *Lord, behold the unclean spirits are subject to us on account of your name*[^35]. And he *who numbers the multitude of the stars, and calls names for all of them*, knowing that many would say: *Did we not cast out demons in your name?* to whom he is to say at the end: *I do not know you;* because he did not number them in the multitude of the stars, nor call names for them: *Do not*, he said, *rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you; but rejoice that your names are written in heaven*[^36]. *Who numbers the multitude of the stars, and calls names for all of them.*

**11.** [v. 5.] *Great is our Lord*[^37]. He was filled with joy, he poured forth unspeakably: he was not able to say something or other; and how was he able to think it? *Great is our Lord, and great is his power, and of his understanding there is no number.* He himself who numbers the multitude of the stars cannot be numbered: *Great is our Lord, and great is his power, and of his understanding there is no number.* Who can expound this? who can worthily even think what was said: *And of his understanding there is no number?* And would that he might pour himself into you, and where we fail, because he is powerful, may he himself illumine your minds, that you may know what it is: *Of his understanding there is no number.* For you see, brothers; is there a number of the sand? For us there is not, for God there is: for whom the hairs of our head are numbered, the sand too is numbered. Whatever, then, of the infinite this world embraces, even if not to man, yet to God: I say little, to God; to the angels it is numbered. *Of his understanding there is no number.* His understanding exceeds all reckoners, it cannot be numbered by us. Number itself, who numbers it? By number whatever is numbered is numbered. If whatever is numbered is numbered by number; of number there cannot be a number, number can in no way be numbered. What, then, is there with God, whence he made all things, and where he made all things, to whom it is said: *You have arranged all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^38]? Or who can either number, or measure, or weigh the very measure, and the very number, and the very weight, where God arranged all things? So: *Of his understanding there is no number.* Let human voices be silent, let human thoughts rest: let them not extend themselves to incomprehensible things as if to comprehend them, but as if to partake of them; for we shall be partakers. We shall not be what we grasp, nor shall we grasp the whole: but we shall be partakers; because it was said of Jerusalem, whose dispersions he gathers, something great was said of her: *Jerusalem which is built as a city, whose sharing is in the selfsame*[^39]. *In the selfsame* what does he say, except what cannot be changed? The other things which were created can be now one way and now another; but he who created cannot be now one way and now another. So he is the selfsame, since it was said to him: *You will change them, and they will be changed; but you are the same, and your years will not fail*[^40]. So if he himself is the selfsame, and can in no part be changed; by partaking of his divinity we too shall be immortal unto eternal life. And this pledge has been given us concerning the Son of God, which I have already said to your holiness, that before we were made partakers of his immortality, he himself first became a partaker of our mortality. But as he was mortal, not from his own substance, but from ours: so we shall be immortal, not from our substance, but from his. We shall be partakers, then: let no one doubt, Scripture said this. And of what shall we be partakers, as though there were parts with God, or God were divided into parts? Who, then, can explain how the many are partakers of the one simple? So do not demand what cannot be aptly said—I think you see this: but return to the remedy of the Savior; contrite the heart, let the hardness of the soul be broken, let the obstinacy of the soul be contrited, let it be accused in evil, let it be born again in good. He himself will set straight, will bind up the fracture, will consolidate health; and the things which are now impossible to us will no longer be impossible. For it is good that he confess weakness, who wishes to come to divinity. *Of his understanding there is no number.*

**12.** [v. 6.] Therefore what you ought to do in the difficulty of understanding, he shows you, when consequently he says: *The Lord taking up the meek*[^41]. For example, you do not understand, you understand little, you do not attain it: honor the Scripture of God, honor the word of God, even when not open; defer your understanding out of devotion. Do not be insolent in accusing either the obscurity, or as it were the perversity of Scripture. Here there is nothing perverse; but there is something obscure, not that it may be denied you, but that it may exercise one who is to receive it. So when it is obscure, the physician made it so, that you may knock: he willed that you be exercised in knocking; he willed that he might open to one knocking[^42]. By knocking you will be exercised; exercised, you will be made wider; made wider, you will grasp what is given. So do not be indignant that it is shut; be gentle, be meek. Do not kick against obscure things, and say: It would be better said, if it were said thus. For when can you say or judge how it is expedient to be said? It was said as it ought to have been said. Let not the sick man correct his medicines; the physician knows how to measure them: trust him who cures you. Therefore what follows? *The Lord taking up the meek.* Do not, then, resist against the shut things of God; be meek, that he may take you up. But if you resist, hear what follows: *But humbling sinners even to the earth.* There are many kinds of sinners: *But humbling sinners even to the earth;* which sinners, except those contrary to the meek? For from the fact that he said: *The Lord taking up the meek, but humbling sinners even to the earth;* he wished a certain kind of sinners to be understood from the foresaid meekness. So in this place we understand by sinners the ungentle, and those who are not meek. Why does he humble them even to the earth? They reprove intelligible things, they will savor earthly things.

**13.** This he did to men who wished to mock the Law, before they knew it; for they were not meek. Let your charity attend. There arose a certain most lost sect of the Manichees; the Scriptures received and read, it mocked: it wished to reprove what it did not understand, and by agitating and reproving things not understood, it ensnared many. But those who wished to do this were humbled even to the earth. They were not permitted to understand heavenly things, they savored earthly things. Whatever you hear in their fables is nothing but blasphemy, and certain figments of bodily images: since, wishing to understand God, they came as far as the thought of this visible light, they could not go beyond; and they made such fields of light in the kingdom of God, as they saw this sun to be, as it were a fruit of that light. But all this which is touched through the earth of the flesh is earth to God. For we have whence we may see, whence hear, whence smell, whence taste, whence touch. This flesh, through five messengers as it were, which we call senses, perceives only bodily things: but intelligible and spiritual things are grasped by the mind. Because, then, they mocked the obscurity of the Scriptures, which were therefore shut, that those knocking might be exercised, not that they might be denied to little ones, they were humbled even to the earth, that they could not think anything more than what is perceived through the earth. Through the earth, what do I say? Through the flesh. For this flesh is earth, and was made of earth. Whatever you grasp through the eyes belongs to the earth; whatever through the ears, whatever through the smell, whatever through the taste, whatever through the touch, belongs to the earth; because it is perceived through the earth. They, then, were not able to understand the understanding of which there is no number; because: *Of his understanding there is no number.* Reproving the Scriptures which by certain mystical things wholesomely cover the understanding, that the little ones may be exercised, and by that very reproof made ungentle, which is contrary to the meek, they were humbled even to the earth, that they could not perceive the incorporeal God, and whatever they thought of God, they thought only bodily.

**14.** [v. 7.] So God is *humbling sinners even to the earth.* What, then, ought we to do, if we do not wish to be humbled even to the earth? Because it is a great thing to make progress as far as intelligible things, a great thing to make progress as far as spiritual things, a great thing for the heart to arrive there, that it may know there is something which is not extended through places, nor varied through times. For what is the appearance of wisdom? who thinks it? Is it long? is it square? is it round? is it now here and now there? Someone thinks wisdom in the East, another thinks it in the West: if they think it well, set in such diversity of places, it is wholly present to both. What is this? who grasps it? This substance, this certain divine and unchangeable nature, who grasps it? Do not hasten, you will be able to grasp it. Hear what follows: *Begin to the Lord in confession.* Hence begin, if you wish to arrive at the clear understanding of the truth. If you wish to be led from the way of faith to the possession of the vision, begin in confession. First accuse yourself; yourself accused, praise God. Call upon him whom you do not yet know, that he may come and be known: not that he himself may come, but that he may lead you to himself. How does he come, from whom he never departs? For it is the perfection of wisdom; it is everywhere, and is far from the evil: it is everywhere, I say, and is far from the evil who are everywhere. I ask you, from whom is that far which is everywhere? Where do you think, except that they lie in their own unlikeness, exterminating in themselves the likeness of God? Made unlike, they withdrew; reformed, let them return. Whence, he says, shall we be reformed? when shall we be reformed? *Begin to the Lord in confession.* After confession what? Let good works follow. *Sing to our God on the cithara.* What is, *on the cithara*? What I have already expounded, as that on the psaltery a psalm, so too the cithara; not only with the voice, but also with works. *Sing to our God on the cithara.*

**15.** [v. 8.] Behold, confess, do works of mercy, *sing to our God.* To which *God*? *Who covers heaven with clouds.* What is: *Who covers heaven with clouds*? Who covers Scripture with figures and sacraments. He who humbles sinners even to the earth, he who takes up the meek, *covers heaven with clouds.* And who may see the heaven which is covered with clouds? Do not fear; hear what follows: *Who covers heaven with clouds, who prepares rain for the earth. Who covers heaven with clouds*: you grew afraid, because you do not see the heaven; when it has rained, you will bear fruit, and you will see the clear sky. *He covers heaven with clouds, who prepares rain for the earth*: perhaps this the Lord our God did. For unless we had the occasion of the obscurity of the Scriptures, we would not say these things to you, over which you rejoice. This, then, is perhaps the rain over which you rejoice. It could not be expressed to you through our tongue, unless God covered the heaven of the Scriptures with the clouds of figures. For this, then, he covers heaven with clouds, that he may prepare rain for the earth. For this he willed the sayings of the Prophets to be obscure, that the servants of God might afterward have what by interpreting they might pour over the ears and hearts of men, receiving from the clouds of God the rich nourishment of spiritual gladness. *Who covers heaven with clouds, who prepares rain for the earth.*

**16.** *Who makes hay rise on the mountains, and grass for the service of men*[^43]. Behold the fruit of the rain. *Who makes*, he says, *hay rise on the mountains.* And not also on the lowly earth? But what is great, *on the mountains.* By mountains he means the lofty of the world: those endowed with some great dignity, in this place take as mountains. And it is no wonder that a certain widow put I know not what two mites into the treasury[^44]; the earth bore hay, the lowly earth: and the mountain bore it. That Zacchaeus, the chief of the publicans[^45]; for this was more wonderful, because the mountain bore hay. For the more exalted men are, the more avaricious they are; and the greater they are in this world, the more they love their riches. Whence that man went away sad, who sought counsel of eternal life from the Lord, and called him good master, saying: *What shall I do that I may have eternal life?* And he: *Keep the commandments. Which*? And he told the commandments of the law. *All these I have done from my youth. One thing*, he says, *is lacking to you. Do you wish to be perfect? Go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^46]. What did the Lord say? Behold, you are a mountain, receive the rain, and give hay. For what are you about to give? is it not hay? For all these things which are given by the rich to the Church for the needs of those who serve God, what are they but hay? For they are fleshly things, appearing for a time: but from them something not fleshly is gained. From cheap things attend to what you buy. For the apostle says, showing that this is hay: *If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great matter if we reap your fleshly things*[^47]? And hear that fleshly things are hay. *All flesh is hay; and all the glory of man as the flower of hay*[^48]. So that man went away sad; and the Lord said: *How hard does a rich man enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^49]! This, then, is great, that *he makes hay rise on the mountains.* And how does *he make hay rise on the mountains*, if that rich man, when he heard that he ought to give his goods to the poor, went away sad? How did he afterward answer the disciples, made sad? *The things that are hard for men, are easy for God*[^50]. So he *makes hay rise on the mountains*, to whom all things are easy. For nothing would be more barren than the hardest mountains. He rains *who makes hay rise on the mountains, and grass for the service of men.* For what *service*? Attend to Paul himself: *But we*, he says, *your servants through Jesus Christ.* He who said: *If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great matter if we reap your fleshly things*, called himself a servant. For we serve you, brothers. Let no one of us say that he is as it were greater than you. We will be greater, if we are more humble: *But whoever wishes to be greater among you*, it is the Lord's sentence, *he will be your servant*[^51]. So *he makes hay rise on the mountains, and grass for the service of men.* The apostle Paul indeed, living by his own hands, was unwilling to receive that very hay of the mountains; he wished to be in want: but yet the mountains gave hay. Was it that, because he was unwilling to receive, the mountains ought therefore not to give, that they should remain barren? The fruit is owed to the rain, food is owed to the servant, as the Lord says: *Eat the things that are from them*[^52]. And lest they should think they were giving something of their own: *For the laborer*, he says, *is worthy of his wages*[^53].

**17.** And so, brothers, since it has happened, from this occasion, to say something to you, especially because we speak, not seeking such things from you, and therefore we say it the more freely: but even if we did seek them, we would seek your fruit, and not your wealth, but your justice: yet I briefly admonish, since we have already said many things, and at some time the sermon must be ended; if you wish not to be barren, that you may render fruitfulness for the rain, lest your barrenness be condemned afterward (for God threatens the barren and thorny earth with fire, as he prepares a barn for the fruitful[^54]); bring it about that you exact of yourselves, become exactors of yourselves. Christ silently exacts; and the voice of the one keeping silence is greater, because in the Gospel he does not keep silence. For he does not truly keep silence, when he says: *Make for yourselves friends of the mammon of iniquity, that they too may receive you into the eternal dwellings*[^55]. He himself does not keep silence; hear his voice. For no one can exact of you; unless perhaps there is need of exaction, that those who serve you in the Gospel may ask of you. If it has come to this, that they ask; see lest what you ask of God, you ask in vain. So be exactors of yourselves, lest those who serve you in the Gospel be forced to anything—I do not say to suffer, for perhaps they do not even ask when forced; but lest by their silence they accuse you. Whence it is written: *Blessed is he who understands over the needy and the poor*[^56]. When he says, *who understands over the needy and the poor,* he does not wait for him to ask. Understand over him. Another seeks you, being in need; another you ought to seek, being in need. Both were said, my brothers: both: *Give to everyone who asks you*, was just now read; and in another place Scripture says: *Let your almsgiving sweat in your hand, until you find a just man to whom to give it*[^57]. There is one who seeks you, another you ought to seek. Do not leave empty him who seeks you; *for give to everyone who asks you*: but there is another whom you ought to seek; *let your almsgiving sweat in your hand until you find a just man to whom to give.* You will never do this, unless you have something set apart of your goods, which pleases each one according to the need of his household, as it were owed as if to be rendered to the treasury. If Christ does not have his own commonwealth, he does not have his own treasury. For do you know what a treasury is? A treasury is a bag; whence too little baskets and hampers are so named. Do not think the treasury is some dragon, because the exactor of the treasury is heard with fear; the treasury is a public bag. The Lord himself had it here on earth, when he had a money-box; and that money-box was entrusted to Judas. The Lord endured Judas the betrayer and a thief[^58], and in him showing everywhere his patience: yet those who contributed, contributed into the money-box of the Lord. Unless perhaps you think that the Lord went and begged, or was in need, to whom the angels ministered, who fed so many thousands of men from five loaves. Why, then, did he wish to be in need, except that he might give an example to the mountains, that they might bear hay, that they might not render barrenness against the rain? Cut off, then, something, and assign something fixed, either from your yearly fruits, or from your daily gains. For you seem as it were to give from the living flesh, and your hand must necessarily tremble, when you stretch it out to that which you had not vowed. Take some part of your incomes. Do you wish tithes? Take out tithes, although it is too little. For it was said that the Pharisees gave tithes: *I fast twice on the sabbath, I give tithes of all that I possess*[^59]. And what does the Lord say? *Unless your justice abounds more than that of the Scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^60]. And he over whom your justice ought to abound gives tithes; but you do not give even a thousandth part. How will you surpass him to whom you are not even equal? *Who covers heaven with clouds, who prepares rain for the earth; who makes hay rise on the mountains, and grass for the service of men.*

**18.** [v. 9.] *And he gives to the beasts their food*[^61]. By these beasts he means the flocks of God. God does not defraud his flock of its food through men, for whose service he makes grass rise. Whence the apostle says: *Who feeds a flock, and does not partake of the milk of the flock*[^62]? *Who gives to the beasts their food, and to the young of the ravens that call upon him.* Shall we perhaps think this, that the ravens call upon God, that he may give them food? Do not think this, that an irrational soul calls upon God; the soul does not know how to call upon God, except the rational alone. Take it as said in a figure, lest you think, as certain impious men say, that human souls are turned back into cattle, into dogs, into swine, into ravens. Exclude this from your hearts, and from your faith. The human soul was made to the image of God[^63]; it will not give its image to a dog or a swine. What is, then: *And to the young of the ravens that call upon him*? Who are the young of the ravens? The Israelites said they alone were just, because they had received the Law; all the rest, the men of all nations, they called sinners. And truly all the nations were in sin, in idolatry, in the adoration of stones and wood; but did they remain so? Even if our fathers themselves were not ravens, yet we, the young of the ravens, call upon God. *He gives to the beasts their food, and to the young of the ravens that call upon him.* They are the young of the ravens, to whom Peter says: *That not with corruptible silver or gold were you redeemed from your vain way of life handed down by your fathers*[^64]. For the young of the ravens, making progress, who seemed to worship the idols of their fathers, were converted to God. And you now hear the young of a raven calling upon the one God. What then? have you abandoned your father, you say to the young of the raven? I have abandoned him plainly: for that raven did not call upon God, but I, the young of the raven, call upon God. *And to the young of the ravens that call upon him.*

**19.** [v. 10.] *He will not have his will in the strength of a horse*[^65]. The strength of a horse is pride. For the horse seems as it were fitted for the mounting of a man, that he may go more loftily. And truly there is in it a neck, indicating a certain pride. Let men not exalt themselves in their dignities, let them not think themselves sublime in honors; let them beware lest they be thrown headlong by an untamed horse. For see what was said in another psalm: *These in chariots, and these in horses; but we will be magnified in the name of the Lord our God.* That is, those in temporal honors, we in the name of the Lord our God will be magnified. Therefore what happened to them? See what follows: *Their feet were bound, and they fell; but we rose up, and were set upright*[^66]. *He will not have his will in the strength of a horse; nor will he think well in the tents of a man. In the tents*, he says, *of a man.* For there is a tent of the Lord, the holy Church itself spread through the whole world. The heretics, dividing themselves from the tents of the Church, set up tents for themselves: God will not think well in those tents of a man. But attend to the young of the ravens saying: *I have chosen to be cast off in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners*[^67]. For if it happens to some good, devout man, confessing his weakness, the young of a raven calling upon God, perhaps to lack temporal honor in the Church; he does not go outside the Church, he does not make for himself outside the Church a tent in which God thinks not well: but what does he say? *I have chosen to be cast off in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners. Nor will he think well in the tents of a man.*

**20.** [v. 11.] But what does he add? *The Lord thinks well in those who fear him, and in those who hope in his mercy*[^68]. The Lord thinks well in those who fear him: but is God feared in the way that a robber too is feared? For a robber too is feared, and a beast is feared, and an unjust and powerful man is feared much. *The Lord thinks well in those who fear him*: but how in those who fear him? *And in those who hope in his mercy.* Behold, Judas the betrayer of Christ feared him, but he did not hope in his mercy. For afterward it repented him that he had betrayed the Lord, and he said: *I have sinned, for I have betrayed innocent blood*[^69]. You feared well; but if only you had hoped in the mercy of him whom you feared. He, despairing, went off, and hanged himself with a noose. So fear the Lord in such a way that you hope in his mercy. If you fear a robber, you hope for help from another, not from him whom you fear: you beg help from him whom you do not fear, against him whom you fear. If you so fear God, and you fear God for this reason, because you are a sinner, from whom are you to receive help against God? Where will you go? what will you do? Do you wish to flee from him? Flee to him. Do you wish to flee from the angry one? Flee to the appeased one. But you will appease him, if you hope in his mercy; and so beware henceforth of sinning, that you may pray for the past, that they may be forgiven you by the Lord, to whom is honor and dominion with the Father and the Holy Spirit forever and ever. Amen.
