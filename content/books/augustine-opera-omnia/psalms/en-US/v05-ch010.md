# Exposition of Psalm 104

**1.** [v. 1.] The hundred and fourth psalm is the first among those that are prefaced with *Alleluia.* The interpretation of which word, or rather of which two words, is "Praise God." And therefore it begins from there: *Confess to the Lord, and call upon his name.* For this confession is to be understood as praise, as is, *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth*[^1]. For praise being set before, invocation is wont to follow, where the petitioner binds his desires to it: whence the Lord's prayer too has at its head a very brief praise, which is, *Our Father who are in heaven*[^2]. Then the things that are asked follow in order. Whence also elsewhere in a psalm it is said: *We will confess to you, O God, we will confess, and we will call upon your name*[^3]. Which is plainer elsewhere: *Praising I will call upon the Lord, and I will be saved from my enemies*[^4]: so too here: *Confess*, he says, *to the Lord, and call upon his name;* which is as if he said: Praise the Lord, and call upon his name. For he hears the one who calls upon him, whom he sees praising: he sees praising, whom he proves loving. And in what did the Lord wish the love of a good servant toward him to be shown most of all, except in that he said to him, *Feed my sheep?*[^5] Whence here too there follows: *Announce among the nations his works;* or rather, to express it from the Greek word for word, which other Latin manuscripts have: *Evangelize among the nations his works.* To whom is this said, except to the Evangelists in prophecy?

**2.** [vv. 2, 3.] *Sing to him, and sing psalms to him.* Praise by word and work: for with the mouth one sings; but with the psaltery, that is, with the hands, one sings psalms. *Tell all his wonders: be praised in his holy name.* These two verses can not absurdly seem to be said from the two earlier words: so that what he says, *Tell all his wonders*, is referred to what was said, *Sing to him;* but what follows, *Be praised in his holy name*, is referred to what was said, *And sing psalms to him*: the former, namely, to the good word by which one sings to him, and his wonders are told; the latter to the good work by which one sings psalms to him, lest anyone wish to be praised for a good work as if by his own virtue. Therefore, when he had said, *Be praised*, which indeed those who work well can deservedly be; he added, *in his holy name*: that he who glories may glory in the Lord. Those, then, who wish to sing psalms not to themselves, but to him, let them beware of doing their righteousness before men, to be seen by them; otherwise they will have no reward with their Father, who is in heaven: but let their works shine before men, not with this end that they themselves be seen by them, but that they may see their good works, and glorify their Father, who is in heaven. This is to be praised in his holy name. Whence also in another psalm it is read: *In the Lord my soul will be praised; let the meek hear, and be glad*[^6]. Which here too in a way follows: *Let the heart of those who seek the Lord be glad*: for so the meek are glad, who do not with bitter zeal envy those now working well.

**3.** [v. 4.] *Seek the Lord, and be strengthened.* For this is more expressly translated from the Greek, although the word seems less Latin: whence other manuscripts have, *be confirmed;* others, *be made strong.* And indeed it is said: *My strength*[^7]; and: *My strength I will keep for you*[^8]: that, by seeking him and drawing near to him, we may be both illumined and strengthened; lest by blindness we not see what is to be done, or by weakness not do even what we see. What, then, is for seeing, *Draw near to him, and be enlightened*[^9]; this is for doing, *Seek the Lord, and be made strong. Seek*, he says, *his face always.* What is the face of the Lord, except the presence of God? Like the face of the wind, and the face of fire: for it is said, *Like stubble before the face of the wind*[^10]; and: *As wax flows from the face of fire*[^11]. And Scripture sets down many such things, wishing nothing else to be understood than the presence of those things whose face it names. But what is it, *Seek his face always?* I know indeed that it is good for me to cling to God; but if he is always sought, when is he found? Or did he say *always*, in this whole life lived here, from when we have come to know that we ought to do it, when even, once found, he is to be sought? For faith has already found him, but hope still seeks him. But love both finds him through faith, and seeks to have him through sight: where then he will be so found that he suffices us, and is sought no further. For unless faith found him in this life, it would not be said, *Seek the Lord;* and, when you have found him: *Let the impious forsake his ways, and the unjust man his thoughts*[^12]. Likewise, if, found by faith, he were not still to be sought out, it would not be said: *For if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it through patience*[^13]: and what John says: *We know that when he appears, we will be like him; for we will see him as he is*[^14]. Or perhaps even when we have seen him face to face as he is, he will still be sought out, and sought without end, because he will be loved without end? For we say even to one present, "I do not seek you"; that is, I do not love you. And through this the one who is loved, even present, is sought, while it is done with perpetual love, that he may not become absent. Accordingly, whomever anyone loves, even when he sees him, he wishes him without weariness to be always present, that is, he always seeks him to be present. And surely this is it: *Seek his face always*, that finding may not put an end to this seeking, by which love is signified, but, love growing, the seeking of the found one may grow.

**4.** [v. 5.] Now then this burning praiser tempers himself, and descends to words within our grasp; nourishing weak and suckling love with God's temporal wonders. *Remember*, he says, *his wonders which he did, his prodigies and the judgments of his mouth.* Which place seems similar to that place, where, to Moses asking who he was, when he had said, *I am who I am, and: You will say to the sons of Israel: He who is sent me to you*[^15]: which only a rare mind grasps from however small a particle: afterward, commemorating his name, he mercifully tempered his grace toward men, saying, *I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob; this is my name forever*[^16]. Where he wished it to be understood both that those whose God he called himself live with him forever, and he said this, which could be grasped even by little ones: so that *I am who I am*, those might understand according to their capacity, who, with the great strength of love, knew how to seek his face always. If, then, what he himself is, is too much for you even to seek to see: *Remember his wonders which he did, his prodigies and the judgments of his mouth.*

**5.** [vv. 6, 7.] And to whom is it said? *Seed of Abraham his servants, sons of Jacob his chosen ones.* You seed of Abraham, you sons of Jacob, *remember his wonders which he did, his prodigies and the judgments of his mouth.* But lest anyone attribute this to the one nation of the Israelites according to the flesh, and not rather understand that the sons of the promise are the seed of Abraham more than the sons of the flesh, to whom the Apostle says, when he was speaking to the gentiles, *You, then, are the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise*[^17]; there follows and he says: *He is the Lord our God; in all the earth are his judgments.* This is said through Isaiah to the free Jerusalem, our mother: *And he who rescued you, he your God, will be called the God of all the earth*[^18]. Is he the God of the Jews only? Far from it. *He is the Lord our God; in all the earth are his judgments*: because in all the earth is his Church, where his judgments are preached. What, then, does he say in another psalm: *Who announces his word to Jacob, his righteous acts and his judgments to Israel; he did not do so to every nation, and his judgments he did not make manifest to them*[^19]? It was said for this reason, because he wished one nation to be understood as pertaining to the seed of Abraham; which was indeed called out of all the nations, and so in it all the nations are spoken of, that it may be one nation called into adoption. Outside this nation he made his judgments manifest to no nation: since those who did not believe them, even if they were announced, were certainly not made manifest to them; because unless they believe, they will not understand.

**6.** [vv. 8-11.] *He was mindful forever of his covenant.* Other manuscripts have, *forever* (in the sense of eternally); which came from the ambiguous Greek. But if it is to be understood as for this age, not eternally; how, in expounding which covenant he was mindful of, does he add and say, *Of the word which he commanded for a thousand generations?* Which can still be understood with some end; but then he says: *Which he arranged with Abraham, and his oath to Isaac; and he established it for Jacob as a precept, and for Israel as an eternal covenant.* Where there is no place for ambiguity: for the Greek has what our translators have nowhere rendered except as "eternal"; but scarcely any anywhere said "everlasting." Unless perhaps, because they more familiarly translate it "of the age," they wish to translate it not "eternal," but "of the age"; which I do not recall that anyone dared to do. But if in this place the Old Testament is to be understood, because of the land of Canaan; for so the discourse is woven: *And he established it for* that same *Jacob as a precept, and for* that same *Israel as an eternal covenant, saying: To you I will give the land of Canaan, the cord of your inheritance*: how is it to be understood as *eternal*, when that earthly inheritance cannot be eternal? And therefore it is called the Old Testament, because it is abolished by the New. But *a thousand generations* too do not seem to signify anything eternal; because they certainly have an end, and for temporal things themselves they are too many. For however few the years by which a generation is determined, which the Greeks call genea; which some have set at the least at fifteen years, from which a man begins to be able to beget; what are these thousand generations, not only from the time of Abraham, when this promise was made to him, to the time of the New Testament, but from Adam himself, to the end of the age? For who would dare to say that this age is extended by fifteen thousand years?

**7.** Accordingly it seems to me that here the Old Testament is not to be understood, which is said to be taken away by the New through the prophet: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will confirm for the house of Jacob a New Testament; not according to the Testament which I established for their fathers, when I led them out of the land of Egypt*[^20]: but the testament of faith, which the Apostle praises, when he sets Abraham before us to imitate, and convicts those who glory in the works of the Law from this, that Abraham believed God even before circumcision, and it was reckoned to him for righteousness. Finally, when he had said, *He was mindful forever of his covenant;* which we ought to understand as eternally, namely the covenant of justification and of the eternal inheritance, which God promised to faith: *Of the word*, he says, *which he commanded for a thousand generations.* What is it, *he commanded?* For what he says, *To you I will give the land of Canaan*, is not commanded, but promised: but what we ought to do is commanded, what we are to receive is promised. The command, then, is faith, that the just man live by faith; and to this faith eternal inheritance is promised. *A thousand generations*, then, because of the perfection of the number, are to be understood for all; that is, as long as generation succeeds generation by following, so long the command is to live by faith. This the people of God observes, the sons of the promise coming by birth, and departing by death, until every generation is finished; which is signified by the number a thousand, because the solid square of the number ten, ten times ten, and this ten times, reaches a thousand. *Which he arranged*, he says, *with Abraham, and his oath to Isaac. And he established it for Jacob*, that is, for Jacob himself, *as a precept.* They are the three patriarchs whose God he specially calls himself, whom the Lord too names in the New Testament, where he says: *Many will come from the east and west, and recline with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven*[^21]. This is the eternal inheritance. For here too, where he says, *He established it for Jacob as a precept;* he makes manifest that it is the precept of faith: for he would not call a promise a precept. For if the work is in the precept, the reward in the promise: *This is the work of God*, says the Lord, *that you believe in the one whom he sent*[^22]. This word, then, which he commanded, *mindful forever of his covenant*, that is, the word of faith which we preach, *he established for* that same *Jacob as a precept; and for* that same *Israel as an eternal covenant*, that is, that from the word itself and the precept fulfilled he would give something eternal. *Saying: To you I will give the land of Canaan, the cord of your inheritance.* How, then, is this eternal, unless it signifies something eternal? For it is itself called the land of promise, the land flowing with milk and honey. Which wholly signifies grace, in which it is tasted how sweet the Lord is; to which not all men pertain: for faith is not of all. Therefore he added: *The cord of your inheritance.* Whence in another psalm, where the one seed of Abraham, which is Christ, is understood to speak, he says: *The cords fell to me in glorious places; for my inheritance is glorious to me*[^23]. But why it was called "the land of Canaan", the interpretation of this name discloses; for Canaan is interpreted "humble." And if this is referred to that sentence of holy Noah, by which he said that the same Canaan would be the servant of his brothers, from this is also servile fear. *But the servant does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever*[^24]. Therefore, the Canaanite excluded, the land of promise is given to the seed of Abraham. For perfect *love casts out fear*[^25], that the son may remain in the house forever. Whence it was said: *And for* that same *Israel as an eternal covenant.*

**8.** [v. 12.] Then he follows the history most known in the truth of the holy Books. *When they were few in number, very few and sojourners in it*: that is, in the land of Canaan. When the fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob dwelt there, before they received it as an inheritance, in their seed they were very few, and sojourners in it. But some manuscripts have, not *very few and sojourners;* but *very few and sojourners* (in the accusative case). Where it appears that those who so translated this followed a Greek expression, which cannot be transferred into Latin, except with an absurdity which could not be borne at all. For if we should try to translate that whole expression, we would say: *In their being few in number, very few and sojourners in it.* But what the Greek says, *In their being*, this in Latin is *When they were*: which word cannot be followed by an accusative case, but by a nominative. For who would say: When they were very few (accusative)? but: When they were very few (nominative).

**9.** [vv. 13-15.] *When*, then, *they were few in number*, or *few in number, very few and sojourners in it; they passed from nation to nation, and from kingdom to another people.* It is a repetition of what he had said, *from nation to nation. He did not allow a man to harm them*: that is, he did not permit it. But the Greek expression is, *to harm them* (in one construction); the Latin is, *to harm them* (in another). *And he rebuked kings for their sake. Do not*, he says, *touch my anointed ones, and do not deal maliciously with my prophets.* He spoke the words of God rebuking or convicting kings, that they should not hurt the holy fathers, when they were few in number, and very few and sojourners in the land of Canaan. Which words, although they are not read in the books of his history, yet must be understood as either secretly said—as God speaks in the hearts of men by hidden and truthful visions—or even expressed through an angel. For both the king of Gerar and the king of the Egyptians were divinely warned not to harm Abraham, and another king not to harm Isaac, and others not to harm Jacob; when they were very few and sojourners, before Jacob with his sons passed over to dwell in Egypt: which is understood to be commemorated in that he said, *They passed from nation to nation, from kingdom to another people.* But because it occurred to ask how, before they passed over and were multiplied in Egypt, they could endure, few in number, very few and sojourners in a foreign land, he went on and added: *He did not permit a man to harm them, and he rebuked kings for their sake. Do not touch my anointed ones, and do not deal maliciously with my prophets.*

**10.** But it can rightly raise the question how they were called anointed, before there was the anointing from which this name was imposed on kings: which began from Saul, whom David succeeded in the kingdom; and thence the other kings both of Judah and of Israel were anointed by a continuation of the hallowed custom: in which anointing was figured the one true Christ, to whom it was said, *God, your God, anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your partakers*[^26]. Whence, then, were they even then called anointed (christi)? For that they were prophets, we read of Abraham; and surely what was manifestly said of him is to be understood of them too. Or were they anointed for this reason, because, even if secretly, they were yet already Christians? For although the flesh of Christ is from them, yet Christ is before them: which he answered the Jews too, saying, *Before Abraham came to be, I am*[^27]. But how could they be ignorant of him, or not believe in him; when they were for this reason called prophets, because, though more secretly, they yet foretold the Lord? Whence he himself says openly: *Abraham desired to see my day, and he saw it, and rejoiced*[^28]. For no one, apart from this faith which is in Christ Jesus, whether before his incarnation, or afterward, was reconciled to God; since it has been most truly defined by the Apostle: *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^29].

**11.** [vv. 16, 17.] Then now he begins to narrate how it came about that they passed from nation to nation, and from kingdom to another people. "And he called," he says, "a famine upon the land; he broke every support of bread. He sent a man before them; Joseph was sold as a servant." Through this it came about that they passed from nation to nation, from kingdom to another people. But the expressions of the holy Scriptures are not to be passed over negligently. *He called*, he says, *a famine upon the land*: as if famine were some person, or some animate body, or some spirit that could obey the one calling; whereas famine is a destruction brought on by want of food, and so is in those who suffer it, like some disease. For as a disease ceases to be, generally by means of medicine; so famine too is in a way healed by nourishment. What, then, is it, *He called a famine?* Or perhaps these evils which men suffer have certain evil angels set over them (for in another psalm too he says that God afflicted men by a sending through evil angels, certainly not with erring judgment), and this is perhaps, *He called a famine*, that is, the angel set over famine, called by the name of the thing over which he is set? From which opinion the old Romans consecrated certain such gods, like the goddess Fever, and the god Pallor. Or, what is more believable, *He called a famine*, is to be understood: He said that there should be a famine; so that to call is to summon; to summon is to say; to say is to command? For he called the famine who *calls the things that are not, as though they were.* Nor did the Apostle there say: *He who calls the things that are not*, that they may be; but, *as though they were*[^30]. For with God what is to be by his arrangement has already been done; because it is said of him elsewhere: *Who has made the things that are to be*[^31]. And here, when the famine was made, then it was said to be called, that is, that it might be present which had already been arranged in his hidden governance. Finally, how he called the famine, he immediately expounded, saying: *He broke every support of bread.* And this too is an unusual expression: for he said *broke*, for what is, "consumed."

**12.** [v. 17.] *He sent a man before them.* What man? Joseph. How did he send him? *Joseph was sold as a servant.* Surely when it was done, it was the sin of the brothers, and yet God sent Joseph into Egypt. A great and very necessary matter is to be gazed upon, then, how God uses well the evil works of men, just as they, on the contrary, use badly the good works of God.

**13.** [vv. 18, 19.] Then he wove together the narration, commemorating what Joseph endured in his lowliness, and how he was raised on high. *They humbled his feet in fetters; iron passed through his soul, until his word came.* That Joseph received fetters, we do not read; but that it was done, is by no means to be doubted. For some things could be passed over in that history, which yet did not lie hidden from the Holy Spirit, who speaks in these Psalms. But the iron which he says passed through his soul, we take to be the tribulation of hard necessity: for he did not say "body," but *soul.* For there is a certain such expression in the Gospel, where Simeon said to Mary: *Behold, this one is set for the fall, and for the rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that will be contradicted; and a sword will pass through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed*[^32]. For the passion of the Lord, which to many was a fall, and in which the hidden things of many hearts were laid open, since it was made plain what they thought of the Lord, without doubt saddened his very mother too, struck grievously by carnal bereavement. But Joseph was in that tribulation *until his word came*, by which he had truly interpreted the dreams: whence he was commended to the king, that he might foretell to him too the future from his dreams. But because he said, *until his word came*, lest we should understand *his* in such a way that anyone might suppose so great a matter should be attributed to a man; he immediately added: *The eloquence of the Lord inflamed him*: or, what other manuscripts have, more expressed from the Greek: *The eloquence of the Lord set him on fire;* so that he too might be counted among those to whom it was said: *Be praised in his holy name*[^33]. *The eloquence of the Lord set him on fire.* Rightly, when the Holy Spirit was sent from the Lord, there appeared to them tongues divided as it were of fire: and the Apostle says, *fervent in spirit*[^34]. From which fire those depart of whom it is said: *The love of many will grow cold*[^35].

**14.** [vv. 20-22.] Finally there follows: *The king sent, and loosed him; the prince of peoples, and let him go.* He himself is the *king*, who is the *prince of peoples: he loosed* the fettered one, *let go* the shut-up one. *He set him lord of his house, and prince of all his possession. That he might instruct his princes like himself, and teach his elders prudence.* The Greek has: *And teach his elders wisdom.* Which could in every way be said word for word thus: *He instructed his princes like himself, and made his elders wise*: for the Greek has a word for those we are wont to call "elders," not the word for "old men"; but the other word, which cannot be said in Latin by one word, is from "wisdom," which in Greek is called sophia, not from "prudence," which is called phronesis. Yet neither do we read this in that exaltation of Joseph, just as we do not read of the fetters in his lowliness. But how could it be that so great a man, a worshiper of the one true God, in Egypt was intent only on feeding bodies, and on governing only bodily things, and bore no care for the souls by which he might make them better? But those things were written in that history, which, according to the intention of the writer, in whom was the Holy Spirit, were judged sufficient to signify, by that narration, things to come.

**15.** [v. 23.] *And Israel entered into Egypt, and Jacob was a sojourner in the land of Ham.* What is Israel, this is Jacob; and what is Egypt, this is the land of Ham. For here it is most plainly shown that from the seed of Ham, son of Noah, whose firstborn was Canaan, the nation of the Egyptians too arose. Accordingly, in those manuscripts where in this place is read *Canaan*, it must be corrected. But it was better translated, *was a sojourner*, than, as other manuscripts have, *dwelt*: which would be the same, if it were said "inhabited"; for it signifies nothing else. For it is the very same word in the Greek in this place, which is also above, where it was said: *Very few and sojourners in it.* But sojourning, or dwelling-as-a-guest, shows not a native, but a newcomer. Behold how *they passed from nation to nation, from kingdom to another people.* What had been briefly proposed, was unfolded by a brief narration. But from what kingdom they passed to another people, can rightly be asked. For they did not yet reign in the land of Canaan, because the kingdom of the people of Israel had not yet been established there. How, then, can it be understood, except perhaps by anticipation, because there the kingdom of their seed was to be?

**16.** [v. 24.] Then the things done in Egypt are narrated. *And he increased*, he says, *his people exceedingly, and made it firm over its enemies.* This too is all briefly proposed, that how it was done may then be narrated. For the people of God was not then made firm over the Egyptian enemies, when their male offspring were being killed, or when they were being worn down in making bricks; but when, by a mighty hand, through the signs and portents of the Lord their God, they were made to be feared and honored, until the contention of the hard king was overcome, and the Red Sea overwhelmed the persecutor with his army.

**17.** [v. 25.] What, then, was briefly set down, *He made his people firm over its enemies*, as if we were asking how it was done, he begins to tell, until by narrating he determines it too. *And he turned their heart, that they hated his people, and dealt deceitfully with his servants.* Is it to be understood, or believed, that God turns the heart of a man to commit sins? or is it not a sin, or a small sin, to hate the people of God, and to deal deceitfully with his servants? Who would say this? Is God, then, the author of these so grave sins, who is to be believed the author of no sin even the lightest? Who is wise, and will understand these things? For this is that wonderful goodness of God, by which he uses well even the bad, whether angels or men. For although they themselves are bad by their own fault, he makes good come from their evil. For they were not good before they hated his people; but malign and impious, such as would easily envy their fellow-dwellers who were prosperous. In that, then, in which he multiplied his people, by this benefit of his he turned the bad to envying. For envy is the hatred of another's prosperity. So, then, he turned their heart, that through envy they hated his people, and dealt deceitfully with his servants. Not, then, by making their heart bad, but by doing good to his people, he turned their heart, of its own accord bad, to hatred. For he did not pervert an upright heart, but turned one of its own accord perverse to the hatred of the people, where he might use that evil well; not by making them bad, but by lavishing on these people goods which the bad could most easily envy. By which hatred of theirs, how he used it both for the exercise of his people, and for the glory of his name, which is useful to us, the consequent things teach: which are commemorated in his praise, when Alleluia is sung.

**18.** [v. 26.] *He sent Moses his servant, Aaron whom he chose, him.* It would suffice, *whom he chose;* but nothing is to be sought in what is added, *him.* It is an expression of the Scriptures, as is: *In which they will dwell in it*: with which expression the divine pages are full.

**19.** [v. 27.] *He set in them the words of his signs and his prodigies in the land of Ham.* We ought not to take *the words of signs and prodigies* as if they were words, by which words the signs and prodigies were done, that is, which they should speak that signs and prodigies might be done. For many were done without words, whether by the rod, or by the hand stretched out, or by ashes cast into the sky. But because those very things that were done were not empty of some signification, just as the words we speak too; therefore they too are called words, not of voices and sounds, but of signs and prodigies. *He set in them*, that is, he did through them.

**20.** [v. 28.] *He sent darkness, and made it dark.* This too is written among the plagues with which the Egyptians were struck. But what follows is read variously in different manuscripts. For some have: *And they exacerbated his words;* but others: *And they did not exacerbate his words*: but what I said first, we find in more; but where the negative particle is added, we could scarcely find two manuscripts. But lest perhaps the corruption has abounded because of the easier sense; for what is more easily understood than that which was said: *And they exacerbated his words*, namely by their obstinate contradictions? we have tried to expound that too according to some right meaning: and this meanwhile occurs: *They did not exacerbate his words*, that is, in Moses and Aaron; because they bore with them most patiently even when most hard, until all the things which God had arranged to do in them were fulfilled in order.

**21.** [vv. 29, 30.] *He turned their waters into blood, and killed their fish. Their land gave forth frogs, in the inner chambers of their kings*: as if he said: He turned their land into frogs. For there had been so great a multitude of frogs, that this could fittingly be said.

**22.** [v. 31.] *He spoke, and there came the dog-fly and the gnats in all their borders.* If it is asked when he spoke, it was in his word before it came to be; and without time it was there, at what time it should come to be: although both through the angels, and through his servants Moses and Aaron, even then in a way he spoke that it should be done, when it was to be done.

**23.** [v. 32.] *He made their rains hail.* The expression is similar to that where he says, *Their land gave forth frogs*; except that there the whole land was certainly not turned into frogs, whereas the rain could be turned wholly into hail. *A burning fire in their land*: it is understood, *he set.*

**24.** [v. 33.] *And he struck their vines and their fig trees, and broke every tree of their borders.* This was done by the force of hail and lightning; whence he said too *burning fire.*

**25.** [v. 34.] *He spoke, and there came the locust and the caterpillar, of which there was no number.* The locust and the caterpillar are one plague; since the one is the parent, the other the offspring.

**26.** [v. 35.] *And it ate all the hay in their land, and ate all the fruit of their land.* Hay too is fruit, as Scripture is wont to speak, which calls even the standing crops of grain "hay"; but in order to say two things, he perhaps wished to make them correspond in number to the two he had said, that is, the locust and the caterpillar. But all this pertains to the variety of expression that cures weariness, not to a diversity of meanings.

**27.** [v. 36.] *And he struck every firstborn in their land, the firstfruits of all their labor.* This plague is the last, except the death in the Red Sea. But I think the firstfruits of their labors were said because of the firstborn of the cattle. Which plagues, although they are ten, were neither all commemorated, nor read in the same order in which they were done there. For the praising is free from the law of the one narrating and weaving the history. The author and speaker of which praising, since it is the Holy Spirit through the Prophet; by the same authority by which he acted through him the man who wrote that history, he both commemorates something done which is not read there, and passes over what is read there.

**28.** [v. 37.] But he adds this too to the praises of God, that he led the Israelites enriched with silver and gold out of Egypt; because they themselves were such as could not yet scorn the wage of their labors, temporal indeed, yet owed and just: nor, in that they deceived the Egyptians, of whom they asked that gold and silver be lent to them, is God to be thought to command, or, if they did it, to approve such deceits in those who have their heart aloft. For rather, through those words of God—certainly by him who saw their heart and examined their desires—they were permitted to do these things, rather than commanded: yet not without some advancement of the carnal soul, in that they did this to those who justly suffered such things, and, though by deceit, yet took away from wicked men what was owed to be rendered to them. But as by the iniquity of the Egyptians, so by the weakness of these, God divinely used it for what was needed to figure and foretell by those deeds. *And he led them out in silver and gold.* And this too is an expression of the Scriptures. For it was said, *in silver and gold*, as if it were said, with silver and gold. *And there was not in their tribes one infirm*: but in body, not in mind. This too was a great benefit of God, that in that necessity of migrating there was no one sick.

**29.** [v. 38.] *Egypt was glad at their departure, because the fear of them fell upon them.* The fear, certainly, of the Hebrews upon the Egyptians. For it is not *their fear*, by which the Hebrews feared, but by which they were feared. Someone will say: How, then, were the Egyptians unwilling to let them go? how did they let them go as if they would return? how did they lend gold and silver to those asking, as if they would return and repay, if *Egypt was glad at their departure?* But it is to be understood that, after that last death of the Egyptians, and so great a slaughter in the Red Sea of so great a pursuing army, the remaining Egyptians feared lest the Hebrews return, and crush their remnants with great ease. Then was fulfilled what above, when he had said, *And he increased his people exceedingly,* he soon added: *And he made it firm over its enemies.* For to expound this sentence, set down in one little verse, how it was done, he added the other things which he narrated in this praise of disaster, up to this place, where he says, *Egypt was glad at their departure, because the fear of them fell upon them;* as it were assigning what he had proposed, that he made his people firm over its enemies.

**30.** [v. 39.] Accordingly now he tells what divine benefits were conferred on them as they journeyed in the wilderness. *He spread out a cloud for their protection, and fire that it might shine for them by night.* These things are as manifest as they are known.

**31.** [v. 40.] *They asked, and the quail came.* They did not desire the quail, but flesh. But because the quail too is flesh, and in this psalm he does not speak of the embittering of those in whom God is not well pleased, but of the faith of the elect, which is the true seed of Abraham; they themselves are to be understood as having asked that there might come that whence the murmur of the embittered might be put down. Now in the verse that follows, *And he satisfied them with the bread of heaven,* he indeed did not name manna, but it is obscure to no one who reads those Scriptures.

**32.** [v. 41.] *He broke open the rock, and waters flowed; rivers went forth in the dry ground.* And this deed is understood as quickly as it is read.

**33.** [vv. 42-44.] But by all these benefits of his, God commends the merit of faith in Abraham. For there follows and he says: *Because he was mindful of his holy word, which he had toward Abraham his servant. And he led out his people in exultation, and his chosen ones in gladness.* What he says, *his people;* this he repeated, *his chosen ones*: and what he says, *in exultation;* this he repeated, *in gladness. And he gave them the regions of the nations, and they possessed the labors of the peoples.* What are *the regions of the nations;* these are *the labors of the peoples*: and what was said, *he gave them;* this was repeated, *they possessed.*

**34.** [v. 45.] And as if we were asking for what good these things were given, lest this very thing be supposed the highest good, that this prosperity of temporal things was given to the people of God; he immediately referred it to something else, where the highest good must be sought: *That they may keep*, he says, *his justifications, and seek out his law.* Where it is to be understood that the servants of God and the chosen sons of the promise, the true and germane seed of Abraham, imitating the faith of Abraham, receive these earthly goods from God for this reason, that they may not flow loose in luxury in them, or grow torpid in perverse security; but that they may have all these things prepared by the divine mercy, in the seeking of which they could be occupied with the most busy labors, that they may be free for that whence the eternal good can be acquired, that is: *That they may keep his justifications, and seek out his law.* Finally, since he wished such men here to be understood as the seed of Abraham, who would truly be the seed of Abraham, such as certainly were not lacking even in that people: which the apostle Paul too sufficiently shows, when he says, *But God was not well pleased with all of them*[^36]; for if *not with all*, surely there were certain there in whom *God was well pleased*: because, then, this psalm commends such, he said nothing here of the iniquities and provocations and embittering of those in whom God is not well pleased. But because to the wicked too not only the justice, but also the mercy of God almighty and clement appeared; of these the following psalm speaks with the praises of God. And yet both were in one people, nor did those defile these by the contagion of their iniquities. For *the Lord knows who are his*: and if in this age he cannot separate from the unjust, *let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity*[^37].

**35.** So that, as it were in the body of this psalm, we may commend its as it were hidden soul, that is, in the as it were outer words the inner understanding: it seems to me that the seed of Abraham is admonished, who are all the sons of the promise pertaining to the eternal inheritance of the eternal covenant, to choose God himself as it were their very inheritance, and to worship him freely, that is, for his own sake, not for any profit of reward outside himself; and to do this praising, calling upon him, announcing him, working well through faith not to their own, but to his glory, rejoicing in hope, fervent in love. All this is sounded by these verses: *Confess to the Lord, and call upon his name, announce among the nations his works. Sing to him, and sing psalms to him, tell all his wonders. Be praised in his holy name; let the heart of those who seek the Lord be glad. Seek the Lord, and be confirmed; seek his face always.*

**36.** Then, to nourish the hearts of the little ones, that they may be strengthened in faith, from the Patriarchs are set forth examples both of their faith and of God's promise, that by imitating and hoping we may be their seed, not from the nation of the Hebrews alone, but as many as receive this grace in all the earth. All of which is contained in these verses: *Remember his wonders which he did, his prodigies and the judgments of his mouth. Seed of Abraham his servants, sons of Jacob his chosen ones. He is the Lord our God, in all the earth are his judgments. He was mindful forever of his covenant, the word which he commanded for a thousand generations. Which he arranged with Abraham, and his oath to Isaac. And he established it for Jacob himself as a precept; and for Israel himself as an eternal covenant, saying: To you I will give the land of Canaan, the cord of your inheritance.* All of which, according to my small measure, I have expounded how they were to be taken.

**37.** Here it occurred to a mind of little faith: If, then, God is to be worshiped freely, and as the inheritance of the eternal covenant is to be sought from himself; does he not, even by the multiplication of his own mercy, fail to desert this mortal life of those who seek him, and their temporal needs? For listen attentively to what he bestowed on our fathers, both those in whom he set examples of faith, and those who, propagated from their flesh, also imitated their faith. *When they themselves were few in number, very few and sojourners in it:* that is, in the land of Canaan. *And they passed from nation to nation, and from kingdom to another people. He did not leave a man to harm them, and he reproved kings for their sake. Do not touch my anointed ones, and do not deal maliciously with my prophets.*

**38.** But if you ask how *they passed from nation to nation, and from kingdom to another people*, hear: *And he called a famine upon the land, he broke every support of bread. He sent a man before them; Joseph was sold as a servant. They humbled his feet in fetters; iron passed through his soul, until his word came. The eloquence of the Lord set him on fire: the king sent, and loosed him; the prince of peoples, and let him go. He set him lord of his house, and prince of all his possession. That he might instruct his princes like himself, and teach his elders prudence. And Israel entered into Egypt, and Jacob was a sojourner in the land of Ham.* Behold how *they passed from nation to nation, and from kingdom to another people.*

**39.** *And he increased his people exceedingly, and made it firm over its enemies.* But if you wish to know how he made it firm over its enemies, hear: *He turned their heart, that they hated his people, and dealt deceitfully with his servants. He sent Moses his servant, Aaron whom he chose, him. He set in them the words of his signs, and of his prodigies in the land of Ham. He sent darkness, and made it dark, and they exacerbated his words. He turned their waters into blood, and killed their fish. Their land gave forth frogs, in the inner chambers of their kings. He spoke, and there came the dog-fly and the gnats in all their borders. He made their rains hail, a burning fire in their land. And he struck their vines and their fig trees, and broke every tree of their borders. He spoke, and there came the locust and the caterpillar, of which there was no number. And it ate all the hay in their land, and ate all the fruit of their land. And he struck every firstborn in their land, the firstfruits of all their labor. And he led them out in silver and gold, and there was not in their tribes one infirm. Egypt was glad at their departure, because the fear of them fell upon them.* Behold how he made his people firm over its enemies.

**40.** But while his justice inflicted these evils on their enemies; what his mercy lavished even of temporal things on them, hear: *He spread out a cloud for their protection, and fire that it might shine for them by night. They asked, and the quail came, and he satisfied them with the bread of heaven. He broke open the rock, and waters flowed; rivers went forth in the dry ground. Because he was mindful of his holy word, which he had toward Abraham his servant. And he led out his people in exultation, and his chosen ones in gladness. And he gave them the regions of the nations, and they possessed the labors of the peoples.* Not that they should worship him because of these things, but that they should refer and turn these things too to the eternal good; that is: *That they may keep his justifications, and seek out his law.* Whatever other goods, then, God gives, are to be referred to the free worship of him: but the worship of him itself is not to be referred to other goods which God gives; for then it will be free. Provoking to which contest, the enemy dared to say to God: *Does Job worship God for nothing?*[^38] Further, if Joseph, sold as a servant, and humbled, and exalted, made a way to temporal goods for the people of God, that it might be made firm over its enemies; how much more does Jesus, sold and humbled by his brothers according to the flesh, and exalted in the heavens, make a way to eternal goods for the people of God, triumphing over the devil and his angels? Hear, then, O seed of Abraham, not glorying in the flesh, but imitating his faith; hear, O servants of God, and chosen of God, having the promise of the present life and of the future. If temptations are hard in this age, think of Joseph in prison, of Jesus on the cross. If the prosperity of temporal things is at hand, use it not for its own sake before God, but use it for the sake of God: and do not suppose that he is worshiped by his worshipers for the necessities of this life, which he gives even to his blasphemers; but seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you[^39].
