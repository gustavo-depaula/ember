# Exposition of Psalm 150

**1.** Although the order of the Psalms, which seems to me to contain the secret of a great mystery, has not yet been revealed to me; nevertheless, because they are all numbered as one hundred fifty, they suggest something even to us who have not yet penetrated the depth of their whole order with the keenness of the mind, from which we may not unbecomingly argue, so far as the Lord helps. First, the number fifteen, from which this number is multiplied (for what fifteen is worth in the order of units, that one hundred fifty is worth in the order of tens, since fifteen times ten makes them; that fifteen hundred is worth in the order of hundreds, since fifteen times a hundred are they; that fifteen thousand is worth in the order of thousands, since fifteen times a thousand are they): the number fifteen, then, signifies the concord of the two Testaments. For in the one the sabbath is observed, which signifies rest[^1]: in the other the Lord's day, which signifies the resurrection. Now the sabbath is the seventh day: but the Lord's day, after the seventh, what is it but the eighth, the same that is to be held as the first? For it is also called *the first of the sabbath*[^2], so that thereafter there is the second, the third, the fourth, and so on to the seventh sabbath. But from the Lord's day to the Lord's day is the eighth day: in which is declared the revelation of the New Testament, which in the Old was covered as it were under earthly promises. Now seven and eight are fifteen. Just so many are the canticles which are called of the steps, since just so many were also the steps of the temple. Then the number fifty too bears a great mystery in itself. For it consists of a week of weeks, with one added as it were as the very eighth, to complete the number fifty. For seven times seven make forty-nine; to which one is added, that there may be fifty. This number fifty is of so great significance that, when so many days were completed after the Lord's resurrection, on the very fiftieth day the Holy Spirit came upon those who had been gathered together in Christ[^3]. Which Holy Spirit is in the Scriptures commended especially by the number seven, whether in Isaiah, or in the Apocalypse[^4]; where most openly seven Spirits of God are spoken of, on account of the sevenfold working of one and the same Spirit. This sevenfold working is recalled through the prophet Isaiah thus: *The Spirit of God will rest upon him; the Spirit of wisdom and of understanding, the Spirit of counsel and of fortitude, the Spirit of knowledge and of piety, the Spirit of the fear of the Lord*[^5]. By this is to be understood the chaste fear of the Lord[^6], abiding for ages of ages. But servile fear perfect love casts out[^7]; and it makes us free, that we may not do servile works, which are forbidden on the sabbath. But *the love of God is poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us*[^8]. Hence too the Holy Spirit is commended by the number seven. But the Lord divided the number fifty also into forty and ten. For on the fortieth day after his resurrection he ascended into heaven, and then, ten days being completed, he sent the Holy Spirit[^9]: commending namely by the number forty the temporal cohabitation in this world. For the number four prevails in forty; and the world and the year have four parts: but with ten added, as it were a wage rendered for the law fulfilled by good works, eternity itself is figured. This number fifty the number one hundred fifty has threefold, as though the Trinity had multiplied it. Whence also, for this reason, we not unfittingly understand this to be the number of the Psalms. For also in that number of fishes which were caught in the nets cast after the resurrection, by adding three to one hundred fifty[^10], an admonition seems to be made into how many parts this number ought to be divided, that it may have fifty three times. Although that number of fishes has also another account, much more subtle and more delightful, in that seventeen, cast into a triangle, that is, all the numbers from one to seventeen being added together, one arrives at the same number. But in ten the Law is signified, in seven Grace; because the Law is not fulfilled except by love poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who is signified by the number seven.

**2.** As for the fact, then, that certain men have believed there to be five books of all the Psalms, they followed this, that as often as it was said at the end of a psalm: *So be it, so be it*[^11]. But when I wished to grasp the account of this division, I was not able: because neither are these five parts themselves equal to one another, even if not in the quantity of writing, at least in the very number of the Psalms, so that they should have thirty each. And if the end of each book is *So be it, so be it*; why the fifth book, and likewise the last, is not terminated by the same ending, may rightly be asked. But we, following the authority of canonical Scripture, where it is read: *For it is written in the book of Psalms*[^12]; know that the book of Psalms is one. And I see indeed how both this is true, and that, if it is true, does not conflict with this. For it can be that by some custom of Hebrew letters one book is called what consists of several; just as out of several Churches one Church consists, and out of several heavens one heaven (for he who said: *My help is from the Lord, who made heaven and earth*[^13], did not pass over any of the heavens. And although Scripture says: *And God called the firmament heaven*[^14]; and that there are waters above the firmament, that is, above the heaven: yet the same Scripture does not lie when it says: *And let the waters that are above the heavens praise the name of the Lord*[^15]; because it does not say, above the heaven), and there is one earth out of many lands. For we say both "the orb of the earth," and "the orb of the lands," in daily usage. And he who says: *It is written in the book of Psalms*, although the manner of speaking is such that he seems to have wished to suggest that there is one book; yet it can be answered, that *in the book of Psalms* was said meaning: In some one of their five books: which is in the usage of speech either not at all, or rarely, so that even the twelve Prophets are believed to be one book for this reason, because it is likewise read: *As it is written in the book of the Prophets*[^16]. There are even those who call all the canonical Scriptures together one book, because they agree in a very wonderful and divine unity: and that hence it was said: *In the head of the book it is written of me, that I should do your will*[^17]; so that it is thus understood that the Father made the world through the Son, whose creation is the beginning of the Scriptures in the book of Genesis. Or rather, because it seems to be prophecy, not narrating things done, but foretelling things to come (for he does not say, what I have done; but, *that I should do your will*, or, *that I should do your will*); this sentence is to be referred to that which is written in the first parts of the same book: *The two shall be in one flesh*[^18]. Which the Apostle says is a great mystery, in Christ and in the Church[^19]. Although this book of Psalms too can be understood as signified, where it is said: *In the head of the book it is written of me, that I should do your will*: for there follows: *My God, I have willed it, and your law in the midst of my heart*[^20]. For of him is taken the very first psalm, prophesied in the head of this book: *Blessed is the man who has not gone away in the counsel of the impious, and has not stood in the way of sinners, and has not sat in the seat of pestilence: but his will is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he will meditate day and night*[^21]: so that this is what was said: *My God, I have willed it, and your law in the midst of my heart.* But what follows: *I have proclaimed your justice in the great Church*[^22]; is more fittingly referred to that: *And the two shall be in one flesh.*

**3.** Whether, then, the one or the other be understood that was said, *In the head of the book*; this book of the Psalms, by fifties, if it is questioned according to these very fifty-fold joints, answers something great and very worthy of consideration. For it does not seem to me without reason that the fiftieth is about penitence, the hundredth about mercy and judgment, the hundred fiftieth about the praise of God in his saints. For thus we tend toward the eternal and blessed life, first by condemning our sins, then by living well, so that, after the evil life is condemned and the good one lived, we may merit the eternal. For according to the purpose of his most hidden justice and goodness, God, *whom he predestined, them he also called; and whom he called, them he also justified; but whom he justified, them he also glorified*[^23]. Our predestination was not made in us, but in secret with him, in his foreknowledge. But the three others are made in us: calling, justification, glorification. We are called by the preaching of penitence: for so the Lord began to preach the gospel: *Do penance; for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near*[^24]. We are justified in the calling of mercy, and in the fear of judgment: hence it is that it is said: *O God, in your name save me, and in your strength judge me*[^25]. He does not fear to be judged, who has first obtained to be saved. Being called, we renounce the devil through penitence, that we may not remain under his yoke: being justified, we are healed through mercy, that we may not fear judgment: being glorified, we pass into the eternal life, where we praise God without end. To this I think pertains what the Lord says: *Behold, I cast out demons, and perform cures today and tomorrow, and on the third day I am consummated*[^26]. Which he showed also in the three days of his passion and sleeping and waking. For he was crucified, and buried, and rose again. On the cross he triumphed over principalities and powers, in the tomb he rested, in the resurrection he exulted. Penitence torments, justice tranquilizes, eternal life glorifies. The voice of penitence is: *Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great mercy; and according to the multitude of your compassions blot out my iniquity.* This offers to God a sacrifice, a troubled spirit, a contrite and humbled heart[^27]. The voice of the justice of Christ in his elect is: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord; I will sing a psalm, and I will understand in the unspotted way, when you will come to me*[^28]. For through mercy we are helped to do justice, that we may come secure to judgment; where there are destroyed *from the city of the Lord all who work iniquity*[^29]. By which verse that psalm is concluded, it is the voice of eternal life.

**4.** [vv. 1-2.] *Praise the Lord in his saints*: surely in those whom he has glorified. *Praise him in the firmament of his power. Praise him in his powers;* or, as others have interpreted it, *in his mighty acts. Praise him according to the multitude of his greatness.* All these things are his saints themselves; as the Apostle says: *That we may be the justice of God in him*[^30]. If, then, the justice of God which he made in them, why not also the power of God which he made in them, that they might rise from the dead? For in Christ's resurrection too the power is especially commended; because in the passion there was weakness, as the Apostle says: *Although he was crucified out of weakness, yet he lives out of the power of God*[^31]: and in another place: *That I may know*, he says, *him, and the power of his resurrection*[^32]. But well he says: *In the firmament of his power.* For it is the firmament of power, because he will no longer die, and death will no longer have dominion over him[^33]. Why may they not also be called the powers of God, which he made in them? Rather let them be his powers; as it was said: *We* are *the justice of God in him.* For what is more powerful than to reign forever, all enemies being placed under the feet? Why may they themselves not also be the multitude of his greatness? Not that by which he himself is great, but by which he made them great, so many, that is, thousands of thousands. As it is understood in one way the justice by which he himself is just; in another way that which he works in us, that we may be his justice.

**5.** These same saints are signified thereafter in all the musical instruments, for praising God. For what he proposed in saying: *Praise the Lord in his saints*, this he pursues, signifying in various ways these same saints of his.

**6.** [v. 3.] *Praise him in the sound of the trumpet*: on account of the most excellent clarity of his praise. *Praise him on the psaltery and the harp.* The psaltery praises God from the higher things, the harp praises God from the lower things; as it were from heavenly and earthly things, as praising him who made heaven and earth. For indeed in another psalm we have expounded that the psaltery has above that sounding board of wood, on which the rows of strings rest, that it may render a better sound: which board the harp has below.

**7.** [v. 4.] *Praise him on the timbrel and in the choir.* The timbrel praises God, when in the flesh now changed there is no weakness of earthly corruption. For the timbrel is made from a hide dried and made firm. The choir praises God, when a society at peace praises him. *Praise him on the strings and the organ.* Both the psaltery and the harp, which were mentioned above, have strings. But the organ is a general name for all instruments of music; although custom has now obtained that those are properly called organs which are inflated by bellows: which kind I do not think to be signified here. For since the word "organ" is Greek, as I said, general to all musical instruments; that one to which bellows are applied the Greeks call by another name. But that it should be called an organ is rather a Latin, and that a common, usage. As for what he says, then, *on the strings and the organ*, it seems to me that he wished to signify some organ which has strings. For not only psalteries and harps have strings: but because in the psaltery and the harp, on account of the sound from the lower and the higher parts, something was found which could be understood according to this distinction, he has admonished us to seek something else in the strings themselves; because they too are flesh, but now freed from corruption. To which perhaps for this reason he added the organ, not that they should sound singly, but that by a most concordant diversity they should sound together, as they are arranged in an organ. For even then the saints of God will have their differences sounding together, not dissonant, that is, agreeing, not disagreeing; just as a most sweet concord is made out of sounds diverse indeed, but not adverse to one another. *For star differs from star in clarity; so also is the resurrection of the dead*[^34].

**8.** [vv. 5-6.] *Praise him on well-sounding cymbals, praise him on cymbals of jubilation.* Cymbals touch one another that they may sound; and therefore they have been compared by some to our lips. But I think it better understood that we praise God in a certain way on cymbals, when each one is honored by his neighbor, not by himself; and honoring one another in turn, they give praise to God. But lest anyone should understand cymbals that sound without a soul, for this reason I think it added, *on cymbals of jubilation.* For jubilation, that is, ineffable praise, proceeds only from a soul. Nor do I think it should be passed over, what the musicians say, and the thing itself is manifest, that there are three kinds of sounds: by voice, by breath, by striking: by voice, as it is through the throat and windpipe of a man singing, without any instrument; by breath, as through the flute, or anything of that kind; by striking, as through the harp, or anything of that kind. No kind, then, has been passed over here: for the voice is in the choir, the breath in the trumpet, the striking in the harp; as it were mind, spirit, body; but by likenesses, not by properties. As for what he proposed, then: *Praise the Lord in his saints*, to whom did he say this, but to those very ones? And in whom that they should praise God, but in themselves? For you, his saints, he says, are his power, but that which he made in you; and his might, and the multitude of his greatness, which he made and showed in you. You are the trumpet, the psaltery, the harp, the timbrel, the choir, the strings, and the organ, and the cymbals of jubilation well-sounding, because sounding together. You are all these things: let nothing vile be thought of here, nothing transitory, nothing for sport. And because to be wise according to the flesh is death: *Let every spirit praise the Lord.*
