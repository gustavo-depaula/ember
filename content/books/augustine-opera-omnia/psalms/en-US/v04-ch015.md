# Exposition of Psalm 87

**1.** [v. 1.] The title of this eighty-seventh psalm has something that brings new work to the expounder. For nowhere in the other psalms is set down what is read here: *For melech, to answer.* For of "a Psalm of a Song" and "a Song of a Psalm" we have already said elsewhere what seemed to us; and "for the sons of Korah" is usual in the titles of the psalms and very often treated, and what is "unto the end"; but what follows: *For melech, to answer,* this title has as unusual. Now *for melech* can be said in Latin "for a chorus": for by the Hebrew word *melech* a chorus is signified. What is, then, For a chorus, to answer; except perhaps that the chorus may answer the one singing by harmonizing? Just as it is to be believed that not only this, but other psalms too were sung, although they received other titles; which I think was done for the sake of variety, by which weariness might be relieved. For not this psalm alone was held worthy to have a chorus answer, since it surely is not the only one written about the Lord's passion. Or if there is another reason why there is so great a variety of titles, by which it could be shown that all the Psalms which are headed in one way and another are so headed that the title of none can fit any other; I, I confess, although I tried much, could not penetrate it: and if I read anything said about this matter by those who treated these things before us, it did not satisfy my expectation, or my slowness. In what mystery, then, it seems to me to be said: For a chorus, to answer—that is, that the one singing through a chorus may be answered—I will expound. Here the passion of the Lord is prophesied. But the apostle Peter says: *Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example, that we should follow his footsteps*[^1]: this is to answer. The apostle John too says: *As Christ laid down his soul for us, so we too ought to lay down our soul for the brethren*[^2]: this is to answer. But a chorus signifies concord, which consists in love. Whoever, then, as an emulator of the Lord's passion, delivers his body that it may burn, if he has not love, does not answer in the chorus; and therefore it profits him nothing[^3]. Accordingly, as are said in the art of music—as learned men could say it in Latin—precentor and succentor; the precentor namely who sends forth the voice first in the song, but the succentor who, singing afterward, answers: so in this song of the passion, the chorus of martyrs follows Christ going before, unto the end of heavenly crowns. For this is sung *for the sons of Korah,* that is, for the imitators of the passion of Christ: since in the place of Calvary Christ was crucified[^4]; which is reported to be the interpretation of that Hebrew name, that is: Korah. And this is the *understanding of Heman the Israelite*: which is set at the end of this title. For Heman is said to be interpreted: His brother. For Christ deigned to make those his brothers who understand the sacrament of his cross, and not only do not blush at it, but even faithfully glory in it, not exalting themselves on their own merits, but not ungrateful to his grace; so that to each of them it can be said: *Behold, truly an Israelite, in whom there is no guile*[^5]: as Scripture commends that Israel himself was without guile[^6]. Now, then, let us hear the voice of Christ singing beforehand in prophecy, to whom his chorus answers either by imitation, or by giving of thanks.

**2.** [vv. 2-3.] *O Lord God of my salvation, in the day I cried and in the night before you. Let my prayer enter into your sight; incline your ear to my entreaty.* For the Lord too prayed, not according to the form of God, but according to the form of a servant: for according to this he also suffered. But he prayed both when he was in glad circumstances, which I judge is signified by the name of day; and when in adverse, which by the name of night. The entrance of prayer into the sight of God is its acceptance; the inclining of the ear of God is a merciful hearing: for God does not have such members as we have in the body. But it is the usual repetition: for what is: *Let my prayer enter into your sight;* is the same as: *Incline your ear to my entreaty.*

**3.** [v. 4.] *For my soul is filled with evils, and my life has drawn near to hell.* Do we dare to say that the soul of Christ was filled with evils, since that affliction of the passion availed in the flesh whatever it availed? Whence he too, kindling his own to endurance of the passion, and as it were exhorting his chorus to answer: *Do not,* he says, *fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul*[^7]. Or can the soul not be killed by persecutors, but be filled with evils? But if it is so, it is to be asked with what. For we cannot say that soul was filled with vices, by which iniquity rules over a man; but perhaps with griefs, by which the soul suffers together with its flesh in its passion. For not even what is called the pain of the body can be without the soul, which inevitably-imminent pain is preceded by sadness, which is the pain of the soul alone. The soul, then, can grieve even when the body does not grieve; but the body cannot grieve without the soul. Why, then, should we not say that the soul of Christ was filled, not with human sins, but yet with human evils? Of whom another prophet says that he grieved for us[^8]: and the evangelist: *Taking,* he says, *Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, he began to be saddened and to be sorrowful;* and the Lord himself then said of himself to them: *My soul is sorrowful even unto death*[^9]. The Prophet, foreseeing these things to come, the writer of this psalm, bringing him in speaking: *For my soul is filled,* he says, *with evils, and my life has drawn near to hell.* For he explained altogether in other words the very statement by which it was said: *My soul is sorrowful even unto death.* For what he says: *My soul is sorrowful;* this was said: *My soul is filled with evils*: and what follows, *even unto death;* this was said, *and my life has drawn near to hell.* But these affections of human weakness, like the very flesh of human weakness, and the death of the human flesh, the Lord Jesus took up, not by the necessity of his condition, but by the will of his compassion, that he might transfigure into himself his body, which is the Church, of which he deigned to be the head, that is, his members in his saints and faithful: so that if it should happen to any of them amid human temptations to be saddened and to grieve, he should not therefore suppose himself estranged from his grace; and that these are not sins, but indications of human weakness, the body might learn from its own head, like a chorus singing in harmony with the voice sent before it. For in this body we read and hear the apostle Paul, the chief of the members, confessing that his soul was filled with such evils, when he says that there is great sadness to him, and continual grief in his heart for his brethren according to the flesh, who are Israelites[^10]. For whom if we say the Lord too was saddened, when his passion drew near, in which they were about to bind themselves with a most monstrous crime, I judge that we say it not incongruously.

**4.** [vv. 5-6.] Finally that which he said on the cross: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^11]; is said in this psalm too next: *I am counted among those who go down into the pit*: surely by those who did not know what they did, who supposed him to die so as other men die, as though subject to necessity and overcome by that necessity. For by *the pit* he meant either the depth of misery or of hell.

**5.** *I have become,* he says, *as a man without help, free among the dead.* In these words especially the person of the Lord appears. For who else is free among the dead, except he who in the likeness of the flesh of sin among sinners was alone without sin[^12]? Whence to those who foolishly thought themselves free: *Everyone,* he says, *who commits sin is the servant of sin.* And because through him who had no sin they had to be freed from sins: *If the Son,* he says, *frees you, then you will be truly free*[^13]. This one, then, *free among the dead,* who had in his power to lay down his soul, and to take it up again[^14]; from whom no one took it, but he himself laid it down by his will; who could also raise up his flesh as a temple loosed by them, when he willed[^15]; whom, when all had deserted him about to suffer, he alone did not remain alone, because the Father did not desert him, as he himself testifies[^16]; yet by enemies, for whom he prayed as not knowing what they did, and saying: *He saved others, himself he cannot. If he is the Son of God, let him come down now from the cross, and we will believe him. Let him save him, if he wants him*[^17]; he became—that is, was esteemed—*as a man without help. As the wounded sleeping in the sepulcher.* But he adds, *whom you do not remember as yet*: where the distinction of the Lord Christ from the other dead is to be noticed. For he too was wounded, and dead, laid in a sepulcher[^18]: but those who did not know what they did, surely not knowing who he was, supposed him like to others slain by a wound and sleeping in a sepulcher, whom God does not remember as yet—that is, whose time of rising has not yet come. For Scripture is wont to call the dead sleeping for this reason, because it wishes them to be understood as about to wake—that is, to rise. But this one, wounded and sleeping in the sepulcher, on the third day awoke, and became as a solitary sparrow on a rooftop[^19]—that is, at the right hand of the Father in heaven; nor does he now die, and death will no longer have dominion over him[^20]. Whence he differs far from those to whose such resurrection God does not remember as yet: for what had to go before in the head, is kept for the body at the end. But then God is said to remember, when he acts; then to forget, when he does not act: for neither does forgetfulness fall upon God, because he is in no way changed; nor recollection, because he does not forget. *I have become,* then, by those not knowing what they did, *as a man without help;* when I was *free among the dead*: I have become, by those not knowing what they did, *as the wounded sleeping in the sepulcher. And they themselves have been expelled from your hand*: that is, when by them I was made these things, *they themselves have been expelled from your hand;* they were deprived of the help of your hand, who supposed me to be without help. *For they dug,* as he says in another psalm, *a pit before my face, and fell into it*[^21]. For I judge it better to be understood thus, than that what was said: *And they themselves have been expelled from your hand,* be referred to those sleeping in the sepulcher, whom he does not remember as yet: since among them are the righteous, of whom, although he does not remember as yet that they may rise; yet of these it is said: *The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God*[^22]—that is, they dwell in the help of the Most High, and abide in the protection of the God of heaven[^23]. But those have been expelled from the hand of God, who believed that the Lord Christ was expelled from his hand, because, reckoned among the wicked, they could kill him.

**6.** [v. 7.] *They have set me,* he says, *in the lower pit;* or rather, *in the lowest pit*: for so it is in the Greek. But what is the lowest pit, except the deepest misery, than which there is none deeper? Whence elsewhere it is said: *You have led me out of the pit of misery*[^24]. *In dark places and in the shadow of death*: they set me surely, supposing this, when they did not know what they did, and were ignorant of him whom none of the princes of this world knew[^25]. For the shadow of death—I do not know whether the death of the body is here to be understood: or rather that of which it is written: *To those who sat in darkness and the shadow of death, light has arisen for them*[^26]; because by believing in the light and in life, they were led out from the darkness and death of impiety. Such, then, those who did not know what they did supposed the Lord, and among such, not knowing, they set him, such as he himself came to the aid of, that they should not be this.

**7.** [v. 8.] *Upon me your indignation has been confirmed;* or, as other codices have, *your anger;* or as others, *your fury.* For what was set in Greek our translators interpreted diversely. For where the Greek codices have orge, there scarcely any interpreter hesitated to say "anger" in Latin; but where thymos is set, very many did not think it should be called anger, although great authors of Latin eloquence translated this too into Latin by the name of anger, from the books of the Greek philosophers: nor is this matter to be disputed at greater length; yet if we too ought to apply another name to it, I would more tolerably say "indignation" than "fury." For fury, as the Latin language has it, is not wont to belong to the sane. What, then, is: *Upon me your anger has been confirmed;* except as those supposed who did not know the Lord of glory? For with them it was so, that the anger of God had not only been stirred, but even confirmed upon him whom they could bring even unto death, nor any death, but the one they held more execrable than the rest, that is, even unto the death of the cross. Whence the Apostle says: *Christ redeemed us from the curse of the Law, made a curse for us; for it is written: Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree*[^27]. And therefore when he wished to commend his obedience unto the utmost humility: *He humbled himself,* he says, *made obedient unto death*: which, since it seemed too little, he added, *even the death of the cross*[^28]. On account of this, as it seems to me, in this psalm too such a verse follows: *And all your suspensions;* or, as some have interpreted, *all your waves;* or, as others, *all your liftings-up you have brought upon me.* It is written in another psalm too: *All your suspensions and your waves have entered upon me*[^29]: or, as some have better translated, *have passed over me;* for in the Greek it is dielthon, not eiselthon. Where, then, both are set, both "suspensions" and "waves," there waves could not be set for suspensions. These two we expounded so that we called suspensions "threats," but waves the very sufferings, both of which come from the judgment of God. But there it was said: *All have passed over me;* but here: *All you have brought upon me.* There, then, even though certain things befell; yet all the evils which he wished to be understood by those words, *passed over me,* he said: but here, *you have brought upon me.* For they pass over, either those which do not touch, like suspensions; or those which touch, like waves. But when, *All your suspensions,* he says not, passed over me, but *you brought upon me;* he signifies that all that impended befell: but they impended, as long as in prophecy they threatened as future, all that was foretold of his passion.

**8.** [vv. 9-10.] *Far,* he says, *you have made my acquaintances from me.* If we take those whom he knew to be called *my acquaintances,* they will be all: for whom did he not know? But he calls those *acquaintances,* to whom he too was known, as far as they could then know him; surely at least to this extent, that they knew him innocent, even if they thought him only a man, not also God. Although he could call even the good whom he approved *acquaintances;* as the evil whom he disapproved, unknown, to whom at the end he will say: *I do not know you*[^30]. But as for what he adds: *They have set me an abomination to themselves*: those too indeed can be understood whom he called his acquaintances, because they too dreaded that kind of death; but it is better understood of those of whom above he was speaking, his persecutors. *I was delivered up,* he says, *and did not go out.* Was it because his disciples were outside, when he himself was being judged within[^31]? or rather, *I did not go out,* ought we to take it more deeply, that is, in my interior parts I lay hidden, I did not show who I was, I was not made public, I was not manifested? and therefore it follows: *My eyes were weakened from want.* For what eyes of his are we to understand? If the exterior ones set in the flesh in which he suffered, we do not read that they were weakened from want in his passion—that is, languished as it were from hunger, as is usual: for after his own supper he was delivered up, and on the same day crucified. If the interior ones, how were they weakened from want, since in them was the indeficient light? But surely he called his eyes the members which in his body, of which he himself is the head, he loved as the brighter and more eminent and chief. Of which body the Apostle speaking, and applying a likeness from our body, says: *If the whole body were an eye, where would be the hearing? if all hearing, where the smelling? if all were one member, where the body? But now there are many members, but one body. The eye cannot say to the hands: I have no need of you. And if the hand should say: Because I am not an eye, I am not of the body; is it therefore not of the body?* But what he wished to be understood by these words, he expressed more evidently, saying: *But you are the body of Christ and members*[^32]. Wherefore those eyes too, that is the holy Apostles; to whom not flesh and blood had revealed, but his Father who is in heaven, that Peter might say: *You are Christ the Son of the living God*[^33], seeing him delivered up, and suffering so great evils; since they did not see him such as they wished, because he did not go out—that is, was not manifested in his strength and power, but hidden in his interior parts, bore all things as though overcome and powerless—*were weakened from want,* as though their food, their light, was withdrawn from them.

**9.** *And I cried,* he says, *to you, O Lord.* This indeed he did most openly, when he hung on the wood. But as for what follows: *All the day I stretched out my hands to you,* how it ought to be taken is rightly asked. For if in what he says: *I stretched out my hands,* we understand the gibbet of the cross; how are we to understand: *All the day?* Did he hang on the wood all the day, since the night too pertains to the whole day? But if in this place he wished "day" to be understood, which apart from the night is wont to be called day; even of such a day, when he was crucified, the first and not small part had already passed. But if we wish to take "day" set for "time" (especially because he set this noun in the feminine gender, which in Latin speech is wont to signify nothing but time: although in Greek it is not so; for always in that tongue "day" is said in the feminine gender, and therefore I think our translators so interpret), the question is more tightly bound. For how, in the whole time, if not even for one whole day he stretched out his hands on the cross? Further, if we say that the whole is to be taken for a part, because Scripture too is wont to use this kind of speaking; no example occurs to me of the whole being able to be taken for a part even then, when the word itself "whole" is added and "whole" is said. For even in that which the Lord said in the Gospel: *So shall the Son of man too be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights*[^34]; therefore the whole is not unusually taken for a part, because he did not say: Whole three days and whole three nights: for one was a whole middle day, but of two the parts; of the first the last, of the last the first. But if not his cross in this prophecy of the Psalm did he signify by these words, but the prayer which we have learned, the Gospel being witness, that he poured out from the form of a servant to God the Father, where we remember that he prayed both long before the passion, and on the day of the passion, and on the cross itself; we nowhere read that he did this all the day. Accordingly we can fittingly understand by the hands stretched out all the day the continuance of good works, from the intention of which he never ceased.

**10.** [v. 11.] But because his good works profited only those predestined to eternal salvation, but not all men, nor those very ones among whom they were done; therefore he consequently added: *Will you do wonders for the dead?* For if we suppose this said of those whose flesh has become lifeless; great wonders were done for the dead, since some of them even came back to life[^35]: and that the Lord penetrated the lower regions, and thence ascended as victor over death, a great miracle was done for the dead. Therefore he signifies by this word in which he says: *Will you do wonders for the dead,* men so dead in heart, that the so great wonders of Christ did not move them to the life of faith. For he did not say that wonders are not done for them, because they do not see them; but because they do not profit them. For as here he says: *All the day I stretched out my hands to you;* because he refers all his works only to the will of the Father, very often testifying that he came for this, that he might fulfill the will of the Father[^36]: so, because the unbelieving people too saw the same works, another prophet says: *All the day I stretched out my hands to a people not believing and contradicting*[^37]. They themselves are the dead, for whom wonders were not done; not that they did not see them, but that through them they did not come back to life. But as for what follows: *Or will physicians raise them up, and they will confess to you?* that is, because men will not be raised up by physicians, to confess to you. In the Hebrew indeed it is asserted to be otherwise; for not "physicians," but "giants" are reported to be: but the Seventy translators, whose authority is so great that they are not undeservedly believed, on account of their marvelous consonance, to have translated by the divine Spirit; from the likeness of the word, by which in the Hebrew tongue "giants" and "physicians" sound almost the same way, and are distinguished by a slight difference, not by error, but rather by occasion, wished to signify how "giants" should be taken in this place. For if we understand the proud intimated by the name of giants, of whom the Apostle says: *Where is the wise? where is the scribe? where is the disputer of this world*[^38]? it was not incongruous that the same be called physicians, as though promising by the art of their wisdom the salvation of souls: against whom it is said: *Salvation is the Lord's*[^39]. But if we take "giants" in the good sense, because of the Lord himself too it was said: *He exulted as a giant to run the way*[^40]; that so he is the giant of giants—namely of the great and most strong, who in his Church excel in spiritual strength; as he is the mountain of mountains, because of him it is written: *In the last times the mountain of the Lord will be manifest, prepared on the top of the mountains*[^41]; as also the holy of holies: it is not absurd that these same great and strong ones be called physicians too. Whence the apostle Paul says: *If by any means I may emulate my own flesh, that I may save some of them*[^42]. But even such physicians, although they do not cure from their own (for neither do physicians of the body cure from their own); yet however much by faithful ministry they aid salvation, they can cure the living, not raise the dead: of whom it is said: *Will you do wonders for the dead?* For too hidden is the grace of God, by which the minds of men in a manner come back to life, that they may be able to hear the precepts of health from any of his ministers. Which grace he commends in the Gospel, saying: *No one can come to me, unless the Father who sent me draw him*: and a little after, repeating this same thing more openly, he says: *The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life; but there are some of you who do not believe.* Then the evangelist interposes: *For Jesus knew from the beginning who were the believers, and who would betray him*: and following, and joining the words of the Lord himself, he says: *And he said, Therefore I said to you, that no one can come to me, unless it be given him by my Father*. Above he had said: *But there are some of you who do not believe;* and as though expounding the cause of this thing: *Therefore I said,* he says, *to you, that no one can come to me, unless it be given him by my Father*[^43]: that he might show that the very faith by which one believes, and the soul comes back to life from the death of its heart, is given us by God. However excellent, then, the preachers of the word, and the persuaders of truth even through miracles, like great physicians, may deal with men; if they are dead and have not come back to life by your grace: *Will you do wonders for the dead; or will physicians raise them up, and* those whom they raise up, *confess to you?* For this confession indicates the living: not as it is written elsewhere: *From the dead, as from one who is not, confession perishes*[^44].

**11.** [v. 12.] *Will anyone recount in the sepulcher your mercy, and your truth in perdition?* What was said above is supplied, so that this verse too is as if it were said: Will anyone recount your truth in perdition? For Scripture loves to join mercy and truth, especially in the Psalms. But as for what he says, *in perdition,* by another word he repeated what he had said above, *in the sepulcher.* And it was said, *in the sepulcher,* so that those are understood who are in the sepulcher, who above too were signified by the name of the dead, where he says: *Will you do wonders for the dead?* For the body is the sepulcher of the dead soul. Whence the Lord in the Gospel says to such: *You are like whitened sepulchers, which outwardly appear to men beautiful, but within are full of the bones of the dead and all filth: so you too outwardly indeed appear to men righteous; but within you are full of hypocrisy and iniquity*[^45].

**12.** [v. 13.] *Will your wonders be known in the darkness, and your righteousness in the land of forgetfulness?* What is, *in the darkness;* is, *in the land of forgetfulness.* For the unbelieving are signified by the name of darkness: hence the Apostle says: *For you were once darkness*[^46]. So too the land of forgetfulness is the man who has forgotten God: for the unbelieving soul can come to such great darkness, that the fool says in his heart: *There is no God*[^47]. This whole sense, then, is thus continued and woven together: *I cried to you, O Lord,* amid my sufferings; *all the day I stretched out my hands to you*: that is, I did not cease to stretch out my works to glorify you. Why, then, do the impious rage against me, except because you will not do wonders for the dead? that is, they are not moved by them to faith, nor will physicians raise them up, that they may confess to you, in whom your hidden grace does not work, by which they may be drawn to believe; because no one comes to me, except him whom you have drawn. For *who will recount in the sepulcher your mercy;* that is, the dead soul, whose death lies under the weight of the body? *and your truth in perdition;* that is, in such death, able to believe and feel none of these things? For *will* in the darkness of this death—that is, in the man who, by forgetting you, lost the light of life—*your wonders and righteousness be known?*

**13.** [v. 14.] But a question occurred, what is the use of these dead; what God does with them for the profit of the body of Christ, which is the Church: that in them may be demonstrated what the grace of God is in the predestined, who are called according to his purpose. Whence the body itself says in another psalm: *My God, his mercy will come before me. My God will show me among my enemies*[^48]. Therefore here too it follows and says: *And I to you, O Lord, have cried.* In which words the Lord Christ is now to be understood as speaking from the voice of his body, that is, the Church: for what is: *And I;* except because we too were once by nature children of wrath, as also the rest[^49]? But *to you I have cried,* that I might be saved. For who distinguishes me from the other children of wrath, when I hear the Apostle terribly rebuking the ungrateful, and saying: *For who distinguishes you? And what have you that you have not received? But if you have received, why do you glory as though you had not received*[^50]? *Salvation is the Lord's*[^51]: but the giant in the multitude of his strength shall not be saved[^52]; but, as it is written: *Whoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved. But how shall they call upon him in whom they have not believed? or how shall they believe him whom they have not heard? But how shall they hear without a preacher? or how shall they preach, unless they be sent? as it is written: How beautiful are the feet of those who announce peace, who announce good things*[^53]! They themselves are the physicians curing the one wounded by robbers; but the Lord brought him to the inn[^54]: because they themselves too are working in the Lord's field; nor yet is he who plants anything, or he who waters, but God who gives the increase[^55]. Therefore I too cried to the Lord; that is, I called upon the Lord that I might be saved. But how should I call upon him, unless I believed? how should I believe, unless I heard? But that having heard I might believe, he himself drew me; because in secret from the death of the heart not any physicians, but he himself roused me. For many heard; because into all the earth their sound went out, and to the ends of the world their words[^56]: but the faith is not of all[^57]; and the Lord knows who are his[^58]. And through this, I could not even have believed, unless the mercy of God had come before me; and because he raises the dead, and calls the things that are not as though they were[^59], by calling me in secret, and rousing, and drawing, he had led me out from the darkness, and brought me to the light of faith. Therefore it follows: *And in the morning my prayer will come before you.* Now in the morning, after the night of unbelief and the darkness have passed. That this morning might be mine, your mercy indeed came before me: but because that glorification remains, where the hidden things of darkness will be enlightened, and the thoughts of the heart will be manifested, and praise will be to each from you[^60]; now in this life, in this pilgrimage, in this light of faith, which in comparison with the darkness of the unbelieving is already day, but in comparison with the day where we shall see face to face is still night, still *my prayer will come before you.*

**14.** [v. 15.] But that this prayer may grow fervent and be exercised, which how much it profits us can be explained, I think, by no words; the good that is to be given in eternity is deferred, and the evils that are to pass increase. Therefore it follows: *Why, O Lord, do you repel my prayer?* This was said too in those words: *O God, my God, look upon me; why have you forsaken me*[^61]? The cause to be known is set forth, not as though the blamed wisdom of God did this without cause: so too here: *Why, O Lord, do you repel my prayer?* Yet if the cause of this thing be diligently attended to, it was already indicated above: for to this end the prayer of the saints is as it were repelled, by the deferment of so great a benefit, and by the adversity of tribulations, that, like fire driven back by a blast, it may be more ardently inflamed.

**15.** [vv. 16-19.] And so he follows out briefly the tribulations of the body of Christ too. For neither were they done in the head alone, since it was said even to Saul: *Why do you persecute me*[^62]? and Paul himself, now set as a chosen member in the same body, says: *That I may fill up what is lacking of the pressures of Christ in my flesh*[^63]. *Why,* then, *O Lord, do you repel my prayer, and turn your face from me? I am needy and in labors from my youth; but being exalted I have been humbled and confounded. Your angers have passed over me, and your terrors have disturbed me. They have surrounded me like water all the day, they have surrounded me together. You have made far from me a friend, and my acquaintances from misery.* All these things have befallen and befall in the members of the body of Christ. And God turns his face from those praying, by not hearing them as to what they wish, when they do not know that what they ask is not expedient for them. And the Church is needy, when, a pilgrim, it hungers and thirsts, whence it may be satisfied in the homeland. And it is in labors from its youth: for the body of Christ itself says in another psalm too: *Often have they fought against me from my youth*[^64]. And to this end certain members of it are exalted even in this world, that in them there may be greater humility. And over the body itself—that is, the unity of the saints and faithful, whose head is Christ—the angers of God pass over; but they do not abide: because not of the believer, but of the unbeliever it was said: *The anger of God abides upon him*[^65]. And the terrors of God disturb the weakness of the faithful; because everything that can befall, even if it does not befall, is prudently feared. And sometimes the very terrors so disturb the soul of one thinking of the evils impending all around, that they seem to flow around like water, and to surround the fearful one together. And because to the Church, a pilgrim in this world, these things are not lacking, while now in these, now in those of its members they befall everywhere; therefore he said, *all the day,* signifying the continuance of time, until this world is ended. And often friends and acquaintances, endangered according to the world, desert the saints from fear: of whom the Apostle says: *All have left me; let it not be charged against them*[^66]. But why all these things, except that the prayer of this holy body in the morning—that is, after the night of unbelief, in the light of faith—may come before God, until that salvation come, in whose reality not yet, but already in hope we have been saved, and we await it faithfully with patience[^67]? Where the Lord will not repel our prayer, since then nothing will be to be asked, but whatever was rightly asked will be obtained: nor will he turn his face from us, since we shall see him as he is[^68]: nor shall we be needy, because our plenty will be God himself all in all[^69]: nor shall we labor, because no weakness will remain: nor, being exalted, shall we be humbled and disturbed, because there will be no adversity there: nor shall we endure even the passing anger of God, because we shall abide in his abiding kindness: nor will his terrors disturb us, because the promises rendered will make us blessed: nor terrified will the acquaintance and friend become far from us, where there will be no enemy to be feared.
