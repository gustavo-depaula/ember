# On the Same Psalm 118 (14)

**1.** [vv. 49-50.] Let us consider how much the Lord grants, and let us treat these verses of this great psalm: *Remember your word to your servant, in which you have given me hope. This has consoled me in my humiliation, for your word has given me life.* Does forgetfulness, as in a man, befall God? Why then is it said to him, *Remember?* Although in other places of holy Scripture the very word is set down, as in *Why have you forgotten me*[^1]; and *You forget our want*[^2]; and God himself through the Prophet says, *All his iniquities I will forget*[^3]; and this is read again and again in many places. But these things are not understood in God as they happen to men. For just as God is said to repent when, beyond men's hope, he changes a thing, though his counsel is not changed—since the counsel of the Lord stands for ever[^4]—so he is said to forget when he seems to delay a help either promised, or not to repay sinners their due, or anything of this kind, as though what is hoped for or feared had slipped from his memory, and does not come about. These things are said by that moral manner of speech by which human feeling is moved; although God does these things by a fixed disposition, with no failing of memory, nor darkened understanding, nor changed will. So when it is said to him, *Remember,* the desire of the one praying, since he begs a promise, is shown and stretched out; God is not, as though it had fallen from his mind, reminded. *Remember,* he says, *your word to your servant;* that is, fulfill your promise to your servant. *In which you have given me hope;* that is, in which word, because you promised, you made me hope.

**2.** *This has consoled me in my humiliation.* This, namely, the hope that has been given to the humble, as Scripture says: *God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble*[^5]. Hence with his own mouth the Lord himself also says, *For whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted*[^6]. We rightly understand here that humility too which is not the one by which a person humbles himself by confessing sins, nor by not arrogating righteousness to himself, but the one by which a person is humbled by some tribulation or abasement which his pride has deserved, or by which his patience is exercised and proved; whence a little later this psalm says, *Before I was humbled, I went astray*[^7]. And there is that passage in the book of Wisdom: *In sorrow endure, and in your humiliation have patience; for gold and silver are tried in the fire, but acceptable men in the furnace of humiliation*[^8]. In saying *acceptable,* there he gave the hope that would console in humiliation. And the Lord Jesus, when he foretold to his disciples that this humiliation would come from persecutors, did not leave them without hope, but gave them this too, by which they might be consoled, saying, *In your patience you will possess your souls*[^9]; and concerning the body itself, which could be killed by enemies and as it were utterly perish, *A hair of your head,* he says, *will not perish*[^10]. This hope was given to the body of Christ, which is the Church, that it might be consoled in its humiliation. On account of this hope the apostle Paul too says, *But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^11]. But that is the hope of eternal rewards; there is also another hope which consoles greatly in the humiliation of tribulation, which has been given to the saints in the word of God promising the help of grace, that no one may fail. Of this hope the Apostle says, *God is faithful, who will not let you be tempted beyond what you are able; but with the temptation will also make a way out, that you may be able to bear it*[^12]. This hope the Savior also gave with his own mouth when he said, *This night Satan has asked to harass you like wheat; and I have prayed for you, Peter, that your faith may not fail*[^13]. This hope he gave also in the prayer he taught, where he admonished us to say, *Lead us not into temptation*[^14]; for in a way he promised that he would give to his own in peril what he willed to be asked by those who pray. And surely this psalm is rather to be understood as speaking of this hope: *This has consoled me in my humiliation, for your word has given me life.* Which they have expressed more plainly who put not *word* but *utterance;* for the Greek has what is *utterance,* not what is *word.*

**3.** [v. 51.] There follows: *The proud acted unjustly even greatly; but from your law I have not turned aside.* By the proud he wished the persecutors of the godly to be understood; and therefore he added, *but from your law I have not turned aside,* because their persecution was compelling him to do this. He says they acted unjustly *even greatly,* because they were not only impious, but were even compelling the godly to be impious. In this humiliation, that is, in this tribulation, hope consoled, the hope which has been given in the word of God promising help, that the faith of the martyrs may not fail; and the presence of his Spirit imparting strength to those who labor, so that, escaping from the snare of the hunters, they might say, *If the Lord had not been among us, perhaps they would have swallowed us up alive*[^15].

**4.** Or perhaps when he says, *This has consoled me in my humiliation,* he speaks of that humiliation by which man was cast down and thrown into death, out of that sin which was so unhappily committed in the happiness of paradise? For in this humiliation, in which man has been made like vanity and his days pass like a shadow[^16], all are sons of wrath, unless they are reconciled to God through the Mediator—those who were predestined to eternal salvation before the foundation of the world. In this Mediator the righteous of old also had hope, when by the spirit of prophecy they foresaw that he would come in the flesh. Therefore the word that came to them about him is also rightly understood here as the word, if we take this too as their voice; the word of which it is said: *Remember your word to your servant, in which you have given me hope. This has consoled me in my humiliation;* that is, in this mortality of mine; *for your utterance has given me life;* so that I might have hope of life, though thrown into death. *The proud acted unjustly even greatly;* since not even the humiliation of mortality tamed their pride. *But from your law I have not turned aside;* which the proud were compelling me to do.

**5.** [v. 52.] *I have remembered your judgments from of old, O Lord, and was consoled;* or, as other manuscripts have it, *and was exhorted;* that is, I received exhortation. For either could be rendered from the Greek word. *From of old,* then, from when the human race took its beginning, *I have remembered your judgments* upon the vessels of wrath, which have been made ready for destruction; *and was consoled,* because through these too you showed the riches of your glory upon the vessels of your mercy[^17].

**6.** [vv. 53-54.] *Weariness took hold of me because of sinners who forsake your law. Your statutes were songs to me in the place of my sojourning;* or, as other manuscripts have it, *in the place of my pilgrimage.* This is that humiliation in the place of mortality of man, who is on pilgrimage away from paradise and from that heavenly Jerusalem, from which a certain man going down to Jericho fell among robbers[^18]; but on account of the mercy that was shown to him through that Samaritan, the statutes of God were songs to him in the place of his pilgrimage; although weariness held him because of the sinners who forsake the law of God, since in this life he is compelled to associate with them even for a time, until the threshing floor is winnowed. Now these two verses can be fitted to the single parts of the one above: so that what he says, *I have remembered your judgments from of old, O Lord,* may be referred to this, *Weariness took hold of me because of sinners who forsake your law;* but what he there says, *And was consoled,* may be referred to this, *Your statutes were songs to me in the place of my pilgrimage.*

**7.** [v. 55.] *I have remembered,* he says, *your name in the night, O Lord, and I have kept your law.* That humiliation, where the misery of mortality is, is night; it is night among the proud who act unjustly even greatly; night in the weariness because of sinners who forsake the law of God; it is night, finally, in this place of pilgrimage, until the Lord comes and lights up the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then there will be praise for each one from God[^19]. In this night, then, a man ought to be mindful of the name of God, so that he who glories may glory in the Lord; for which reason that too is written, *Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory*[^20]. For thus each one keeps the law of God not in his own glory but in God's glory—because also not in his own righteousness but in God's, that is, given to him by God—as this man says: *I have remembered your name in the night, O Lord, and I have kept your law.* Which he would not have kept, had he, trusting in his own strength, not been mindful of the name of God; for *our help is in the name of the Lord*[^21].

**8.** [v. 56.] On account of which he went on and added: *This was made for me, for I sought out your righteous acts. Your righteous acts,* surely, by which you justify the ungodly; not mine, which never make me godly, but proud. For this man was not one of those who, *ignorant of the righteousness of God, and wishing to establish their own, did not submit to the righteousness of God*[^22]. These righteous acts, then, by which those who cannot be righteous through themselves are justified freely by the grace of God, others have better rendered *statutes;* for in truth it is not *righteous acts* that the Greek has, but what are *statutes.* But what does he mean by *This was made for me? This* what? Perhaps the law, because he had said, *And I kept your law;* to which words he joined, *This was made for me,* as if he said, "This law was made for me"? But we must not linger in expounding how the law of God was made for him. For the Greek expression from which this was translated sufficiently indicates that it was not said of the law, *This was made for me;* because the word "law" in that tongue is masculine, and yet there too it is written with a feminine pronoun, *This was made for me.* We must ask, therefore, first what was made for him, then how it was made for him, whatever it may be. *This,* he says, *was made for me;* not, surely, "This law," because, as I said, the Greek excludes this sense. Perhaps, then, "This night," because the whole sentence above runs thus: *I have remembered your name in the night, O Lord, and I have kept your law;* and there follows, *This was made for me;* so since it is not the law, surely it is the night that was made for him. What, then, is "Night was made for me, *because I sought out your statutes?*" For light rather than night was made for him, since he sought out the statutes of God. And so *was made for me* is rightly understood as if it were said, was made on my behalf, that is, it came about that it benefited me. For if that humiliation of mortality is, not absurdly, understood as night, where the hearts of mortals are hidden from one another, so that out of such darkness innumerable and grievous temptations arise, so that in that same night even the beasts of the forest pass through, the young lions roaring, seeking their food from God[^23]—whence too about that lion, roaring and seeking whom he may devour[^24], the Lord says what I have already mentioned above, *This night Satan has asked to harass you like wheat*[^25]; that is, in this night, in which the beasts of the forest pass through, that great lion sought you from God as his food—surely this very humiliation in this place of pilgrimage, which is rightly understood as night, benefits those who are exercised in it for their health, that they may learn not to be proud, on account of which evil man was driven into this night. For the beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God[^26]. But, justified freely, and set against the various temptations of this night that he may advance in this humiliation, let him now say, understanding, what is said a little later in this psalm: *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your statutes*[^27]. For what else is *It is good for me that you have humbled me,* except: this humiliation which is called night *was made for me,* that is, it came about that it benefited me? But why this? *Because, namely, I sought out your statutes,* not mine.

**9.** We can also understand what was said, *This was made for me,* in such a way that neither "law" nor "night" is supplied, but this pronoun *This* is taken just as that passage in another psalm where it reads, *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this I will seek*[^28]. He does not say what one thing, or which one thing, of which he said, *This I will seek;* but the feminine gender is placed as it were for the neuter. For it was said in an unusual way, *One thing I have asked, this I will seek,* where it is not supplied what that one thing is; which would more usually be said: "One thing I have asked of the Lord, this I will seek, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord." For in these neuter expressions a neuter to be supplied is not usually required, as, say, "one good," or "one gift," or anything of this kind; but whatever it is, even if it is named in the masculine or feminine gender, or is conveyed by any words without any noun of any gender, it is, by an altogether customary manner of speech, understood under the neuter gender. So here too it could be said in this way, *This was made for me,* as if it had been said, "This was made for me." But if we ask what that is, there occurs what he had said above, *I have remembered your name in the night, O Lord, and I have kept your law.* This was made for me; that is, this, that I kept your law, I did not do through myself, but it was made for me, surely by you; *because I sought out your statutes,* not mine. *For it is God,* the Apostle says, *who works in you both to will and to work, for his good pleasure*[^29]. This too God says through the prophet: *And I will make you walk in my statutes, and keep my judgments, and do them*[^30]. Therefore, since God says, *I will make you keep my judgments, and do them,* this man most rightly says, "This was made for me;" so that when you ask what that is, he may answer what he had said above, namely, that I kept the law of God. But because this sermon has gone on at length, what follows will, by the Lord's gift, be better treated from another beginning.
