# On the Same Psalm 118 (7)

**1.** [v. 19.] A sermon must be rendered to the expectation of your Charity about the following parts of this greatest psalm, namely from that verse where he says: *I am a sojourner on earth; do not hide from me your commandments*: or, as some manuscripts have: *I am a stranger on earth.* For what in the Greek is paroikos, some of ours translated *sojourner* (inquilinus), some *dweller* (incola), sometimes even *newcomer* (advena). Sojourners, not having their own house, dwell in another's; but dwellers or newcomers are certainly held to be adventitious. Where a great question about the soul arises. For it does not seem it could be said according to the body: *I am a dweller,* or *newcomer,* or *sojourner on earth;* since the body draws its origin from the earth. But in this most profound question I dare define nothing. For whether because of the soul it could rightly be said: *I am a sojourner,* or *dweller,* or *newcomer on earth;* or according to the whole man, because he was once a citizen of paradise, where surely he was not who said these things; or, what is freest from all controversy, not every man can say this, but he to whom an eternal homeland in the heavens is promised: this I know, that human life on earth is a temptation; and a heavy yoke is upon the sons of Adam. But it pleases me more to discuss according to that opinion, that we say we are sojourners or dwellers on earth for this reason, because we have found the supernal homeland, whence we received a pledge, and to which arriving we never migrate from it. For even he who says in another psalm: *I am a sojourner with you and a pilgrim, like all my fathers*[^1]; does not say: Like all men; but, by saying, *like all my fathers,* he wishes without doubt the just to be understood, who preceded him in time, and in this pilgrimage with pious groaning sighed for the supernal homeland. Of whom it is written to the Hebrews: *According to faith these all died, not having received the promises, but seeing them from afar and saluting them, and confessing that they are guests and pilgrims on the earth. For those who say such things show that they seek a homeland. And if indeed they had been mindful of that from which they departed, they would have had time to return; but now they desire a better, that is, a heavenly one: therefore God is not confounded to be called their God; for he prepared for them a city*[^2]. And that which we read: *As long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims from the Lord*[^3]; can be understood that this is not of all, but of the faithful: *For faith is not of all*[^4]. And we see what the Apostle joins to these words. For when he had said: *As long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims from the Lord: For by faith,* he says, *we walk, not by sight*[^5]; that we might understand this pilgrimage to be of those who walk by faith. But the unbelievers, whom God did not foreknow nor predestine to be conformed to the image of his Son, cannot truly say that they are pilgrims on earth, when they are there where they were born according to the flesh: for they do not have a city elsewhere; and through this they are not strangers on earth, but earthborn. Whence another Scripture says of a certain one: *For he set his house at death, and at hell his axles with the earthborn*[^6]. But they too are pilgrims and sojourners, not to this earth, but to the people of God, from which they are strangers. Whence to the believers, and to those beginning to have the holy city, which is not of this world, the Apostle says: *Therefore now you are no longer pilgrims and sojourners; but you are fellow citizens of the saints and members of the household of God*[^7]. They themselves, then, are earthly citizens, who are pilgrims to the people of God: but those who are citizens in the people of God are themselves pilgrims on earth; because the whole same people, as long as it is in the body, is a pilgrim from the Lord. Let him say, then: *I am a dweller on earth; do not hide from me your commandments.*

**2.** But who at last are those from whom God hides his commandments? Did not God wish them to be preached everywhere? Would that they were clear to as many as they are clear to. For what is clearer than: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart, and with your whole soul, and with your whole mind;* and: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself? On these two commandments the whole law hangs, and the Prophets*[^8]. And who is there from whom these commandments lie hidden? Surely they are known both to all the faithful, and to very many of the unbelieving. Why, then, does a believer ask that what he sees is not hidden even from an unbeliever not be hidden from himself? Or is it that, because it is difficult to know God, it is consequent that: *You shall love the Lord your God,* is difficult to understand, lest one thing be loved for another? For the knowledge of the neighbor seems easier. For every man is a neighbor to every man, nor is any remoteness of race to be thought of, where there is a common nature. Although neither did he know his neighbor who said to the Lord: *And who is my neighbor?*[^9] When there was set before him a certain man, who, going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, fell among robbers: to whom none was a neighbor, except him who did mercy with him, he himself who had asked judged; and it became plain that in doing mercy no one is to be reckoned a stranger by him who loves his neighbor. But many do not even know themselves; because to know oneself too, in the way a man ought to become known to himself, is not of all men. How, then, does he love his neighbor as himself, who does not know even himself? For not in vain did that younger son, who, having set out into a far country, where he scattered his substance living prodigally, so that he might say: *I will arise, and go to my father*[^10], first return to himself, except because he was a pilgrim so far that he left even himself. Nor yet would he return to himself, if he in every way did not know himself; nor would he say: *I will arise, and go to my father,* if he were utterly ignorant of God. Wherefore both these things are in some measure known, and, that they may be more and more known, they are not undeservedly asked to be known. Wherefore, that we may know how to love God, God must be known; and that a man may know how to love his neighbor as himself, he first ought, by loving God, to love himself: whence will he be able to do this, if he does not know God, if he does not know himself too? Rightly, then, it is said to God: *I am a dweller on earth; do not hide from me your commandments.* For deservedly they are hidden from those who are not dwellers on earth: for these commandments, even if they hear them, they do not savor; because they savor earthly things. But those whose citizenship is in heaven, insofar as they here dwell, surely are pilgrims. Let them ask, then, that the commandments of God not be hidden from them, through which they may be freed from this sojourning, by loving God, with whom they will be forever; and by loving the neighbor, that he may be there where they too will be.

**3.** [v. 20.] But what is loved by loving, if love itself is not loved? Whence consequently this dweller on earth, when he had prayed that the commandments of God not be hidden from him, in which love is commanded either alone or chiefly; proclaims that he wishes to have the love of love itself, saying: *My soul has coveted to desire your justifications in all time.* This coveting is praiseworthy, not damnable. It is not of this that it was said: *You shall not covet*[^11]; but of that by which the flesh covets against the spirit. But of this good coveting by which the spirit covets against the flesh, seek where it is written; and you will find: *The coveting of wisdom, then, leads to the kingdom*[^12]: and many other testimonies of good coveting are found. But there is this difference, that what is coveted is not kept silent, when a good coveting is commemorated: but when it is not added what is coveted, but it is set alone, only a bad one is understood. As in this which I commemorated: *The coveting of wisdom, then, leads to the kingdom;* if he did not add "of wisdom," he would in no way say: Coveting leads to the kingdom. But what the Apostle set down: *I did not know coveting, unless the Law said: You shall not covet*[^13]; he certainly did not add the coveting of what thing, or what you shall not covet: for it is certain that, when it is so said, nothing but bad coveting is understood. What, then, did the soul of this one covet? *To desire,* he says, *your justifications in all time.* I believe it did not yet desire them, when it coveted to desire. But justifications are just deeds, that is, the works of righteousness. Since, then, even he who already desires does not yet have, how far from them was he who still coveted to desire them? and how much farther from them are those who do not even yet covet this?

**4.** But it is a wonder how desire is coveted, and that there is not in us that whose coveting is already in us. For it is not some beautiful body, like gold, or some shapely flesh, which a man can covet, and not have, because, set outside, it is not in the man. Who does not know that coveting is in the man, that desire is in the man? Why, then, is it coveted to be had, as if it were brought in from outside? Or how can the coveting of it be had without itself, since it itself is nothing else than coveting? For to desire also is without doubt to covet. What is this wonderful and inexplicable languor? And yet it is. For a sick man too, who labors with distaste, and wishes to escape this evil, certainly covets to desire food, while he covets not to have distaste: but this distaste is a disease of the body. But the coveting by which he covets to desire food, that is, to lack distaste, is in the mind, not in the body: and it has it not the delight of the throat and jaws, which is restricted by distaste; but the reason of recovering health, by which is provided for the distaste of food to be driven away. And therefore it is no wonder if the mind seeks that the body may seek, when the mind seeks and the body does not seek. But when both are of the mind, and both are coveting, why do I covet the desire of the justifications of God? How, in one and the same mind of mine, do I have the coveting of this desire, and do not have the desire itself? or how are these two, and not one? For why do I covet to desire the justifications, and not rather the justifications themselves than their desire? or by what manner can I covet the desire of the justifications, and not covet the justifications themselves; since for this reason I covet their desire, because I wish to have them? But if it is so, I now certainly covet them themselves. What need is there, then, that I covet their desire, since I already have it, and feel that I have it? For I could not covet the desire of righteousness, except by coveting righteousness. Or is this what I said above, that even the love itself by which is loved what ought to be loved is to be loved; just as the love by which is loved what ought not to be loved is to be hated? For we hate our coveting by which the flesh covets against the spirit: and what is this coveting, except a bad love? And we love our coveting by which the spirit covets against the flesh: and what is this coveting, except a good love? But when it is said: It is to be loved, what else is said than, It is to be coveted? Wherefore, since the justifications of God are rightly coveted, the coveting of the justifications of God is rightly coveted. For this can be said in another way thus: if the justifications of God are rightly loved, the love of the justifications of God is rightly loved. Or is it one thing to covet, another to desire? Not that desire is not coveting, but because not all coveting is desire. For both the things which are had, and the things which are not had, are coveted: for by coveting, a man enjoys the things which he has; but by desiring, he covets the absent. What, then, is desire, except the coveting of absent things? But how can the justifications of God be absent, except when they are not known? Or, when they are known and not done, are they to be held absent? For what are justifications, except just works, not words? And through this they can, by the weakness of the soul, not be desired; and by the reason of the mind, where it is seen how useful and wholesome they are, their desire can be coveted. For often we see what must be done, and do not do it; because it does not delight us to do it, and we wish that it might delight us. The understanding flies ahead; and slowly follows, and sometimes does not follow, the human and weak affect. Therefore he coveted to desire the things which he saw to be good, wishing to have the delight of those things whose reason he was able to see.

**5.** But he does not say, it covets; but: *My soul has coveted to desire your justifications.* For perhaps this dweller on earth was such as had already come to that which he had coveted, and now desired those things whose desire he commemorates that he once coveted. But if he already desired, why did he not have them? For nothing impedes that the justifications of God be had, except that they are not desired, while love does not burn for those things whose love shines. Or did he have them, and do them? For a little after he says: *But your servant was exercised in your justifications*[^14]. But by what as it were steps one arrives at them, he shows. For first it is that it be seen how useful and honorable they are; then, that their desire be coveted; finally, that, with light and health advancing, even the doing of them delight, whose reason alone used to delight. But the things that follow, since this one is now lengthy, will be more conveniently discussed in another sermon, with the Lord helping.
