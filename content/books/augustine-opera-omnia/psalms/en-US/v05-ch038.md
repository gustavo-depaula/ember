# On the Same Psalm 118 (13)

**1.** [vv. 45-48.] The earlier verses of this long psalm contain prayer; but those that follow, which we must now discuss, contain narration. For above, the man of God was asking for the help of God's grace when he said, *In your righteousness give me life; and let your mercy come upon me, O Lord*[^1], and other things like it, whether before or after. But now he says, *And I walked in a broad place, for I sought out your commandments. And I spoke of your testimonies in the sight of kings, and was not put to shame. And I meditated on your commandments, which I loved. And I lifted up my hands to your commandments, which I loved; and I exercised myself in your statutes*[^2]. Here the words are those of one narrating, not of one asking; as though, having obtained what he had asked, he confesses in the praises of God what the mercy of the Lord, which he had begged to come upon him, had made him. For he did not join these to the preceding in such a way as to say: "And take not the word of truth utterly out of my mouth, for in your judgments I have hoped beyond hope; and I shall keep your law always, for ever and ever; and I shall walk in a broad place, for I sought out your commandments; and I shall speak of your testimonies in the sight of kings, and shall not be put to shame," and so on in that manner—for thus he would seem bound to connect what follows to what precedes; but he says, *And I walked in a broad place.* Here the connective conjunction, that is, *And,* is placed as though inconsistently; for he did not say "And I shall walk," as he had said, *And I shall keep your law always.* Or certainly, if it was said in the optative mood, *May I keep your law,* he does not say "And may I walk in a broad place," as though he had wished and asked for both; but he says, *And I walked in a broad place.* Here, if this conjunction were not present, but the sentence were brought in free and loosed from the connection with what precedes—*I walked in a broad place*—nothing in the unusual manner of speaking ought to disturb the reader here, so as to make him suppose that some hidden sense must be sought. Surely, then, what he did not say he wished to be understood, namely, that he had been heard; and then he added what he had been made: as if he said, When I prayed these things, you heard me; *and I walked in a broad place,* and the rest that he wove together as said in that manner.

**2.** What, then, is *And I walked in a broad place,* except: I walked in love, which has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us[^3]? In this broad place he walked who said, *Our mouth is open to you, O Corinthians; our heart is enlarged*[^4]. And this love is contained, whole and entire, in those two commandments, namely the love of God and the love of neighbor, on which the whole Law depends, and the Prophets[^5]. Hence here too, when he had said, *And I walked in a broad place,* he added the cause and said, *For I sought out your commandments.* But some manuscripts do not have *commandments* but *testimonies;* yet we find *commandments* in more, and especially in the Greek; and who would doubt that the Greek—as the prior tongue, from which these things were translated to us—is the more to be trusted? If, then, we wish to know how he sought these commandments, or how they are to be sought, let us consider what the good master, and teacher, and giver says: *Ask, and you will receive; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you.* And a little later: *If you then,* he says, *who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him*[^6]? Here he plainly shows that what he had said—*Ask, seek, knock*—pertains to nothing but the insistence of begging, that is, of praying. Another evangelist, moreover, does not say, *he will give good things to those who ask him,* which can be understood in many ways, whether bodily or spiritual things; but he cut away other things and expressed quite carefully what the Lord willed us to beg vehemently and insistently, and says, *How much more will your Father from heaven give the good Spirit to those who ask him*[^7]? This is that Spirit through whom love is poured out in our hearts, so that by loving God and neighbor we may do the divine commandments. This is that Spirit in whom we cry, *Abba, Father*[^8]. And through him he makes us ask, whom we desire to receive; he makes us seek, whom we long to find; he makes us knock, to whom we strive to come. This the Apostle teaches, who, though he says that we cry in the Holy Spirit, *Abba, Father,* again in another place says, *God has given the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, Abba, Father*[^9]. How do we cry, if he cries in us, except that he has made us cry, since he has begun to dwell in us? This too, then, he does when received, that he may be sought, to be received more abundantly, by asking, seeking, knocking. For whether it be that a good life be asked for, or that one may live well, as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are the sons of God[^10]. Therefore: *I walked,* he says, *in a broad place, for I sought out your commandments.* He had sought and found, because he had asked and received the good Spirit, by which, made good, he might do good works well, out of faith that works through love.

**3.** *And I spoke,* he says, *of your testimonies in the sight of kings, and was not put to shame;* like one who had asked and received that he might answer those who reproached him with a word, and that the word of truth might not be taken out of his mouth. And so, striving for it even to death, he was not put to shame to speak it even in the sight of kings. For the testimonies of which he says that he spoke are in Greek called *martyria,* a word we now use in place of the Latin. Hence too comes the name of the Martyrs, of whom Jesus foretold that they would confess him even before kings.

**4.** *And I meditated,* he says, *on your commandments, which I loved, and I lifted up my hands to your commandments, which I loved;* or, as some manuscripts have in both verses, *which I loved greatly,* or *exceedingly,* or *vehemently,* as it pleased some to render what is said in Greek. He loved God's commandments, then, through that by which he walked in a broad place; namely, through the Holy Spirit, through whom that very love is poured out and enlarges the hearts of the faithful. And he loved both by thinking and by working. For what pertains to thought, he says, *And I meditated on your commandments;* but what pertains to working, *And I lifted up my hands to your commandments.* And to both clauses he added, *which I loved;* for the end of the commandment is love from a pure heart[^11]. When God's commandment is done with this end, that is, with the contemplation of this thing, then a good work is truly done; and then the hands are lifted up, because that to which they are lifted is on high. Therefore the Apostle, about to speak of love itself, says, *I show you a more excellent way*[^12]; and in another place, *To know also the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge*[^13]. For if from the work of God's commandments a reward of earthly happiness is sought, the hands are put down rather than lifted up; because earthly profits, which are not above but below, are sought by that work. And to both pertains what follows: *And I exercised myself in your statutes;* which more interpreters preferred to say rather than *I rejoiced* or *I prattled,* which some rendered from what the Greek has. For he is exercised in God's statutes, joyful and in a way talkative, who keeps the commandments he loves with the delight both of thinking and of working.
