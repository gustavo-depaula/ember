# Exposition of Psalm 6

**1.** [v 1.] *Concerning the eighth*: this seems obscure here; for the rest in this title are plainer. Now it has seemed to some to signify the day of judgment, that is, the time of the coming of our Lord, when he is to come to judge the living and the dead. This coming, with the years reckoned from Adam, is believed to be in the future after seven thousand years; so that seven thousand years pass as though seven days, and then that time comes as though an eighth day. But since it was said by the Lord: *It is not for you to know the times which the Father has placed in his own power*[^1], and: *But of that day and that hour no one knows, neither the angel, nor the power, nor the Son, but the Father alone*[^2], and what is written, that the day of the Lord comes as a thief, shows plainly enough that no one ought to claim for himself the knowledge of that time by any reckoning of years. For if that day is to come after seven thousand years, every man can learn of his coming by reckoning the years: where then will it be, that not even the Son knows this? This was certainly said because men do not learn this through the Son, not that he himself does not know it within himself, according to that expression: *The Lord your God tests you, that he may know*[^3], that is, that he may make you know; and: *Rise up, Lord*, that is, make us rise up. Since, then, the Son is said not to know this day, not because he does not know it, but because he makes those not know it for whom it is not expedient to know it, that is, does not show it to them; what does that presumption, I know not what, mean for itself, which, with the years reckoned, hopes for the day of the Lord most certainly after seven thousand years?

**2.** Let us, then, gladly be ignorant of what the Lord wished us to be ignorant, and let us ask what this title means, which is written *Concerning the eighth.* The day of judgment can indeed be understood as the eighth, even without any rash reckoning of years, because after the end of this world, eternal life having been received, then the souls of the righteous will not be subject to times: and since all times roll on by the repetition of these seven days, perhaps that one is called the eighth which will not have this variation. There is another thing that can not absurdly be taken here, why the judgment is called the eighth: that it is to be after two generations, one pertaining to the body, the other to the soul. For from Adam to Moses the human race lived from the body, that is, according to the flesh: which is also called the outer and old man, and to which the Old Testament was given, that, by works that were religious indeed yet still carnal, it might prefigure the spiritual things to come. During this whole time in which life was lived according to the body, *death reigned*, as the Apostle says, *even over those who did not sin.* But it reigned *after the likeness of the transgression of Adam*, as the same says; since *until Moses* is to be understood as the time during which the works of the Law, that is, those sacraments observed carnally, held even those bound, for the sake of a certain mystery, who were subject to the one God. But from the coming of the Lord, from the time when there was a passage from circumcision of the flesh to circumcision of the heart, a calling was made, that life should be lived according to the soul, that is, according to the inner man, who is also called the new man on account of regeneration and the renewal of spiritual ways. Now it is manifest that the number four pertains to the body, on account of the well-known four elements of which it consists, and the four qualities, dry, moist, hot, cold. Whence it is also administered in four seasons, spring, summer, autumn, winter. These are well known. For the number four of the body is treated also elsewhere more subtly, but more obscurely; which is to be avoided in this discourse, which we wish to be suited even to the less learned. But the number three can be understood to pertain to the soul, from the fact that we are commanded to love God in a threefold way, with the whole heart, with the whole soul, with the whole mind: of each of which there is to be a disputation not in the Psalter, but in the Gospel. For now, as a testimony of the number three pertaining to the soul, I think what has been said is enough. The numbers of the body pertaining to the old man and to the Old Testament being completed, the numbers of the soul referred to the new man and to the New Testament being also completed, then, just as when the sevenfold number has passed (because each thing is done in time), with the number four allotted to the body and the number three to the soul, there will come the eighth day of judgment, which, rendering to merits what is owed, will now transfer the saints not to temporal works, but to eternal life, and will condemn the impious forever.

**3.** [v 2.] Dreading this condemnation, the Church prays in this psalm, saying: *Lord, do not rebuke me in your anger.* The Apostle too speaks of the anger of judgment: *You are storing up for yourself*, he says, *wrath on the day of wrath of the just judgment of God*[^4]; in which whoever desires to be healed in this life does not wish to be rebuked. *Nor chastise me in your fury*: *chastise* seems milder; for it tends toward amendment. For he who is rebuked, that is, accused, is to be feared lest he have condemnation as his end. But since fury seems to be more than anger, it can raise the question why what is milder, that is, chastisement, is placed with what is harsher, that is, with fury. But I think one thing is signified by two words: for in Greek *thymos*, which is in the first verse, signifies what *orgē* does, which is in the second verse; but since the Latins wished themselves too to put two words, it was asked what was close to anger, and fury was put. Hence the copies vary: for in some, anger is found first, then fury; in others, fury first, then anger; in others, in place of fury, indignation is put, or bile. But whatever it is, it is a movement of the soul provoking to the inflicting of punishment: which movement, however, is not to be attributed to God as to a soul, of whom it was said: *But you, Lord of powers, judge with tranquillity*[^5]; and what is tranquil is not disturbed. Therefore disturbance does not befall God the judge; but what happens in his ministers, because it happens through his laws, is called his anger. In which anger the soul that now prays wishes not only not to be rebuked, but not even to be chastised, that is, amended or instructed: for in Greek *paideusēs* is put, that is, instruct. Now all are rebuked on the day of judgment who do not have the foundation that is Christ: but they are amended, that is, purified, who build upon this foundation wood, hay, stubble; for they will suffer loss, but will be saved as through fire. What, then, does this one pray, who wishes neither to be rebuked nor amended in the anger of the Lord? What, but to be healed? For where there is health, neither is death to be feared, nor the hand of the physician who burns or cuts.

**4.** [vv 3.4.] And so it follows, and she says: *Have mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak: heal me, Lord, for my bones are troubled*, that is, the firmness of my soul, or its strength; for this is what bones signify. The soul, then, says that its strength is troubled, when it names bones: for it is not to be believed that it has bones such as we see in the body. For which reason what is added pertains to the explanation: *And my soul is troubled exceedingly*; lest what she called bones should be understood of the body. *And you, Lord, how long?* who would not understand that there is signified a soul wrestling with its diseases, but long put off by the physician, that it might be persuaded into what evils it has cast itself headlong by sinning? For what is easily healed is not much guarded against; but from the difficulty of healing there will be more diligent guarding of health regained. God, then, is not to be reckoned as cruel, to whom it is said: *And you, Lord, how long?*, but as a good persuader of the soul as to what evil it has brought upon itself; for this soul does not yet pray so perfectly that it can be said to it: *While you are still speaking I will say: Behold, here I am*[^6]. At the same time, that it may also recognize how great a punishment is prepared for the impious, who will not turn to God, if those who turn suffer such great difficulty; as it is written in another place: *If the righteous one will scarcely be saved, where will the sinner and the impious appear?*[^7]

**5.** [v 5.] *Turn, Lord, and rescue my soul.* Turning herself, she prays that God too may turn to her, as it was said: *Turn to me, and I will turn to you, says the Lord*[^8]. Or is it to be understood from that expression: *Turn, Lord*, that is, make me turn, since in her very conversion she felt difficulty and toil? For our perfect conversion finds God ready, as the Prophet says: *We shall find him ready as the dawn*[^9]. Since it was not the absence of him who is everywhere present, but our turning away, that made us lose him: *He was in this world*, he says, *and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^10]. If, then, he was in this world, and the world did not know him, our uncleanness does not bear the sight of him. But while we turn ourselves, that is, by the change of the old life recarve our spirit, we feel it hard and toilsome to be wrenched back from the fog of earthly desires to the serenity and tranquillity of the divine light. And in such difficulty we say: *Turn, Lord*, that is, help us, that the conversion may be perfected in us, which finds you ready and offering yourself to be enjoyed by your lovers. And therefore, after she said: *Turn, Lord*, she added, *and rescue my soul*, as it were clinging to the perplexities of this world, and suffering in the very conversion certain thorns of desires that tear at it. *Save me*, she says, *because of your mercy.* She understands that it is not of her own merits that she is healed, since to one sinning and transgressing the given precept just condemnation was owed; heal me, then, she says, not because of my merit, but because of your mercy.

**6.** [v 6.] *For in death there is no one who is mindful of you.* She understands also that now is the time of conversion, because when this life has passed, there remains nothing but the repaying of merits. *And in hell who will confess to you?* That rich man confessed in hell, of whom the Lord speaks, who saw Lazarus in rest, but grieved that he himself was in torments: so far did he confess that he even wished his own to be warned to hold themselves back from sins, on account of the punishments which are not believed to be in hell. Although, then, in vain, yet he confessed that those torments had befallen him deservedly; since he also desired his own to be taught, lest they fall into them. What, then, is: *And in hell who will confess to you?* does he wish hell to be understood as that into which the impious will be cast headlong after the judgment, where, on account of the deeper darkness, they will see no light of God, to whom they might confess anything? For that rich man, with his eyes still lifted up, though an immense gulf was interposed, could yet see Lazarus set in rest, from the comparison of whom he was forced to a confession of his merits. That too can be understood, that he calls sin death, which is committed in contempt of the divine law: so that we call sin the sting of death, because it produces death; for the sting of death is sin. In which death, this is to be unmindful of God, to despise his law and precepts: so that he called hell the blindness of soul, which receives and enwraps the one sinning, that is, the one dying; *As they did not approve of having God in their knowledge*, he says, *God gave them up to a reprobate sense*[^11]. From this death and from this hell the soul prays to be kept safe, while it strives at conversion to God, and feels the difficulties.

**7.** [v 7.] For which reason she goes on, saying: *I have labored in my groaning.* And as though that profited little, she adds and says: *I will wash my bed every night.* The bed is here named as the place where the sick and weak soul rests, that is, in the pleasure of the body and in every worldly delight; which delight he washes with tears who tries to draw himself out of it. For he now sees himself condemning carnal desires; and yet his weakness is held by delight, and lies willingly in it, from which the soul cannot rise unless healed. But what he says, *every night*, this perhaps he wished to be understood, that he who, ready in spirit, perceives some light of truth, and yet at times rests in the delight of this world through the weakness of the flesh, is as though forced to suffer days and nights by alternating affection: so that when he says: *With the mind I serve the law of God*, he feels as it were a day; again when he says: *But with the flesh the law of sin*[^12], he is inclined toward night, until every night passes, and there comes the one day of which it is said: *In the morning I will stand before you, and will see*[^13]. For then he will stand; but now he lies, while he is on the bed which he will wash every night, that with such great tears he may obtain from God's mercy the most effective medicine. *I will water my couch with my tears* is a repetition: for when he says *with tears*, he shows how he said above, *I will wash*; and we take this *couch* as what above was *bed*: although *I will water* is something more than *I will wash*; since something can be washed on the surface; but watering penetrates to the interior, which signifies weeping down to the depths of the heart. As for the variety of tenses, that he put the past when he said: *I have labored in my groaning*; and the future, when he said: *I will wash my bed every night*; again the future: *I will water my couch with my tears*, this shows what one ought to say to himself when he has labored in vain in groaning; as if he said: It did not profit when I did this, therefore I will do that.

**8.** [vv 8.9.] *My eye is troubled by anger*: whether her own, or God's, in which she asks not to be rebuked or chastised? But if that signifies the day of judgment, how can it be understood now? Or is it the beginning of it, that here men suffer pains and torments, and especially the loss of the understanding of truth, as I have already mentioned was said: *God gave them up to a reprobate sense*[^14]? For that is the blindness of mind; into which whoever has been given up is shut off from the inner light of God, but not yet utterly while he is in this life. For there is an outer darkness, which is understood to pertain rather to the day of judgment, so that whoever, while there is time, has refused to be corrected, may be utterly outside God; for what is it to be utterly outside God, but to be in the highest blindness? since God dwells in inaccessible light, into which they enter to whom it is said: *Enter into the joy of your Lord*[^15]. The beginning, then, of this anger is what every sinner suffers in this life: therefore, fearing the day of judgment, she labors and laments, lest she be brought to that whose beginning, so destructive, she now experiences; and therefore she did not say: It is extinguished, but: *My eye is troubled by anger.* But if she says her eye is troubled by her own anger, neither is this strange: for perhaps from this it was said: *Let not the sun go down upon your anger*[^16], because the mind thinks it suffers in itself as a setting of the inner sun, that is, the wisdom of God, the mind which by its own disturbance is not allowed to see him.

**9.** *I have grown old among all my enemies.* She had spoken only of anger, if indeed she had spoken of her own anger: but considering the remaining vices, she finds herself hemmed in by all. And since these vices are of the old life and of the old man, whom we are to put off that we may put on the new, it was rightly said: *I have grown old.* Now *among all my enemies*, she means either among the vices themselves, or among men who will not turn to God: for these too, even if they do not know it, even if they spare, even if they share the same banquets and houses and cities without any quarrel, and use frequent conversations as though in concord, nonetheless, by a contrary intention, are enemies to those who turn themselves to God. For when some love and crave this world, and others long to be set free from this world, who does not see that the former are enemies to the latter? for if they could, they would drag them with themselves to punishment. And it is a great gift, to be daily engaged among their words, and not to depart from the way of God's precepts: for often the mind, striving to advance toward God, is shaken and trembles on the very way: and for the most part for this reason does not fulfill its good resolve, lest it offend those with whom it lives, who love and pursue other goods that will perish and pass away. From these every healthy soul is separated, not by places, but in mind; for bodies are contained by places, but for the mind its place is its own affection.

**10.** For which reason, after the labor and the groaning and the most frequent showers of tears, since that cannot be empty which is so vehemently asked of him who is the fountain of all mercies, and it was most truly said: *The Lord is near to those who are contrite of heart*[^17], the pious soul, signifying that after such great difficulties she has been heard (whom it is permitted also to understand as the Church), see what she adds: *Depart from me, all you who work iniquity; because the Lord has heard the voice of my weeping.* Either it was said by prophecy, since they are to depart, that is, the impious will be separated from the righteous, when the day of judgment comes; or now, because, even if they are contained together and in the same assemblies, yet on the bare threshing floor the grains are already separated from the chaff, though they lie hidden among the chaff: and so they can be engaged together, but they cannot together be carried off by the wind.

**11.** [v 10.] *Because the Lord has heard the voice of my weeping; the Lord has heard my supplication; the Lord has taken up my prayer.* The frequent repetition of the same sentence shows not, as it were, the necessity of one narrating, but the affection of one exulting; for those who rejoice are accustomed to speak in this way, so that it does not suffice them to declare once what they rejoice over. This is the fruit of that groaning in which one labors, and of those tears with which the bed is washed and the couch watered; since he who sows in tears will reap in joy, and blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

**12.** [v 11.] *Let all my enemies be ashamed and troubled.* Depart from me, all of you, she said above; which can come about even in this life, as has been explained: but what she says, *Let them be ashamed and troubled*, I do not see how it can come to pass, except on that day when the rewards of the righteous and the punishments of sinners will be manifest. For now the impious are so far from being ashamed that they do not cease to insult us: and for the most part they prevail so much by their mockeries that they make weak men ashamed of the name of Christ, whence it was said: *Whoever is ashamed of me before men, I will be ashamed of him before my Father*[^18]. And now whoever wishes to fulfill those sublime precepts, to scatter abroad, to give to the poor, so that his righteousness may remain forever, and, all his earthly goods being sold and given out to the needy, wishes to follow Christ, saying: *We brought nothing into this world, and truly we can take nothing out; having food and covering, with these let us be content*[^19]; he falls into their sacrilegious scoffing, and is called insane by those who refuse to be healed; and often, lest he be so called by desperate men, he trembles to do, and puts off, what the most faithful and most powerful physician of all has commanded. Now, then, these cannot be ashamed, for whose sake it is to be wished that we be not ashamed, and that we be not called back, or hindered, or delayed from the way we have resolved upon. But the time will come when they will be ashamed, saying as it is written: *These are they whom we once held in derision and as a parable of reproach: we fools esteemed their life madness, and their end without honor: how are they reckoned among the sons of God, and their lot is among the saints? Therefore we have erred from the way of truth, and the light of righteousness has not shone for us, and the sun has not risen upon us: we were filled with the way of iniquity and of perdition, and we walked difficult solitudes, but the way of the Lord we did not know. What did pride profit us, or what did the boasting of riches bring us? All those things have passed away like a shadow*[^20].

**13.** But as for what she says: *Let them be turned back, and confounded*, who would not judge it to be a most just punishment, that they should have for confusion the conversion which they refused to have for salvation? Then she added, *very swiftly*; for when the day of judgment has begun to be despaired of, when they shall have said: Peace, then sudden destruction will come upon them. But whenever it comes, that comes most swiftly which is despaired of as ever coming: and the length of this life is not made to be felt except by the hope of living; for nothing seems swifter than whatever in it has already passed. When, then, the day of judgment comes, then sinners will feel how not long is every life that passes; nor can it seem to them at all to have come late, which will have come to those not desiring, but rather not believing, it. Although this too can be taken here, that because God heard her, as it were groaning and so often and so long weeping, she is understood to be set free from sins, and to have tamed all the perverse movements of carnal affection, as she says: *Depart from me, all you who work iniquity, because the Lord has heard the voice of my weeping*; and when this comes to her, it is no wonder if she is now so perfect that she prays for her enemies. To this, then, can pertain what she said: *Let all my enemies be ashamed and troubled*, that they may do penance for their sins, which cannot come about without confusion and trouble. Nothing, therefore, hinders our taking what follows also in this way: *Let them be turned back and ashamed*, that is, let them turn back to God, and be ashamed that they once gloried in the former darkness of sins; as the Apostle says: *For what glory did you once have in those things of which you are now ashamed?*[^21] But what she added, *very swiftly*, is to be referred either to the affection of one wishing, or to the power of Christ, who, with such swiftness of times, turned the nations that were persecuting the Church on behalf of their idols to the faith of the Gospel.
