# Exposition of Psalm 7

**1.** [v 1.] The history from which this prophecy took its occasion is easy to learn in the second book of Kings. For there Cushi, a friend of King David, crossed over to the side of Absalom his son, who was waging war against his father, to spy out his counsels and report what that man was contriving against his father at the instigation of Ahithophel, who had fallen away from friendship with David, and was instructing his son against his father with such counsels as he could. But since it is not the history itself that is to be considered in this psalm, of which the Prophet took up the veil of mysteries, if we have made the passage over to Christ, let the veil be taken away. And first let us inquire what the signification of the names themselves means. For there have not been lacking interpreters who, investigating these very names not carnally according to the letter, but spiritually, have declared to us that Cushi is interpreted as "Silence"; and Jemini as "Right-hand"; Ahithophel as "Brother's ruin." By which interpretations again that betrayer Judas meets us, so that Absalom bears his image, according as he is interpreted "the father's peace"; because his father was at peace toward him, although he himself had war in his heart by his deceits, as was treated in the third psalm. And just as in the Gospel the disciples of our Lord Jesus Christ are found to have been called sons, so in the same Gospel they are found also to have been called brothers; for the Lord, rising, says: *Go, and tell my brothers*[^1], and the Apostle calls him the firstborn among many brothers: the ruin, then, of his disciple who betrayed him is rightly understood as "Brother's ruin," which we said Ahithophel is interpreted. But Cushi, which is interpreted "Silence," is rightly taken to mean that our Lord contended against those deceits by silence, that is, by that deepest secret by which blindness came in part upon Israel, when they persecuted the Lord, that the fullness of the Gentiles might enter in, and so all Israel might be saved. When the Apostle had come to this deep secret and high silence, he cried out as though struck by a kind of awe at its very depth: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God, how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?*[^2] so that great silence he opens not so much by explanation as commends by admiration. By this silence the Lord, hiding the sacrament of his venerable passion, turned the voluntary ruin of his brother, that is, the wicked crime of his betrayer, into the order of his mercy and providence; so that what that man, with a perverse mind, was doing for the destruction of one man, he himself, by provident governance, might turn to the salvation of all men. Therefore the perfect soul sings a psalm to the Lord, which is now worthy to know the secret of God; it sings *for the words of Cushi*, because it has deserved to know the words of that silence. For among the unbelievers and persecutors that is silence and secret: but among his own, to whom it was said: *Now I do not call you servants, for the servant does not know what his lord does; but I have called you friends, because all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you*[^3]: among his friends, then, there is not silence, but the words of the silence, that is, the reason of that silence set forth and made manifest. Which silence, that is, Cushi, is called the son of Jemini, that is, of the right hand: for what was done for them was not to be hidden from the saints. And yet, *Let not the left hand know what the right hand does*[^4]. The perfect soul, then, to which that secret has become known, sings in prophecy *for the words of Cushi*, that is, for the knowledge of that same secret; which secret the right-hand God, that is, favoring and propitious to it, wrought: wherefore is this silence called the son of the right-hand one, which is *Cushi, the son of Jemini*?

**2.** [vv 2.3.] *Lord my God, in you I have hoped; save me from all who persecute me, and rescue me.* As one to whom, now perfected, there remains only the envious devil, every war and adversity of the vices having been overcome, he says: *Save me from all who persecute me, and rescue me: lest at any time he seize my soul like a lion.* The Apostle says: *Your adversary the devil goes about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour*[^5]. And so, when he had said in the plural number: *Save me from all who persecute me*, he afterward brought in the singular, saying, *lest at any time he seize my soul like a lion*: for he did not say, lest they seize, knowing who it is that has withstood his enemies, and is vehemently opposed to the perfect soul. *While there is no one to redeem, nor anyone to save*: that is, lest he seize, while you do not redeem nor save; for if God does not redeem nor save, that one seizes.

**3.** [vv 4.5.] And that it may be manifest that it is now the perfect soul that says this, which has to beware of the most fraudulent snares of the devil alone, see what follows: *Lord my God, if I have done this.* What is it that he calls *this*? is it that, because he does not name the sin, sin in general is to be understood? If this understanding displeases, let us take what follows to be meant: as if we had asked, What is this that you say, *this*? he answers: *If there is iniquity in my hands.* Now, then, it is manifest that it is said of every sin: *If I have repaid those who repaid me with evils*; which no one can truly say but the perfect. For the Lord says: *Be perfect, as your Father who is in heaven, who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^6]; he, then, who does not repay those who repay with evils, is perfect. Since, therefore, the perfect soul prays *for the words of Cushi, the son of Jemini*, that is, for the knowledge of that secret and silence, which the propitious and merciful Lord wrought for our salvation, that he might tolerate and most patiently bear with the deceits of his betrayer: as though this perfect soul says, setting forth the reason of that secret: I, for you, impious and a sinner, that your iniquities might be washed by the shedding of my blood, bore with my betrayer with great silence and great patience; will you not imitate me, that you too may not repay evil for evil? Therefore, considering and understanding what the Lord did for him, and advancing toward perfection by his example, he says: *If I have repaid those who repaid me with evils*, that is, if I have not done what you taught by doing, *then let me fall away empty from my enemies.* And it is well that he did not say: If I have repaid those who gave me evils, but, *those who repaid*: for he who repays had already received something; but it is of greater patience not to render evils even to him who, having received benefits, repays evil for good, than if, no benefit having been received before, one had wished to harm. *If I have repaid*, then, he says, *those who repaid me with evils*, that is, if I have not imitated you in that silence, that is, in your patience which you wrought for me, *let me fall away from my enemies empty.* For he boasts emptily who, being himself also a man, desires to avenge himself on a man: and when he openly seeks to overcome a man, he is secretly overcome by the devil, made empty by a vain and proud gladness, that he could not, as it were, be conquered. This one, then, understands where the greater victory is achieved, and where the Father, who sees in secret, repays. And so, lest he repay those who repay with evils, he overcomes anger rather than a man, instructed too by those letters in which it is written: *Better is he who overcomes anger, than he who takes a city*[^7]. *If I have repaid those who repaid me with evils, then let me fall away from my enemies empty.* He seems to swear by an imprecation, which is the gravest kind of oath, when a man says: If I did that, may I suffer that. But the swearing in the mouth of one swearing is one thing, in the signification of one prophesying another: for here he says what truly befalls men who repay those who repay with evils; not that he imprecates upon himself, or upon anyone, as by an oath.

**4.** [v 6.] *Let the enemy, then, persecute my soul, and seize it.* Again naming the enemy in the singular number, he makes more and more manifest him whom above he called as it were a lion. For he himself persecutes the soul; and if he deceives it, he will seize it. For men rage even to the slaying of the body, but the soul, after this visible death, they cannot have in their power; whereas the devil will possess whatever souls he persecutes and seizes. *And let him trample my life into the earth*: that is, by trampling, let him make my life earth, namely his own food; for he was called not only a lion, but also a serpent, to whom it was said: *You will eat earth*[^8]; and to sinful man it was said: *You are earth, and into earth you will go*[^9]. *And let him bring down my glory into the dust.* This is that dust which the wind casts from the face of the earth, namely the vain and foolish boasting of the proud, puffed up and not made solid, like a globe of dust raised by the wind. Rightly, then, he here put glory, which he does not wish brought down into the dust. For he wishes to have it solid in his conscience before God, where there is no boasting; *He who glories*, he says, *let him glory in the Lord*[^10]. This solidity is brought down into dust, if through pride anyone, despising the secrets of conscience, where God alone proves a man, wishes to glory among men; hence it is that elsewhere he says: *God will break the bones of those who please men*[^11]. But he who has well learned, or by experience knows, the degrees of vices to be overcome, understands that this vice of vainglory is to be guarded against by the perfect either alone or above all: for by which vice the soul first fell, this it overcomes last. *Now the beginning of all sin is pride*; and: *The beginning of man's pride is to apostatize from God*[^12].

**5.** [v 7.] *Rise up, Lord, in your anger.* Why does this one, whom we call perfect, still provoke God to anger? Should it not rather be seen that he is the more perfect who, when he was being stoned, said: *Lord, do not lay this sin to their charge*[^13]? Or does this one too pray these things not against men, but against the devil and his angels, whose possession sinners and impious men are? Not raging, then, but merciful toward him, does whoever prays pray, when he prays that this possession be taken from him by that Lord who justifies the impious: for when the impious one is justified, from impious he becomes righteous, and migrates from the possession of the devil into the temple of God; and since it is a punishment for anyone to have taken from him a possession in which he longs to rule, this punishment he calls the anger of God against the devil, that he may cease to possess those whom he possesses. *Rise up, Lord, in your anger*: *Rise up*, here he said appear, namely in human and hidden words, as though God sleeps, when he lies unknown in his secret places. *Be exalted in the borders of my enemies.* He called the very possession their borders, where he wishes God rather to be exalted, that is, honored and glorified, than the devil, while the impious are justified and praise God. *And rise up, Lord my God, in the precept that you have commanded*: that is, because you commanded humility, appear humble; and you first fulfill what you commanded, that by your example, overcoming pride, they may not be possessed by the devil, who persuaded pride against your precepts, saying: *Eat, and your eyes will be opened, and you will be like gods*[^14].

**6.** [v 8.] *And the congregation of peoples will surround you.* There is a double understanding. For the congregation of peoples can be taken either of believers or of persecutors, each of which came about through that same humility of our Lord: which the multitude of persecutors, despising, surrounded him, of which it was said: *Why have the nations raged, and the peoples meditated vain things?*[^15] but the multitude of believers, through his humility, so surrounded him that it was most truly said: *Blindness came in part upon Israel, that the fullness of the Gentiles might enter in*[^16]; and that other: *Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession*[^17]. *And for the sake of this return on high*: that is, for the sake of this congregation return on high; which he is understood to have done by rising again and ascending into heaven. For so glorified he gave the Holy Spirit, who before his glorification could not be given, as it is set down in the Gospel: *But the Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified*[^18]. Having returned, then, on high for the sake of the congregation of peoples, he sent the Holy Spirit; filled with whom the preachers of the Gospel filled the world with Churches.

**7.** This sense too can be understood thus: *Rise up, Lord, in your anger, be exalted in the borders of my enemies*: that is, rise up in your anger, and let my enemies not understand you; so that this is *be exalted*, that is, made high so that you are not understood, which is referred to that silence. For thus of this exaltation it is said in another psalm: *And he ascended above the Cherubim, and flew. And he made darkness his hiding place*[^19]: by which exaltation, that is, concealment, when those who crucify you have not understood you, by the merit of their own sins, the congregation of believers will surround you. For he was exalted by that very humility, that is, he was not understood; so that to this is referred: *And rise up, Lord my God, in the precept that you have commanded*; that is, when you appear humble, be high, that my enemies may not understand you: for to the righteous, sinners are enemies, and to the pious, the impious. *And the congregation of peoples will surround you*: that is, by this very fact that those who crucify you do not understand you, the Gentiles will believe in you, and so the congregation of peoples will surround you. But what follows, if it truly signifies this, has more of sorrow, because it now begins to be felt, than of gladness, because it is understood. For it follows: *And for the sake of this return on high*: that is, and for the sake of this congregation of the human race, by which the Churches are filled, return on high, that is, again cease to be understood. What, then, is: *And for the sake of this*, but that this too will offend you; so that you most truly foretell, saying: *Do you think that when the Son of man comes, he will find faith on the earth?*[^20] Again he speaks of the false prophets, who are understood as heretics: *Because of their iniquity the love of many will grow cold*[^21]. When, therefore, even in the Churches, that is, in that congregation of peoples and nations where the Christian name has spread most widely, there will be so great an abundance of sins, which is now in great part felt, is not that here foretold which was also announced through another prophet, the famine of the word? And is it not also for the sake of this congregation, estranging from itself the light of truth by its sins, that God returns on high, that is, so that the sincere faith, purified from the stain of all depraved opinions, is either not held, or held by very few, of whom it was said: *Blessed is he who perseveres to the end, he will be saved*[^22]? Not without reason, then, is it said: *And for the sake of this* congregation *return on high*; that is, withdraw again into the height of your secret places, even for the sake of this congregation of peoples which has your name and does not do your works.

**8.** But whether the earlier or this be the more fitting exposition of this place, without prejudice to any other better or equal one, most fittingly there follows: *The Lord judges the peoples.* For whether he returned on high, when after the resurrection he ascended into heaven, well does it follow: *The Lord judges the peoples*; because thence he is to come to judge the living and the dead: or whether he returns on high when the understanding of truth deserts Christian sinners; because of that very coming it was said: *Do you think the Son of man, when he comes, will find faith on the earth?* *The Lord*, then, *judges the peoples.* What Lord, but Jesus Christ? *For the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son*[^23]. For which reason, see how this soul, which prays perfectly, does not fear the day of judgment, and with a truly secure desire says in prayer: *May your kingdom come*[^24]: *Judge me*, he says, *Lord, according to my righteousness.* In the earlier psalm the weak one prayed, imploring the mercy of God rather than recalling any merit of his own; since the Son of God came to call sinners to penance. And so there he had said: *Save me, Lord, because of your mercy*, that is, not because of my merit; but now, since, being called, he has held and kept the precepts which he received, he dares to say: *Judge me, Lord, according to my righteousness, and according to my innocence upon me.* This is true innocence, which harms not even an enemy; and so well does he ask to be judged according to his innocence, who could truly say: *If I have repaid those who repaid me with evils.* But what he added, *upon me*, can be understood not only of innocence, but also of righteousness; so that this is the sense: Judge me, Lord, according to my righteousness, and according to my innocence, which righteousness and innocence is upon me. By which addition he shows that the very fact that the soul is righteous and innocent it has not of itself, but through God who illumines and enlightens it; for of this he says in another psalm: *You will light my lamp, Lord*[^25]; and of John it is said, that *he was not the light, but bore witness to the light*[^26]. *He was a lamp burning and shining*[^27]. That light, then, from which souls as lamps are kindled, shines forth not by a borrowed but by its own splendor, which is the truth itself. So, then, it is said, *according to my righteousness, and according to my innocence upon me*, as if a lamp, burning and shining, should say: Judge me according to the flame that is upon me, that is, not as I am, but as I shine, kindled from you.

**9.** [v 10.] *But let the wickedness of sinners be consummated.* *Let it be consummated*, he says, let it be perfected, according to that which is in the Apocalypse: *Let the righteous become more righteous, and the filthy be filthy still*[^28]. For the wickedness of those who crucified the Son of God seems consummated; but greater is that of those who refuse to live rightly, and hate the precepts of truth for which the Son of God was crucified. *Let the wickedness of sinners, then, be consummated*, he says; that is, let it come to the height of wickedness, that the just judgment may now be able to come. But since it was not only said: *Let the filthy be filthy still*, but also said: *Let the righteous become more righteous*, he links and says: *And you will direct the righteous, searching hearts and reins, O God.* How, then, can the righteous be directed, except in secret; when even through those things which at the beginning of Christian times, while the saints were still pressed by the persecution of worldly men, seemed wonderful to men, now, after the Christian name has begun to be at so great a height, hypocrisy has grown, that is, the dissimulation of those, namely, who under the Christian name prefer to please men rather than God? How, then, is the righteous directed in such great confusion of dissimulation, except while God searches hearts and reins, seeing the thoughts of all, which are signified by the name of the heart, and the delights, which are understood by the name of the reins? For rightly is the delight in temporal and earthly things attributed to the reins; because it is the lower part of man, and that is the region where the pleasure of carnal generation dwells, through which, by the succession of offspring, human nature is poured into this toilsome life of deceitful gladness. God, then, searching our heart, and seeing it to be there where our treasure is, that is, in heaven; searching also the reins, and seeing that we do not yield to flesh and blood, but delight in the Lord, directs the righteous in his very conscience before himself, where no man sees, but he alone who sees through what each one thinks, and in what each one delights. For the end of care is delight; because each one strives with cares and thoughts to arrive at his own delight. He sees, then, our cares, who searches the heart: but he sees the ends of our cares, that is, the delights, who thoroughly searches the reins; so that when he has found our cares inclined not to the desire of the flesh, nor to the desire of the eyes, nor to the ambition of the world, all of which pass away like a shadow, but raised up to the joys of eternal things, which are violated by no change, he may direct the righteous, searching hearts and reins, O God. For our works, which we do in deeds and words, can be known to men; but with what mind they are done, and to what we desire to come through them, he alone knows who searches hearts and reins, O God.

**10.** [v 11.] *My righteous help is from the Lord, who saves the upright of heart.* There are two offices of medicine; one by which infirmity is healed, the other by which health is preserved. According to that first, it was said in the earlier psalm: *Have mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak*: according to this other, in this psalm it is said: *If there is iniquity in my hands, if I have repaid those who repaid me with evils, then let me fall away from my enemies empty*; for there the weak one prays that he may be set free, here, already healthy, that he may not be corrupted. According to that, there it is said: *Save me because of your mercy*: according to this, here it is said: *Judge me, Lord, according to my righteousness*; for there he asks a remedy that he may escape from disease, but here a protection that he may not fall back into disease. According to that it is said: *Save me, Lord, according to your mercy*; according to this it is said: *My righteous help is from the Lord, who saves the upright of heart.* For both that and this save; but that transfers from sickness to health, this preserves in health itself. And so there the help is merciful, because the sinner has no merit, who still desires to be justified, believing in him who justifies the impious; but here the help is righteous, because it is now granted to the righteous. Let the sinner, then, who said: *I am weak*, say there: *Save me, Lord, because of your mercy*; and let the righteous, who said: *If I have repaid those who repaid me with evils*, say here: *My righteous help is from the Lord, who saves the upright of heart.* For if he provides the medicine by which we, the weak, are healed, how much more that by which we, healthy, are kept safe? Since, if while we were still sinners, Christ died for us, how much more, being now justified, shall we be saved from wrath through him?

**11.** *My righteous help is from the Lord, who saves the upright of heart.* He directs the righteous, searching hearts and reins, O God: but with righteous help he saves the upright of heart. Not as he searches hearts and reins, does he so save the upright of heart and reins: because thoughts are both evil in a crooked heart, and good in an upright heart; but delights that are not good pertain to the reins, because they are lower and earthly, but good ones not to the reins, but to the heart itself. Therefore men cannot so be called upright of reins as they are called upright of heart, since now where the thought is, there is also the delight: which cannot come about except when divine and eternal things are thought of. *You have put*, he says, *gladness in my heart*, when he had said: *The light of your countenance is sealed upon us, Lord*[^29]. For the phantasms of temporal things, which the soul fashions for itself, when it is tossed about with vain and mortal hope, although by empty imaginations they often bring a raving and insane gladness, yet this delight is to be attributed not to the heart, but to the reins; because all those imaginations are drawn from lower, that is, earthly and carnal things. So it comes about, that God, searching hearts and reins, and seeing in the heart upright thoughts, in the reins no delights, provides righteous help to the upright of heart, where to clean thoughts the higher delights are joined. And therefore in another psalm, when he had said: *Moreover even till night my reins have rebuked me*, he added concerning help, saying: *I beheld the Lord in my sight always, because he is at my right hand that I be not moved*[^30]. Where he shows that he suffered only suggestions from the reins, not also delights, which if he had suffered, he would surely have been moved. But he said: *The Lord is at my right hand, that I be not moved*; then he adds: *For this my heart has been delighted*: so that the reins could rebuke him, not delight him. Therefore the delight came about not in the reins, but there, where, against the rebuke of the reins, God was provided to be at the right hand, that is, in the heart.

**12.** [v 12.] *God a righteous judge, strong and longsuffering.* What God a judge, but the Lord, who judges the peoples? He himself righteous, who will repay each one according to his works. He himself strong, who, although most powerful, tolerated even impious persecutors for our salvation. He himself longsuffering, who did not, immediately after the resurrection, snatch even those who persecuted him to punishment, but bore with them, that they might at some time turn themselves from that impiety to salvation: and he still bears with them, reserving the last punishment for the last judgment, and even now inviting sinners to penance. *Not bringing on anger every single day.* Perhaps it is said more significantly, *bringing on anger*, than being angry, and thus we find it in the Greek copies: so that there is not in him the anger by which he punishes, but in the souls of those ministers who obey the precepts of truth; through whom command is given also to the lower ministries which are called angels of wrath, for the punishing of sins, whom now human punishment delights not on account of righteousness, in which they do not rejoice, but on account of malice. *Not*, then, does God *bring on anger every single day*, that is, he does not every single day gather his ministers for vengeance: for now the patience of God invites to penance; but in the last time, when men, by their hardness and impenitent heart, shall have stored up for themselves wrath in the day of wrath and revelation of the just judgment of God, he will brandish his sword.

**13.** [v 13.] *Unless you turn back*, he says, *he will brandish his sword.* The Lord as man can himself be understood as the sword of God, twice sharpened, that is, a broadsword, which he did not brandish at his first coming, but hid as it were in the sheath of humility: but he will brandish it when, coming at the second coming to judge the living and the dead, in the manifest splendor of his brightness, he will flash light to his righteous ones, and terrors to the impious. For in other copies, in place of *he will brandish his sword*, there is put *he will make his broadsword to shine*: by which word I think the last coming of the Lord's brightness is most fittingly signified; since from his own person is understood what another psalm has: *Deliver, Lord, my soul from the impious, your broadsword from the enemies of your hand*[^31]. *He has bent his bow, and made it ready.* The tenses of the words are not to be passed over carelessly, that he said of the sword in the future, *he will brandish*; of the bow in the past, *he has bent*: then words of the past tense follow.

**14.** [v 14.] *And in it he has prepared the vessels of death: his arrows he has wrought for those who burn.* This bow, then, I would gladly take to be the holy Scriptures, where by the strength of the New Testament, as by a kind of string, the hardness of the Old has been bent and tamed. From it as arrows the Apostles are sent forth, or divine proclamations are hurled. Which arrows he *has wrought for those who burn*, that is, by which those struck might blaze with divine love. For by what other arrow was she struck who says: *Bring me into the house of wine, set me among the perfumes, hem me in among the honey; because I am wounded with love*[^32] By what other arrows is he kindled who, desiring to return to God and to come back from this pilgrimage, asks help against deceitful tongues, and to whom it is said: *What may be given you, or what may be added to you against a deceitful tongue? The sharp arrows of the mighty, with desolating coals*[^33]: that is, by which, struck and inflamed, you may burn with so great a love of the kingdom of heaven, that you despise the tongues of all who resist and wish to call you back from your resolve, and deride their persecutions, saying: *Who will separate me from the love of Christ? Tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword? For I am sure*, he says, *that neither death, nor life, nor angel, nor principality, nor things present, nor things to come, nor power, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^34]. Thus he wrought his arrows for those who burn: for in the Greek copies it is found thus: *His arrows he has wrought for those who burn*; but the Latin copies for the most part have *burning.* But whether the arrows themselves burn, or make others burn, which they certainly cannot do unless they themselves also burn, the sense is whole.

**15.** But since he said the Lord prepared in the bow not only arrows, but also *vessels of death*, it can be asked what the vessels of death are. Are they perhaps heretics? For they too, from the same bow, that is, from the same Scriptures, leap upon souls, not to be inflamed with love, but to be destroyed with poisons, which does not happen except according to deserts. Therefore this arrangement too is to be attributed to divine Providence, not because it makes sinners, but because it orders them when they have sinned: for, reading with an evil will through sin, they are evilly forced to understand, so that this is the very punishment of sin; by whose death, nevertheless, the sons of the catholic Church, as by certain thorns, are roused from sleep, and advance to the understanding of the divine Scriptures. *For there must be heresies too, that the approved*, he says, *may be made manifest among you*[^35], that is, among men, since they are manifest to God. Or perhaps he disposed these same arrows and vessels of death for the destruction of unbelievers, and wrought them burning, or for those who burn, for the exercise of the faithful? For it is not false what the Apostle says: *To some we are the odor of life unto life, to others the odor of death unto death, and who is sufficient for these things?*[^36] It is no wonder, then, if the same Apostles are both vessels of death in those by whom they suffered persecution, and fiery arrows for the inflaming of the hearts of believers.

**16.** [v 15.] But after this dispensation the just judgment will come: of which he so speaks that we understand that to each man punishment is made from his own sin, and his iniquity is turned into a punishment; lest we suppose that that tranquillity and ineffable light of God brings forth from itself that by which sins are punished, but that it so orders the sins themselves that what were delights to the man sinning are instruments to the Lord punishing. *Behold*, he says, *he has travailed with injustice.* For what had he conceived, that he should travail with injustice? *He has conceived*, he says, *toil.* Hence, then, is that: *In toil you will eat your bread*[^37]; hence too that: *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened. For my yoke is easy, and my burden light*[^38]. For toil cannot be ended unless each one loves that which cannot be taken from him against his will. For when those things are loved which we can lose against our will, it is necessary that we toil most wretchedly for them; and that we may obtain them, in the straits of earthly troubles, when each one desires to snatch them and forestall another, or to wrest them from another, we contrive injustices. Rightly, then, and altogether in order, he has travailed with injustice who conceived toil. But what does he bring forth, but what he travailed with, although he did not travail with what he conceived? for that is not born which is conceived; but the seed is conceived, and what is formed from the seed is born. Toil, then, is the seed of iniquity; but sin is the conception of toil, that is, that first sin, to apostatize from God: he travailed with injustice, then, who conceived toil. *And he brought forth iniquity*: iniquity is the same as injustice; this, then, he brought forth which he travailed with. What follows next?

**17.** [v 16.] *He has opened a pit and dug it out.* To open a pit is, in earthly things, that is, as it were in the earth, to prepare a fraud, into which another may fall whom the unjust man wishes to deceive: now this pit is opened when consent is given to the evil suggestion of earthly desires; but it is dug out when, after the consent, the working of the fraud is pressed on. But how can it come about that iniquity should first harm the righteous man against whom it proceeds, rather than the unjust heart from which it proceeds? And so a defrauder of money, for example, while he desires to wound another with loss, is himself wounded by the wound of avarice: but who, even mad, would not see how great a distance there is between these two, when the one suffers loss of money, the other loss of innocence? *He will fall*, then, *into the ditch that he made*; which is said in another psalm: *The Lord is known executing judgments, the sinner is caught in the works of his own hands*[^39].

**18.** [v 17.] *His toil will be turned upon his own head, and his iniquity will come down upon his own crown.* For he himself did not wish to escape sin; but he was made under sin as a slave, the Lord saying: *Everyone who sins is a slave*[^40]. His iniquity, then, will be over him, when he himself is subjected to his own iniquity: because he could not say to the Lord what the innocent and upright say: *My glory, and the one who lifts up my head*[^41]. So, then, he himself will be lower, so that his iniquity may be higher, and may come down upon him; because it weighs him down, and burdens him, and does not allow him to fly back to the rest of the saints. This comes about when, in a perverse man, reason serves, and lust dominates.

**19.** [v 18.] *I will confess to the Lord according to his righteousness.* This is not a confession of sins: for he says this who above most truly said: *If there is iniquity in my hands*; but it is a confession of God's righteousness, by which we so speak: Truly, Lord, you are righteous, when you both so protect the righteous that you illumine them through yourself, and so order sinners that they are punished not by your malice, but by their own. This confession so praises the Lord that the blasphemies of the impious can avail nothing, who, wishing to excuse their crimes, refuse to attribute to their own fault that they sin, that is, refuse to attribute to their own fault their own fault. And so they find either fortune or fate to accuse; or the devil, whom not to consent to he willed to be in our power who made us; or they bring in some other nature, which is not from God, wretchedly fluctuating and erring, rather than confessing to God, that he may pardon them. For it is not fitting to pardon anyone except one who says: I have sinned. He, then, who sees the deserts of souls so ordered by God that, while to each is rendered his own, the beauty of the universe is in no part violated, praises God in all things: and this is the confession not of sinners, but of the righteous. For it is not the confession of sinners, when the Lord says: *I confess to you, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise, and revealed them to little ones*[^42]. Likewise in Ecclesiasticus it is said: *Confess to the Lord in all his works. And these you will say in confession, all the works of the Lord are very good*[^43]. Which can be understood in this psalm, if anyone, with a pious mind, the Lord helping, distinguishes between the rewards of the righteous and the punishments of sinners, how by these two the whole creation, which God, having founded it from himself, governs, is adorned with a wonderful beauty known to few. So, then, he says: *I will confess to the Lord according to his righteousness*, as one who has seen that darkness was not made by God, but was nevertheless ordered. For God said: *Let there be light, and there was light*[^44]. He did not say: Let there be darkness, and there was darkness; and yet he ordered it. And therefore it is said: *God divided between the light and the darkness, and God called the light day, and the darkness he called night*[^45]. This is the distinction: one thing he made, and ordered; another he did not make, but yet ordered even this. Now that darkness signifies sins is found both in the prophet, who said: *And your darkness will be as the noonday*[^46], and in the apostle saying: *He who hates his brother is in darkness*[^47], and especially that: *Let us cast off the works of darkness, and put on the armor of light*[^48]. Not that there is any nature of darkness: for every nature, insofar as it is a nature, is compelled to be; but to be pertains to light, not to be to darkness. He, then, who deserts him by whom he was made, and is inclined toward that from which he was made, that is, toward nothing, is darkened in this sin; and yet he does not utterly perish, but is ordered among the lowest things. And so, after he said: *I will confess to the Lord*, lest we should understand a confession of sins, he adds at the end: *And I will sing to the name of the Lord most high.* Now to sing pertains to gladness, but penance for sins to sorrow.

**20.** This psalm can also be understood in the person of the Lord as man; provided that the things said there humbly be referred to our weakness, which he bore.
