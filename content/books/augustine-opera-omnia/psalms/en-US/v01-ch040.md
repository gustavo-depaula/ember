# On the Same Psalm 31

**1.** This psalm—of the grace of God and of our justification, with no merits of ours going before, but the mercy of the Lord our God coming before us, a psalm commended especially by the mouth of the Apostle, as the reading preceding this psalm made known to all—has been taken up by our littleness to be treated with your Charity. Whence first I commend my weakness to your prayers, as the Apostle says, *that speech may be given me in the opening of my mouth*[^1], to speak to you in such a way that it be not dangerous for me to say nor unwholesome for you to hear. For the human mind, uncertain, and wavering between the confession of weakness and the boldness of presumption, is for the most part battered on this side and that, and so driven that to fall to either side is a headlong ruin for it. For if it gives itself over wholly to its own weakness, and inclines itself to this thought, so as to say that the mercy of God is for all sinners, persevering in whatever sins, provided only they believe that God frees, God forgives, so that in the end it is prepared that none of the unrighteous faithful perish; that is, that none perish of those who say to themselves: I will do whatever I please, I will be defiled with whatever crimes and disgraces, I will sin however much I will, God frees me by his mercy, because I have believed in him. He, then, who says that none of such men perish, is inclined by an evil thought into the impunity of sins; and that righteous God, to whom mercy and judgment are sung—not mercy alone, but also judgment—finds a man presuming evilly upon himself, and abusing the mercy of God unto his own ruin, and it is necessary that he condemn him. Such a thought, then, casts a man headlong: terrified by which, if anyone raises himself into a certain boldness of presumption, and presumes upon his own powers and righteousness, and sets in his mind to fulfill righteousness, and to do all that is commanded in the Law so as to offend in nothing, and that he has his own life in his power, so that he never anywhere slips, never fails, never wavers, never is darkened, and attributes this to himself and to the might of his own will: even if perhaps he fulfills all that seems righteous before the eyes of men, so that nothing is found in his life that could be rebuked by men, God condemns that very presumption and the boast of pride. What then happens, if a man justifies himself, and presumes upon his own righteousness? He falls. If, considering and thinking of his own weakness and presuming upon the mercy of God, he neglects to cleanse his life of his sins, and plunges himself into every whirlpool of disgraces; he too falls. Presumption upon righteousness is, as it were, the right hand: the thought of the impunity of sins is, as it were, the left. Let us hear the voice of God saying to us: *Do not turn aside to the right hand or to the left*[^2]. Do not presume upon the kingdom from your righteousness, do not presume upon sinning from the mercy of God. From both the divine precept calls you back; both from that height, and from this depth. Thither if you climb, you will be cast down; here if you slip, you will be sunk. *Do not*, he says, *turn aside to the right hand or to the left*. Again I say what you should all hold briefly fixed in your minds: Do not presume upon the kingdom from your righteousness, do not presume upon sinning from the mercy of God. You will answer: What then shall I do? This psalm teaches us: which, when read through and treated, I judge—with the mercy of the Lord helping—that we shall see the way, either where we already walk, or which we ought to hold. Let each one hear according to his own measure; and as he is conscious to himself, so let him either grieve as one to be corrected, or rejoice as one to be approved. If he finds that he has strayed, let him return that he may walk in the way: if he finds himself in the way, let him walk that he may arrive. Let no one be proud outside the way, no one slothful in the way.

**2.** That this psalm pertains to that grace by which we are Christians, the apostle Paul testified: whence we wished that very reading to be read to you. The Apostle said this, when he was commending the righteousness which is from faith, against those who glory in the righteousness which is from works; he said this: *What then shall we say that Abraham our father found according to the flesh? For if Abraham was justified by works, he has glory, but not before God*[^3]. May God himself turn such glory away from us, and let us rather hear this: *He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^4]. For many glory in works, and you find many pagans unwilling for this reason to become Christians, because they as it were suffice to themselves out of their own good life. To live well is needful, he says: what will Christ command me? That I live well? I already live well: what need have I of Christ? I commit no murder, no theft, no robbery, I do not covet others' goods, I am defiled by no adultery. Let something be found in my life that may be rebuked, and let him who rebukes it make me a Christian. He has glory, but not before God. But not so our father Abraham. For this saying of Scripture wishes to direct our attention to this. For since we confess—and this is our faith concerning the holy Patriarch, who pleased God—that we say and know him to have glory before God, the Apostle says: Surely it is known to us and manifest that Abraham has glory before God: but if Abraham was justified by works, he has glory, but not before God: now he has glory before God; therefore he was not justified by works. If, then, Abraham was not justified by works, whence was he justified? He goes on, and says whence: *For what does Scripture say?* that is: Whence does Scripture say Abraham was justified? *Now Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him unto righteousness*[^5]. Therefore Abraham was justified by faith.

**3.** Now he who hears, not by works, but by faith, let him watch out for that gulf of which I spoke: You see, then, that Abraham was justified by faith, not by works; I will do, then, whatever I please, because, even though I have no good works, and only believe in God, it is reckoned to me unto righteousness. If he has said and decided this, he is slipped and sunk: if he still thinks it and wavers, he is in peril. But the Scripture of God and the true understanding not only frees from peril the one in peril, but also lifts the sunk one from the depth. I answer, then, as though against the Apostle, and say concerning that same Abraham what we find also in the Epistle of another apostle, who wished to correct men who had badly understood this apostle. For James, in his Epistle, against those who were unwilling to work well, presuming upon faith alone, commended the works of that same Abraham, whose faith Paul commended: and the apostles are not contrary to each other. He mentions a work known to all: Abraham offered his son to God to be sacrificed. A great work, but from faith. I praise the superstructure of the work, but I see the foundation of faith: I praise the fruit of the good work, but in faith I recognize the root. But if Abraham did this apart from right faith, that work of whatever kind would profit him nothing. Again, if Abraham held faith in such a way that, when God commanded him to offer his son to him to be sacrificed, he said within himself: I will not do it, and yet I believe that God frees me even when I despise his commands; faith without works would be dead, and like a root without fruit would remain barren and dry.

**4.** What then? Ought we to set no works before faith, that is, so that before faith anyone be said to have worked well? For those very works which are said to be before faith, although they seem praiseworthy to men, are empty. So they seem to me to be, like great strength and a most swift running off the road. Let no one, then, count his good works before faith: where faith was not, there was no good work. For the intention makes the good work, and faith directs the intention. Do not pay great heed to what a man does, but to what he looks to when he does it, whither he directs the arms of his best steering. For suppose a man steers a ship most excellently, and has lost where he is going; what does it avail that he holds the topsail most excellently, moves it most excellently, gives the prow to the waves, takes care that the sides be not stove in; that he is of such strength that he can turn the ship wherever he wishes, and from wherever he wishes: and it is said to him: Where are you going? and he says: I do not know: or does not say, I do not know, but says: I am going to that port, and yet hastens not into the port but onto the rocks? Does not this man, the more agile and effective he seems to himself in steering the ship, the more dangerously steer it so as to bring it, by hastening, to shipwreck? Such too is he who runs most excellently off the road. Was it not, then, better and more tolerable that that steersman should be somewhat weaker, so that with labor and some difficulty he should hold the helm, and yet hold the right and due course; and again, that the other should walk, even more slothfully and feebly, yet in the road, than run strongly off the road? Best, then, is he who both holds the road and walks well in it: but of a hope to be ranked second is he who, even if he limps somewhat, does not altogether so as to stray, or to remain behind, but advances, even if little by little. For perhaps it is to be hoped that he, though more slowly, will arrive whither he is tending.

**5.** Therefore, brothers, Abraham was justified by faith: but if works did not precede faith, yet they followed it. For will your faith be barren? If you are not barren, it itself is not barren. You believed something evil, and with the fire of your malice you burned up the root of your faith. Hold faith, then, as one who will work. But you say: The apostle Paul does not say this. Nay, the apostle Paul says this: *Faith*, he says, *which works through love*[^6]; and in another place: *The fullness of the law, therefore, is love*[^7]; and in another place: *For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^8]. See whether he does not wish you to work, who says: *You shall not commit adultery, You shall not commit murder, You shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself: love of neighbor works no evil: but the fullness of the law is love*[^9]. Does love allow you to do any evil to the one you love? But perhaps you only do no evil, and not also some good. Does love, then, allow you not to render whatever you can to the one you love? Is it not that love which prays even for enemies? Does he, then, forsake a friend, who wishes well to an enemy? And so, if faith is without love, it will be without work. But that you may not think much about the work of faith, add to it hope and love, and do not think about what you should work. Love itself cannot be idle. For what does even any man work evilly, except love? Give me a love that is idle and works nothing. Disgraces, adulteries, crimes, murders, all manner of self-indulgence—does not love work them? Cleanse, then, your love: the water flowing into the sewer, turn it to the garden: what impulses it had toward the world, let it have such toward the maker of the world. Is it said to you: Love nothing? God forbid. You will be slothful, dead, detestable, wretched, if you love nothing. Love, but see what you love. The love of God, the love of neighbor, is called charity: the love of the world, the love of this age, is called cupidity. Let cupidity be reined in, let charity be stirred up. For that very charity of one who works well gives him the hope of a good conscience. For a good conscience bears hope: as an evil conscience is wholly in despair, so a good conscience is wholly in hope. And these three will be, of which the Apostle speaks, *Faith, Hope, Charity*[^10]. And in another place the same three, but for hope he put a good conscience: *For the end of the commandment is*, he said. What is the end of the commandment? That by which the commandments are perfected, not that by which they are consumed. For in one way we say: The food is finished; in another way we say: The tunic that was being woven is finished: the food is finished so that it may not be; the tunic is finished so that it may be perfected: and here it is called an end, and there. He did not, then, here mean an end of the commandment by which the commandments as it were perish; but by which they are perfected, and consummated, not consumed. The end, then, is by reason of those three: *The end*, he says, *of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned*[^11]. For hope he put a good conscience. For he hopes who bears a good conscience. But whom an evil conscience pricks draws himself back from hope, and hopes for himself nothing but condemnation. That he may hope, then, for the kingdom, let him have a good conscience; and that he may have a good conscience, let him believe and work. What he believes belongs to faith, what he works belongs to charity. In that place, then, the Apostle began from faith, *Faith, Hope, Charity*: in another place he began from charity itself, *Charity out of a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned*. Now we have begun from the middle, from conscience itself and hope. He who wishes, I say, to have good hope, let him have a good conscience; and that he may have a good conscience, let him believe and work. From the middle we go to the beginning and the end: let him believe, and work. What he believes belongs to faith: what he works belongs to charity.

**6.** How then does the Apostle say that a man is justified without works by faith, when in another place he says: *Faith which works through love*? Let us not, then, set the apostle James against Paul, but Paul himself against Paul, and let us say to him: By this you in a manner permit us to sin with impunity, when you say: *We judge that a man is justified by faith without works*[^12]: and by this you say: *Faith which works through love*. How by this do I become as it were secure, if I have not worked: but by this I seem to have neither hope nor faith itself good, unless I have worked through love? I hear you, O Apostle. Surely here you wish to commend to me faith without works: but the work of faith is love; which love cannot be idle, but it both works no evil and works whatever good it can. For what does love do? *Turn away from evil, and do good*[^13]. This faith, then, you commend without works; and in another place you say: *Even if I have faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not charity, it profits me nothing*[^14]. Therefore, if faith without charity profits nothing, but charity, where it is, must work, faith itself works through love. How, then, will a man be justified by faith without works? The Apostle himself answers: For this reason I said this to you, O man, lest you should seem to presume as it were upon your works, and to have received the grace of faith by the merit of your works. Do not presume, then, upon works before faith. Know that faith found you a sinner, and although faith, once given, made you righteous, it found ungodly the one whom it would make righteous. *To one who believes*, he says, *in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness*[^15]. If the ungodly is justified, out of the ungodly he is made righteous: if out of the ungodly he is made righteous, what are the works of the ungodly? Let even the ungodly man boast of his works, and say: I give to the poor, I take nothing from anyone, I do not covet another's wife, I kill no one, I commit fraud against no one, I render a deposit left with me though there is no witness; let him say all these things: I ask whether he is godly or ungodly. And how, he says, am I ungodly, doing these things? As those, of whom it is said: *And they served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever*[^16]. How are you ungodly? What if from all these good works you either hope for that which is to be hoped for, but not from him from whom it is to be hoped for; or hope for that which is not to be hoped for, even from him from whom eternal life is to be hoped for? For your good works you have hoped for some earthly happiness, you are ungodly. This is not the reward of faith. Faith is a precious thing, you have sold it cheap. You are ungodly, then, and these works of yours are nothing. Though you ply your arms in good works, and seem to steer the ship most excellently, you hasten onto the rocks. What if you hope for what is to be hoped for, that is, eternal life, but not from the Lord God through Jesus Christ, through whom alone eternal life is given; but think you can come to eternal life through the host of heaven, through the sun and moon, through the powers of the air, and sea, and earth, and stars? You are ungodly. Believe in him who justifies the ungodly, that your good works too may be able to be good works. For I would not even call them good, as long as they do not proceed from a good root. What is this? Either you hope for temporal life from the eternal God, or you hope for eternal life from demons: on either side you are ungodly. Correct your faith, set your faith straight, set the road straight: and if you have good feet, walk now secure, run, you hold the road: the better you run, the more easily you will arrive. But perhaps you limp somewhat. At least do not depart from the road; though more slowly, you will arrive: do not remain behind, do not turn back, do not stray.

**7.** What then? Who are the blessed? Not those in whom God has found no sin, for in all he finds it. For all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God. If, then, sins are found in all, it remains that none are blessed except those whose sins are forgiven. So the Apostle commended this in these words: *Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness. Now to one who works* (that is, who presumes upon his works, and says that the grace of faith was given him by their merit), *his wages are not reckoned as a gift but as his due*[^17]. What is this, except that our wages are called grace? If it is grace, it is given freely. What is it to be given freely? It is established as free. You have done no good, and forgiveness of sins is given you. Your works are examined, and all of them are found to be evil. If God were to render what is owed to those works, he would surely condemn you, for *the wages of sin is death*[^18]. What is owed to evil works, except damnation? What is owed to good works? The kingdom of heaven. But you have been found in evil works: if what is owed you were rendered, you would have to be punished. What happens then? God does not render you the punishment that is owed, but bestows the grace that is not owed. He owed vengeance, he gives indulgence. So you begin to be in faith through pardon; and now that faith, joined to hope and love, begins to work well. Yet do not even then boast or exalt yourself: remember by whom you were set upon the way; remember that you went astray even with sound and swift feet; remember that, though you lay sick and half-dead on the road, you were lifted onto the beast and brought to the inn. *But to one who works,* he says, *his wages are not reckoned as a gift but as his due.* If you wish to be a stranger to grace, vaunt your merits. But he sees what is in you, and knows what is owed to whom. *And to one who does not work,* he says? Take some ungodly sinner, who does not work. What of it? Yet he believes in him who justifies the ungodly. For from the fact that he does not work well, he is ungodly: even if he seems to do good things, still, because they are without faith, they are not to be called good. *And to one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness; just as David also speaks of the blessedness of the man to whom God reckons righteousness apart from works*[^19]. But what righteousness? The righteousness of faith, which good works did not precede, but which good works follow.

**8.** Pay attention, then; otherwise, by understanding badly, you fling yourselves into that abyss of sinning with impunity. And I am free, just as the Apostle himself was free of all who understood him badly. For they were glad to understand him badly, so that good works would not follow. Do not be of their number, brothers. It is said in a certain psalm, about a man of this kind—that is, about this whole class of men, as though about one man: *He refused to understand so as to act well*[^20]. It is not said: he could not understand. So you must be willing to understand, in order to act well. For a clear understanding will not be lacking to you. What is the clear understanding? Let no one vaunt his good works before faith; let no one be lazy in good works once faith has been received. God therefore gives pardon to all the ungodly, and justifies them by faith.

**9.** [vv. 1-2] *Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord imputes no sin, and in whose mouth there is no guile.* Now the psalm begins, and the understanding begins. The understanding, then, or the very insight, is this: that you should know you ought neither to boast of your merits nor presume upon impunity for sin. For the title of the psalm runs thus: *For David himself, of understanding.* This psalm is called one "of understanding." The first understanding, then, is to know yourself a sinner. The consequent understanding is that, when by faith working through love you have begun to act well, you should attribute this not to your own powers but to the grace of God. So there will be no guile in your heart, that is, in your inner mouth; nor will you have one thing on your lips and another in your thought. You will not be of those Pharisees of whom it is said: *You are like whitewashed tombs; outwardly you appear righteous to men, but within you are full of guile and iniquity*[^21]. Whoever, then, being wicked, presents himself as righteous, is he not full of guile? Such a one is not the Nathanael of whom the Lord says: *Behold, a true Israelite, in whom there is no guile*[^22]. But how was there no guile in that Nathanael? *When you were under the fig tree,* he says, *I saw you*[^23]. He was under the fig tree, he was under the condition of the flesh. If he was under the condition of the flesh, it was because he was held by the ungodliness of his descent; he was under that fig tree in which there is groaning in another psalm: *Behold, I was conceived in iniquity*[^24]. But he saw him, he who came with grace. What is it, he saw him? He had mercy on him. So he commends a man without guile in such a way that he commends his own grace in him. *When you were under the fig tree, I saw you. I saw you*—what is great in this, unless you understand it as said in some special way? What is great about seeing a man under a fig tree? If Christ had not seen the human race under that fig tree, either we would have withered utterly, or, like the Pharisees in whom there was guile—that is, who justified themselves with words but were evil in their deeds—only leaves would be found in us, not fruit. For when Christ saw such a fig tree, he cursed it, and it withered[^25]. I see, he says, only leaves, that is, only words, without fruit: *Let it wither,* he says, that it may not even have leaves. And why does he take away even the words? Because a dry tree cannot even have leaves. So the Jews, the Pharisees, were that tree: they had words, they did not have deeds; by the Lord's sentence they earned their withering. Let Christ, then, see us under the fig tree; let him see in our flesh also the fruit of good work, lest by his curse we wither. And because the whole is credited to his grace, not to our merits: *Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered*—not those in whom no sins have been found, but those whose sins are covered. The sins are covered over, hidden, abolished. If God has covered sins, he willed not to look upon them; if he willed not to look upon them, he willed not to take notice of them; if he willed not to take notice of them, he willed not to punish them; if he willed not to punish them, he willed not to recognize them, he chose rather to pardon. *Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered.* Do not so understand what he said—that sins are covered over—as though they were there and alive. Why, then, did he say the sins are covered? So that they might not be seen. For what was it for God to see sins, except to punish sins? That you may know that God's seeing sins is the same as punishing sins, what is said to him? *Turn your face from my sins*[^26]. Let him, then, not see your sins, that he may see you. How will he see you? As he saw Nathanael: *When you were under the fig tree, I saw you.* The shadow of the fig tree did not hinder the eyes of God's mercy.

**10.** *And in whose mouth there is no guile.* But those who refuse to confess their sins labor without cause in defending their sins. And the more they labor in defending their sins, vaunting their merits and not seeing their iniquities, the more does their strength and vigor fail. For he is strong who is strong not in himself but in God. As it says: *Three times I begged the Lord to take it from me; and he said to me: My grace is sufficient for you. My grace,* he said; not: your power. *My grace is sufficient for you,* he says, *for power is made perfect in weakness*[^27]. Hence in another place he himself says: *When I am weak, then I am strong*[^28]. So whoever wishes to be strong as though presuming upon himself and vaunting his merits, of whatever sort they may be, will be like that Pharisee who proudly vaunted even what he claimed to have received from God: *Thanks,* he said, *to you.* Notice, my brothers, what sort of pride God here marks out: truly the sort that can creep into a righteous man, the sort that can steal up even on a man of good hope. *Thanks to you,* he was saying. So when he said: *Thanks to you,* he was acknowledging that he had received from him what he had. For what do you have that you did not receive? So: *I give you thanks,* he said; *I give you thanks that I am not like the rest of men, robbers, unjust, adulterers, like this tax collector too.*[^29] How, then, was he proud? Not because he gave thanks to God for his goods, but because over another man he exalted himself on the basis of those very goods.

**11.** Notice, brothers: for the Evangelist set down beforehand what prompted the Lord to begin telling this very parable. For when Christ had said: *Do you think the Son of man, when he comes, will find faith on earth?*[^30]—and lest there arise certain heretics who, observing and supposing that the whole world had as it were fallen (for all heretics are few and partial), should vaunt themselves that what perished from the whole world remained in them—there at once, where the Lord says: *Do you think the Son of man, when he comes, will find faith on earth?* the Evangelist added and said: *He told this parable also to some who trusted that they were righteous and despised the rest: A certain Pharisee and a tax collector went into the temple to pray,*[^31] and the rest that you know. That Pharisee, then, was saying: *I give you thanks.* But how was he proud? Because he despised the rest. How do you prove it? From his very words. How? The Pharisee, it says, despised the man standing far off, to whom God, as he confessed, was drawing near. *The tax collector,* it says, *stood far off*[^32]: but did God not stand far off from him? Why did God not stand far off from him? Because elsewhere it is said: *The Lord is near to those who are broken in heart*[^33]. See whether this tax collector had broken his heart, and there you will see that the Lord is near to those who are broken in heart. *But the tax collector stood far off, and would not even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast.* The beating of the breast, the breaking of the heart. What did he say as he beat his breast? *God, be merciful to me, a sinner.*[^34] And what was the Lord's verdict? *Amen I say to you, that tax collector went down to his house justified rather than the Pharisee.*[^35] Why? This is the judgment of God. *I am not like this tax collector, I am not like the rest of men, unjust, robbers, adulterers: I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.*[^36] The other does not dare to lift his eyes to heaven, he attends to his own conscience, he stands far off, and he is justified rather than that Pharisee. Why? I ask you, Lord, expound to us this righteousness of yours, expound to us the equity of your law. God expounds the rule of his law. Do you wish to hear why? *Because everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted*[^37].

**12.** Let your Charity attend, then. We said that the tax collector did not dare to lift his eyes to heaven. Why did he not attend to heaven? Because he attended to himself. He attended to himself, so that he might first be displeasing to himself, and so be pleasing to God. But you vaunt yourself, you stiffen your neck. The Lord says to the proud man: You do not wish to attend to yourself? I attend. But do you wish me not to attend? Then attend to yourself. For this reason the tax collector did not dare to lift his eyes to heaven; because he gazed upon himself, he punished his own conscience: he was his own judge, so that the other might intercede; he punished himself, so that the other might set him free; he accused himself, so that the other might defend him. Indeed the Lord defended him, for he pronounced sentence on his behalf. *That tax collector went down justified rather than the Pharisee: because everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted.* He attended to himself, he says, and I was unwilling to attend; I heard the one saying: *Turn your eyes from my sins.* For who is it that said that, except the one who also said: *For I acknowledge my iniquity*[^38]? And so, my brothers, that Pharisee too was a sinner. For it was not because he said: *I am not like the rest of men, unjust, robbers, adulterers,* nor because he fasted twice in the week, nor because he gave tithes, that he was not a sinner. For even if he were without any sins, that very pride was a great crime: and yet, behold, he was saying all those things. Who, after all, is without sin? Who will boast that he has a chaste heart, or who will boast that he is clean from sins? So that man had sins; but, perverse and not knowing where he had come, he was as it were standing at the physician's station to be healed, and he was showing his sound limbs and hiding his wounds. Let God cover your wounds; do not you. For if, in shame, you wish to cover them, the physician will not heal them. Let the physician cover them and heal them; for he covers with a plaster. Under the physician's covering the wound is healed; under the covering of the wounded man the wound is concealed. From whom do you conceal it? From him who knows all things.

**13.** [vv. 3-6] And so here, brothers, see what he said: *Because I kept silence, my bones grew old, while I cried out all the day long.* What is this? It seems as it were contradictory: *Because I kept silence, my bones grew old, while I cried out.* If from crying out, how did he keep silence? He kept silence about one thing, he did not keep silence about another: he kept silence about what would have profited him, he did not keep silence about what brought him loss; he kept silence about confession, he cried out his presumption. *I kept silence,* he says, that is, I did not confess. There it behooved him to speak; let him keep silence about his merits, let him cry out his sins: but as it was, he perversely kept silence about his sins, he cried out his merits. And what happened to him? His bones grew old. Note that if he had cried out his sins and kept silence about his merits, his bones—that is, his virtues—would be renewed: he would be vigorous in the Lord, because he would be found weak in himself. But now, because he willed to be firm in himself, he was made weak, and his bones grew old. He remained in oldness, who would not by confessing love newness. For those who become new, you know, brothers: for *blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered.* This man would not have his iniquities forgiven him; he exaggerated them, he defended them, he vaunted his merits. So, because he kept silence from confession, his bones grew old. *While I cried out all the day long.* What is: *while I cried out all the day long?* Persevering in the defense of his sins. And yet see what sort he is, for he acknowledges himself. For now there will be understanding: he will look upon nothing but himself, and he will be displeasing to himself, because he acknowledges himself. Now you will hear, so that you may be healed.

**14.** *Blessed is the man to whom the Lord imputes no sin, and in whose mouth there is no guile. Because I kept silence, my bones grew old, while I cried out all the day long. Because day and night your hand was heavy upon me.* What is, *your hand was heavy upon me?* A great matter, brothers. Consider that right sentence between the two, the Pharisee and the tax collector. What was said of the Pharisee? That he is humbled. What was said of the tax collector? That he is exalted. Why is the one humbled? Because he exalted himself. Why is the other exalted? Because he humbled himself. So, that God may humble the one who exalts himself, he lays his hand heavy upon him. The man would not be humbled by confession of his iniquity; he was humbled by the weight of God's hand. When would that man have borne the heavy hand of the one who humbles? How light was the hand of the one who lifts up! In the one he is strong, and in the other he is strong: strong to press him down, strong to lift him up.

**15.** So, *Because day and night your hand was heavy upon me: I was turned in my distress, while the thorn was driven in.* By that very weight of your hand, by that very humbling, I was turned in my distress, I was made wretched, the thorn was driven into me, my conscience was pricked. What happened when the thorn was driven in? A sense of pain was given him, he found his own weakness. And he who had kept silence about confession of his sin, so that by crying out in defense of his sin his vigor—that is, his bones—might grow old and be turned to oldness, what did he do now that the thorn was driven in? *I have acknowledged my sin.* So now he acknowledges. If he acknowledges, the other pardons. Hear what follows; see whether he does not himself say: *I have acknowledged my sin, and my injustice I have not covered up.* This is what I was saying a while ago: Do not you cover it, and God covers it. *Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered.* Those who cover their sins are laid bare: but this man laid bare, so that he might be covered over. *My iniquity I have not covered up.* What is, *I have not covered up?* A while ago I had kept silence: now what? *I said.* Something contrary to that silence. *I said.* What did you say? *I will declare against myself my injustice to the Lord; and you forgave the ungodliness of my heart. I said.* What did you say? He does not yet declare, he promises that he will declare; and the other already forgives. Notice, brothers; a great matter: he said: *I will declare*: he did not say: I have declared, and you forgave; he said: *I will declare, and you forgave*: because by the very fact that he said: *I will declare,* he showed that he had not yet declared with his mouth, but had declared in his heart. This very saying, *I will declare,* is itself to declare: therefore *you forgave the ungodliness of my heart.* My confession, then, had not yet come to my mouth; for I had said: *I will declare against myself*: nevertheless God heard the voice of my heart. My voice was not yet in my mouth, but God's ear was already in my heart. *You forgave the ungodliness of my heart;* because I said: *I will declare.*

**16.** But this was not enough: he did not say: *I will declare my injustice to the Lord*: not without cause did he say: *I will declare against myself*: and this makes a difference. For many declare their iniquity, but against the Lord God himself: when they are found in sins, they say, God willed this. For if a man says: I did not do it; or: this deed of which you accuse me is not a sin: he declares neither against himself nor against God. If he says: I did it outright, and it is a sin, but God willed this, so what did I do? this is to declare against God. Perhaps you say: No one says this; who is there who says: God willed this? Many even say this: but those who do not say this, what else do they say who say: Fate did it to me, my stars did it? Thus they wish to arrive at God by a roundabout way. By a roundabout way they wish to arrive at accusing God, those who refuse to come by the short way to placating God, and they say: Fate did it to me. What is fate? My stars did it. What are the stars? Surely those we behold in the sky. And who made them? God. Who ordered them? God. So you see that what you meant to say was: God made me sin. Thus he is unjust, you are just: because unless he had done it, you would not have sinned. Away with these excuses in sins: remember that psalm: *Incline not my heart to evil words, to make excuses in sins, with men who work iniquity.* But they are great men who defend their sins; great too are those who count the stars and reckon the constellations and the seasons, and say who sins or lives well, and when, and when Mars makes a man a murderer, and Venus an adulteress: great, learned men, and they seem chosen in this world. But what does it say in the psalm? *Incline not my heart to evil words, with men who work iniquity, and I will not share in the things they choose*[^39]. Let them call those counters of the stars chosen and learned, let them call those wise who as it were sift human destinies on their fingers and trace human characters from the stars. God created me with free choice: if I sinned, I sinned; so that I declare my iniquity not only to the Lord, but against myself, not against him. *I said: Lord, have mercy on me*: the sick man cries to the physician: *I said.* Why: *I said?* It would have sufficed: *I said: I* is said with emphasis; *I,* I, not fate, not fortune, not the devil; for not even he forced me, but I consented to him as he persuaded: *I said: Lord, have mercy on me, heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^40]. So here too he resolved and proposed: *I said: I will declare against myself my iniquity to the Lord, and you remitted the ungodliness of my heart.*

**17.** [v. 6] *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time.* At what time? *For this.* For what this? For ungodliness. For what? For the very pardon of sins. *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time.* Therefore every holy one will pray to you, because you have forgiven sins. For if you did not forgive sins, there would be no holy one to pray to you: *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time*: when the New Testament will be made manifest, when the grace of Christ will be made manifest, which is the fitting time. *But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman,* that is, of a female (for the ancients used this word indifferently), *made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law*[^41]. From what would he redeem them? From the devil, from perdition, from their sins, from him to whom they had sold themselves. *That he might redeem those who were under the Law.* For they were under the Law, because the Law pressed upon them. Their condition pressed upon them, convicting them of guilt, not saving them. And indeed it forbade evils: but because they did not have the powers to justify themselves by themselves, they had to cry out to him, as one cried out who was being led captive under the law of sin: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^42] All men were under the Law, not in the Law: the Law now pressing them down, now convicting them of guilt. For the Law showed sin: it drove in the thorn, it made the heart be pricked; it itself admonished each to recognize himself guilty, and to cry out to God for pardon. *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time.* So I was speaking of the fitting time: *When the fullness of time came, God sent his Son.* Likewise the Apostle says: *In an acceptable and pleasing time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you.* And because this had been foretold of all Christians by the Prophet, the Apostle added: *Behold, now is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation*[^43]. *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time.*

**18.** *Nevertheless, in a flood of many waters they shall not draw near to him. To him*: to whom? To God. For he is wont to change the person: as in: *Salvation is the Lord's, and your blessing is upon your people*[^44]. He did not say, Salvation is the Lord's, and his blessing is upon his people; nor, Lord, salvation is yours, and your blessing is upon your people: but when he had begun: *Salvation is the Lord's,* speaking not to him but about him, there he turned to him and said: *And your blessing is upon your people.* So here too, when you hear first *to you,* then *to him,* do not suppose it is another; *For this every holy one will pray to you in a fitting time. Nevertheless, in a flood of many waters they shall not draw near to him.* What is, *in a flood of many waters?* Those who swim in a flood of many waters do not draw near to God. What is the flood of many waters? The multiplicity of various teachings. Attend, brothers. The many waters are the various teachings. The teaching of God is one; they are not many waters, but one water, whether of the sacrament of baptism, or of the saving teaching. Of that very teaching by which we are watered through the Holy Spirit it is said: *Drink water from your own vessels, and from the springs of your own wells*[^45]. To these springs the ungodly do not come, but those who believe in him who justifies the ungodly, now justified, come. The other waters, the many waters, the many teachings, defile the souls of men, as I was saying a little while ago. One teaching is: Fate did it to me. Another teaching: Chance did it to me, fortune did it. If men are governed by chances, nothing is done by any providence: and this too is a teaching. Another has said: There is a contrary race of darkness, which rebelled against God; this makes men sin. In this flood of many waters they shall not draw near to God. What is that water, that true water which flows from the inmost spring of the pure vein of truth? What is that water, brothers, except that which teaches us to confess to the Lord? What is that water, except that which teaches: *It is good to confess to the Lord*[^46]? What is that water, except that which teaches this saying: *I said: I will declare against myself my injustice to the Lord*: and: *I said: Lord, have mercy on me, heal my soul, for I have sinned against you?* This water of confession of sins, this water of the heart's humbling, this water of saving life, of one who casts himself down, presumes nothing of himself, proudly attributes nothing to his own power. This water is in none of the books of strangers, not among the Epicureans, not among the Stoics, not among the Manichees, not among the Platonists. Wherever even the best precepts of morals and discipline are found, still this humility is not found. The way of this humility flows from elsewhere: it comes from Christ. This way is from him who, though he was on high, came humble. For what else did he teach by humbling himself, made obedient unto death, even death on a cross? What else did he teach by paying what he did not owe, that he might free us from our debt? What else did he teach, baptized though he committed no sin, crucified though he had no guilt? What else did he teach but this humility? Not without reason does he say: *I am the way and the truth and the life*[^47]. In this humility, then, one draws near to God, because the Lord is near to those who are broken in heart. But in the flood of many waters of those who exalt themselves against God and teach proud ungodliness, they shall not draw near to God.

**19.** [v. 7] But what of you, who, even though you have been justified, are in the midst of those waters? On every side, my brothers, even when we confess our sins, those waters of the flood roar around us. We are not in the flood itself, but we are surrounded by the flood. They press us, but they do not overwhelm us; they urge against us, but they do not sink us. What, then, will you do, since you are in the midst of the flood, walking in this world? For does not such a one hear such teachers, does he not hear such proud men, or does he not suffer in his heart daily persecutions from their words? What, then, shall this man say, already justified and presuming upon God, who is surrounded by this flood? *You are my refuge from the distress that has surrounded me.* Let those others flee for refuge, whether to their gods, or to their demons, or to their own powers, or to the defense of their sins: for me, in this flood, there is no refuge except you, from the distress that has surrounded me.

**20.** *My exultation, redeem me.* If you already exult, why do you wish to be redeemed? *My exultation, redeem me.* I hear a voice of joy: *My exultation*: I hear a groan: *Redeem me.* You rejoice, and you groan. Yes, he says, I both rejoice and groan: I rejoice in hope, I still groan in the reality. *My exultation, redeem me. Rejoicing in hope,* says the Apostle. So rightly: *My exultation, redeem me.* From where, *redeem me?* It follows: *patient in tribulation*[^48]. *My exultation, redeem me.* Even the Apostle was already justified: and what does he say? *Not only that,* he says, *but we ourselves also, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves.* From where, *redeem me?* Because *we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body.* Behold, then, from where, *redeem me*; because we still await, groaning within ourselves, the redemption of our body. From where, then, *my exultation?* The same Apostle follows on and says: *For in hope we were saved: but hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it with patience*[^49]. If you hope, you rejoice; if you await with patience, you still groan: for there is no need of patience where you suffer no evil. What is called endurance, what is called patience, what is called sufferance, what is called long-suffering, exists only in evils. Where you are pressed, there is anguish. So if we await with patience, we still say: Redeem me from the distress that has surrounded me; but because in hope we were saved, we say both at once: *My exultation, redeem me.*

**21.** [v. 8] The answer is given: *I will give you understanding.* This is the psalm of understanding. *I will give you understanding, and I will set you in this way in which you shall walk.* What is, *I will set you in this way in which you shall walk?* Not that you should be stuck there, but that you should not stray from it. I will give you understanding, that you may always know yourself, and always rejoice in hope toward God; until you come to that homeland where there will no longer be hope, but the reality. *I will fix my eyes upon you*: I will not take my eyes from you, because you too will not take your eyes from me. Now that you are justified, now, after the remission of sins, lift up your eyes to God. For your heart had rotted while it was on the earth. Not for nothing do you hear: Lift up your heart, lest it rot. So you too now lift up your eyes always to God, that he may fix his eyes upon you. But do you fear that, while you have your eyes on God, you may stumble, may not look in front of you, and perhaps run into a snare? Do not fear: for there are his eyes, which he fixes upon you; *Be not anxious,* he says; and the apostle Peter: *Cast all your anxiety upon him, for he cares for you*[^50]. So *I will fix my eyes upon you.* Do you, then, raise your eyes to him, and you will not fear, as I said, lest you run into a snare. Hear another psalm: *My eyes are ever toward the Lord.* And as if it were said to him: What of your feet, when you do not look in front of you? *For he,* he says, *will pluck my feet out of the snare*[^51]. *I will fix my eyes upon you.*

**22.** [v. 9] He has promised this man both understanding and his protection: he turns to the proud who defend their sins, and shows us what understanding is: *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding.* The horse and the mule are stiff-necked. They are not like that ox which knew its owner, or the ass that knew its master's manger[^52]. *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding.* For what do such suffer? *With bit and bridle bind fast the jaws of those who do not draw near to you.* Do you wish to be a horse and a mule, do you wish to have no rider? Your mouth and your jaws will be bound with bit and bridle: that very mouth of yours will be bound, with which you vaunt your merits and excuse your sins. *Bind fast the jaws of those who do not draw near to you* by humbling themselves.

**23.** [v. 10] *Many are the scourges of the sinner.* It is no wonder if, once the bridle is applied, scourges follow. For the animal wished to be untamed; it is tamed by bridle and scourge: and would that it might be thoroughly tamed! For it is to be feared that, by resisting too much, it may deserve to be left untamed and to go into its own wandering license, so that it may be said of it: *Their iniquity will come forth as it were from fatness*[^53]: as of those whose sins are now unpunished. So when it is scourged, let it be corrected, let it be tamed; for this man too declared himself thus tamed. He had called himself a horse and a mule, because he kept silence: but how was he tamed? By scourges. *I was turned,* he says, *in my distress, while the thorn was driven in.* Whether you call them scourges, or call them goads, God tames the beast on which he sits: for it is to the beast's advantage to be sat upon. For God does not, wearied with walking on foot, sit upon a beast. Or is it not full of mystery, that a young ass was led to the Lord[^54]? A gentle and meek people, bearing the Lord well, is the young ass, and it makes for Jerusalem. *For he will guide the meek in judgment,* as another psalm says, *he will teach the gentle his ways*[^55]. What gentle ones? Those who do not raise their neck against their tamer, who endure scourges and bridle; afterward so tamed that they walk without scourge, and keep the way without bit and bridle. If you are without this rider, you will fall, not he. *Many are the scourges of the sinner: but mercy will surround the one who hopes in the Lord.* How is he a refuge from distress? The one whom distress first surrounds, mercy afterward surrounds: for he who gave the law will give mercy; the law in scourges, the mercy in consolations. *But mercy will surround the one who hopes in the Lord.*

**24.** [v. 11] So what is the conclusion? *Be glad in the Lord, and exult, you righteous.* O you who are glad in yourselves! O you ungodly, O you proud, who are glad in yourselves: now, believing in him who justifies the ungodly, let your faith be reckoned to you as righteousness. *Be glad in the Lord, and exult, you righteous. And exult,* understand: in the Lord. Why? Because you are now righteous. From where, righteous? Not from your own merits, but from his grace. From where, righteous? Because you have been justified.

**25.** *And glory, all you upright of heart.* What is, *upright of heart?* Those who do not resist God. Let your Charity attend, and understand the upright heart. I say it briefly, but it is most of all to be commended: and—thanks be to God—since it comes at the end, let it cling to your minds. Between an upright heart and a crooked heart this is the difference: whoever, in whatever he suffers against his will—afflictions, sorrows, labors, humiliations—attributes it only to the just will of God, not charging him with foolishness, as though he did not know what he does when he scourges such a one and spares such others; this man is upright of heart. But those are perverse of heart, crooked and twisted, who say that all the evils they suffer they suffer unjustly, charging with iniquity him by whose will they suffer; or, because they do not dare to charge him with iniquity, they take away from him his governance. For he, they say, cannot do anything unjust; but it is unjust that I should suffer and he not suffer; I grant that I am a sinner, but surely there are worse men who rejoice while I am afflicted: so, because this is unjust, that even worse men than I should rejoice while I, who am either just or less a sinner than they, am afflicted, and it is certain to me that this is unjust, and it is certain to me that God does not do what is unjust; God does not govern human affairs, nor does he care for us. So the crooked of heart—that is, the twisted of heart—have three opinions. Either: There is no God: for the fool said in his heart, There is no God[^56]. And it was said of that flood: such a teaching of philosophers was not wanting, there were not wanting those who said that there is no God who governs all and founded all; but that there are many gods at leisure to themselves apart from the world, not caring for these things. So either: There is no God: this the ungodly man says, to whom whatever befalls him against his will is displeasing, while it does not befall another whom he sets above himself: or: God is unjust, since these things please him and he does them: or: God does not govern human affairs, nor does he care for all. In these three opinions there is great ungodliness: either to deny God, or to call him unjust, or to take from him the governance of things. Why this? Because he is twisted of heart. God is upright, and therefore the twisted heart does not accord with him. As it is in another psalm: *How good is the God of Israel to the upright of heart!* And because he himself once held such an opinion: *How does God know, or is there knowledge in the Most High?* therefore he added there: *But my feet had almost slipped*[^57]. Just as a twisted board, even if you place it on a level floor, is not set firm, is not joined, does not fit, but is always shifting and wobbling—not because the place where you put it is uneven, but because what you put there is twisted: so too your heart, as long as it is crooked and twisted, cannot be aligned to the uprightness of God, and cannot be set firm in him so as to cling to him and become *one spirit* with him, for *he who clings to the Lord is one spirit*[^58]. Therefore he said, *glory, you upright of heart.* How do the upright of heart glory? Hear their glorying: *Not only that,* says the Apostle, *but we also glory in tribulations.* For it is no great thing to glory in joys, to glory in gladness: the upright of heart glory even in tribulation. And hear how he glories in tribulation; for no one does so in vain, no one to no purpose: see the upright heart: *knowing,* he says, *that tribulation works patience, and patience approval, and approval hope, and hope does not disappoint, because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^59].

**26.** So this is the upright heart, brothers. To whomever anything befalls, let him say: The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away. Behold the upright heart: As it pleased the Lord, so has it been done: blessed be the name of the Lord[^60]. Who took it away? What did he take away? From whom did he take it away? When did he take it away? Blessed be the name of the Lord. And he did not say, The Lord gave, the devil took away. Let your Charity attend, then, lest perhaps you say: The devil did this to me. Refer your scourge wholly to your God, for the devil himself does nothing to you unless he permits it who has power from above, either for punishment or for discipline: for punishment of the ungodly, for discipline of the son. *For he scourges every son whom he receives*[^61]. Do not hope to be without a scourge, unless perhaps you think to be disinherited. He scourges every son whom he receives. Every one? Where did you wish to hide yourself? Every one: and none excepted, none will be without a scourge. What? Every one? Do you wish to hear how every one? Even the Only-begotten, without sin, yet not without a scourge. Hence the Only-begotten himself, bearing your weakness and prefiguring your person in himself, as the head bearing the person also of his body, when he drew near to his passion, was made sorrowful from the manhood he bore, that he might gladden you; he was made sorrowful, that he might console you. For the Lord could surely have been without sorrow, about to go to his passion. If the soldier could, could not the commander? How could the soldier? Consider Paul exulting as he drew near to his passion: *For I am already,* he says, *being poured out as a libation, and the time of my dissolution is at hand. I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith: henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord will render to me on that day, the just judge. And not to me only, but to all who love his coming*[^62]. See how he exults, about to come to his passion. So the one to be crowned rejoices; the one who is to crown is made sorrowful. What, then, was he bearing? The weakness of certain ones who, when tribulation or death comes, are made sorrowful. But see how he leads them into uprightness of heart. Behold, you wished to live, you did not wish anything to befall you; but God willed otherwise: there are two wills; but let your will be corrected to the will of God, let not the will of God be twisted to yours. For yours is crooked, his is the rule: let the rule stand, that what is crooked may be corrected to the rule. See how the Lord Jesus Christ teaches this: *My soul is sorrowful unto death*: and: *Father, if it can be, let this cup pass from me.* Behold, he shows the human will. But see the upright heart: *Yet not what I will, but what you will, Father*[^63]. Do this, then, rejoicing in the things that befall you: and if that last day comes, rejoice. Or if some frailty of human will creeps in, let it be quickly set straight toward God; that you may be among those to whom it is said: *Glory, all you upright of heart.*
