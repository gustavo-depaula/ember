# On the Same Psalm 118 (28)

**1.** [v. 145.] The cry to the Lord which is made by those who pray, if it is made by the sound of the bodily voice, with the heart not intent on God, who doubts that it is made in vain? But if it is made by the heart, even when the bodily voice is silent, it can be hidden from any other man, but not from God. Whether, then, with the voice of the flesh, when there is need of that, or with silence, we must cry to God with the heart when we pray. But the cry of the heart is a great intentness of thought; which, when it is in prayer, expresses a great feeling of the one desiring and asking, so that he does not despair of the effect. And then, moreover, it is cried with the whole heart, when nothing else is thought about. Such prayers are rare with many, but frequent with few; whether they are present in anyone always, I do not know. He who sings this psalm mentions such a prayer of his own, saying: *I have cried with my whole heart; hear me, O Lord.* But to what end his cry profits, he added: *I will search out your statutes.* For this, then, he cried with his whole heart, and this he desired to be granted him by the Lord who hears, that he may search out his statutes. Accordingly, what we are bidden to do, it is prayed that we may search out. How far still from the doer is one who searches out? For it does not follow that one who searches out finds; or that one who finds does. But he cannot do unless he has found; and cannot find unless he has sought. But the Lord Jesus gave great hope by saying, *Seek, and you will find*[^1]. But again Wisdom says (and what is it, except he himself?): *The evil will seek me, and not find me*[^2]. Not to the evil, then, but to the good was it said, *Seek, and you will find.* Indeed it was said to those to whom, in the same place a little later, *If you then,* he says, *being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children*[^3]. How, then, is it said to the evil, *Seek, and you will find;* when again it is said, *The evil will seek me, and not find me?* Or did the Lord wish them to seek something other than wisdom, when he promised that, if they sought, they would find? For in it are all the things that are to be sought by those who desire to be blessed. There, then, are also the statutes of God. Wherefore it remains that we understand, not that all the evil do not find wisdom, if they seek it; but those who are so evil that they hate it. For so he said: *The evil will seek me, and not find me; for they have hated wisdom*[^4]. Therefore they do not find, because they hate. But again, if they hate, how do they seek; unless because they do not seek it for its own sake, but for the sake of something which the evil love, and to which they think they will the more easily arrive through it? For there are many who most studiously search out the sayings of wisdom, and wish to have it in doctrine, not in life; so that not through the morals which wisdom commands they may arrive at the light of God, which is wisdom itself, but through the discourses which wisdom has they may arrive at the praise of men, which is vainglory. They do not, then, seek wisdom even when they seek it; because they do not seek it itself, otherwise they would live according to it: but they wish to be puffed up by its words; and the more they are puffed up, the more they are made outside it. But this man, asking of the Lord the very thing which the Lord bids him do, that he himself may work in him even what he commands—for it is God who works in us both to will and to work, for his good pleasure: *I have cried,* he says, *with my whole heart; hear me, O Lord: I will search out your statutes;* to be done, surely, not only to be known; lest he become like that hard servant, who, even if he understand, will not obey.

**2.** [v. 146.] *I have cried; save me:* or, as some manuscripts have, both Greek and Latin, *I have cried to you; save me.* What is, *I have cried to you,* except, by crying I have called upon you? But when he had said, *save me,* what did he add? *And I will keep your testimonies;* lest, namely, through weakness I deny you. For the salvation of the soul brings it about that what is known to be done is done, and that, even to the death of the body, if the last temptation demands this, one strives for the truth of the divine testimonies: but where there is no salvation, weakness succumbs, and the truth is forsaken.

**3.** [v. 147.] But what follows has some obscurity, which on this occasion is to be expounded at greater length. *I anticipated in the dead of night, and cried out.* More manuscripts do not have *in the dead of night,* but *in immaturity;* scarcely one was found that had the preposition doubled, that is, *in immaturity.* "Immaturity," then, in this place is the nocturnal time, which is not mature—that is, not opportune for anything to be done by keeping watch: which is also commonly wont to be called an inopportune hour. The "dead" of night too—that is, the middle, when one ought to rest—is without doubt so named because it is inopportune for the actions of those who keep watch. For the ancients called what is opportune "seasonable" (tempestivum), and what is inopportune "unseasonable" (intempestivum), the word being drawn from "time" (tempus), not from that "storm" (tempestas) which, by the usage of the Latin tongue, is now called a disturbance of the sky. Although historians gladly use this word, so as to say "at that storm" (ea tempestate), where they wish "at that time" to be understood: and what the excellent speaker said, *Whence comes this sudden spell of such clear weather?*, he signified by this name a sky not disturbed by rain-clouds or winds, but rather shining with a sudden and splendid serenity[^5]. So what was said in Greek—not in one word, but in two, that is, a preposition and a noun—some of our interpreters rendered *in the dead of night,* more *in immaturity,* not in two words but in one, of which word the nominative is "immaturity" (immaturitas); but some in two words, as the Greek put it, *in immaturity;* for "immaturity" is the Greek noun, *in immaturity* the phrase: as though even he who said *in the dead of night* wished to say it with the preposition doubled, *in the dead;* so that one preposition signifies in what hour, the other pertains to the composition of the noun. It makes no difference at all to the sense whether one says he did something "at cockcrow," or "at the cockcrow": so it would make no difference whether he said he cried *in the dead,* or *in the dead of night,* that is, *in the night's dead hour.* Yet the Greek said *in the dead of night,* which means the same as if it were said *in immaturity,* that is, in the immature nocturnal time. So far the obscure word has been discussed; now let us see what the sense itself is.

**4.** *I anticipated,* he says, *in the dead of night, and cried out; in your words I have hoped.* If we refer this to each one of the faithful, and to the literal fact, it often happens that at such a time of night the love of God keeps watch, and, a great feeling of prayer urging, the time of praying that is wont to be after cockcrow is not awaited but anticipated. But if we take this whole age as night; surely in the dead of night we cry to God, and anticipate the maturity of the time in which he will render us what he promised, as elsewhere it is read: *Let us anticipate his face in confession*[^6]. Although, if we wish to understand the immature time of this night, before the fullness of time had come—that is, the very maturity, when Christ would be manifested in the flesh—not even then was the Church silent, but, anticipating that maturity, it cried out by prophesying, and hoped in the words of God, who is able to do what he promised, that in the seed of Abraham all nations might be blessed.

**5.** [v. 148.] It says also what follows: *My eyes anticipated the morning, that I might meditate on your utterances.* For let us set the morning as when, to those who sat in the shadow of death, the light arose for them; did not, in the saints who were formerly on earth, the eyes of the Church anticipate this morning, because they foresaw beforehand that this would come about, that they might meditate on the utterances of God, which then existed, and in the Law and the Prophets announced these things to come?

**6.** [v. 149.] *Hear my voice,* he says, *O Lord, according to your mercy: and according to your judgment give me life.* For first God, according to mercy, takes away the penalty from sinners, and afterward, to them now made righteous, according to judgment will give life; for not in vain is it said to him in this order, *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^7]: although the time of mercy itself is not without judgment, of which the Apostle says, *For if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged by the Lord: but when we are judged, we are corrected by the Lord, that we may not be condemned with the world*[^8]; and his fellow apostle, *It is time,* he says, *that judgment begin at the house of the Lord: and if it begins with us, what will be the end of those who do not believe the Gospel of the Lord*[^9]? And the last time of judgment will not be without mercy, because *he crowns you,* the psalm says, *in pity and mercy*[^10]. But judgment without mercy will be for those on the left who did not show mercy[^11].

**7.** [v. 150.] *They have drawn near,* he says, *who persecute me with iniquity;* or, as some manuscripts have, *unjustly.* Then those who persecute draw near, when they reach even to torturing and destroying the flesh. Whence the twenty-first psalm, where the Lord's passion is prophesied: *Do not depart,* it says, *from me, for tribulation is near*[^12]; though those things are said which he suffered with the passion itself not threatening, but now present. But he called near the tribulation which was happening in the flesh; for nothing is nearer to the soul than the flesh it bears. They drew near, then, those who persecute, by afflicting the flesh of those whom they persecuted. But attend to what follows: *But from your law they have been made far.* The more they drew near to persecuting the righteous, the more they were made far from righteousness. But what did they harm those to whom, by persecuting, they drew near; when the nearness of their Lord is more inward, by whom they are in no way forsaken?

**8.** [v. 151.] Finally there follows: *You are near, O Lord, and all your ways are truth.* Even in their tribulations, to attribute truth to God—that they do not undeservedly suffer them—is the accustomed confession of the saints. So queen Esther, so holy Daniel, so the three men in the furnace, so others, companions of their holiness, confess. But it can be asked how it is here said, *All your ways are truth;* when in another psalm it is read, *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^13]. But toward the saints both all the ways of the Lord are mercy, and all the ways of the Lord are truth: because both in judging he comes to aid, and so mercy is not lacking; and in showing mercy he exhibits what he promised, that truth be not lacking. But toward all, both those whom he sets free and those whom he condemns, all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth; because where he does not show mercy, the truth of vengeance is exhibited. For he sets free many who are undeserving, but condemns no one who is undeserving.

**9.** [v. 152.] *From the beginning I have known,* he says, *concerning your testimonies, that you have founded them for ever.* What the Greek says, some of ours interpreted *from the beginning,* some *at the beginning,* some *in the beginnings.* But those who preferred to say this in the plural followed the Greek expression. But to the Latin tongue that is rather usual, that *from the beginning* or *at the beginning* be said, which in Greek is said as though plurally, but adverbially: as is the case with us when we say "otherwise" (alias) "I do this"; we seem to say the plural number of the feminine gender, but it is an adverb, and signifies "at another time." What is it, then: *From the beginning I have known,* or rather, that we too may say it adverbially, *At the beginning I have known concerning your testimonies, that you have founded them for ever?* He says that the testimonies of the Lord were founded by him for ever, and testifies that he knew this at the beginning, and knew it from no other source than from those very testimonies. What are these testimonies, except those by which God testified that he would give to his sons an eternal kingdom? And because he testified that he would give this in the Only-begotten, of whom it was said, *And of his kingdom there will be no end*[^14]; he said that those testimonies were founded for ever, because what God promised through them is eternal. For the testimonies themselves will then not be necessary, when the very thing will be seen, for the believing of which the testimonies are now required. And therefore it is well understood to be said, *you have founded them,* because in Christ they are shown to be true. For no one can lay another foundation, besides that which has been laid, which is Christ Jesus[^15]. Whence, then, did this man know this at the beginning, except that the Church speaks, which was not lacking to the earth from the beginning of the human race, whose firstfruits is holy Abel, slain himself too in testimony of the future blood of the Mediator to be shed by an impious brother? For that too was said from the beginning: *The two shall be in one flesh*[^16]; which great mystery the apostle Paul, expounding, *I,* he says, *speak in Christ and in the Church*[^17].
