# Exposition of Psalm 83

**1.** [v. 1.] This psalm is inscribed: *For the winepresses.* And as far as Your Charity noticed with us (for we noticed that you were listening most attentively), nothing in its text is said either of the press, or of the baskets, or of the vat, or of the instruments or building of the winepress; nothing at all of the kind did we hear in it: whence what its title means, which is inscribed: *For the winepresses,* is no small question. For surely if after this title it said anything of such things as I have recalled, it would be believed by the carnal that it truly wished to sing of those visible winepresses: but because it set the title: *For the winepresses,* and afterward said nothing in any verse of the Psalm about those winepresses most known to our eyes; it is not doubted that there are other winepresses which the Spirit of God here wished us to seek and understand. Wherefore let us recall what is done in these visible winepresses, and see how this is enacted spiritually in the Church. The grape surely hangs on the vines, and the olive on the trees (for of these two fruits the winepresses are wont to be prepared); and as long as they hang on their shrubs, they enjoy, as it were, the free air; and neither is the grape wine, nor the olive oil, before the pressure. So are the men whom God predestined before the ages to be conformed to the image of his only-begotten Son[^1], who especially in the passion was pressed out as a great cluster. Men of this kind, then, before they come to the service of God, enjoy in the world as it were a delightful freedom, like grapes or olives hanging: but since it was said: *Son, when you draw near to the service of God, stand in righteousness and fear, and prepare your soul for temptation*[^2]; let each one drawing near to the service of God know that he has come to the winepresses; he will be crushed, worn, pressed; not that he may perish in this world, but that he may flow into the storehouses of God. He is stripped of the coverings of carnal desires, as of the grape-skins: for this befalls him in the carnal desires, on account of which the Apostle too says: *Put off the old man, and put on the new*[^3]. All this comes about only from the pressure: therefore the Churches of God of this time are named winepresses.

**2.** But who are we, set in the winepresses? The sons of Korah. For so it was added: *For the winepresses, for the sons of Korah.* We have the sons of Korah interpreted as the sons of the Bald One, as far as those who knew that tongue could interpret it for us, by the ministry they owed to God: and in this I do not shun to behold a great mystery, and, with the Lord helping, to find it with you. For not, as by the children of pestilence, is all baldness to be mocked; lest, when anyone mocks a consecrated baldness, he be torn to pieces by demons. For Elisha too was going along, and senseless boys cried after him: *Bald-head, bald-head;* and for the fulfilling of a sacrament, turning to the Lord he asked that bears, coming out of the wood, devour them[^4]. Their infancy was indeed snatched away to the end of their life from this world; the boys died, old men one day to die: yet the terror of a sacrament was given to men. For Elisha then bore the person of one whose sons we are, the sons of Korah, namely of our Lord Jesus Christ. Now it occurs to Your Charity from the Gospel why he, bald, bore the person of Christ; recall that he was crucified in the place of Calvary[^5]. Whether, then, this is the interpretation of *the sons of Korah,* as following our predecessors we have said; or something else, which perhaps is hidden from us; meanwhile, what occurs, see how full it is of sacrament. The sons of Korah, the sons of Christ: for the bridegroom too calls them his sons, when he says: *The sons of the bridegroom cannot fast, as long as the bridegroom is with them*[^6]. These winepresses, then, are of the Christians.

**3.** But set in the pressures, we are worn to this end, that, by our love, with which we were borne into those worldly, secular, temporal, fleeting and perishing things, having suffered in them, in this life, the torments and tribulations of pressures and the abundance of temptations, we may begin to seek that rest which is not of this life, nor of this earth: and the Lord becomes, as it is written, *a refuge for the poor*[^7]. What is *for the poor*? As for one destitute, without resource, without help, without any thing in which to presume on earth. For to such poor God is present. Because men, even if they abound in money on this earth, look to what the Apostle says: *Charge the rich of this world not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*[^8]: and considering how uncertain is that in which they rejoiced, before they came to the service of God—that is, before they entered the winepresses—they see that from those very riches they have either the pressures of cares, how they may be managed, how guarded; or, if they have bent their cupidity a little to love them, they are filled more with fears than with fruits. For what is so uncertain as a rolling thing? And not without reason is money itself stamped round, because it does not stand. Such men, then, even if they have something, are poor. But those who have none of these things, and desire to have them, are reckoned among the rich to be reprobated: for God attends not to the means, but to the will. The poor, then, destitute of all worldly resource, because even if they flow with it, they understand how uncertain it is; and groaning to God, having nothing in this world in which to be delighted, in which to be held, set in the abundance of pressures and temptations as in winepresses, flow forth wine, flow forth oil. What are these, except good desires? For there remains for them God to be desired; they no longer love the earth. For they love him who made heaven and earth: they love, and are not yet with him. Their desire is deferred, that it may grow; it grows, that it may have capacity. For God will not give some small thing to one desiring, nor is one to be exercised a little for the capacity of so great a good: God will not give something which he made, but himself who made all things. Be exercised for receiving God; long for that which you will always have. They were reprobated among the people of Israel, who hastened: this affection of those hastening is constantly reproved in Scripture. For who are those who hasten? Those who, converted to God, when they have not found here the rest they sought, and the joys that were promised, as those failing on the way, and supposing that something long remains until this world or this life is ended, and seeking here some rest, which if it is had, is false, look back, and fall away from the resolve; and do not attend with how great a terror it was said: *Remember Lot's wife*[^9]. For why was she made a pillar of salt[^10], if not that she might season men, that they may have sense? Therefore that bad example of hers becomes good for you, if you beware. Remember, he says, Lot's wife: for she looked back, from where she had been freed from Sodom, and there remained, where she looked back; herself remaining in the place, and seasoning others passing by. Freed, then, from the Sodom of our past life, let us not look back: for this is to hasten, not to attend to what God promised, because it is far off, and to look to that which is near, from which you have now been freed. Of such what does the apostle Peter say? *There has happened to them the thing of the true proverb: The dog returned to its vomit*[^11]. For the conscience of sins pressed your breast; pardon received, you as it were vomited, and your breast was relieved; a good conscience was made from a bad conscience: why do you again turn to your vomit? If a dog doing this is a horror to your eyes, what will you be to the eyes of God?

**4.** But each one, dearly beloved brothers, looks back from the place of his journey, to which by advancing he has come, and which he vowed to God, when he has abandoned it. For example, he resolved to keep conjugal chastity (for from there righteousness begins); he withdrew from fornications and from that unlawful uncleanness: when he turns himself to fornications, he has looked back. Another, by the gift of God, vowed something greater, resolved not even to undergo marriage; who would not be condemned if he had taken a wife, after the vow which he promised to God, if he takes one, will be condemned: when he does this which that other who had not promised does; yet that one is not condemned, this one is condemned. Why, except because this one looked back? For he was already in advance, but this one had not yet arrived there. So a virgin, who if she married would not sin[^12], a consecrated woman if she marries will be reckoned an adulteress of Christ. For she looked back from the place to which she had come. So those to whom it is pleasing, leaving all worldly hope and all earthly action, to betake themselves into the fellowship of the saints, into that common life where no one says anything is his own, but all things are common to them, and they have one soul and one heart toward God[^13]; whoever wishes to withdraw from there is not held to be such as he who did not enter: for that one has not yet come; this one has looked back. Wherefore, dearly beloved, as each one can, make vows, and render to the Lord your God what each one can[^14]; let no one look back, let no one be delighted with his former things, let no one turn away from what is before, to what is behind: let him run until he arrives; for we run not with the feet, but with desire. But let no one in this life say that he has arrived. For who can be as perfect as Paul? And yet he says: *Brothers, I do not consider myself to have laid hold; but one thing, forgetting the things that are behind, stretched out to the things that are before, according to my intention I follow on to the palm of the supernal calling of God in Christ Jesus*[^15]. You see Paul still running, and do you already suppose yourself to have arrived?

**5.** [v. 2.] If, then, you feel the pressures of this world, even when you are happy, you have understood that you are in the winepress. For do you suppose, my brothers, that the unhappiness of the world is to be feared, and the happiness is not to be feared? Rather, no unhappiness breaks him whom no happiness corrupts. How, then, is that corruptress to be guarded against and feared, lest by flattering she seduce you? Do not lean on the staff of a reed[^16]; for this too is written, that some lean on the staff of a reed. Do not trust yourself; fragile is that on which you rely, it breaks and slays you. If, then, this world smiles on you with happiness, reckon yourself in pressure, so that you may say: *I found tribulation and grief, and I called upon the name of the Lord*[^17]. He did not say, *I found tribulation,* except a certain hidden one: for a certain tribulation lies hidden in this world from certain men, who suppose it is well with them, when they are pilgrims away from the Lord. *For as long as we are in the body,* he says, *we are pilgrims away from the Lord*[^18]. If you were a pilgrim away from your father, a man, you would be wretched: you are a pilgrim away from the Lord, and are you happy? Therefore there are those who suppose it is well with them. But those who understand that, in whatever flowing of riches and pleasures, although all things serve them at their nod, although nothing troublesome creeps in, nothing adverse terrifies, yet they are in evil as long as they are pilgrims away from the Lord; with a most keen eye they found tribulation and grief, and called upon the name of the Lord. Such is he who sings in this psalm. Who is he? The body of Christ. Who is this? You, if you will; all of us, if we will; all the sons of Korah, and all one man, because one body of Christ. How is he not one man, who has one head? The head of us all is Christ: the body of that head are all we. And all in this life are in the winepresses: if we savor well, we have already come to the winepresses. Therefore, set in the pressures of temptations, let us utter this voice, and send before us our desire: *How most beloved,* he says, *are your tabernacles, O Lord of hosts!* He was in certain tabernacles—that is, in the winepresses; but he desired other tabernacles, where there is no pressure: in these he sighed toward those, from these into those he flowed, in a manner, through the channel of desire.

**6.** [v. 3.] And what follows? *My soul longs and faints for the courts of the Lord.* It is little: *It longs and faints;* but whither does it faint? *For the courts of the Lord.* The pressed grape fainted; but whither did it faint? Into wine and into the vat, and into the rest of the storehouse, to be kept in great quiet. Here it is desired, there grasped, here sighed for, there rejoiced over; here prayed, there praised; here groaned, there exulted. Let no one shun those things I have said as if hard; let no one as it were be unwilling to suffer. It is to be feared lest the grape, while it fears the winepress, be eaten by birds or by beasts. He seems to be in great sadness, when he says: *My soul longs and faints for the courts of the Lord;* for he does not have what he desires: but is he without joy? With what joy? That which the Apostle says: *Rejoicing in hope.* There he will now rejoice in reality, now still in hope. And therefore those who rejoice in hope, because they are certain that they will receive, bear in the winepress all the pressures. Therefore the Apostle himself too, when he had said: *Rejoicing in hope;* as if he spoke to those who are already in the winepress, added straightway: *Patient in tribulation. In tribulation,* he says, *patient*: what then? *Persevering in prayer*[^19]. What is, *persevering*? Because you are deferred. You pray and are deferred; bear that you are deferred: let what is deferred be borne; because when it comes, it is not taken away.

**7.** [vv. 3-4.] You have heard the groaning in the winepress: *My soul desires and faints for the courts of the Lord;* hear whence it endures, rejoicing in hope: *My heart and my flesh have exulted in the living God.* They have exulted here for that. Whence the exultation, except from hope? Whither have they exulted? *In the living God.* What things have exulted for you? *My heart and my flesh.* Whence have they exulted? *For even the sparrow,* he says, *has found itself a house, and the turtledove a nest for itself, where it may place its young.* What is this? He had said two things, and rendered two in the likenesses of birds: he had said that his heart and his flesh exulted, and to these two he rendered the sparrow and the turtledove; the heart as it were a sparrow, the flesh as it were a turtledove. The sparrow has found itself a house, my heart has found itself a house. It exercises its wings in the virtues of this time, in faith itself, and hope, and love, by which it may fly to its house: and when it comes it will remain, and now the querulous voice of the sparrow which is here will not be there. For he himself is the querulous sparrow, of whom in another psalm he says: *Like a solitary sparrow on a rooftop*[^20]. From the rooftop it flies to the house. Let it now be on the rooftop, let it tread the carnal house: it will have a certain heavenly place, a perpetual house; this sparrow will end its complaints. But to the turtledove he gave young too—that is, to the flesh: *the turtledove found a nest, where it may place its young.* The sparrow a house, the turtledove a nest, and a nest surely where it may place its young. The house is chosen as it were for the eternal, the nest is built up for a time: with the heart we think of God, as the sparrow flying to its house; but with the flesh we do good works. For you see how great goods are done through the flesh of the saints: for through this we work the things we were bidden to work, by which we are helped in this life. *Break your bread to the hungry, and bring the needy who is without a roof into your house; if you see one naked, clothe him*[^21]; and other such things which are commanded us, we do not work except through the flesh. The sparrow, then, who thinks of his house, does not withdraw from the turtledove seeking a nest for itself, where it may place its young: for it does not cast them anywhere, but finds a nest for itself, where it may place them. But we say, brothers, what you know: how many seem to do good works outside the Church? how many even pagans feed the hungry, clothe the naked, take in the stranger, visit the sick, console the imprisoned? how many do these things? It seems as if the turtledove brings forth; but it does not find a nest for itself. How many things do many heretics work not in the Church, do they not place their young in a nest? They will be trodden down and worn out; they will not be preserved, not guarded. For in the person of this working flesh a certain woman is set by the apostle Paul, when he says: *Adam was not seduced; but the woman was seduced*[^22]. For afterward Adam consented to the woman: for the woman was seduced by the serpent[^23]. Nor can any evil persuasion now move except first the desire of your flesh, to which if you afterward consent with the mind, the sparrow too has fallen: but if the desires of the flesh are conquered, the members are held to good works, the arms of concupiscence are taken away; and the turtledove begins to have young. Therefore what does the Apostle say there? *But she will be saved through the generation of children*[^24]. A widow woman without children, if she perseveres, will she not be more blessed[^25]? will she not be saved, because she does not bear children? Will not a virgin of God be better? will she not be saved, because she does not have children? or does she not belong to God? The woman, then, who is taken in the type of the flesh, will be saved through the generation of children—that is, if she does good works. But not anywhere should the turtledove find a nest for itself, where it may place its young: in the true faith, in the catholic faith, in the fellowship of the unity of the Church let it bring forth its works. Therefore, when the Apostle was speaking of her too, he thus subjoined: *But she will be saved through the generation of children, if she remains in faith, and love, and sanctification, with sobriety*[^26]. By remaining, then, in faith, faith itself is the nest of your young. For on account of the weakness of the young of your turtledove the Lord deigned to provide you with something from which to make a nest: for he was clothed in the hay of flesh, that he might come to you. In this faith place your young; in this nest work your works. For what the nests are, or what the nest is, follows straightway: *Your altars, O Lord of hosts.* When he had said: *And the turtledove found a nest for itself, where it may place its young;* as if you had asked: What nest? *Your altars, O Lord of hosts, my king and my God.* What is, *my king and my God?* Who rule me, who created me.

**8.** [v. 5.] But here is the nest, and here the pilgrimage, and here the sighing, and here the threshing, and here the pressure, because here is the winepress: but what is it that he desires? what does he long for? whither does it go? whither does our desire tend? whither does it sweep us? Set here, he meditates on those things, set among temptations, set among pressures, set in the winepresses, sighing toward the supernal promises; as if he were about to do something there, already he pre-meditates the future joys. *Blessed,* he says, *are those who dwell in your house.* Whence blessed? what will they have? what will they do? All who are called blessed on earth have something and do something. That man is blessed by so many estates, so great a household, so much gold and silver: by having he is called blessed. He is blessed; he has come to those honors, to the proconsulate, to the prefecture: by doing he is called blessed. Therefore either by having, or by doing. But whence blessed there? what will they have? what will they do? What they will have, I have already said above: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house.* If you have your own house, you are poor: if you have the house of God, you are rich. In your house you will fear robbers; for the house of God the very wall is God. *Blessed,* then, *are those who dwell in your house.* They possess the heavenly Jerusalem without straitness, without pressure, without diversity and division of boundaries: all have it, and each has the whole. Great are those riches. Brother does not straiten brother; there is no want there. What, then, will they do there? For the mother of all human actions is necessity. Now, brothers, I have said it briefly: run in mind through any actions whatever, see whether anything but necessity bears them. Those very memorable arts which seem great in helping, the patronages of the tongue and the aids of medicine; for they are excellent actions in this world: take away litigants; whom does the advocate help? take away wounds and diseases; what does the physician cure? And all these actions of ours which are exacted and done for daily life, come from necessity. To plow, to sow, to plant vines, to sail; all such works—what bears them, except necessity and want? Take away hunger, thirst, nakedness; for whom are all these things needed? These too which are commanded us as good works: for those which I recalled are honorable, but of all men (excepting the worst works I speak, detestable works, shameful deeds and crimes, murders, burglaries, adulteries; those I do not even reckon among human actions); these honorable ones I speak of, nothing but the necessity of carnal frailty bears. These too which I said are commanded us: *Break your bread to the hungry*: to whom do you break it, where no one hungers? *Bring the needy without a roof into your house*[^27]: what stranger do you take in, where all live in their own homeland? what sick one do you visit, where they rejoice in perpetual health? what litigious one do you reconcile, where there is everlasting peace? what dead one do you bury, where it is always lived? You will do none of these things, then, of those honorable works of all men: you will do nothing of those good works; because those young of the turtledove will now fly from the nest. What then? You have now said what we will have: *Those who dwell in your house are blessed.* Say too what they will do, because I do not see there any necessities which impel me to act. Behold, even now what I speak and discuss, necessity bears. For will there be there such a discussion, as one teaching the ignorant, as one reminding the forgetful? or indeed in that homeland will the Gospel be recited, where the very Word of God will be contemplated? Therefore, since this man desiring and sighing said in our voice what we will have in that homeland for which he sighs, and said: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house;* let him say too what we will do. *Forever and ever they will praise you.* This will be our whole business, an Alleluia without failing. Let it not seem to you, brothers, as if it would be a weariness there: because if now you say this for a long time, you do not endure it; necessity turns you away from that joy. And because what is not seen does not delight so much; if with so great eagerness in the very pressure and frailty of the flesh we praise what we believe, how shall we praise what we shall see? When death has been swallowed up in victory, when this mortal has put on immortality, and this corruptible has put on incorruption[^28], no one will say: I have stood long; no one will say: I have fasted long, watched long. For great stability there, and that immortality itself now of our body, will be suspended in the contemplation of God. Even if now this word which we dispense to you holds the frailty of our flesh standing so long; what will that joy do for us? how will it change us? For we shall be like him, since we shall see him as he is[^29]. Now like him, when shall we fail? whither shall we be turned away? Let us, then, be secure, brothers; the praise of God, the love of God will not satiate us. If you fail from love, you fail from praise: but if love will be everlasting, because that beauty will be insatiable; do not fear lest you be unable always to praise him whom you will always be able to love. Therefore: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house; forever and ever they will praise you.* For this life let us sigh.

**9.** [vv. 6-7.] But how shall we come there? *Blessed is the man whose acceptance is from you, O Lord.* He understood where he was, that through the frailty of his flesh he could not fly to that blessedness; he looked around at his own weights; for it is said in another place: *For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul; and the earthly habitation presses down the sense thinking many things*[^30]. The spirit calls upward, the weight of the flesh calls back downward: between the two efforts of the lifting-up and the weight there is a certain struggle; and that very struggle pertains to the pressure of the winepress. Hear that very struggle of the winepress expressed by the Apostle; for he too was worn there, pressed there: *I delight,* he says, *in the law of God according to the inner man: but I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and leading me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members.* A great struggle, and a great despair of escaping, unless help come from what follows: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^31]. Therefore here too in this psalm he saw those joys, thought in mind: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house, O Lord; forever and ever they will praise you.* But who will ascend there? what do I do about the weight of the flesh? *Blessed are those who dwell in your house; forever and ever they will praise you. For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man.* But what shall I do? how shall I fly? how shall I arrive? *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind.* He said he was wretched, and said: *Who will free me from the body of this death,* that I may dwell in the house of the Lord, and forever and ever praise him? *who will free me? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord.* As, then, in the words of the Apostle he came to the aid of that difficulty and as it were inextricable struggle by what he subjoined: *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord;* so here too, when he sighed with flaming desire for the house of God and for those praises of God, the heaviness of his body and the mass of his flesh being considered, and a certain despair having arisen, he again awoke into hope, and said: *Blessed is the man whose acceptance is from you, O Lord.*

**10.** [vv. 6-7.] What, then, does God bestow in this grace, on him whom he has accepted to be led through? It follows, and says: *Ascents in his heart.* He makes for him steps by which he may ascend. Where does he make steps for him? In the heart. The more, then, you love, the more you ascend. *Ascents,* he says, *in his heart he has disposed.* Who? He who accepted him: *For blessed is he whose acceptance is from you, O Lord.* Because through himself he cannot, it is necessary that your grace accept him. And what does your grace do? It disposes ascents in the heart. Where does it dispose ascents? *In the heart, in the vale of weeping.* Behold, you have the winepress, the vale of weeping: those very tears of the pious, the crushed, are the new wine of the loving. *Ascents in his heart he has disposed.* Where, then, has he disposed them? *In the vale of weeping.* For here he disposed ascents, *in the vale of weeping;* for here it is wept where it is sown: *Going,* he says, *they went and wept, casting their seeds*[^32]. Therefore let the ascents in your heart be disposed by God through his grace. By loving, ascend: thence is sung the Song of Ascents. And where has he disposed these ascents for you? *In the heart, in the vale of weeping.* He said where he disposed them, whither he disposed them. What did he dispose? *Ascents.* Where? Within, *in the heart.* In what region, and as it were place of habitation? *In the vale of weeping.* That whither one may ascend? *To the place which he has disposed.* What is this, brothers: *To the place which he has disposed?* What place he would say he has disposed, if it could be said. It has been said to you: *He has disposed ascents in the heart, in the vale of weeping.* Do you ask whither? What will he say to you? *What eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man*[^33]. It is a hill, it is a mountain, it is earth, it is a meadow, nearly all these things has that place been called. But what it is by its own property, not by likeness (for we now see through a glass in a riddle what that place is, then we shall see face to face[^34]), who can explain? Do not, then, ask whither he has disposed, *to the place which he has disposed.* He himself knows whither, he himself knows, who has disposed, whither he will lead you, whose ascents he has disposed in the heart. What? do you fear to ascend, lest he who leads you err? Behold, in the vale of weeping he has disposed ascents: *To the place which he has disposed.* We weep now. Whence? Where our ascents are disposed. Whence do we weep, except from that whence the Apostle cried out that he was wretched, because he saw another law in his members fighting against the law of his mind[^35]? And whence is this for us? From the punishment of sin. And we supposed that we could easily be righteous as it were by our own strength, before we received the commandment; but when the commandment came, sin revived: but I died; this the Apostle says[^36]. For the Law was given to men, not to save them already, but that through it they might know in what sickness they lay. Hear the words of the Apostle: *For if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the Law; but Scripture concluded all under sin, that the promise from the faith of Jesus Christ might be given to believers*[^37]: that grace might come after the Law, and find man not only lying, but now even confessing and saying: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?* and the physician might come opportunely to the vale of weeping, and say: Surely you have known that you have fallen; hear me that you may rise, you who despised me that you might fall. The Law, then, was given to convict the sick man of his disease, who seemed to himself sound; that sins might be shown, not that they might be taken away. Sin being shown through the Law given, sin was increased, because it is sin even against the Law: *An occasion,* he says, *being taken, sin through the commandment wrought in me all concupiscence*[^38]. What is, *an occasion being taken through the commandment?* The commandment received, men tried as it were by their own strength to do it; conquered by concupiscence, they were made guilty even by the transgression of the commandment itself. But what does the Apostle say? *But where sin abounded, grace super-abounded*[^39]; that is, the disease was increased, the medicine was commended. Therefore, brothers, those five porches of Solomon, did they cure the sick, where the pool was in the midst of them? *And there lay,* it says, *the sick in five porches*[^40]; in the Gospel we have read it. Those five porches are the Law in the five books of Moses. To this end the sick were brought forth from their houses, that they might lie in the porches. The Law, then, brought forth the sick, did not heal them; but by the blessing of God the water was troubled, as if an angel descended: the water seen troubled, he who could, one, descended and was healed. That water, surrounded by five porches, was the people of the Jews concluded by the Law: this the Lord disturbed by his presence, that he might be killed. For unless the Lord by his descent disturbed the people of the Jews, would he be crucified? And so the troubled water signified the passion of the Lord, which was done with the nation of the Jews disturbed. In this passion the sick man believes, as it were descending into the troubled water, and is healed. He who was not healed by the Law—that is, the porches—is healed by grace, through the faith in the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. One, because unity. Therefore here too what does he say? *Ascents in his heart he has disposed, in the vale of weeping, to the place which he has disposed*: now in that place we shall rejoice.

**11.** [v. 8.] But why *in the vale of weeping?* And from what vale of weeping shall we come to that place of joy? *For he also will give a blessing,* he says, *who gave the law.* He afflicted us with the Law, he pressed us with the Law, he showed us the winepress; we saw the pressure, we knew the tribulation of our flesh, we groaned with sin rebelling against our mind, we cried out: *Wretched man that I am*[^41]: we groaned under the Law; what remains, except that he who gave the Law give a blessing? Grace will come after the Law; that is the blessing. And what has this grace and blessing bestowed on us? *They will walk from virtues unto virtue.* For here through grace many virtues are given: *To one indeed is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge, according to the same Spirit, to another faith, to another the gift of healings, to another kinds of tongues, to another the interpretation of words, to another prophecy*[^42]. Many virtues, but here necessary; and from these virtues we go unto a virtue. What virtue? Christ, the Power of God, and the Wisdom of God[^43]. He himself gives diverse virtues in this place, who, in place of all the virtues necessary and useful in the vale of weeping, will give one virtue, himself. For the virtues of our active life too are described by many as four, and are found in Scripture. Prudence is the one by which we distinguish between good and evil. Justice is the one by which we render to each his own; owing nothing to anyone, but loving all[^44]. Temperance is the one by which we rein in lusts. Fortitude is the one by which we bear all troublesome things. These virtues are now given to us in the vale of weeping through the grace of God: from these virtues we go unto that virtue. And what will that virtue be, except of the contemplation of God alone? This prudence will not be necessary there, where no evils will occur which we should avoid. But what do we suppose, brothers? Not this justice, where there will be no want of anyone whom we ought to help. Not this temperance, where there will be no lust to be reined in. Not this fortitude, where there will be no evils to be borne. Therefore from these virtues of this action we shall go unto the virtue of that contemplation, by which we may contemplate God: as it is written: *In the morning I will stand before you, and I will contemplate*[^45]. And hear that from the virtues of this action we shall go unto that contemplation. There follows: *They shall go from virtues unto virtue*: what virtue? Of contemplating. What is, of contemplating? *The God of gods will appear in Zion. The God of gods,* the Christ of Christians. How is the Christ of Christians *the God of gods?* *I said: You are gods, and all sons of the Most High*[^46]. For he gave them power to become sons of God[^47], he in whom we have believed, the beautiful bridegroom, who here on account of our deformity appeared deformed: because *we saw him,* he says, *and he had no form nor comeliness*[^48]. Every necessity of mortality being finished, just as he is God with God, the Word with the Father, through whom all things were made, he will appear to the pure of heart: for blessed are the pure of heart, for they shall see God[^49]. *The God of gods will appear in Zion.*

**12.** [v. 9.] And again this man returns from the thought of those joys to his own sighing. He sees what he has anticipated in hope, and where he still is in reality. *The God of gods will* then *appear in Zion*: this is whence we shall rejoice; him forever and ever we shall praise. But still now it is the time of praying, the time of beseeching; and even if somewhat of rejoicing, yet still in hope: we are in pilgrimage, we are in the vale of weeping. Returning, then, to the groaning of this place, he says: *O Lord God of hosts, hear my prayer; give ear, O God of Jacob*: because you made Jacob himself, from Jacob, Israel. For God appeared to him, and he was called Israel, "seeing God." Hear me, then, O God of Jacob, and make me Israel. When shall I become Israel? When the God of gods will appear in Zion[^50].

**13.** [v. 10.] *Behold, O God, our protector.* Under the shadow of your wings they will hope[^51]; therefore: *Behold, O God, our protector. And look upon the face of your Christ.* For when does God not look upon the face of his Christ? what is: *Look upon the face of your Christ?* By the face we are made known: what, then, is: *Look upon the face of your Christ?* Make your Christ known to all. *Look upon the face of your Christ*: let your Christ be known to all, that we may be able to go from virtues unto virtue, that grace may be able to super-abound, since sin abounded[^52].

**14.** [v. 11.] *For better is one day in your courts above thousands.* Those are the courts for which he sighed, for which he fainted. My soul desires and faints for the courts of the Lord: better is there one day above thousands of days. Men desire thousands of days, and wish to live much here: let them despise thousands of days, let them desire one day, which has no rising and setting; one day, an everlasting day, to which yesterday does not yield, which tomorrow does not press on. Let that one day be desired by us. What have we to do with thousands of days? We go from thousands of days to one day, as we go from virtues unto virtue.

**15.** *I have chosen to be cast aside in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners.* For this man found the vale of weeping, this man found the humility from which he may ascend: he knows that if he wishes to exalt himself, he will fall, if he humbles himself, he will be raised up; he chose to be cast aside, that he may be lifted up. How many, outside this tent of the Lord's winepress—that is, outside the catholic Church—wishing to be exalted, and loving their own honors, refuse to know the truth? If this verse were in their heart: *I have chosen to be cast aside in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners;* would they not cast aside their honors, and run to the vale of weeping, and from here find ascents in the heart, and from here go from virtues unto virtue, placing their hope in Christ, not in I know not what man? A good voice, a voice to be rejoiced in, a voice to be chosen: *I have chosen to be cast aside in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners.* He himself chose to be cast aside in the house of the Lord; but he who invited to the banquet calls the one choosing the lower place to the higher, and says to him: *Come up*[^53]. Yet he himself chose only to be in the house of the Lord, in whatever place, yet not outside the threshold.

**16.** [v. 12.] Why did he choose to be cast aside in the house of the Lord, rather than to dwell in the tents of sinners? *Because God loves mercy and truth.* The Lord loves mercy, by which he first came to my aid: he loves truth, that to the believer he may give what he promised. Hear mercy and truth in the apostle Paul, formerly Saul the persecutor. He needed mercy, and said it was wrought in him: *I who formerly was a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and insolent; but I obtained mercy, that in me Christ Jesus might show forth all longsuffering toward those who are to believe in him unto eternal life*[^54]. So that, when Paul received the indulgence of so great crimes, no one might despair that whatever sins could be forgiven him. Behold, you have mercy. God did not then wish to exercise truth, so as to punish the sinner. For if the sinner were punished, would it not be truth? or would he dare to say: I ought not to be punished, who could not say: I have not sinned? And if he said: I have not sinned: to whom would he say it? whom would he deceive? Therefore first the Lord prepaid mercy upon him; after mercy, truth. Hear him now exacting truth. First, then, he says: *I obtained mercy, I who formerly was a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and insolent; but by the grace of God I am what I am*[^55]. Afterward he says, when he was drawing near to his passion: *I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith; there remains for me a crown of righteousness.* He who bestowed mercy, keeps truth. Whence keeps he truth? *Which the Lord will render to me on that day, the just judge*[^56]. He gave indulgence, he will render a crown: he is the giver of indulgence, the debtor of the crown. Whence a debtor? did he receive something? To whom does God owe anything? Behold, we see that Paul holds him as a debtor, having obtained mercy, exacting truth: *The Lord will render to me,* he says, *on that day.* What will he render you, except what he owes you? Whence does he owe you? what did you give him? Who first gave to him, that it should be repaid him[^57]? The Lord made himself a debtor, not by receiving, but by promising: it is not said to him: Render what you received; but: Render what you promised. He prepaid mercy to me, he says, that he might make me innocent: for formerly I was a blasphemer and insolent; but by his grace I was made innocent. But he who prepaid mercy, will he be able to deny the debt? *He loves mercy and truth. He will give grace and glory.* What grace, except that of which he himself said: *By the grace of God I am what I am?* What glory, except that of which he himself said: *There remains for me a crown of righteousness.*

**17.** [v. 13.] Therefore: *The Lord,* he says, *will not deprive of goods those walking in innocence.* Why, then, men, do you refuse to hold innocence, except that you may have goods? He refuses to hold innocence, lest he render what is assigned to him: he wishes to have gold and loses innocence. What does he gain? what does he lose? He has the gain of gold, he has suffered the loss of innocence. Is there anything more precious than innocence? But if I keep innocence, he says, I shall be poor. Are those small riches, innocence itself? If you have a chest full of gold, you will be rich: if you have a heart full of innocence, will you be poor? But behold, desiring goods, now in want, in tribulation, in the vale of weeping, in pressure, in temptations, keep innocence. For afterward there will be also your good which you desire; rest, eternity, immortality, impassibility will be afterward: these are the goods which God keeps for his righteous. For the goods which you now desire as something great, on account of which you wish to be guilty, and not innocent, mark who have them, who abound in such things. You see riches among robbers, among the impious, among the wicked, among the base; among the shameful and criminal you see riches: God gives these to them on account of the communion of the human race, on account of the abundant affluence of his goodness; who also makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust[^58]. He gives so much even to the evil, and keeps nothing for you? Is what he promised you false? He keeps it; be secure. He who had mercy on you when you were impious; does he desert you when you have been made pious? he who gave to the sinner the death of his Son, what does he keep for one saved through the death of his Son? Be secure, then. Hold him as debtor, because you believed in the promiser. *The Lord will not deprive of goods those walking in innocence.* What, then, remains for us here in the winepress, in affliction, in a hard situation, in the presence of a perilous life? what remains for us, that we may arrive there? *O Lord God of hosts, blessed is the man who hopes in you.*
