# Exposition of Psalm 89

**1.** [v. 1.] *A Prayer of Moses the man of God,* this psalm is headed: through whom, his man, God gave the Law to his people, whom, through the same his man freeing from the house of slavery, he led for forty years through the desert. Moses, then, was a minister of the Old Testament, and a prophet of the New Testament. *For all those things happened to them in figure,* as the Apostle says: *but they were written for our correction, upon whom the end of the ages has come*[^1]. According to this dispensation, then, which was made through Moses, this psalm is to be examined, which from his prayer received its title.

**2.** *O Lord,* he says, *you have become a refuge for us from generation to generation*: whether in every generation, or in two generations, old and new; because, as I said, he was a minister of the Testament belonging to the old generation, and a prophet of the Testament belonging to the new generation. Of which Testament Jesus himself is the surety, and the bridegroom of the marriage which from that generation he obtained, says: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too; for he wrote of me*[^2]. For it is not to be believed that this psalm was written by Moses himself at all, who is set in none of his writings, in which his canticles are written: but for the sake of some signification the name of so great a servant of God, of so great merit, was applied, from which the attention of the reader or hearer might be directed. *You have become,* then, he says, *O Lord, a refuge for us from generation to generation.*

**3.** [v. 2.] But what kind of refuge he has become, since namely he began to be for us what he was not—that is, a refuge, but he himself was not as though he was not before he was a refuge for us—he went on to add: *Before the mountains were made, and the earth and the world were formed, and from age unto age you are.* You, then, who always are, both before we were, and before the world was, became a refuge for us from the time we were converted to you. But it does not seem to me to be understood lightly what he says: *Before the mountains were made, and the earth was formed;* or, as other codices have, which is expressed from one Greek word, *the earth was molded.* For mountains are the higher parts of the earth: and surely if before the earth was formed God is, by whom the earth was formed; what great thing is said of mountains or any other of its parts, since God is not only before the earth, but also before heaven and earth, and before every bodily and spiritual creature? But surely the whole rational creation is perhaps distinguished by this difference, that by the name of mountains the heights of the angels are signified, and by the name of earth the lowliness of men. And therefore, although all things that were created are not incongruously said to be either made or formed; yet if there is any propriety in these words, the angels were made, who, when they were enumerated among his heavenly works, the enumeration itself was so concluded: *He spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created*[^3]: but whence man might be made according to the body, the earth was formed. For Scripture uses this word, where we read, either: *God molded;* or: *God formed man from the slime of the earth*[^4]. Therefore before there were made those things which in your creation are the highest and greatest; for what is greater than the rational heavenly creature? and before the earth was molded, that there might be one who would acknowledge you and praise you on the earth: and this is little, because these things began, either in time, or with time; but *from age and unto age you are.* Which would be more fittingly said: From eternity unto eternity: for not from age is God, who is before the ages; or unto age, of which there is an end, since he is without end. But from the ambiguous Greek word it often comes about in the Scriptures that the Latin translator sets either "age" for "eternal," or "eternal" for "age." But excellently he does not say: From age you were, and unto age you will be: but he set the word of present signification, intimating the substance of God altogether unchangeable, where there is not: He was, and He will be; but only: He is. Whence it was said: *I am who I am;* and: *He who is sent me to you*[^5]; and: *You will change them, and they will be changed; but you are the same, and your years shall not fail*[^6]. Behold what eternity has become a refuge for us, that, in it to abide, we may flee to it from this mutability of time.

**4.** [v. 3.] But since, while we are here, we live amid great and many temptations, by which it is to be feared lest we be turned away from that refuge; let us look at what consequently the prayer of the man of God demands. *Turn not man away into lowliness*: that is, lest man, turned away from your eternal and lofty things, covet temporal things, and savor earthly things. And he asks this from God which God himself commanded; in a sentence altogether similar to that by which in prayer we say: *Lead us not into temptation*[^7]. Finally here too he adds: *And you said: Be converted, you sons of men.* As though he said: This I ask of you which you yourself commanded: giving the glory to his grace, that he who glories may glory in the Lord[^8]; without whose help, by the choice of the will, we cannot overcome the temptations of this life. *Turn not away,* he says, *man into lowliness*: and yet you *said: Be converted, you sons of men.* But give what you commanded, by hearing the prayer of the one asking, and by aiding the faith of the one willing.

**5.** [v. 4.] *For a thousand years before your eyes are as yesterday, which has passed.* Therefore we ought to be converted to your refuge, where without any mutability you are, from those passing and slipping things; since however long a time be wished for this life, *a thousand years before your eyes are as a yesterday which has passed;* not even as a tomorrow, which is to come: so all things that are closed by the end of time are to be held as past. Whence too the Apostle's intention put after itself those things which are behind, forgetting them, where all temporal things ought to be understood; and stretched himself out to those things which are before, which is the longing for eternal things[^9]. But lest some should suppose that a thousand years are so reckoned with God, as though God has such long days, whereas this was said to make us despise the length of time: therefore he added: *And as a watch in the night;* since the span of a watch has no more than three hours. And yet men dared to presume a knowledge of the times; which the Lord, to the disciples desiring to know it, said: *It is not yours to know the times, which the Father has set in his own power*[^10]: and they defined this world by six thousand years, as though it could be ended in six days. Nor did they attend to what was said, *as one day which has passed*: for not, when it was said, had a thousand years alone passed. And this ought especially to have admonished them, lest they be deluded by the uncertainty of times, which is, *as a watch in the night*: for neither, as of six days they seem to have supposed something verisimilar, on account of the first six days in which God perfected his works; so can they fit six watches—that is, eighteen hours—to that supposition.

**6.** [vv. 5-6.] Then this man of God, or rather the prophetic spirit, as it were the law of God written in his secret wisdom, where he set for the sinful life of mortals the measure of running its course and the trouble of mortality, seems in a manner to recite, when he says: *What are reckoned for nothing, their years shall be. In the morning let it pass like grass, in the morning let it flower, and pass; in the evening let it fall, harden, and dry up.* The happiness, then, of the heirs of the Old Testament, which they sought as a great good from the Lord their God, deserved to receive this law in his hidden providence, which Moses seems to recite: *What are reckoned for nothing, their years shall be.* For those things are reckoned for nothing, which before they come, are not yet; when they have come, now are not: for they do not come that they may be present, but that they may not be. *In the morning,* that is, first, *let it pass like grass, in the morning let it flower, and pass; in the evening,* that is, afterward, *let it fall, harden, and dry up. Let it fall,* surely in death; *let it harden,* in the corpse; *let it dry up,* in the dust. Which is it, except the flesh, where is the condemned concupiscence of carnal things? For all flesh is grass, and the glory of man as the flower of grass: the grass has dried up, the flower has fallen; but the word of the Lord abides forever[^11].

**7.** [v. 7.] But not keeping silent that this punishment came from sin, he straightway subjoined: *For we have failed in your anger, and in your indignation we have been troubled. We have failed,* in weakness; *we have been troubled,* by the fear of death. For we were made weak, and we dread to end our weakness. *Another,* he says, *will gird you, and carry you where you do not wish*[^12]; although it is to be punished not by martyrdom, but to be crowned: and the soul of the Lord himself, transfiguring us into himself, was sorrowful even unto death[^13]; since the departure even of the Lord was none but of death.

**8.** [v. 8.] *You have set our iniquities in your sight*: that is, you did not dissemble them. *Our age in the enlightenment of your countenance*: there is supplied, *you have set.* But *in the enlightenment of your countenance* he said, what above was *in your sight,* and *our age,* what above was *our iniquities.*

**9.** [vv. 9-10.] *For all our days have failed, and in your anger we have failed.* It is sufficiently shown in these words that this mortality is penal. He says the days have failed, either because in them men fail by loving the things that pass, or because they have been reduced to fewness; which he seems to disclose in what follows, when he says: *Our years have meditated like a spider. The days of our years are in themselves seventy years: but if among the mighty, eighty years, and more of them is labor and grief.* These words indeed seem to express the brevity and misery of this life; because in this time even those are called long-lived who have lived seventy years. But up to eighty they seem to have some strength: but if they live beyond these, they live with labor and grief multiplied. But many even within seventy years bear a most weak and wretched old age, and often old men beyond eighty years have been found to flourish marvelously. It is better, then, to search out something spiritual in these numbers. For not upon the sons of Adam, through whom one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so death passed to all men[^14], is there a greater anger of God, because they live much more briefly than the ancients lived; since the length of their life too was mocked, where a thousand years were compared to a yesterday past, and three hours: and surely they lived long, then, when they provoked the anger of God even unto the flood by which they perished.

**10.** Seventy years, moreover, and eighty, make a hundred fifty; which a sacred number this very book of Psalms sufficiently intimates. For a hundred fifty has the same reason in signification as fifteen: which number is made of seven and eight joined; of which the first intimates, on account of the observance of the sabbath, the Old Testament, the second the New Testament on account of the Lord's resurrection. Hence in the temple there are fifteen steps; hence in the Psalms there are fifteen songs of ascents; hence by fifteen cubits the water of the flood surpassed the highest mountains[^15]; and if in some other places this sacred number is commended. Therefore: *Our years,* he says, *have meditated like a spider.* In corruptible things we labor, corruptible works we wove: which, according to the prophet Isaiah, by no means covered us[^16]. *The days of our years are in themselves seventy years: but if among the mighty, eighty years.* It is one thing *in themselves,* another *among the mighty. In themselves,* that is, in those very years or days which gives to be understood in temporal things: therefore *seventy;* because temporal things seem to be promised in the Old Testament. *But if,* not in the years themselves, but *among the mighty,* that is, not in temporal things, but in eternal, *eighty years;* because the New Testament is in the hope of renewal and resurrection unto eternity: *and more of them is labor and grief;* that is, whoever transgresses this faith, and seeks something more, finds labors and griefs. It can also be understood thus: That although we are set in the New Testament, which the octogenary number signifies, more does this our life have of labor and grief, while we groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body: for in hope we have been saved; and what we do not yet see, we await with patience[^17]. And this pertains to the mercy of God: whence it follows, and he says: *For mansuetude has come upon us, and we shall be rebuked.* For whom the Lord loves, he rebukes; and he scourges every son whom he receives[^18]: and he gives even to certain great ones a thorn of the flesh, by which they are buffeted, lest they be exalted in the greatness of their revelations, that power may be perfected in weakness[^19]. Some codices indeed do not have, *we shall be rebuked;* but *we shall be instructed,* which is brought back to the same mansuetude. For no one can be instructed without labor and grief; because power is perfected in weakness.

**11.** [vv. 11-12.] *Who knows the power of your anger, and for fear of you to number your anger?* It belongs to very few men, he says, to know the power of your anger; because to most you are rather angry, when you spare, so that the labor and grief, by which you rebuke and instruct those whom you love, lest they be tormented with eternal punishments, is understood to pertain not to your anger, but rather to your mansuetude: for as it is read in another psalm: *The sinner has provoked the Lord; according to the greatness of his anger he will not inquire*[^20]. *Who,* then, *knows,* that is, how rarely is one found who knows *the power of your anger, and for fear of you to number your anger?* Here too is supplied, *who knows.* How difficultly is one found who knows so to number your anger for fear of you, that he add even this, and understand it to pertain to your anger, that to some, with whom you are more angry, you seem to spare, so that the sinner prospers in his way, and receives greater things at the last? For the power of human anger, when it has killed the body, has nothing more it can do; but God has power both to punish here, and after the death of the body to cast into hell[^21]. And by few instructed ones the vain and seductive happiness of the impious is understood to be his greater anger. This that man did not know, whose feet had almost slipped, because he was envious of sinners, beholding the peace of sinners: but he learned it, when he entered into the sanctuary of God, and understood in the last things[^22]; whither few enter, that they may learn for fear of God to number his anger, and to assign the prosperity of evil men to the number of punishments.

**12.** *Make your right hand so known.* For this the Greek codices rather have; not as certain Latin ones: *Make your right hand known to me.* What is, then: *Make your right hand so known;* except: make your Christ, of whom it was said: *And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed*[^23], so known, that in him your faithful may learn to ask of you and to hope for rather those rewards of faith which do not appear in the Old Testament, but are revealed in the New; lest they suppose that the happiness of earthly and temporal goods is to be held and coveted and loved as something great, and their feet be moved, when they see it even in those who do not worship you; and their steps be poured out into a fall, while they do not know how to number your anger? Finally, according to this prayer of his man, he so made his Christ known, that by his sufferings he demonstrated that not those gifts which seem to sound in the Old Testament, where there are shadows of things to come, but eternal things are to be coveted. In this sense the right hand of God too can be understood, in which he will separate his righteous from the impious[^24]: because it too is well thus made known, when he scourges every son whom he receives[^25], and does not allow him to prosper in his sins by being more angry, but in mansuetude, with the left hand he scourges, that he may set the amended one at the right. And that which most codices have: *Make your right hand known to me,* can be referred to both, whether to Christ, or to eternal happiness: for God so does not have a right hand of bodily form, as he does not have anger turbulent with commotions.

**13.** But as for what he adds: *And those fettered in heart in wisdom*: other codices do not have, *fettered,* but, *instructed.* For the Greek word so sounds similarly in both significations, that they differ a little by one syllable. But since those are instructed in wisdom who put, as it is written, *their foot into her fetters*[^26] (surely not the foot of the body, but the foot of the heart), and, bound by her as it were golden bonds, do not wander from the way of God, nor become fugitives from him; whichever of these be read, the meaning of the truth is safe. For those very ones *fettered,* or *instructed in heart in wisdom,* God so made known in the New Testament, that for the faith which the impiety of the Jews and Gentiles detested, they despised all things; and let themselves be deprived of those things which those who judge carnally suppose to be great promises in the Old Testament.

**14.** [v. 13.] And since, when they were so made known, that they despised those things, and bore testimony to the desiring of eternal things through their sufferings (whence they were also called witnesses; for so in Greek martyrs are named), they endured many and very many evils and bitter temporal things; this man of God attends to this, and the prophetic spirit figured by the name of Moses, and says: *Turn, O Lord; how long; and be entreatable over your servants.* It is the voice of those, or for those, who, bearing many evils while this world persecutes, are made known as fettered in heart in wisdom, so that not even, forced by so great evils, do they flee from the Lord to the goods of this world. But according to that which is written elsewhere: *How long do you turn your face away from me*[^27]? this too was said: *Turn, O Lord; how long?* And that those who very carnally attribute to God the form of the human body may know that the turning away of his face and his turning do not happen by a motion like the motions of our body; let them in this same psalm recall the things above: *You have set our iniquities in your sight, our age in the enlightenment of your countenance.* How, then, does he say here: *Turn,* that he may be propitious, as though, angry, he had turned away his face; when there he so intimates him angry, that he did not turn his face away from the iniquities and the age of those with whom he was angry, but rather set them in his sight and in the enlightenment of his countenance? But what is said, *how long,* is the word of a praying righteousness, not of an indignant impatience. Surely what is here set, *be entreatable;* others interpreted word for word, *entreat.* But he who says, *be entreatable,* avoided the ambiguity; because to entreat is a common word: for both he entreats who pours out the entreaty, and he to whom it is poured out; for we say: I entreat you, and, I entreat from you.

**15.** [vv. 14-15.] Then, anticipating in hope those goods which are to come, and reckoning them as it were already done: *We have been filled,* he says, *in the morning with your mercy.* Therefore in these as it were nocturnal labors and griefs the prophecy has been kindled for us, like a lamp in a dark place, until the day dawns, and the day-star arises in our hearts[^28]. For blessed are the pure of heart, for they shall see God[^29]. Then the righteous will be filled with that good which now they hunger and thirst for, while, walking by faith, they are pilgrims away from the Lord[^30]. Whence that too is said: *You will fill me with joy with your countenance*[^31]. In the morning they will stand and contemplate[^32]; and, as others interpreted: *We have been satisfied in the morning with your mercy,* then they will be satisfied: as he says elsewhere: *I shall be satisfied, when your glory shall be made manifest*[^33]. Whence it is said: *Show us the Father, and it suffices us;* and the Lord himself says: *I will show myself to him*[^34]: which until it happen, nothing good suffices us; nor ought it to suffice, lest our desire remain on the way, which until it arrives is to be stretched out. *We have been filled in the morning with your mercy; and we have exulted, and been made glad in all our days.* That day is a day without end. All those days are at once; therefore they satisfy. For they do not yield to those succeeding, where there is not something which by not coming is not yet, and by coming is now not. They are all at once, because there is one which stands and does not pass: that is eternity. These are the days of which it is said: *What man is there who wishes life, and loves to see good days*[^35]? These days are called years in another place too, where it is said to God: *But you are the same, and your years shall not fail.* For these are not years which are reckoned for nothing, nor days which have declined like a shadow[^36]: but they are days which are, whose number he prayed to be made known to him who said: *Make known to me, O Lord, my end* (by arriving at which I may abide, and seek nothing further), *and the number of my days which is;* surely *which is,* not which is not. Because these days, of which there too consequently he says: *Behold, you have set my days old*[^37], are not, because they do not stand, do not abide, run through with the swiftest mutability; nor is one hour found in them in which we so are, that not one part of it has passed, another is to come, none stand that it may be. But those years and days do not fail, in which we too shall not fail, but without failing be refreshed. Let our soul be fervent with the desire of those days, let it thirst ardently and vehemently; that there we may be filled, there be satisfied, there say what here we foretell: *We have been satisfied in the morning with your mercy; and we have exulted, and been made glad in all our days. We have rejoiced for the days in which you humbled us, the years in which we saw evils.*

**16.** [v. 16.] But now, in days still evil, let us say what follows: *And look upon your servants, and upon your works.* For your servants themselves are your works, not only that they may be men, but also that they may be your servants, that is, obedient to your commands. For we are his workmanship, not only in Adam, but also created in Christ Jesus, in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them[^38]. For it is God who works in us both to will and to work, for the good will[^39]. *And direct their sons*: that they may be right in heart, to whom God is good. For God is good to Israel, but to the upright in heart. Not as that man whose feet were moved, because God had begun to displease him beholding the peace of sinners, as though he did not know these things, as though he did not care for these things, and was dissembling from the governing of the human race[^40].

**17.** [v. 17.] *And let the splendor of the Lord our God be upon us.* Whence it is said: *The light of your countenance is sealed upon us, O Lord*[^41]. *And direct the works of our hands upon us*: that we do them not for the reward of earthly things; for then they are not direct, but crooked. Up to here many codices have this psalm; but in some another, last, verse is read: *And direct the work of our hands.* To which verse the diligent and learned prefix a star, which they call asterisks, by which they signify those things which are found in the Hebrew or in other Greek translators, but in the Septuagint translation are not. Which verse, if we wish to expound, seems to me to have this meaning, that all our good works are one work of love: for the fullness of the law is love[^42]. For in the earlier verse, when he had said: *And direct the works of our hands upon us;* in this last he did not say *works,* but *direct the work of our hands*: as though wishing in the last verse to show that those very works are one work, that is, are directed to one work. For then works are right, when they are directed to this one end: for the end of the precept is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned[^43]. The work, then, is one, in which are all things, faith which works through love[^44]: whence the Lord too in the Gospel says: *This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he sent*[^45]. Since, then, in this psalm both the old life and the new life, and the mortal life and the life that lives, and the years which are reckoned for nothing and the days having the fullness of mercy and true gladness—that is, both the punishment of the first man and the kingdom of the second—are sufficiently and openly distinguished; to this end I judge the name of the man of God, Moses, was inscribed in its title, that to those who piously and rightly search the Scriptures, it might be intimated in this way that even the law of God, which was ministered through Moses, where for good works God seems to promise the rewards of earthly goods alone, or nearly alone, without doubt has under a veil something such as this psalm shows. But when anyone passes over to Christ, the veil will be taken away[^46], and his eyes will be revealed, that he may consider the wonders of the law of God; with him granting it to whom we say: *Reveal my eyes, and I will consider the wonders of your law*[^47].
