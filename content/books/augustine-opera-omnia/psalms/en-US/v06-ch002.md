# Exposition of Psalm 123

**1.** I have undertaken to consider with your holiness, in order, the songs of one who ascends; of one who ascends and loves, and ascends precisely because he loves. Every love either ascends or descends. For by good desire we are lifted up to God, and by evil desire we are cast down into the depths. But because we have already fallen by evil desire, it remains for us, if we recognize the one who did not fall to us but came down to us, to ascend by clinging to him; for by our own strength we cannot. Our Lord Jesus Christ himself said: *No one ascends into heaven except him who descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven*[^1]. He seems to have said this of himself alone. Did the rest, then, remain behind, because he alone ascended who alone descended? What ought the rest to do? To be united to his body, so that there may be one Christ, who descended and ascended. The head descended, and ascends with the body; clothed in his Church, which he presented to himself without spot or wrinkle[^2]. He alone, then, ascends. But we too, when we are with him in such a way that we are his members in him, then he alone is with us also, and therefore one, and always one. Unity joins us together into one; and they alone do not ascend with him who have refused to be one with him. But now, since he who has been set in heaven, with his flesh raised up and made immortal—the flesh through which he was for a time mortal—and suffering in heaven none of the persecutions, none of the malicious deeds and reproaches that he endured on this earth when he deigned to bear all things for us; yet, in compassion for his body laboring on earth, he said: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*[^3]—no one was now touching him, and yet he cried out from heaven that he was suffering persecution—we ought not to be without hope, but rather to presume with great confidence that, if through love he is with us on earth, through that same love we too are with him in heaven. But we have said how he himself is with us on earth; we have spoken of his voice sounding from heaven: *Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?*—when Saul was not touching him at all, nor even seeing him. But how is it shown that we too are with him in heaven? By the same apostle Paul saying: *If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God; savor the things that are above, not the things that are on earth. For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^4]. So he too is still below, and we are already above: he is below by the compassion of love, we are above by the hope of love. *For by hope we have been saved*[^5]. But because our hope is sure, although it is yet to be, it is so spoken of concerning us as if it were already done.

**2.** [v. 1.] Let this singer, then, ascend; but let this man sing out of each of your hearts, and let each one be this man. For when you say it individually, since you are all one in Christ, it is one man who says it: not, 'To you, Lord, we have lifted up our eyes,' but: *To you, O Lord, I have lifted up my eyes*[^6]. You ought indeed to suppose that each one of you is speaking; but it is that one above all who speaks, who is also spread throughout the whole world. It is that one who speaks, who says elsewhere: *From the ends of the earth I have cried to you, when my heart was in anguish*[^7]. Who is it that cries from the ends of the earth? what one man is spread out as far as the ends of the earth? Each man can cry out in his own region; but from the ends of the earth? But the inheritance of Christ, of which it was said: *I will give you the nations for your inheritance, and the ends of the earth for your possession*[^8], it is this that cries out and says: *From the ends of the earth I have cried to you, when my heart was in anguish.* Let our heart be in anguish, and let us cry out. Whence should our heart be in anguish? Not over those things which even the evil suffer here—because, for example, they suffer loss: for if the heart is in anguish over that, it is ashes. Because you have lost some one of your own by the will of God, if over that the heart is in anguish, what is great in it? Over this the hearts of unbelievers too are in anguish; these things are suffered also by those who have not yet believed in Christ. Whence should the heart of a Christian be in anguish? Because he does not yet live with Christ. Whence should the heart of a Christian be in anguish? Because he is a pilgrim and longs for his homeland. If your heart is in anguish over that, then even if you have been happy according to this world, you groan; and if all prosperous things flow together, and this world smiles on you from every side, yet you groan, because you see yourself set in a pilgrimage; and you feel that you have happiness already according to the eyes of fools, but not yet according to the promise of Christ: seeking that, you groan; seeking that, you long; and by longing you ascend, and by ascending you sing the *Song of ascents*, and by singing the *Song of ascents* you say: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven.*

**3.** Ascending, where was he to lift up his eyes, except there where he was tending and longing to ascend? For he ascends from earth to heaven. Behold the earth below, which we tread with our feet; behold also the heaven above, which we see with our eyes; and ascending we sing: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven.* Where, then, are the ladders? For we behold such great intervals between heaven and earth, so great a separation, such great expanses of space: we wish to ascend there, and we see no ladders. Are we perhaps deceiving ourselves, because we sing the *Song of ascents*, that is, the song of ascension? We ascend to heaven if we think of God, who has made an ascent in the heart. What is it to ascend in the heart? To make progress toward God. As everyone who fails does not descend but falls, so everyone who makes progress ascends—but only if he so makes progress as not to grow proud; if he so ascends as not to fall. But if in making progress he grows proud, in ascending he falls again. But that he may not grow proud, what ought he to do? Let him lift up his eyes to him who dwells in heaven; let him not attend to himself. For every proud man attends to himself, and he seems great to himself who pleases himself. But he who pleases himself pleases a foolish man; for he himself is a fool when he pleases himself. He alone pleases safely who pleases God. And who is it that pleases God? He whom God has pleased. God cannot displease himself; let him please you too, so that you may please him. But he will not be able to please you unless you have displeased yourself. But if you displease yourself, take your eye off yourself. For why do you attend to yourself? For if you truly attend to yourself, you find in yourself something that displeases you; and you say to God: *My sin is ever before me*[^9]. Let your sin be before you, so that it may not be before God; and do not be before yourself, so that you may be before God. For just as we wish that God may not turn his face away from us, so we wish that he may turn his face away from our sins: for both are sung to him in the Psalms. *Do not turn your face away from me*[^10]: this is the voice of the psalm, so it is our voice; and he who says: *Do not turn your face away from me*—see what he says in another place: *Turn your face away from my sins*[^11]. If you wish him to turn his face away from your sins, you yourself turn your face away from yourself, and do not turn it away from your sins. For if you do not turn your face away from them, you yourself are angry at your sins; but if you do not turn your face away from your sins, you acknowledge them, and he pardons them.

**4.** But take your eyes off yourself, lift them to him, and say: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven.* Brothers, if we understand heaven in a bodily way, as this thing we see with our bodily eyes, we shall truly err so far as to think that we cannot ascend there except by setting up ladders or some contrivances; but if we ascend spiritually, we ought to understand heaven spiritually; if the ascent is in our affection, heaven is in justice. What, then, is the heaven of God? All holy souls, all just souls. For the apostles too, although they were on earth in the flesh, were heaven; because the Lord sitting in them was walking through the whole world. So then he dwells in heaven. How? As he says in another psalm: *But you dwell in the holy place, the praise of Israel*[^12]. He who dwells in heaven dwells in the holy place; what is the holy place, except his temple? *For the temple of God is holy, which you are*[^13]. But all who are still weak and walk according to faith[^14] are, according to faith, the temple of God; one day they will also be, according to form, the temple of God. How long are they the temple according to faith? As long as Christ dwells in them through faith; as the apostle says: *that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts*[^15]. But there are already heavens in whom God now dwells through form, seeing him face to face: all the holy angels, all the holy Virtues, Powers, Seats, Dominions, that heavenly Jerusalem from which we groan in our pilgrimage, and which by longing we pray for: there God dwells. There this man lifted his faith, there he ascended by affection, by longing; and that very longing makes the soul sweat out the filth of its sins and be cleansed of every stain, so that it too may become heaven; because it lifted its eyes to him who dwells in heaven. For if we understand that bodily heaven which we see with our eyes to be the dwelling of God, then God's dwelling will pass away, because heaven and earth will pass away[^16]. And then, before God made heaven and earth, where did he dwell? But someone says: And before God made the saints, where did he dwell? God dwelt in himself, he dwelt with himself, and God is with himself. And when he deigns to dwell in the saints, the saints are not the house of God in such a way that, if it were taken away, God would fall. For we dwell in a house in one way, God in the saints in another: you dwell in a house; if it is taken away, you fall; but God so dwells in the saints that, if he himself departed, they would fall. Whoever, then, so bears God as to be the temple of God, let him not think that he so bears God as to make God afraid that he might withdraw himself. Woe to that man if God withdraws himself, because he himself falls; for God abides ever in himself. The things in which we dwell contain us; the things in which God dwells, he himself contains. See now how great is the difference between our dwelling and God's; and so let the soul say: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven*—so that it may understand that God has no need of heaven to dwell in, but heaven has need of him, that it may be inhabited by him.

**5.** [v. 2.] What, then, follows, since he said: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven*? How have you lifted up your eyes? *Behold, as the eyes of servants are on the hands of their masters, and as the eyes of a maidservant are on the hands of her mistress, so are our eyes on the Lord our God, until he have mercy on us*[^17]. We are both servants and a maidservant: he is both master and mistress. Or what do these words mean? and what do these likenesses of things mean? Let your charity attend a little. It is no wonder if we are servants and he is master; but it is a wonder if we are a maidservant and he is a mistress. But neither is this a wonder, that we are a maidservant; for we are the Church: nor is that a wonder, that he too is a mistress; for he is the strength and the wisdom of God. Hear, then, the apostle saying: *But we preach Christ crucified: to the Jews indeed a stumbling block, but to the Gentiles foolishness; but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the strength of God and the wisdom of God*[^18]. So that now the people may be the servant, and the Church the maidservant; *Christ the strength of God and the wisdom of God*: you have heard both: *Christ the strength of God and the wisdom of God.* When, then, you hear 'Christ,' lift up your eyes to the hands of your master; when you hear 'the strength of God and the wisdom of God,' lift up your eyes to the hands of your mistress: because you are both a servant and a maidservant; a servant, because you are the people; a maidservant, because you are the Church. But that maidservant has found great dignity with God: she has been made a wife. But until she comes to those spiritual embraces, where she may securely enjoy to the full the one whom she has loved and for whom she has sighed in this long pilgrimage, she is a betrothed; and she has received a great pledge, the blood of the bridegroom, for whom she securely sighs. Nor is it said to her: Do not love—as is sometimes said to a virgin already betrothed but not yet married: and rightly is it said: Do not love; when you have been made a wife, then love—rightly is it said, because the desire is over-hasty and out of order, and not chaste, to love one whom she does not know whether she will marry. For it can happen that one man betroths and another takes her to wife. But since there is no other who may be set before Christ, let her love him securely; and before she is joined to him, let her love and sigh from afar and from a far-off pilgrimage. He alone will take her to wife, because he alone gave such a pledge. For who can take a woman to wife in such a way as to die for her whom he wishes to marry? For if he were willing to die for her, there would be no one to marry her. But he, secure, died for the bride whom, rising again, he was to marry. Nevertheless, brothers, meanwhile let us be as servants and a maidservant. It was indeed said: *I will not call you servants, but friends*[^19]: but did the Lord perhaps say this to the disciples only? Hear the apostle Paul saying: *And so now*, he says, *he is no longer a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir through God*[^20]. He was speaking to the people, he was speaking to the faithful. So then now, in the name of the Lord, redeemed by his blood, washed in his bath, we are sons—we are a son; for we are so many as to be one in him. What, then, does it mean that we still speak as servants? Can we have as great merit in the Church—although already made sons out of servants—as the apostle Paul himself had? And yet what does he say in his Letter? *Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus*[^21]. If he still calls himself a servant, through whom the Gospel was preached to us, how much more ought we to acknowledge our condition, so that his grace may be the greater in us? For first he made servants of those whom he redeemed. For that blood is a price for servants, a pledge for the bride. Acknowledging, then, our condition—even though we are already sons through grace, yet servants on account of being creatures, since the whole of creation serves God—let us say: 'As the eyes of servants are on the hands of their masters, and as the eyes of a maidservant are on the hands of her mistress, so are our eyes on the Lord our God, until he have mercy on us.'

**6.** He said too why, just as servants have their eyes on the hands of their master, and as maidservants have their eyes on the hands of their mistress, *so*, he says, *are our eyes on the Lord our God.* And, as though you were asking: For what purpose? *Until*, he says, *he have mercy on us.* What kind of servants, then, did he wish us to understand, brothers, who have their eyes on the hands of their masters; and what kind of maidservants, who have their eyes on the hands of their mistress, until their mistress has mercy on them? Who are these servants and maidservants, who thus have their eyes on the hands of their masters, except those who are ordered to be beaten? *Our eyes are on the Lord our God, until he have mercy on us.* How? *As the eyes of servants are on the hands of their masters, and as the eyes of a maidservant are on the hands of her mistress.* So both the men and the women, until either the master or the mistress have mercy. Suppose, then, that some master has ordered a servant to be beaten: the servant is flogged, he feels the pain of the blows; he watches the hands of his master, until he says: Spare him. For by 'hand' he means power itself. So what do we say, brothers? Our Lord ordered us to be beaten, and our mistress the wisdom of God ordered us to be beaten; and in this life we are flogged, and this whole mortal life is our flogging. Hear the voice of the psalm: *For iniquity you have chastised man; and you have made my soul waste away like a spider*[^22]. Attend, brothers, how frail the spider is, so that by a light touch it is crushed and dies. And lest we should think that we have only our flesh frail through the weakness of mortality, he did not say: You have made me waste away—lest we should understand it of the flesh; but he said: *You have made my soul waste away like a spider.* For nothing is frailer than our soul set in the midst of the temptations of this world, in the midst of the groanings and birth-pangs of troubles; nothing is frailer than it, until it cleaves to the heavenly solidity and is in the temple of God, whence it no longer falls; for first, in order that it might come to this frailty and wasting away, it was made frail like a spider, and was cast out of paradise. Then the servant was ordered to be beaten. My brothers, see from what we are flogged. In all who from the beginning of the human race were born, in all who now are, in all who shall hereafter be born, Adam is flogged. Adam is flogged, that is, the human race; and many have so hardened themselves that they do not even feel their blows. But those of that very race who have been made sons have received a sense of pain: they feel that they are flogged, and they know who ordered them to be flogged; and they have lifted up their eyes to him who dwells in heaven; and so their eyes are on the hands of their Lord, until he have mercy, as the eyes of servants are on the hands of their masters, and as the eyes of a maidservant are on the hands of her mistress. You see some happy in this world, laughing, boasting; they are not flogged: nay rather, they are flogged worse; by this very thing they are beaten more heavily, in that they have already lost their sense. Let them wake up, and be flogged; let them feel that they are flogged, let them know that they are flogged, and grieve that they are flogged. For *he who adds knowledge adds sorrow*; this Scripture has said[^23]. Therefore the Lord in the Gospel: *Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted*[^24].

**7.** [v. 3.] We shall hear the voice of a man who is flogged; and let these be the words of each one of us even when things go well with us. For who would not understand that he is flogged when he is sick, when he is in prison, when perhaps in chains, when perhaps he suffers robbers? When some troubles are inflicted on him by wicked men, he feels that he is flogged. It is a great perception, to see that one is flogged even when things go well with one. For Scripture does not say in Job: Human life abounds in temptations; but it says: *Is not human life on earth a temptation?*[^25] It called the whole of life itself a temptation. So your whole life on earth consists of your blows. Lament as long as you live on earth: whether you live happily, or are set in some tribulation, cry out: *I have lifted up my eyes to you, who dwell in heaven.* To the hands of the Lord, who ordered you to be beaten, to whom you say in another psalm: *For iniquity you have chastised man; and you have made my soul waste away like a spider*[^26]; cry out to the hands of the one who beats, and say: *Have mercy on us, O Lord, have mercy on us*[^27]. Are these not the words of one who is flogged: *Have mercy on us, O Lord, have mercy on us*?

**8.** *For we have been greatly filled with contempt. Our soul has been very greatly filled; a reproach to those who abound, and contempt for the proud*[^28]. He who is despised is held in contempt. All who wish to live devoutly according to Christ must necessarily suffer reproaches, must necessarily be held in contempt by those who do not wish to live devoutly, whose happiness is wholly earthly. Those are mocked who call 'happiness' what they cannot see with their eyes, and it is said to them: What do you believe, you madman? Do you see what you believe? Has anyone come back from the dead and reported to you what goes on there? Behold, I see what I love, and enjoy it. You are despised, because you hope for what you do not see; and he despises you who, as it were, holds what he sees. Look closely whether he himself holds it: do not be troubled; see whether he holds it, that he may not exult over you; lest, while you think him happy in the present, you lose the true happiness to come: do not be troubled; look closely whether he holds it. Either this slips away from him, the thing that he holds, or he himself slips away from the thing he holds; one must necessarily pass: either he himself through his goods, or his goods through him. Through whom do his goods pass? Through one who, while alive, will be brought down. Who passes through his own goods? He who dies amid his riches; for when he dies, he does not carry them with him to the underworld. 'I have my house,' he boasts. You ask: Which house of yours? The one my father left me. And he, whence did he have this house? Our grandfather left it to him. Go back also to the great-grandfather, and from there to the great-great-grandfather, and already he cannot say the names. Are you not rather frightened by this—that you notice that many have passed through that house, and that none of them took it with him to the eternal home? Your father left it here; he passed through it: so you too will pass through. If, then, you only pass through in your house, it is an inn for those passing through, not a dwelling for those who stay. And yet, because we hope for the things that are to come, and sigh for the happiness to come, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be, although we are already sons of God[^29]—because *our life is hidden with Christ in God*[^30]—*we have been filled with contempt*, that is, with scorn, by those who seek or have happiness in this world.

**9.** *Our soul has been very greatly filled; a reproach to those who abound, and contempt for the proud.* We were asking who they are *who abound*; he explained it to you when he said *the proud.* For what is *a reproach* is the same as *contempt*; and what is *those who abound* is the same as *the proud.* It is a repetition of the same thought: *a reproach to those who abound, and contempt for the proud.* Why do the proud abound? Because they wish to be happy here. What then? When they too are wretched, do they abound? But perhaps when they are wretched, they do not exult over us. Let your charity attend. They exult, perhaps, when they are happy, when they boast in the pomp of their riches, when they boast in the windiness of false honors; then they exult over us, and as it were say: Behold, things go well with me, I enjoy present things; let those depart from me who promise what they do not show; what I see, I hold; what I see, I enjoy; let things go well with me in this life. You, be more secure; for Christ has risen, and has taught you what he will give in the other life: be sure that he gives it. But that man exults, because he holds. Bear with him as he exults, and you will laugh at him as he groans: for afterward the time will come when these very men will say: *These are the ones whom we once held in derision*[^31]. These are the words of the Book of Wisdom; for Scripture has revealed to us what these very men will say who now mock us, and who despise us, and by whom we are filled with reproach and contempt: what words they will then have, when they will be despised by the truth. For they will see flashing at the right hand those whom they had held in contempt while mingled with them, when there shall have been fulfilled in them what the apostle said: *When Christ your life shall appear, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^32]; and they will say: *These are the ones whom we once held in derision, and as a byword of reproach. We fools thought their life madness, and their end without honor: how have they been counted among the sons of God, and how is their lot among the saints?* And they will go on in that discourse and add: *So we have strayed from the way of truth, and the light of justice did not shine for us, and the sun did not rise for us. What has pride profited us? and what has the boasting of riches brought us?*[^33] Then you do not exult over them, because they already exult over themselves. Until that comes to pass, brothers, let us lift our eyes to him who dwells in heaven; and let us not take our eyes off him, until he have mercy on us, and free us from every temptation and reproach and contempt.

**10.** To this is added that sometimes even those who are under the lash of temporal unhappiness exult over us. You find someone, for the deserts of his iniquities—whether by the hidden judgment of God or by a manifest condemnation—sent to prison, made to wear a chain: and he himself exults. And when it is said to him: Why did you not live well? behold to what you have come by living badly—he answers: Why do those who live well suffer these things? But they suffer for this reason, that they may be tested, that they may be exercised by temptations, that they may make progress through their floggings; for God flogs every son whom he receives[^34]. And if he flogged his Only-begotten, who was without sin, and handed him over for all[^35], how ought we to be flogged, who have done what we are flogged for? When we have said these things, they still grow proud even over their unhappiness, and, afflicted but not yet humbled, they answer and say: These are the words of empty Christians, who believe what they do not see. If they too exult, what do we think, brothers—as though they were not mentioned in this psalm, when it said: *a reproach to those who abound, and contempt for the proud*—since Christians are exulted over even by those who do not abound, but who, though they are in want and hardship, themselves do not cease to exult? Surely, then: *a reproach to those who abound*: or is it perhaps that no one is found who exults even when set in calamity? Did not the robber exult, who was crucified with the crucified Lord[^36]? So then, if even those who do not abound exult, why does the psalm say: *a reproach to those who abound*? If we examine carefully, they too abound. Whence do they abound? For unless they abounded, they would not be proud. For one abounds in money, and from that is proud; another abounds in honors, and from that is proud; another thinks he abounds in justice, and from that grows proud, which is worse. Those who seem not to abound in money seem to themselves to abound in justice against God; and, set in calamities, they justify themselves, accuse God, and say: What have I committed, or what have I done? And you say: Look back, recall your sins, if you have done nothing. The conscience is a little tickled, and he returns to himself, and thinks over his evil deeds; and when he has thought over his evil deeds, not even so will he confess that he suffers what he deserves: but he says: Behold, I have plainly done many things; but I see that many have done worse, and suffer nothing bad. He is just against God. So he too abounds; he has a breast full of justice; since to him God seems to do evil, and he himself seems to suffer unjustly. And if you gave him a ship to steer, he would go to shipwreck with it: yet he wishes to shake God out of the governance of this world, and himself to hold the helm of creation, and to distribute to all sorrows and joys, punishments and rewards. Unhappy soul! And why do you wonder? he abounds, but he abounds in wickedness, abounds in malice; and the more he abounds in wickedness, the more he seems to himself to abound in justice.

**11.** But indeed the Christian man ought not to abound, but ought to recognize himself as poor; and if he has riches, he ought to know that those are not the true riches, so that he may long for others. For he who longs for false riches does not seek the true; but he who seeks the true is still poor; and rightly he says: *I am poor and in pain*[^37]. Again, that man who is both poor and full of wickedness—whence is he said to abound? Because it displeases him that he is poor, and that very justice seems to abound in his heart against the justice of God. And what is the abundance of our justice? However much justice there may be in us, it is some kind of dew compared to that fountain; compared to that great fullness, they are certain drops that soften our life and dissolve hard iniquity. Now let us long to be fattened from the full fountain of justice, let us long to be satisfied with that richness of which it is said in the psalm: *They will be inebriated with the richness of your house, and you will give them to drink of the torrent of your delight*[^38]. But while we are here, let us understand that we are needy and in want, not only of these riches which are not the true riches, but also of health itself. And when we are well, let us understand that we are weak. For as long as this body hungers and thirsts, as long as this body is wearied by keeping awake, wearied by standing, wearied by walking, wearied by sitting, wearied by eating; wherever it turns for relief from weariness, there it finds another weariness: there is not, then, perfect health, not even in the body itself. So those are not riches, but beggary; because the more they abound, the more want and greed grow. This is not health of the body, but weakness. We are daily soothed by the medicines of God, because we eat and drink: the things set before us are themselves medicines. Brothers, if you wish to see what disease holds us: one who fasts for seven days is consumed by hunger. So that hunger is there; but you do not feel it, because you tend to it daily: so not even our health itself is perfect.

**12.** Let your charity attend to how we understand ourselves to be needy, so that we may rejoice toward him, and lift our eyes to him who dwells in heaven. Those are not the true riches; they increase desire all the more in those who possess them. That is not true health of the body; for a weakness is carried about everywhere, deficient: wherever it turns, it fails. In its very aid you will find no firmness: it grows tired standing; it wishes to sit; will it endure even by sitting? The very thing it chose for itself so as not to be wearied—there it has its failing. It is wearied by keeping awake, it is about to sleep: does it not fail because it has slept? It is wearied by fasting, it is about to refresh itself: if it refreshes itself too much, from that it fails. In nothing can this weakness persevere. What of justice? How much justice is there amid so many temptations? We can refrain from murder, from adultery, from theft, from perjury, from frauds: but can we refrain from wicked thoughts? can we refrain even from the suggestions of evil desires? What, then, is our justice? Let us, then, hunger for the whole, thirst for the whole—for true riches, true health, and true justice. What are the true riches? That heavenly dwelling in Jerusalem. For who is called rich on this earth? When a rich man is praised, what is said? He is very rich; he lacks nothing. The praise of the one praising is the sure praise: for that is not so, when it is said: He lacks nothing. Look closely whether he lacks nothing. If he desires nothing, he lacks nothing: but if he still desires greater things than those he has, riches have come to him for this, that want might grow. But in that city there will be true riches, because there nothing will be lacking to us; because we will neither be in need of anything, and there will be true health. What is true health? When death shall have been swallowed up in victory, and when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality[^39]; then there will be true health, then there will be true and perfect justice, so that we can not only do nothing evil, but not even think it. But now, needy, poor, in want, in pain, we sigh, we groan, we pray, we lift our eyes to God: since both those who are happy in this world despise us—for they abound—and those who are unhappy in this world despise us; because they too abound, and there is justice in their heart, but a false one. Therefore they do not arrive at the true, because they are filled with the false. But you, that you may arrive at the true, be in want and a beggar for justice itself; and hear the Gospel: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice, for they shall be satisfied*[^40].
