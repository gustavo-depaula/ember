# On the Same Psalm 118 (17)

**1.** [v. 73.] When God made man from dust, and gave him life by a breath, it is not there recorded that he made him with hands. Why, then, it has seemed to some that God made the rest by his word, but man as something special by his hands, I do not see; unless perhaps, because man's body is read to have been formed from dust, they suppose it could not have been made except by hands; and they do not attend to what is written in the Gospel about the Word of God: *All things were made through him*[^1]—which cannot stand, if the human body too was not made through the Word. But they bring forward a testimony from this psalm, and say: Behold where a man cries out most plainly, *Your hands have made me, and fashioned me.* As if it were not also plainly said, *I shall behold the heavens, the works of your fingers*[^2]; and no less plainly, *And the heavens are the works of your hands*[^3]; and much more plainly, *And his hands fashioned the dry land*[^4]. The hands of God, then, are the power of God. Or if the plural number troubles them, because it is not said "your hand," but *your hands;* let them take the hands of God as the power and wisdom of God, both of which together are called the one Christ[^5]; who is also understood as the arm of the Lord, where it is read, *And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed*[^6]? Or let them take the hands of God as the Son and the Holy Spirit; because the Holy Spirit too is a co-worker with the Father and the Son: whence the Apostle says, *All these things one and the same Spirit works*[^7]. He said *one and the same* for this reason: lest as many spirits be thought as works, not because the Spirit works without the Father and the Son. It is free, then, however the hands of God be understood; provided that neither the things he makes by hands be denied to be made by the Word, nor the things he makes by the Word be thought not made by hands; nor, on account of the hands, be a bodily form, or one hand the left and another the right, believed to be in the working God; nor, on account of the Word, a sound of the mouth, or a passing motion of the mind.

**2.** Nor have there been lacking those who would so distinguish these two words, *made me, and fashioned me,* as to say that God made the soul, but fashioned the body; because of the soul God said, *Every breath I have made*[^8]; but of the body it is read, *And God fashioned man, the dust of the earth*[^9]: as though indeed everything that is fashioned is made, yet not everything that is made is fashioned. And therefore they say that the soul is rather made than fashioned, because it is not body but spirit; as though it were not written, *Who fashioned the spirit of man within him*[^10]. Nevertheless, when both these words are set down about man in one place, and neither one of man—that is, both soul and body—is denied to have been divinely established, it is not inelegant that single things be distributed to single ones, so that the soul be understood as made, but the body as fashioned, whether formed or molded. For some interpreters were unwilling to say *fashioned me,* but *molded me,* preferring to decline less in Latin from the Greek rather than to say *fashioned,* which is sometimes also wont to be said of pretense.

**3.** But whether this was said with respect to Adam, from whom, since all men have been propagated, which of men, when he was made, cannot say that he too was made, by reason of his origin and seed? Or whether it could also rightly be said with respect to this, that each one comes to be even from his parents not without the work of God, he creating, they begetting? For if the operative power of God be withdrawn from things, they perish; nor does anything at all, whether from the elements of the world, or from parents, or from seeds, come to be, if God does not work it. On account of which he says to the prophet Jeremiah, *Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you*[^11]. But did God make man without understanding, whether the first or each one of those being born, that he should now say to him, *Your hands have made me, and molded me; give me understanding?* Is not understanding implanted in human nature itself, so that by it man is distinguished from the beast? Or has it been so deformed by sinning that this too must be reformed in it? On account of which the Apostle too says to all who pertain to regeneration, *Be renewed in the spirit of your mind*[^12]; and surely understanding is in the mind. Hence again he says, *Be reformed in newness of your sense*[^13]; but of those who were not partakers of this regeneration: *This,* he says, *I say and testify in the Lord, that you no longer walk as the Gentiles walk, in the vanity of their mind, darkened in understanding, alienated from the way of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart*[^14]. On account of these inner eyes, then, whose blindness is not to understand, that they may be opened and more and more cleared, hearts are cleansed by faith. For although, unless he understands something, no one can believe in God; yet by that very faith by which he believes, he is healed, that he may understand greater things. For some things are those which, unless we understand, we do not believe; and others which, unless we believe, we do not understand. For since faith is from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ[^15], how does he believe one preaching the faith who—to be silent about other things—does not understand the very tongue he speaks? But unless there were again some things which we cannot understand unless we first believe, the prophet would not say, *Unless you believe, you will not understand*[^16]. Our understanding, then, advances toward understanding the things it may believe, and faith advances toward believing the things it may understand; and that these very things may be more and more understood, the mind advances in understanding itself. But this comes about not by its own, as it were natural, powers, but by God helping and granting; just as it comes about by medicine, not by nature, that a damaged eye recovers the power of seeing. Whoever, then, says to God, *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments,* is not altogether devoid of it, like a beast; nor so, though a man, as to be reckoned in the number of those who *walk in the vanity of their mind, darkened in understanding, alienated from the way of God.* For if he were such, he would not say even this. But it is no small understanding to know from whom understanding is to be begged. And it must be considered how much more deeply the divine commandments are to be understood, when, to learn them, one who already so understands, and who has already said before that he kept the utterances of God, asks that understanding still be given him.

**4.** But what our interpreters rendered, *Give me understanding,* the Greek said more briefly with a single word; for he comprised it in one word, *Give me understanding,* which in Latin cannot be said in one word: just as if "Heal me" could not be said in Latin, and one said "Give me health," as it is said here, *Give me understanding;* or "Make me whole," as can also be said here, "Make me understanding." This indeed even an angel could do; for he said to Daniel, *I have come to give you understanding*[^17]: and this word is in Greek, which is also here; as if a Latin should say "to give you health," which the Greek would have said "to heal you." For the Latin interpreter would not use a circumlocution by saying "to give you understanding," if, as it can be said from "health," "to heal you," so it could be said from "understanding," "to en-understand you." But if even an angel can do this, what is it that this man begs of God that it be done for him? Is it because God had commanded the angel to do it? Just so: for Christ is understood to have commanded the angel to do this, in that place where the prophet says, *And it came to pass, when I, Daniel, saw the vision, and was seeking understanding, behold, there stood before me as the appearance of a man, and I heard the voice of a man between the banks of the Ulai, and he called and said, Make him understand the vision*[^18]: and there in the Greek is this word which is here also. God, therefore, through himself, because he is light, enlightens godly minds, that they may understand the things that are divinely said or shown. But if for this he uses an angel as a minister, the angel can indeed do something in the mind of man, that it may take in the light of God, and through this may understand; but it is said to give understanding to man, and as it were, so to speak, to "en-understand" man, in the way someone is said to give light to a house, or to enlighten a house, for whom he makes a window; though he does not penetrate and illumine it with his own light, but only opens the entrance by which it may be penetrated and illumined. But neither did the sun, which illumines the house through the window, itself create that house, or the man who made the window in the house, or itself command him to make it, or itself help the one making it, or do anything that it might be opened by which it might pour in its light: but God both made the rational and intellectual mind of man, by which it can take in his light; and made such an angel, who could work something whereby the human mind might be helped toward taking in the light of God; and so helps that very mind, that it may receive the angelic working; and so enlightens it from himself, that it may perceive, by advancing, not only the things that are shown by the truth, but the truth itself. But since we have treated of matters indeed necessary, as I judge, yet with a lengthy discussion, let us close this sermon at this end, deferring the following verses of this psalm.
