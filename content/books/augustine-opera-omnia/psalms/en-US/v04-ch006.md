# Exposition of Psalm 78

**1.** [v. 1.] On the title of this psalm, so brief and simple, I do not think we must linger. But the prophecy which we read prefaced here, we evidently know to have been fulfilled. For when these things were being sung in the times of King David, nothing of the kind had yet befallen the city of Jerusalem from the hostility of the nations, nor the temple of God, which had not yet even been built. For after the death of David, who does not know that his son Solomon made a temple for God? It is said, then, as though past, what in the spirit was seen to be future: *O God, the nations have come into your inheritance.* By which custom of speaking that too was prophesied of the Lord's passion: *They gave gall for my food, and in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink*[^1]; and other things which in that same psalm are narrated as to come, as though done. Nor is it to be wondered at that these things are said to God. For they are not indicated to one who is ignorant, by whose revealing they are foreknown; but the soul speaks with God in the affection of piety which God knows. For the angels too, what they announce to men, they announce to those who do not know; but what they announce to God, they announce to one who knows, when they offer him our prayers, and in an ineffable manner consult the eternal truth concerning their own acts, as an unchangeable law. And so this man of God too says to God what he learns from God, as a disciple to a master who is not ignorant, but judging; and so either approving what he taught, or reproving what he did not teach: especially because under the appearance of one praying the Prophet transfigures into himself those who would then exist when these things were to come. But it is customary in prayer to say to God what he has done in taking vengeance, and to add the petition that he may now have mercy and spare. In this manner here too they are said by the one who foretells them, as though they were said by those to whom they happened; and that very lamentation and entreaty is a prophesying.

**2.** *O God, the nations have come into your inheritance; they have defiled your holy temple, they have set Jerusalem as a place to keep fruit. They have set the dead bodies of your servants as food for the birds of heaven, the flesh of your saints for the beasts of the earth. They have poured out their blood like water around Jerusalem, and there was none to bury them.* If any of us should think that in this prophecy is to be understood that devastation of Jerusalem which was done by Titus the Roman emperor, when now the Lord Jesus Christ, after his resurrection and ascension, was being preached among the nations; it does not occur to me how that people could already be called the inheritance of God, not holding Christ, by whose rejection and killing it was made reprobate, which would not believe in him even after his resurrection, and moreover killed his martyrs. For of that people of Israel, whoever believed in Christ, to whom the presentation of Christ was made, and in a manner the rendering of the promise, wholesome and fruitful, of whom the Lord himself says: *I am not sent except to the sheep that are lost, of the house of Israel*[^2], they from among those *are the children of the promise,* they *are reckoned as seed*[^3], they belong to the inheritance of God. Of these are Joseph, a righteous man, and the Virgin Mary who bore Christ[^4]: of these is John the Baptist, the friend of the bridegroom, and his parents Zechariah and Elizabeth[^5]: of these is the old man Simeon and the widow Anna, who heard Christ not speaking by the sense of the body[^6]; but still an infant not speaking, they recognized in the spirit: of these are the blessed apostles: of these is Nathanael in whom there was no guile[^7]: of these is that other Joseph who also himself awaited the kingdom of God[^8]: of these is that great multitude which preceded and followed his beast, saying: *Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord*[^9]; in which there was also the gathering of children of whom he said it was fulfilled: *Out of the mouth of infants and sucklings you have perfected praise*[^10]. Of these too are those after his resurrection, of whom on one day three, and on another five thousand were baptized[^11], fused into one soul and one heart by the fire of love; of whom no one said anything was his own, but all things were common to them[^12]: of these are the holy deacons, of whom Stephen was crowned with martyrdom before the apostles[^13]: of these are so many Churches of Judea which were in Christ[^14], to whom Paul was unknown by face, but known by his most famous savagery, but more known by the most merciful grace of Christ: of these is he himself, according to the prophecy prefaced concerning him: *A ravening wolf, in the morning seizing prey, and in the evening dividing the spoils*[^15], that is, first seizing as a persecutor unto death, afterward feeding as a preacher unto life. These were, from that people, the inheritance of God. Whence the least of the apostles himself[^16], the teacher of the Gentiles, says: *I say then: Has God cast off his people? God forbid. For I too am an Israelite, of the seed of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin. God has not cast off his people whom he foreknew*[^17]. This people which from that nation came to the body of Christ, is the inheritance of God. For what the Apostle says: *God has not cast off his people whom he foreknew,* surely answers to that psalm where it is written: *For the Lord will not cast off his people*: but there it follows: *And he will not forsake his inheritance*[^18]; where it evidently appears that such a people is the inheritance of God. For that the Apostle might say this, he had recalled above the prophetic testimony concerning the foretold future unbelief of the people of Israel: *All day long I stretched out my hands to a people that does not believe and contradicts*[^19]. Here, then, lest anyone, misunderstanding, should think that that whole people was condemned by the charge of unbelief and contradiction, he straightway subjoined: *I say then: Has God cast off his people? God forbid. For I too am an Israelite, of the race of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin.* Here he shows what people he meant, namely of the former people, which, if God had reprobated and condemned it wholly, he himself surely would not be Christ's apostle, an Israelite of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin. But he adduces a very necessary testimony, saying: *Or do you not know what the Scripture says in Elijah, how he intercedes with God against Israel? Lord, they have killed your prophets, they have dug down your altars, and I am left alone, and they seek my life. But what does the divine response say to him? I have left myself seven thousand men who have not bowed their knees before Baal. So then, even at this time a remnant has been saved according to the election of grace*[^20]. These are the remnant from that nation of God's inheritance; not those of whom a little after he says: *But the rest were blinded.* For he says: *What then? What Israel sought, this it did not obtain: but the election obtained it; but the rest were blinded*[^21]. This election, then, this remnant, this people of God which God did not cast off, is called his inheritance. But in that Israel which did not obtain this, in those rest who were blinded, there was no longer the inheritance of God of which it could be said, after Christ's glorification in heaven, in the time of the emperor Titus: *O God, the nations have come into your inheritance,* and the rest which seem to be foretold in this psalm concerning the devastation of that people and temple and city.

**3.** Accordingly, we must either understand those things that were done by other enemies, before Christ had come in the flesh (for there was then another inheritance of God, where there were also holy Prophets, when the migration into Babylon was made, and that nation was gravely devastated, and under Antiochus the Maccabees too, having endured horrible things, were most gloriously crowned[^22]. For those things are said in this psalm which are wont to happen even in the slaughter of wars); or certainly, if the inheritance of God here recalled is to be taken after the resurrection and ascension of the Lord; those things are to be understood which his Church suffered from the worshipers of idols and enemies of the name of Christ, in so great a number of martyrs. For although Asaph is interpreted "synagogue," which is "gathering," and that name has more usually settled upon the nation of the Jews; yet that this Church too can be called a gathering, and that that ancient people was called a Church, we have already shown sufficiently and openly in another psalm. This Church, then, this inheritance of God, was gathered out of the circumcision and the uncircumcision—that is, out of the people of Israel and out of the rest of the Gentiles, through *the stone which the builders rejected,* and *which became the head of the corner*[^23]; in which corner, as it were, two walls coming from different sides were joined. *For he is our peace, who made both one, that he might create the two in himself into one new man, making peace, and reconcile both in one body to God*[^24]; in which body we are the children of God, crying, *Abba, Father*[^25]: *Abba* on account of their tongue, *Father* on account of ours; for this is *Abba,* which is *Father.* Whence the Lord, who said: *I am not sent except to the sheep that are lost, of the house of Israel*[^26], showing that to that nation the promise of his presence was rendered; yet says in another place: *I have other sheep that are not of this fold; I must bring them too, that there may be one flock and one shepherd*[^27]: signifying the Gentiles, whom he was to bring, not indeed by his bodily presence, that that might be true: *I am not sent except to the sheep that are lost, of the house of Israel*; but yet through his Gospel, which the *beautiful feet of those announcing peace, announcing good things* were to disseminate[^28]: *For into all the earth their sound went out, and to the ends of the world their words*[^29]. Hence the Apostle too says: *I say, then, that Christ Jesus was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises of the fathers*: behold what is, *I am not sent except to the sheep that are lost, of the house of Israel.* Then the Apostle subjoins: *But that the Gentiles should glorify God for his mercy*: behold what is: *I have other sheep that are not of this fold; I must bring them too, that there may be one flock and one shepherd.* Which both are briefly said in that which the same apostle recalls from the prophet: *Rejoice, O Gentiles, with his people*[^30]. This one flock, then, under one shepherd, is the inheritance of God, not only of the Father, but also of the Son. For it is the Son's voice: *The lines have fallen for me in goodly places, for my inheritance is goodly to me*[^31]: and the voice of the inheritance itself is in the prophet: *O Lord our God, possess us*[^32]. This inheritance the dying Father did not leave to the Son; but the Son himself marvelously acquired it by his own death, and possessed it by his resurrection.

**4.** If, then, of this is to be understood what is sung in the prophecy of this psalm: *O God, the nations have come into your inheritance,* so that we take it that the nations came into the Church, not by believing, but by persecuting—that is, that they invaded it with the will of destroying and utterly ruining it, as so many examples of persecutions have shown; it is necessary that what follows: *They have defiled your holy temple,* be understood not in wood and stones, but in those very men, of whom, as of living stones, the apostle Peter says the house of God is constructed[^33]. Whence the apostle Paul too most openly says: *The temple of God is holy, which you are*[^34]. This temple, then, the persecutors defiled in those whom, by terrifying or torturing, they forced to deny Christ, and by vehement pressure made to supplicate idols: of whom penitence restored many, and cleansed them from that defilement. For it is the voice of a penitent: *And from my offense cleanse me*; and, *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me*[^35]. But as for what follows: *They have set Jerusalem as a place to keep fruit*: the Church itself too is rightly understood by this name: *Jerusalem the free, our mother, of which it is written: Rejoice, O barren one who do not bear, break forth and cry out, you who do not travail; for many are the children of the desolate, more than of her who has a husband*[^36]. *As a place to keep fruit,* which was said, I think is to be understood as the desolation which the destruction of persecution wrought: that is, as it were a place to keep fruit; for the places to keep fruit are deserted when the fruit has passed. And surely, when, with the Gentiles persecuting, the Church seemed deserted, the spirits of the martyrs passed to the heavenly table, as it were from the Lord's garden many and most sweet fruits.

**5.** [v. 2.] *They have set,* he says, *the dead bodies of your servants as food for the birds of heaven, the flesh of your saints for the beasts of the earth.* What was said, *dead bodies,* this was repeated, *flesh;* and what was said, *of your servants,* this was repeated, *of your saints:* only this was varied, *for the birds of heaven,* and *for the beasts of the earth.* Better surely have they interpreted who put *dead bodies,* than, as some, *mortal things.* For dead bodies are said only of the dead; but "mortal" is the name even of living bodies. When, then, as I said, the spirits of the martyrs had passed, as it were fruits, to their husbandman, they set their dead bodies and their flesh for the birds of heaven, and the beasts of the earth: as though anything of them would perish for the resurrection, when he is to restore all from the hidden recesses of nature, to whom even the hairs of our head are numbered[^37].

**6.** [v. 3.] *They have poured out their blood like water,* that is, abundantly and cheaply: *around Jerusalem.* If we here take Jerusalem as the earthly city, we understand their blood poured out around it, of those whom the enemies could find outside the walls. But if we understand that Jerusalem of which it was said: *Many are the children of the desolate, more than of her who has a husband,* its circuit is throughout the whole earth: for in that prophetic reading where it is written: *Many are the children of the desolate, more than of her who has a husband,* a little after it is said to her: *And he who rescued you, the God of Israel, shall be called the God of the whole earth*[^38]. The circuit, then, of this Jerusalem in this psalm is to be understood as far as that same Church had then been spread, fructifying and growing in the whole world, when in every part of it persecution raged, and made a slaughter of martyrs, whose blood was poured out like water, with great gains of heavenly treasures. But as for what was added: *And there was none to bury them*: either it ought not to seem incredible that in some places there was so great a terror, that there were no buriers at all of the holy bodies; or certainly because in many places the unburied corpses could lie long, until they were, in a manner, stolen by the religious and buried.

**7.** [v. 4.] *We have become,* he says, *a reproach to our neighbors.* Therefore precious—not in the sight of men, from whom this was a reproach, but in the sight of the Lord—is the death of his saints[^39]. *A mockery and a derision*: or, as some have interpreted, *an illusion, to those who are around us.* It is a repetition of the earlier statement: for what was said above, *a reproach,* this is repeated, *a mockery and a derision;* and what was said above, *to our neighbors,* this is repeated, *to those who are around us.* Accordingly, according to the earthly Jerusalem, the neighbors, and those around that nation, are surely understood as other nations: but according to Jerusalem the free, our mother, neighbors and those around it are those among whom the Church dwells with her enemies in the circuit of the world.

**8.** [v. 5.] Then now, pouring out a plain prayer, whence it may be understood that the recalling of the earlier affliction is not as it were an indication, but a lamentation: *How long,* he says, *O Lord, will you be angry unto the end, will your zeal blaze like fire?* He surely asks that God not be angry unto the end—that is, that so great a pressure, and tribulation, and devastation not persevere unto the end; but that he temper his rebuke, according to what is said in another psalm: *You will feed us with the bread of tears, and give us tears to drink in measure*[^40]. For, *how long, O Lord, will you be angry unto the end?* was so said, as if it were said: Do not, O Lord, be angry unto the end. And in what follows: *Will your zeal blaze like fire,* both are to be supplied, both *how long,* and *unto the end;* as if it were said: How long will your zeal blaze like fire unto the end? For here these two are to be supplied, as above the one word that was said, *they have set:* for though the former statement has: *They have set the dead bodies of your servants as food for the birds of heaven,* the latter does not have this word, where it was said: *The flesh of your saints for the beasts of the earth;* but there is surely supplied what the former has, *they have set.* Now the anger and zeal of God are not perturbations of God, as some, arguing the Scriptures they do not understand, suppose: but by the name of anger is understood the vengeance of iniquity; by the name of zeal the exaction of chastity, lest the soul despise the law of its Lord, and by fornicating from its God utterly perish. These things, then, in their very effect, are turbulent in the affliction of men; but in the disposition of God they are tranquil, to whom it was said: *But you, O Lord of hosts, judge with tranquility*[^41]. But it is sufficiently shown by these words that on account of sins, even of the faithful, these tribulations come about; although hence the glory of the martyrs flowers by the merit of patience, and of the discipline's measure piously borne under the Lord's scourge. This the Maccabees amid savage torments[^42], this the three men amid harmless flames[^43], this the holy Prophets in captivity, attest. For although they bear the paternal rebuke most bravely and most piously; yet they do not keep silent that these things happen by the merits of sins: for it is their voice too in the Psalms: *Chastening the Lord has chastened me, and has not given me over to death*[^44]. For he scourges every son whom he receives: and what son is there to whom his father does not give discipline[^45]?

**9.** [v. 6.] But as for what he adds: *Pour out your anger upon the nations that have not known you, and upon the kingdoms that have not called upon your name,* this too is a prophesying, not a wish. These things are not said with a wish of ill will, but are foretold, foreseen in the spirit; as concerning Judas the betrayer, the evils that were to befall him by his own merits were so prophesied, as though they were wished for. For just as the Prophet does not command Christ, although in the imperative mood he pronounces what he says: *Gird your sword upon your thigh, O most mighty; in your comeliness, and your beauty, both bend the bow, and go forward prosperously, and reign*[^46]: so he does not wish, but prophesies, who says: *Pour out your anger upon the nations that have not known you.* Which he repeats in his usual manner, saying: *And upon the kingdoms that have not called upon your name*: for the nations are repeated by the kingdoms; and that they did not know him, by this is repeated, that they did not call upon his name. How, then, is to be understood what the Lord says in the Gospel: *The servant who does not know the will of his lord, and does things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few; but the servant who knows the will of his lord, and does things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with many*[^47], if a greater anger of God is upon the nations that did not know the Lord? For in that he says: *Pour out your anger,* he sufficiently indicated by this word how great an anger he wished to be understood: whence afterward he says: *Render to our neighbors sevenfold*[^48]. Or is it because there is much difference between servants who, though they do not know the will of their Lord, yet call upon his name, and those estranged from the household of so great a head of the household, who so are ignorant of God, that they do not even call upon God? For they call upon, in his place, either idols, or demons, or any creature; not the Creator, who is blessed forever. For he does not signify these, of whom he prophesies this, as so ignorant of their Lord's will, that yet they fear the Lord himself; but as so ignorant of the Lord himself, that they do not even call upon him, and exist as enemies of his name. Much, then, is the difference between servants ignorant of their lord's will, and yet living in his household and in his house, and enemies not only refusing to know the lord himself, but also not calling upon his name, and moreover attacking him in his servants.

**10.** [v. 7.] Finally it follows: *For they have devoured Jacob, and made his place desolate.* For Jacob bore a figure of the Church, as Esau of the old synagogue; whence it was said: *And the elder shall serve the younger*[^49]. By this name can be understood the inheritance of God of which we were speaking, into which, to be invaded and devastated, the Gentiles came by persecuting, after the resurrection and ascension of the Lord. But how we are to view *the place of Jacob* must be understood. For the place of Jacob can rather be supposed to be that city in which there was also the temple to which the Lord had commanded that whole nation to assemble, for the sake of sacrificing and adoring and celebrating the Passover. For if the Prophet wished the assemblies of Christians, prohibited and suppressed by the persecutors, to be understood, he would seem to have had to say "places" made desolate, not "place." But we can take the singular number set in place of the plural, as "garment" for "garments," "soldier" for "soldiers," "beast" for "beasts": for it is the custom that many things of this kind be said, not only by those speaking in the common way, but even by the most eloquent authors of eloquence. Nor is this kind of speech foreign to the divine Scripture itself: for it too set "frog" for "frogs," "locust" for "locusts," and innumerable such things[^50]. But as for what was said: *They have devoured Jacob,* this is well understood, that they forced many, by terrifying them, to pass over into their malignant body—that is, into their fellowship.

**11.** [v. 8.] He surely remembers that, although by the anger of God things worthy of the merit of their worst will were to be rendered to them, yet they could not avail anything against his inheritance, unless he himself had willed to amend it by scourging it for its sins. Whence he subjoins: *Do not remember our ancient iniquities.* He does not say "past," which could be even more recent; but *ancient,* that is, coming from our parents: for to such iniquities damnation, not rebuke, is due. *Let your mercies speedily anticipate us.* Let them anticipate, surely, your judgment: *For mercy is exalted above judgment*; but *judgment without mercy* is *to him who has not done mercy*[^51]. But as for what he adds: *For we have become exceedingly poor,* he wishes us to understand the mercies of God to anticipate us to this end, that our poverty—that is, our weakness—may, with him having mercy, be helped to do his precepts, lest we come to his judgment to be condemned.

**12.** [v. 9.] Therefore it follows: *Help us, O God our savior.* By this word which he says, *our savior,* he sufficiently expounds what poverty he wished to be understood in what he had said: *For we have become exceedingly poor*: that is surely the weakness for which a *Savior* is necessary. But when he wishes us to be helped, he is neither ungrateful to grace, nor does he take away free choice: for he who is helped also does something through himself. He also added: *For the glory of your name, O Lord, free us,* that he who glories may glory not in himself, but in the Lord[^52]. *And be propitious,* he says, *to our sins for your name's sake*: not for our sake; for what else do our sins merit, but due and worthy punishments? but *be propitious to our sins for your name's sake.* So, then, you free us—that is, rescue us from evils—when you both help us to do righteousness, and are propitious to our sins, without which we are not in this life: *For in your sight no living person shall be justified*[^53]. But *sin is iniquity*[^54]; and *if you mark iniquities, who shall stand*[^55]?

**13.** [v. 10.] But as for what he adds: *Lest at any time they say among the nations: Where is their God?* it is rather to be taken for the sake of the nations themselves. For they perish ill who despair of the true God, supposing him either not to exist, or not to help his own, nor to be propitious to them. But that which follows: *And let the vengeance for the blood of your servants which was poured out become known among the nations before our eyes,* is understood either thus, when those who persecuted his inheritance believe in the true God; because that too is a vengeance, by which their savage iniquity is slain by the sword of the word of God, of which it was said: *Gird your sword*[^56]; or when the enemies persevering are punished at the end. For the bodily evils which they suffer in this world they can have in common with the good. There is also another kind of vengeance, by which the sinner, and the unbeliever, and the enemy sees and is angry at the spreading and fruitfulness of the Church in this world, after so great persecutions by which they thought it would utterly perish, and gnashes his teeth and wastes away[^57]: for who would dare to deny that this too is a most grievous punishment? But I do not know whether what he says, *before our eyes,* is elegantly enough taken, if we understand that kind of punishment which goes on in the inmost parts of the heart, and torments even those who fawningly smile upon us, nor can it be seen by us what they suffer in the inner man. But that which, whether by their believing their iniquity is slain, or to those persevering in malignity the last punishment is rendered, is understood without a scruple of question to be said: *Let vengeance become known before our eyes among the nations.*

**14.** And these things indeed, as we said, are a prophesying, not a wish: but on account of that which is written in the Apocalypse, that the martyrs under the altar of God cry to God and say: *How long, O Lord, do you not avenge our blood*[^58]? it is not to be passed over how it ought to be taken; lest the saints be believed to desire vengeance for the sake of glutting hatred, which is far from their perfection. And yet it is written: *The righteous shall rejoice when he sees the vengeance of the wicked; he shall wash his hands in the blood of the sinner*[^59]: and the Apostle says: *Not avenging yourselves, dearly beloved, but give place to anger; for it is written: Vengeance is mine, and I will repay, says the Lord*[^60]. So he himself does not command that they should not wish to be avenged, but that, not avenging themselves, they should give *place to the anger* of God who said: *Vengeance is mine, and I will repay.* And the Lord in the Gospel sets forth a widow in a likeness, who, desiring to be avenged, kept entreating an unjust judge, who at last heard her, led not by justice, but overcome by weariness[^61]: but the Lord set this forth, to show that much more the just God will quickly make judgment for his elect, who cry to him day and night[^62]. Thence too is that cry of the martyrs under the altar of God, that they may be avenged by God's judgment. Where, then, is that: *Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute you*[^63]? where too is that: *Not rendering evil for evil, nor curse for curse*; and: *To no one rendering evil for evil*[^64]? For if evil is not to be rendered to anyone for evil; not only an evil deed for an evil deed, but not even an evil wish is to be rendered for an evil deed or wish. But he renders an evil wish who, even if he himself does not avenge, yet awaits and wishes that God may punish his enemy. Wherefore, since both the righteous man and the evil man wish to be avenged by the Lord on their enemies, whence shall they be distinguished, except that the righteous man both desires more that his enemy be corrected than punished; and when he sees him avenged by the Lord, is not delighted at his punishment, because he does not hate him, but at the divine justice, because he loves God? Finally, if in this world vengeance is taken on him, he rejoices even for that man's sake, if he is corrected; or certainly for the sake of others, that they may fear to imitate him. He himself too is made better, not by feeding his hatreds on that man's punishment, but by amending his own errings. And through this it is of benevolence, not of malice, that the righteous man rejoices when he sees vengeance, and washes his hands—that is, makes his works purer—in the blood, that is, in the destruction of the sinner; taking from it not joy at another's evil, but an example of the divine admonition. But if in the future world, at the last judgment of God, vengeance is taken, this pleases him which pleases God; lest it be well with the wicked, lest the impious too enjoy the rewards of the pious: which is surely unjust, and foreign to the rule of truth, which the righteous man loves. For when the Lord was exhorting us to love our enemies, he set forth the example of our Father who is in heaven, *who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^65]; yet does he therefore not amend them by temporal rebukes, or not condemn the stubbornly hard at the end? So, then, let the enemy be loved, in such a way that the justice of the Lord by which he is punished does not displease; and let the justice by which he is punished so please, that there be rejoicing not at the man's evil, but at the good Judge. But a malevolent mind is saddened if its enemy, corrected, escapes the punishment: and when it sees him punished, it so rejoices to be avenged, that it is delighted not by the justice of God whom it does not love, but by the misery of him whom it hates: and when it leaves the judgment to God, it wishes that God may harm more than it itself could harm: and when it gives food to a hungry enemy, and drink to a thirsty one, it maliciously savors what is written: *For doing this you will heap coals of fire upon his head*[^66]. For it acts so as to burden him more, and to stir up against him the indignation of God, which it thinks is signified by coals of fire; not understanding that that fire is the burning grief of penitence, until the head, raised up by pride, is laid down by the benefits of the enemy to a wholesome humility, and in his good this man's evil is overcome. Whence the Apostle watchfully added: *Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good*[^67]. But how can he overcome evil with good who is good on the surface, and evil in the depths; who spares in deed, and rages in heart; gentle in hand, cruel in will? So, then, under the appearance of one asking, future vengeance on the impious is prophesied in this psalm, that we may understand that the holy men of God loved their enemies, and wished no one anything but good, which is piety in this world, eternity in the world to come: but in the punishments of the wicked they were delighted not by their evils, but by God's good judgments; and wherever in the holy Scriptures their hatreds against men are read, that they were hatreds of vices, which each one of men too ought to hate in himself, if he loves himself.

**15.** [v. 11.] But now as for what follows: *Let it enter before your sight,* or as other codices have, *in your sight, the groaning of the fettered*: no one easily finds saints cast into fetters by persecutors; and if it happened, in so great and manifold a variety of punishments, it happened so rarely, that it is not to be believed that the Prophet chose to recall this especially in this verse. But surely the fetters are the weakness, and the corruptibility of the body, which weighs down the soul. For through its frailty, as the material of certain griefs and troubles, the persecutor could drive one to impiety. From these fetters the Apostle desired to be released, and to be with Christ; but to remain in the flesh was necessary on account of those to whom he ministered the Gospel[^68]. Until, then, this corruptible puts on incorruption, and this mortal puts on immortality[^69], the weak flesh, as with fetters, impedes the ready spirit. These fetters, then, none feel, except those who within themselves groan, being burdened, desiring to be clothed over with the habitation which is from heaven[^70], because both death is a horror, and mortal life a grief. For these who groan the Prophet groans, that their groaning may enter into the sight of God. They too can be understood as fettered, who are bound by the precepts of wisdom, which, patiently borne, are turned into ornaments; whence it is written: *Put your foot into her fetters*[^71]. *According to the greatness,* he says, *of your arm, receive into adoption the children of those put to death,* or as in other codices it is read, *possess the children of those put to death.* Where Scripture seems to me to have shown sufficiently what was the groaning of the fettered, who for the name of Christ bore most grievous persecutions, which in this psalm are most openly prophesied. For, set in diverse passions, they prayed for the Church, that their blood might not be fruitless for posterity; that the Lord's harvest, from which the enemies thought it would perish, might thence more fruitfully sprout. For he calls *the children of those put to death* those who not only were not terrified by the passions of those who went before, but, kindled by their glory exhorting them to imitation, believed in him for whose name they had known them to suffer, in most copious throngs. Therefore he said: *According to the greatness of your arm.* For a thing so great followed in the Christian peoples, that those who thought they were profiting something by persecuting, would in no way have believed it would follow.

**16.** [v. 12.] *Render,* he says, *to our neighbors sevenfold into their bosom.* He does not wish for evils; but foretells just things, and prophesies things to come. By the sevenfold number—that is, the sevenfold retribution—he wishes the perfection of punishment to be understood, because by this number fullness is wont to be signified. Whence that too is among the good things: *He shall receive in this world sevenfold*[^72]: which is set for all things; *As having nothing, and possessing all things*[^73]. He says "neighbors," because among them the Church dwells until the day of separation: for the bodily departure does not happen now. *Into their bosom* he says, surely now in hidden things; so that the vengeance which is done in this life in secret may afterward *become known among the nations before our eyes.* For when a man is given over to a reprobate sense, he receives in the inner bosom the merit of future punishments. *Their reproach, with which they reproached you, O Lord.* Render this to them sevenfold into their bosom—that is, for that reproach reprobate them most fully in their hidden parts. For there they reproached your name, supposing that they were blotting you out from the earth in your servants.

**17.** [v. 13.] *But we are your people*: to be taken generally of every kind of pious and true Christians. *We,* then, whom they thought they could destroy, *your people, and the sheep of your flock,* that he who glories may glory in the Lord[^74], *will confess to you forever.* But other codices have, *we will confess to you unto eternity.* From the ambiguous Greek this diversity was made: for what the Greek has can be interpreted both *unto eternity,* and *for the age;* but according to the place it must be understood which is better to interpret. The sense, then, of this place seems to me rather to show that it ought to be said, *for the age,* that is, until the end of the age. But the following verse, in the manner of the Scriptures, and especially of the Psalms, is a repetition of the earlier with the order changed, having first what is there last, and last what is there first. For what was said there, *we will confess to you,* in its place is here said, *we will announce your praise.* So what was said there, *for the age,* in its place is here said, *from generation to generation.* For this repetition of "generation" signifies perpetuity: or, as some understand, because there are two generations, the old and the new. But both come to be in this world: since whoever is not reborn of water and the Spirit shall not enter the kingdom of heaven[^75]; and then, because in this world the praise of God is announced, for in the world to come, when we shall see him as he is, there will be no one to whom it may thereafter be announced[^76]. *We,* then, *your people, and the sheep of your flock,* which by persecuting they believed they could consume, *will confess to you for the age,* with the Church surely remaining until the end, which they tried to destroy: *from generation to generation we will announce your praise;* which praise, that it might be silenced, they tried to put an end to us. But in many places of the holy Scriptures we have intimated that confession is set also for praise: as is that: *These things you shall say in confession: All the works of the Lord are exceedingly good*[^77]; and especially that which the Savior himself says, who had no sin at all which by repenting he should confess: *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and revealed them to little ones*[^78]. This I have said, that it may be more openly understood that this is repeated in that which was said, *we will announce your praise,* which above had been said, *we will confess to you.*
