# On the Same Psalm 118 (11)

**1.** [v. 37.] There follows in the psalm we have undertaken to discuss: *Turn away my eyes, lest they see vanity; in your way give me life.* Vanity and truth differ from one another as contraries. But the cupidity of this world is vanity: but Christ, who frees from this world, is truth. He is both the way in which this one wishes to be given life, because he is the life too; for he himself says: *I am the way, the truth, and the life*[^1]. But what is it: *Turn away my eyes, lest they see vanity?* Can we, as long as we are in this world, not see vanity? *For every creature is subjected to vanity*[^2], which is understood to be in man; and: *All is vanity: what abundance has a man in all his labor, by which he labors under the sun?*[^3] Or does this one perhaps pray for this, that his life be not under the sun, where all is vanity; but be in him in whom he asks to be given life? For he *ascended* not above the sun only, but *above all the heavens, that he might fill all things*[^4]. And in him rather than under the sun live those who do not vainly hear what the Apostle said: *Seek the things that are above, where Christ is sitting at the right hand of God; taste the things that are above, not the things on earth. For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^5]. And through this, if our life is there where truth is, our life is not under the sun where vanity is. But this so great good we have rather in hope, we do not hold it in fact: and according to our hope blessed the Apostle spoke these things; for that too, when he had said: *The creature is subjected to vanity,* he added and said: *Not willingly, but because of him who subjected it in hope*[^6]. Therefore in hope, by which we hope that we will cling to the truth to be contemplated, we are meanwhile subjected to vanity. For this creature, spiritual, animal, and corporeal, is all in man; rather, is man. He sinned willingly, and was made an enemy to the truth: but, that he might be deservedly punished, not willingly was he subjected to vanity. Finally, after a few words: *And not only so,* he says, *but we ourselves too, having the firstfruits of the spirit*[^7], that is, who, not yet wholly in that which we are, but in that part by which we are better than the beasts, are subject to God, not to vanity, that is, through the firstfruits of the spirit: *and we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body. For by hope we were made safe. But hope that is seen is not hope: for what one sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, with patience we await it*[^8]. As long, then, as we are here according to the flesh, whose adoption and redemption we still await through the patience of hope; so long, according to that by which we are under the sun, we are subjected to vanity. As long, then, as we are so, whence can we not see vanity, to which we are even subjected in hope? What is it, then, that this one says: *Turn away my eyes, lest they see vanity?* Or does he ask this, that not indeed in this life what we bear in hope be fulfilled, but that he be in that lot which can at some time be fulfilled in him, when *he will be freed from the slavery of corruption,* both in spirit and in soul and in body, *into the liberty of the glory of the sons of God*[^9], where he will no longer see vanity?

**2.** These words can indeed be so understood, not contrary to the rule of faith; but there is here another sense which I confess pleases me more. The Lord in the Gospel says: *If your eye be simple, your whole body will be lightsome. But if your eye be evil, your whole body will be dark. If, then, the light that is in you is darkness, how great will that darkness be?*[^10] Accordingly it matters greatly, when we do something good, by the contemplation of what thing we do it. For our duty is to be weighed not by the duty, but by the end; namely, that we consider not only whether what we do is good, but chiefly whether that for the sake of which we do it is good. These eyes, by which we contemplate why we do what we do, he asks to be turned away lest they see vanity; that is, lest he attend to this, for the sake of which he does it, when he does something good. In which vanity the chief place is held by the love of human praise, for the sake of which many great things were done by those who were named great in this world, and much praised in the cities of the nations, seeking glory not with God, but with men, and for the sake of this living as it were prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly; at which arriving they received their reward, vain men a vain one. From this vanity the Lord, wishing to turn away the eyes of his own: *Attend,* he says, *lest you do your righteousness before men, to be seen by them; otherwise you will not have a reward with your Father, who is in heaven*[^11]. Then, when he followed out certain parts of righteousness itself, commanding about alms, about prayer, about fasting, he everywhere admonished that none of these be done for the glory of men, and everywhere says that those who do it for this reason have received their reward, that is, not the eternal one which is laid up for the saints with the Father, but the temporal one which those seek who contemplate vanity in their works: not because human praise itself is to be blamed (for what is so to be wished for men, as that there please them the things which they ought to imitate?), but to work well for the sake of praise itself, this is to gaze at vanity in one's works. Since indeed even the praise itself from men, however great it comes to a just man, ought not to be the end of his good there, but even it is to be referred to the praise of God, for whose sake the truly good do good things; since they are made good not by themselves, but by him. Finally in the same sermon the Lord had already said to them: *Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father, who is in heaven*[^12]. Where he set the end, that is, in the glory of God, this we ought to gaze at when we do something good, if our eyes are turned away from vanity. Let not, then, the end of a good work be in the praises of men, but let us correct the very praises of men, and refer all things to the praises of God, from whom is given to us whatever in us is praised without the error of the one praising. Further, if it is vain to do good for the sake of the praises of men, how much vainer for the sake of obtaining money, or increasing it, or retaining it, and if there is any such temporal advantage, which accrues to us from without? Because *all is vanity: what abundance has a man in all his labor, by which he labors under the sun?*[^13] Finally, for the sake of temporal salvation itself we ought not to do our good works, but rather for the sake of that which we hope eternal, where we may fully enjoy the unchangeable good, which will be to us from God, or rather which is to us God himself. For if the saints of God did good works for the sake of this temporal salvation, the martyrs of Christ would never accomplish the good work of confession in the loss of that same salvation. But they received help from tribulation, not gazing at vanity, because vain is the salvation of man; and they did not covet the day of man, because man is made like to vanity, his days pass like a shadow.

**3.** [v. 38.] But when God is asked, that those things which seem to be in our power, that is, the turning away of the eyes from seeing vanity, be granted to us by him, what is commended except his grace? For some did not turn their eyes away from that vanity, because they supposed that they were made just and good by themselves, and loved the glory of men more than of God; because they themselves too are men, who pleased themselves too much, and presumed on the powers of their own choice: but this too is vanity and the presumption of the spirit. When, then, he had said: *Turn away my eyes, lest they see vanity; in your way give me life;* which way is not vanity, but truth: then he subjoined: *Establish your eloquence for your servant in your fear.* Which is nothing else than: Give me that I may do what you say? For the eloquence of God is not established for those who move it within themselves by doing against it; but it is established for those in whom it is immovable. So God establishes his eloquence in his fear for those to whom he gives the spirit of his fear: but the fear not of that of which the Apostle says: *For you did not receive the spirit of slavery again in fear*[^14]; for this fear consummated love casts out: but of that fear which the prophet calls the spirit of the fear of God; the fear certainly chaste, abiding for ages of ages, the fear by which it is feared to offend the one who is loved. For in one way an adulteress fears her husband, in another a chaste wife: the adulteress, lest he come; the chaste, lest he forsake.

**4.** [v. 39.] *Cut off,* he says, *my reproach which I have suspected, because your judgments are sweet.* Who is there who suspects his own reproach, and does not rather each one know his own reproach better than anyone's else? For a man can rather suspect another's, not his own; since what he suspects, he does not know: but in his own reproach there is not suspicion for anyone, but knowledge, where conscience speaks. What is it, then, that he says: *My reproach which I have suspected?* Surely this one too must be led from the higher sense: since, as long as a man does not turn away his eyes lest they see vanity, what is done in himself, this he suspects of others; so that, for the sake of which he himself worships God, or for the sake of which he does good works, for this he believes another too to do them. For men can see what we do; but by the contemplation of what thing we do it is hidden: and therefore place is given to suspicions, so that a man dares to judge of the hidden things of men, and rashly to suspect things often false, and, if true, yet unknown. Therefore the Lord, when he spoke about the very end for the sake of which we ought to do righteousness, that he might turn away our eyes from the contemplation of vanity, admonished that we not do good works for the sake of the praises of men, saying: *Attend lest you do your righteousness before men, to be seen by them.* He admonished, lest for the sake of money, saying: *Do not lay up for yourselves treasure on earth;* and: *You cannot serve God and mammon.* He admonished, lest for the sake of the very necessary food and clothing, saying: *Be not anxious for your soul what you may eat, nor for your body what you may put on*[^15]. And when he had admonished all these things, because we can suspect those whom we see living justly, and with what end they do it we do not see, to do good for the sake of something of this kind; he immediately subjoined: *Do not judge, lest you be judged*[^16]. Whence here too, when he had said: *Cut off my reproach which I have suspected;* he added, *because your judgments are sweet,* that is, your judgments are true. For the lover of truth proclaims that what is true is sweet. But the judgments of men about the hidden things of men are not sweet, because rash. And therefore he said his own reproach which he has suspected about others; because even this which the Apostle says: *Comparing themselves with themselves*[^17], they do not understand: for this a man more readily suspects in another, which he feels in himself. This his own reproach, then, he asked to be taken away, which he had felt in himself, and had suspected in others; that he be not like the devil, who suspected of the hidden things of holy Job that he did not worship God freely, whom he demanded to be tempted, that he might find a crime to cast at him.

**5.** [v. 40.] But since none but rivalry willingly suspects another's reproach, while a good work cannot be reproved, because it asserts itself, which is open; and the end for which it is done is reproved, because it does not show itself, which is hidden; and so it pleases whom it pleases to suspect ill, by not seeing what lies hidden, and by envying what stands out: surely against this evil, by which each one willingly suspects of a man the evil which he does not perceive, love must be had, which is not jealous; which the Lord especially commends, saying: *A new commandment I give you, that you love one another;* and: *In this all will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another*[^18]: and, speaking of the love of God and neighbor: *On these,* he says, *two commandments the whole Law hangs, and the Prophets*[^19]. Whence this one too, against the reproach of his own suspicion which he desires to be cut off: *Behold,* he says, *I have coveted your commandments; in your righteousness give me life.* Behold, I have coveted with my whole heart, with my whole soul, with my whole mind to love you, and my neighbor as myself: not in my own, but *in your righteousness give me life,* that is, with this love which I have coveted, fill me. Help that I may do what you commend, give yourself what you command. *In your righteousness give me life:* because in myself I had whence I might die; but whence I may live I do not find except in you. Your righteousness is Christ, *who became for us wisdom from God, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption; that, as it is written: He who glories, may glory in the Lord*[^20]. And in him I find your commandments which I have coveted, that in your righteousness, that is, in him, you may give me life. For he is the Word God; and the Word became flesh, that he might be my neighbor too.
