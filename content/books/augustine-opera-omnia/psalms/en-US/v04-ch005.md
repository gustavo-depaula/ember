# Exposition of Psalm 77

**1.** [v. 1.] This psalm contains the things that are narrated as having been done among the ancient people: but the more recent and later people is admonished to take care not to be ungrateful for the benefits of God, and not to provoke against itself the wrath of him whose grace it ought obediently and faithfully to receive: *Let them not become,* he says, *like their fathers, a perverse and embittering generation; a generation that did not direct its heart, and whose spirit was not entrusted to God*[^1]. This, then, is the intention of this psalm, this its usefulness, this its most abundant fruit. But although all things seem to be said and narrated clearly and openly, its title first moves us and makes us attentive. For it is not without cause inscribed: *The understanding of Asaph;* unless because perhaps these things seek a reader who understands not what the surface sounds, but something more inward. Then, about to narrate and recall all these things, which seem to need a hearer rather than an expounder: *I will open,* he says, *my mouth in parables, I will utter propositions from the beginning*[^2]. Who would not be roused from sleep at this? Who would dare, in reading, to run through as it were manifest parables and propositions, which by their very names indicate that they ought to be more deeply searched out? For a parable carries before it a likeness of some thing; and though this be a Greek word, yet it is now used as Latin. And it is known that in parables, which are called likenesses of things, things are compared to the matters under discussion. But propositions, which in Greek are called problems, are questions, having something that must be resolved by disputation. Who, then, would read parables and propositions in passing? who, on hearing these, would not bend the watchfulness of his mind, that he may by understanding arrive at their fruit?

**2.** *Attend, my people, to my law,* he says. Whom should we believe to be speaking here but God? For he himself gave the law to his people, whom, freed from Egypt, he gathered—which gathering is properly called a synagogue, which is interpreted Asaph. Was it, then, said *The understanding of Asaph,* in the sense that Asaph himself understood; or is it to be understood figuratively, that the synagogue itself—that is, that same people—understood, to whom it is said: *Attend, my people, to my law*? What, then, is it that he rebukes the same people through the prophet, saying: *But Israel has not known me, and my people has not understood*[^3]? But surely there were even in that people those who understood, having the faith which was afterward revealed, pertaining not to the letter of the Law, but to the grace of the Spirit. For they were not without that faith, who were able to foresee and foretell its future revelation in Christ; since even those ancient sacraments were significations of things to come. Or did the prophets alone have this faith, and not the people too? Rather, even those who faithfully heard the prophets were aided by the same grace, so that they understood what they heard. But surely the sacrament of the kingdom of heaven was veiled in the Old Testament, which in the fullness of time would be revealed in the New. *For I do not wish you to be ignorant, brothers,* says the Apostle, *that our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea, and all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink: for they drank of the spiritual rock that followed them; and the rock was Christ*[^4]. The food and drink of those men, then, in mystery, was the same as ours; but the same in signification, not in appearance; because the same Christ was figured for them in the rock, and manifested to us in the flesh. *But,* he says, *God was not well pleased with most of them*[^5]. *All* indeed *ate the same spiritual food, and drank the same spiritual drink,* that is, signifying something spiritual; *but God was not well pleased with all of them.* When he says: *Not with all,* there were therefore there some with whom God was well pleased; and although all the sacraments were common, the grace, which is the power of the sacraments, was not common to all. So too now, the faith which then was veiled having been revealed, the washing of regeneration is common to all who are baptized in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit[^6]; but the grace itself, of which these are the sacraments, by which the members of the body of Christ with their head have been regenerated, is not common to all. For heretics too have the same baptism, and false brethren in the communion of the catholic name. Therefore here too it is rightly said: *But God was not well pleased with all of them.*

**3.** Yet neither then nor now is the voice fruitless that says: *Attend, my people, to my law.* Which manner of speaking is well known in all the Letters, that he does not say: Attend (singular); but: *Attend* (plural). For a people consists of many; to which many what follows is said in the plural: *Incline your ear to the words of my mouth.* What is *Attend*; this is *Incline your ear*: and what he there says, *my law;* this he here says, *the words of my mouth.* For he attends piously to the law of God and the words of his mouth, whose ear humility inclines; not whose neck pride raises. For what is poured in is received by the hollow of humility, and is repelled by the prominence of the swelling. Hence in another place: *Incline,* he says, *your ear, and receive the words of understanding*[^7]. We are sufficiently admonished, then, to receive even this psalm of this understanding of Asaph (for it is set in the title in the genitive case, of this understanding; not, this understanding) with inclined ear, that is, with humble piety. Nor is it said, of Asaph himself; but, to Asaph himself: which is apparent by the Greek article, and is found in certain Latin codices. These words, then, are of the understanding, that is, of the understanding which was given to Asaph himself; which we take better not of one man, but of the gathering of the people of God, from which we ought by no means to estrange ourselves. For although properly the synagogue is said of the Jews, but the Church of Christians—because a "gathering" (congregatio) is usually understood rather of cattle, but a "calling together" (convocatio) rather of men—yet we find that too called the Church, and perhaps it rather befits us to say: *Save us, O Lord our God, and gather us from the nations, that we may confess to your holy name*[^8]. Nor ought we to disdain, rather to give ineffable thanks, that we are the sheep of his hands, whom he foresaw when he said: *I have other sheep that are not of this fold; I must bring them too, that there may be one flock and one shepherd*[^9], joining, namely, the faithful people of the Gentiles to the faithful people of the Israelites, of whom he had first said: *I am not sent except to the sheep that are lost, of the house of Israel*[^10]. For all nations will be gathered before him, and he will separate them as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats[^11]. So, then, let us hear what was said: *Attend, my people, to my law, incline your ear to the words of my mouth,* not as though these things were said to the Jews, but rather to us, or certainly to us as well. For when the Apostle had said: *But God was not well pleased with all of them,* thus showing that there were there also those with whom God was well pleased; he straightway subjoined: *For they were laid low in the desert*: then he added: *But these things were made our figures, that we should not be desirers of evil things, as they too desired: nor servants of idols, as some of them; as it is written: The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play. Nor let us commit fornication, as some of them committed fornication, and there fell in one day twenty-three thousand. Nor let us tempt Christ, as some of them tempted, and perished by the serpents. Nor murmur, as some of them murmured, and perished by the destroyer. But all these things happened to them in figure: but they were written for our correction, upon whom the end of the ages has come*[^12]. Therefore these things were sung rather for us. Hence in this psalm, among other things, it was said: *That another generation may know it, the children who shall be born and shall arise*[^13]. Further, if that destruction by serpents, and that ruin by the destroyer, and the slaughter by the sword were figures, as the Apostle evidently says, since it is manifest that all those things happened; for he does not say: They were said in figure, or: They were written in figure; but: *In figure,* he says, *they happened to them:* with how much greater diligence of piety are the punishments to be guarded against of which those were the figures! For without doubt, just as in good things there is much more of good in what is signified through the figure than in the figure itself; so too in evil things, those are by far worse which are signified by the figures, when even the signifying figures are so great evils. For just as the land of promise, to which that people was led, is nothing in comparison with the kingdom of heaven, to which the Christian people is led: so too those punishments which were figures, though they are so dreadful, are nothing in comparison with the punishments which they signify. And what the Apostle called figures, this psalm calls, as far as we can judge, parables and propositions: having their end not in the fact that they happened, but in those things to which by a reasonable comparison they are referred. Let us attend, then, to the law of God, his people, and let us incline our ear to the words of his mouth.

**4.** [v. 2.] *I will open,* he says, *my mouth in parables; I will utter propositions from the beginning.* From what beginning he speaks, sufficiently appears in what follows. For not from when heaven and earth were made, nor from when the human race was created in the first man; but from when the gathering of the people that was led out of Egypt: so that the sense pertains to Asaph, which is interpreted "gathering." But would that he who said: *I will open my mouth in parables,* might deign to open our understanding too into them! For if, as he opened his mouth in parables, so he would open also the parables themselves; and as he utters propositions, so he would utter also their expositions; we would not here be in a sweat: but as it is, all things are so covered and closed, that even if, with his help, we can arrive at something by which we may be wholesomely fed, yet in the sweat of our face we eat our bread[^14]; and we pay the penalty of the ancient sentence, by the labor not only of the body, but also of the heart. Let him speak, then, and let us hear the parables and propositions.

**5.** [v. 3.] *How great things we have heard, and have known them, and our fathers have recounted to us.* The Lord was speaking above. For whose other words are to be believed: *Attend, my people, to my law*? What is it, then, that now a man suddenly speaks? For now they are the words of a man: *How great things we have heard, and have known them, and our fathers have recounted to us.* Surely God, about to speak now through the ministry of a man, as the Apostle says: *Do you wish to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^15] willed that the words should first be made from his own person, lest the man speaking his words be despised as a man. For so it is with the utterances of God, which are conveyed to us through the sense of our body. The Creator moves the subject creature by an invisible work; his substance is not changed into anything corporeal and temporal, so that by corporeal and temporal signs, whether pertaining to the eyes or to the ears, as far as men can grasp, he makes his will known. For if an angel can use ether, air, cloud, fire, and any other nature or corporeal appearance; and a man his face, tongue, hand, pen, letters, or other signifying things, to indicate the secrets of his mind; finally, since he is a man, he sends men as messengers, and says to one: Go, and he goes; and to another: Come, and he comes; and to his servant: Do this, and he does it[^16]: with how much greater and more efficacious power does God, the Lord to whom all things are subject, use both angel and man, to announce what pleases him! Although, then, a man now says: *How great things we have heard, and have known them, and our fathers have recounted to us;* yet let us hear them as the words of God, not as human fables. For on this account it was set before: *Attend, my people, to my law, incline your ear to the words of my mouth. I will open my mouth in parables, I will utter propositions from the beginning. How great things,* then, he says, *we have heard, and have known them, and our fathers have recounted to us.* So it is, *we have heard, and have known;* as: *Hear, O daughter, and see*[^17]. For the things were heard in the Old Testament that are known in the New; heard when they were prophesied, known when they were fulfilled. Where the promise is rendered, the hearing is not cheated. *And our fathers,* Moses and the Prophets, *have recounted to us.*

**6.** [v. 4.] *They have not been hidden from their children, in another generation.* This is our generation, in which regeneration has been granted to us. *Announcing the praises of the Lord, and his powers, and his wonders that he has done.* The order of the words is: *And our fathers recounted to us, announcing the praises of the Lord.* The Lord is praised that he may be loved: for what is more wholesomely loved?

**7.** [v. 5.] *And he raised up a Testimony in Jacob, and set a Law in Israel.* This is the beginning of which it was said above: *I will utter propositions from the beginning.* The beginning, then, is the Old Testament, the end the New. For fear prevails in the Law: and *the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord*[^18]. But *the end of the Law is Christ, unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^19]: by whose gift *love is poured out in our hearts, through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^20]; and *perfect love casts out fear*[^21], since *now without the Law the righteousness of God has been manifested.* But because it *has testimony from the Law and the Prophets*[^22], therefore *he raised up a Testimony in Jacob, and set a Law in Israel.* For that the tabernacle, established with so notable a work and full of so many significations, is called the tabernacle of the Testimony[^23], in which there was a veil before the ark of the Law, as there was a veil before the face of the minister of the Law; because in that dispensation there were parables and propositions. For the things that were preached and done were stored up with veiled significations, and were discerned not in revealed manifestations. *But when you have passed over to Christ,* says the Apostle, *the veil will be taken away*[^24]. For as many as are the promises of God, in him they are *Yes, Amen*[^25]. Whoever, then, clings to Christ, has all the good which even in the letter of the Law he does not understand; but whoever is a stranger to Christ neither understands nor has it. *He raised up,* then, *a Testimony in Jacob, and set a Law in Israel.* He repeats in his usual manner. For what is *He raised up a Testimony;* this is *He set a Law:* and what is *in Jacob;* this is *in Israel.* For as these two are the names of one man, so Law and Testimony are two names of one thing. There is some difference, someone says, between *he raised up* and *he set.* Yes, surely; just as there is a difference even between *Jacob* and *Israel:* not because they were two men, but the same two names were imposed on one man for different reasons; Jacob on account of the supplanting, because at his birth he held the foot of his brother; Israel on account of the vision of God[^26]. So *he raised up* is one thing, *he set* another. For *he raised up a Testimony,* as I judge, was said because something was raised up by it. *For without the Law,* says the Apostle, *sin was dead. But I was alive once without the Law: but when the commandment came, sin revived*[^27]. Behold what was raised up by the Testimony which is the Law, so that what was hidden might appear, as he says a little after: *But sin, that it might appear sin, through what is good wrought death in me*[^28]. But *he set a Law,* it was said, as a yoke for sinners: whence it is said: *That the law is not set for the righteous*[^29]. It is a Testimony, then, insofar as it proves something; but a Law, insofar as it commands; though it is one and the same thing. Wherefore, just as Christ is a stone; but to those who believe, unto the head of the corner[^30], but to those who do not believe, a stone of stumbling and a rock of scandal[^31]; so the Testimony of the Law is, to those who use the Law not lawfully, a Testimony by which sinners are convicted to be punished; but to those who use it lawfully, a Testimony by which is shown to whom sinners ought to flee to be freed. For in his grace is *the righteousness of God, having testimony from the Law and the Prophets*[^32], by which the wicked man is justified: which certain men, *being ignorant of, and wishing to establish their own, were not subject to the righteousness of God*[^33].

**8.** [vv. 5-8.] *How great things he commanded our fathers to make known to their children: that another generation may know it, the children who shall be born and shall arise, and shall recount them to their children; that they may put their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, and seek out his commandments. Let them not become, like their fathers, a perverse and embittering generation; a generation that did not direct its heart, and whose spirit was not entrusted to God.* These words indicate, in a manner, two peoples; the one pertaining to the Old, the other to the New Testament: for when he says: *How great things he commanded our fathers to make known to their children,* he said that they had received the commands to *make them known to their children,* yet not that they had recognized or done them; but they received them to this end, *that another generation may know it,* what that one did not know. *The children who shall be born and shall arise.* For those who were born did not arise, because they had their heart not above, but rather on earth. For with Christ one arises: whence it is said: *If you have risen with Christ, savor the things that are above*[^34]. *And let them recount,* he says, *to their children, that they may put their hope in God.* For thus the righteous do not wish to establish their own righteousness, but reveal their way to God, and hope in him, that he himself may act[^35]. *And not forget the works of God*: namely, by magnifying and boasting of their own works, as though they themselves do them; whereas it is God who works in those who work good things, both to will and to work, for the good will[^36]. *And seek out his commandments.* When they have already learned them, how do they seek them out? For *how great things,* he says, *he commanded our fathers to make known to their children, that another generation may know it.* What may it know? Surely the commandments which he commanded. How, then, do they still seek them out, except because, by putting their hope in God, they then seek out his commandments, that by them, with his help, they may be fulfilled? *Let them not become, like their fathers, a perverse and embittering generation; a generation that did not direct its heart.* And he says why, straightway subjoining: *And whose spirit was not entrusted to God*: that is, because it did not have the faith which obtains what the Law commands. For when the spirit of man cooperates with the working Spirit of God, then what God commanded is fulfilled; and this does not happen except by believing in him who justifies the wicked[^37]. Which faith the perverse and embittering generation did not have; and therefore it was said of it: *Its spirit was not entrusted to God.* For this was said much more expressly, to signify the grace of God, which not only works the remission of sins, but also makes the spirit of man cooperate with it in the work of good deeds; as though he said: His spirit did not believe God. For this is to have one's spirit entrusted to God: not to believe that one's spirit can do righteousness without God, but with God. This is also to believe in God; which is surely more than to believe God. For any man whatever is often to be believed, although one is not to believe in him. This, then, is to believe in God: by believing to cling to God, so as to cooperate well with God who works good things: *For without me,* he says, *you can do nothing*[^38]. And what more could the Apostle say of this than what he said: *But he who clings to the Lord is one spirit*[^39]. Otherwise that law is a testimony by which the guilty one is condemned, not by which he is absolved. For it is a threatening letter, which convicts transgressors; not an aiding spirit, which frees and justifies sinners. That generation, then, whose example is to be guarded against, was for this reason perverse and embittering, because *its spirit was not entrusted to God*: because even if it believed God in some things, yet it did not believe in God; it did not by faith cling to God, so that, healed by God, it might cooperate well with God working in it.

**9.** [v. 9.] In short: *The sons of Ephraim, who bend and shoot bows, turned back in the day of battle.* Pursuing the law of righteousness, they did not arrive at the law of righteousness[^40]. Why? Because not by faith. For it was a generation whose spirit was not entrusted to God: but as it were from works; because, just as it bent and shot bows (which appears outwardly, as in the works of the Law), it did not so also direct the heart, where the righteous man lives by faith[^41], which works through love[^42], by which one clings to God, who works in man both to will and to work, for the good will[^43]. For what else is it to bend a bow, and shoot, and turn back in the day of battle, except to attend and promise in the day of hearing, and to desert in the day of temptation; to make a kind of prelude with arms, and at the hour of combat refuse to fight? Now he says, *bending and shooting bows;* whereas he seems to have had to say, bending bows, and shooting arrows; for the bow is not shot, but something is shot from the bow: either it is that manner of speaking which we discussed above, where it was said: *He raised up a Testimony,* because he raised up something by the Testimony; so too here, *shooting bows,* because they shot something with bows: or the order of the words is obscure, with the omission of a word, which is to be supplied though unspoken, so that this is the order: *The sons of Ephraim, bending bows, and shooting,* and there is to be supplied: Arrows; and more fully it is: Bending bows, and shooting arrows: because even if he said: Bending, and shooting arrows, we ought surely not to understand: Bending arrows; but, on hearing what he said: Bending, we should rather supply: A bow, though it was not said. Indeed certain Greek codices are said to have, *bending and shooting with bows,* so that without hesitation we supply Arrows. But that by the sons of Ephraim he wished that whole embittering generation to be understood, is a manner of speaking signifying the whole from a part. And perhaps this part was chosen, by which that whole people was signified, because from these especially some good was to be expected; because they were born from him whom Jacob, though set by his father as the younger at the left hand, yet with his right hand blessed, and by a hidden blessing preferred to his elder brother[^44]; so that, because that tribe is thus blamed in this place, and it is shown that there did not appear in it what that blessing promised, we may sufficiently understand that even then, by the words of the patriarch Jacob, something far other was figured than the prudence of the flesh expects. For it was figured that those would be last who were first, and those would be first who were last[^45], through the coming of the Savior, of whom it was said: *He who comes after me has been made before me*[^46]. So the righteous Abel was preferred to his elder brother[^47], so Isaac to Ishmael[^48], so Jacob himself to Esau, his twin, but yet born first[^49]; so Perez to his own twin, who had first put out his hand from the womb, and had begun to be born, yet Perez preceded even in birth[^50]; so David was preferred to his elder brothers[^51]: and because all these and other similar parables, not only of words, but also of deeds, went before, so the Christian people was preferred to the Jewish people, for the redeeming of which, just as Abel by Cain[^52], so Christ was killed by the Jews. For this was prefigured even when Jacob, with his hands stretched crosswise, with his right hand touched Ephraim set at the left; and preferred him to Manasseh set at the right, whom he himself touched with his left hand. For according to the flesh *the sons of Ephraim, bending and shooting bows, turned back in the day of battle.*

**10.** [v. 10.] But what it is that he says: *They turned back in the day of battle,* the following words teach, by which he most plainly expounded it: *They did not observe,* he says, *the testament of God, and in his law they refused to walk.* Behold what is: *They turned back in the day of battle*: they did not observe the testament of God. When, bending and shooting bows, they even uttered words of most ready promise, saying: *Whatever the Lord our God has spoken, we will do and we will hear*[^53]. *They turned back in the day of battle*: because the promise of obedience is tested not by hearing, but by temptation. But he whose spirit is entrusted to God holds God faithful, who will not permit him to be tempted beyond what he can; but will make with the temptation also a way of escape, that he may be able to bear it[^54], lest he turn back in the day of battle. But he who glories in himself, not in God[^55], however much he boasts the pledge of his own virtue, as one bending and shooting bows, turns back in the day of battle. For because his spirit is not entrusted to God, neither is the Spirit of God with him; and, as it is written: *Because he did not believe, therefore he will not be protected*[^56]. But when he had said: *They did not observe the testament of God,* he added and said: *And in his law they refused to walk;* it is a repetition of the earlier statement, with a certain exposition of it: for he called *his law* what he had above called *the testament of God;* so that what was said, *they did not keep,* is understood as repeated in, *they refused to walk.* But since it could be said more briefly: And in his law they did not walk, it seems to me that in this expression he wished us to seek something not negligible, in that he preferred to say, *they refused to walk,* rather than, they did not walk. For the law of works could be thought to suffice, as it were, for justification, when the things that are commanded are done outwardly by those men who would prefer not to be commanded the things they do not do from the heart, and yet do them; and through this they seem, as it were, to walk in the law of God, but they refuse to walk: for they do not do them from the heart. But from the heart that can in no way be done which is done from fear of punishment, not from love of righteousness. For as far as concerns the deeds that are done outwardly: both those who fear punishment, and those who love righteousness, do not steal; and therefore they are equal in hand, unequal in heart; equal in work, unequal in will. Therefore those men were thus marked: *A generation,* he says, *that did not direct its heart.* It was not said, its works: but, its *heart.* For with the heart directed, the works are right: but when the heart is not directed, the works are not right, even if they seem right. And whence the perverse generation did not direct its heart, he sufficiently disclosed, when he said: *And its spirit was not entrusted to God.* For God is right: and therefore, clinging to the Right One, as to an unchangeable rule, the heart of man, which in itself was perverse, can be made right. But that the heart may be with him, and through this be able to be right, one approaches him, not with the foot, but with faith. Therefore it is said also in the Epistle to the Hebrews concerning that very perverse and embittering generation: *The word that was heard did not profit them, not being mingled with the faith of those who obeyed*[^57]. The will, then, that is in a right heart is prepared by the Lord, faith going before, by which one approaches the right God, that the heart may be made right. Which faith, the mercy of God preventing and calling, is roused up through obedience; and begins to apply the heart to God, that it may be directed; and the more and more it is directed, the more it sees what it did not see, and is strong in what it was not strong. This Simon had not done, to whom the apostle Peter said: *You have no part nor lot in this faith; for your heart is not right with God*[^58]. For there he showed that it cannot be right without God, so that men may begin, not as servants under the law walking in fear, but in the law as sons willing, in which those refused to walk, and under it remained guilty. For this will love has, not fear, which is poured out in the hearts of believers through the Holy Spirit[^59]. To whom it is said: *By grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves, but it is the gift of God; not from works, lest perhaps anyone be exalted. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus in good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them*[^60]. Not as those men, who *in his law refused to walk,* by not believing in him, nor revealing their way to him and hoping in him, that he himself might act.

**11.** [vv. 11-12.] *And they forgot his benefits, and his wonders that he showed them, the wonders he did before their fathers.* What this is, is a question not to be neglected. Of these same fathers he was saying a little before that they had been a perverse and embittering generation. *Let them not become,* he says, *like their fathers, a perverse and embittering generation; a generation that did not direct its heart,* and the rest that is still said of that generation, the imitation of which he commands the other generation to guard against in this way, *that they may put their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, and seek out his commandments*: of which we have already discussed what seemed sufficient. What, then, does he mean, when he speaks of that perverse generation, how *they forgot the benefits of God, and his wonders that he showed them,* in what he added and says: *The wonders he did before their fathers*? What fathers, when these very men are the fathers, whose descendants he does not wish to be like? If we take those from whom these were born, such as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; they had long ago fallen asleep when God showed wonders in Egypt. For it follows: *In the land of Egypt, in the field of Tanis*: where he says God showed these men wonders before their fathers. Or were they perhaps present in spirit, because of them the Lord says in the Gospel: *For all those live*[^61]? Or do we more fittingly take as fathers Moses, and Aaron, and the other elders who are recorded in the same Scripture to have received also the spirit from which Moses too had received, that they might help him to rule and bear the same people[^62]? For why should they not be called fathers? Not as God the Father is one, who regenerates by his Spirit those whom he makes sons unto the eternal inheritance; but for the sake of honor, on account of their age and care of piety: as Paul the elder says: *I write these things not to confound you; but I admonish you as my dearest children*[^63]; though he surely knew it was said by the Lord: *Call no one your father on earth; for one is your Father, God*[^64]. Which was not said in order that this word of human honor should be taken away from the custom of speech; but lest the grace of God, by which we are regenerated unto eternal life, should be attributed to the nature, or power, or even holiness of any man. Therefore, when he said: *I have begotten you*: he premised: *In Christ,* and *through the Gospel*[^65]; lest what is God's be thought to be his own.

**12.** Therefore that *perverse and embittering generation forgot the benefits of God, and his wonders that he showed them, the wonders he did before their fathers in the land of Egypt, in the field of Tanis.* And he begins to weave again the recollection of those same wonders. Which, if they are parables and propositions, are surely to be referred by comparison to something. Nor ought we to take away the eye of our attention from what the psalm itself intends to show, that this is the fruit of all the things that are said, and why we were admonished to hear these things most attentively, with God saying: *Attend, my people, to my law; incline your ear to the words of my mouth*[^66]; that we may put our hope in God, and not forget the works of God, and seek out his commandments; that we may not become, like those fathers, a perverse and embittering generation; a generation that did not direct its heart, and whose spirit was not entrusted to God. To this end, then, are all things to be referred, and through this, whatever these things signify by a figured action, can so come to pass spiritually in man; either by the grace of God, if they are good; or by the judgment of God, if they are evil: as those good things were done in the Israelites, or evil things, both in them and in their enemies. And if we do not forget these, but put our hope in God, and are not ungrateful for his grace; and fear him, not with servile fear, by which only bodily evils are dreaded, but with the chaste fear that endures forever[^67], which fear judges it a great punishment to be deprived of the light of righteousness; we shall not be made, like those fathers, a perverse and embittering generation. The land of Egypt, then, is to be understood in figure of this world. The field of Tanis is the plain of the humble commandment. For Tanis is interpreted "humble commandment." In this world, then, let us receive the commandment of humility, that in the other world we may deserve to receive the exaltation which he promised who for our sake here became humble.

**13.** [vv. 13-16.] For he who *broke open the sea, and led them across, set the waters as it were in skins,* so that the wave stood still as though it had been shut in, is able by his grace to restrain the fluid and slipping things of carnal concupiscences, when this world is renounced, so that, all sins being destroyed like enemies, the faithful people may be led across through the sacrament of baptism. He who *led them in a cloud by day, and all night in the light of fire,* is able also spiritually to direct their journeys, if faith cries to him: *Direct my journeys according to your word*[^68]. Of whom it is said elsewhere: *For he himself will make your courses straight, and will bring forth your journeys in peace*[^69]: through Jesus Christ our Lord, whose sacrament in this world, as in the day, has been manifested in the flesh, as in a cloud; but in the judgment, as in the terror of night, because then there will be a great tribulation of the world like fire, and it will shine for the righteous, and burn for the unrighteous. He who *broke open the rock in the desert, and gave them to drink as from a great deep; and brought forth water from the rock, and led down waters like rivers,* is surely able to pour into thirsting faith the gift of the Holy Spirit, which that deed signified spiritually, from the spiritual rock that followed[^70], which is Christ: who stood and cried out: *If anyone thirsts, let him come to me;* and: *He who drinks of the water that I will give, rivers of living water shall flow from his belly. For this he said,* as is read in the Gospel, *of the Spirit whom those believing in him were to receive*[^71]. To whom, as a rod, the wood of the passion drew near, that grace might flow forth to believers.

**14.** [v. 17.] And yet they, as a *perverse and embittering generation, added still to sin against him*: that is, not to believe. For this is the sin of which the Spirit convicts the world, as the Lord says: *Of sin, indeed, because they did not believe in me*[^72]. *And they embittered the Most High in the drought*: which other codices have as *in the waterless place,* which is more exact from the Greek, and signifies nothing other than drought. Whether in that drought of the desert, or rather in their own? For although they had drunk from the rock, they had not bellies, but minds, that were dry, flourishing with no fruitfulness of righteousness. In which drought they ought rather to have been faithfully suppliant to God, that he who had given fullness to their throats might give equity also to their conduct. For to him the faithful soul cries out: *Let my eyes behold equity*[^73].

**15.** [vv. 18-20.] *And they tempted God in their hearts, by asking for food for their souls.* It is one thing to ask by believing, another by tempting. For there follows: *And they spoke against God, and said: Can God prepare a table in the desert? Because he struck the rock, and waters flowed, and torrents overflowed, can he also give bread, or prepare a table for his people?*[^74] Not believing, then, they asked for food for their souls. Not so does the apostle James direct that the food of the mind be asked for; but he admonishes that it be sought by those who believe, not by those who tempt and speak against God: *But if any of you,* he says, *lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all abundantly, and does not reproach; and it shall be given him: but let him ask in faith, hesitating in nothing*[^75]. This faith the generation did not have, which had not directed its heart, and whose spirit was not entrusted to God.

**16.** [v. 21.] *Therefore the Lord heard, and deferred, and a fire was kindled in Jacob, and anger arose in Israel.* He expounded what he called fire. For he called the anger fire: although a fire properly also kindled many of them. What, then, is it that he says: *The Lord heard, and deferred*? Did he defer to bring them into the land of promise, whither they were being led; which could have been done within a few days, but on account of their sins they had to be worn down in the wilderness, where they were indeed worn down for forty years? But if it is so, he deferred, then, the people, not those very men who, tempting, spoke against God; for all of these perished in the wilderness, and their children entered the land of promise. Or did he defer the punishment, that he might first satisfy even their unbelieving concupiscence, lest he should be thought angry for the reason that they asked of him—even though tempting and speaking against—what he could not do? *He heard,* then, *and deferred* to take vengeance; and after he had done what they thought he could not do, then *anger arose in Israel.*

**17.** [vv. 22, 31.] Finally, having briefly set down both, he thereafter plainly follows out the order of the narrative. *Because they did not believe in God, nor hope in his salvation.* For when he had said why a fire was kindled in Jacob, and anger arose in Israel—that is, *because they did not believe in God, nor hope in his salvation*—straightway subjoining with what evident benefits they had been ungrateful: *And he commanded the clouds from above, and opened the gates of heaven. And he rained down manna on them to eat, and gave them the bread of heaven. Man ate the bread of angels: he sent them provisions in abundance. He removed the south wind from heaven, and by his power brought in the southwest wind. And he rained flesh upon them like dust, and winged birds like the sand of the sea. And they fell in the midst of their camp, around their tents. And they ate, and were filled to excess; and he brought them their desire: they were not deprived of their desire*[^76]. Behold why he had deferred. But what he had deferred, let us hear: *The food was still in their mouth, and the anger of God arose upon them*[^77]. Behold what he had deferred. For first, *he deferred;* and afterward, *a fire was kindled in Jacob, and anger arose in Israel.* He had deferred, then, that he might first do what they had believed he could not do, then bring on what they had to suffer. For if they had put their hope in God, not only those desires of the flesh, but even those of the spirit would be fulfilled for them. For he who *commanded the clouds from above, and opened the gates of heaven, and rained down manna on them to eat, and gave them the bread of heaven,* that *man* might eat *the bread of angels,* who *sent them provisions in abundance,* to satisfy the unbelieving, is not powerless to give to those who believe that very true bread from heaven which the manna signified; which is truly the food of the angels, whom the Word of God incorruptibly feeds incorruptible; which, that man might eat it, was made flesh, and dwelt among us[^78]. For that very bread is rained through the evangelical clouds upon the whole world; and, with the hearts of the preachers opened as heavenly gates, it is preached not to a murmuring and tempting synagogue, but to a believing Church that puts its hope in him. He who *removed the south wind from heaven, and by his power brought in the southwest wind, and rained flesh upon them like dust, and winged birds like the sand of the sea; and they fell in the midst of their camp, around their tents: and they ate, and were filled to excess, and he brought them their desire, nor were they deprived of their desire,* is able also to feed the little faith of those who do not tempt but believe, by signs of words brought forth in the flesh and passing through the air, as it were by winged birds: yet not coming from the north, where cold and gloom prevail—that is, the eloquence which pleases this world—but by removing the south wind from heaven. Whither, except to the earth? that those who are little ones in faith may, by hearing earthly things, be nourished toward grasping heavenly ones. *For if I have told you earthly things,* he says, *and you do not believe; how, if I tell you heavenly things, will you believe?*[^79] For that one had been, in a manner, removed from heaven, where in mind he had gone beyond God; that he might be temperate toward those to whom he said: *I could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal*[^80]. For there he had heard ineffable words[^81], which on earth it was not lawful for him to speak through sounding words, as it were through *winged birds;* and by bringing in *by his power the southwest wind,* that is, through the southern winds, the fervent and shining spirits of the preachers; and this *by his power,* lest the southwest wind attribute to itself what it has from God. But these winds come of their own accord to men, and bring them words sent from God; that in their own seats and around their own tents they may gather such *birds,* and that each one may adore the Lord from his own place, all the islands of the nations[^82].

**18.** But for the unbelieving, as for a perverse and embittering generation, while the food was still in their mouth, *the anger of God arose upon them, and killed many of them*: that is, the greater part of them, or, as some codices have, *the fat ones among them.* Which indeed in the Greek codices we had, we did not find. But if this is more true, what else are *the fat ones among them* to be understood as, except those prevailing in pride, of whom it is said: *Their iniquity shall come forth as out of fatness*[^83]? *And he hindered the chosen of Israel.* For there were even there chosen ones, to whose faith the perverse and embittering generation was not tempered. But they were hindered from profiting at all those whom with paternal affection they desired to counsel. For to those with whom God is angry, what does human mercy confer? Or did he rather wish the chosen too to be understood as fettered together with them; so that those who were distinguished in mind and life might, as an example not only of righteousness but also of patience, endure troubles with them? For we have learned that the saints were led captive together with sinners perhaps for no other reason; since in the Greek codices we read not the word that means *he hindered;* but the word that means rather *he fettered.*

**19.** [vv. 32-33.] But the *perverse and embittering generation, in all these things sinned still, and did not believe in his wonders. And their days failed away in vanity*: when they could, if they believed, have their days in truth without failing, with him to whom it is said: *Your years shall not fail*[^84]. *Their days,* then, *failed away in vanity, and their years with haste.* For the whole life of mortals is hastened on, and what seems more prolonged is a vapor somewhat more lasting.

**20.** [vv. 34-35.] Yet *when he killed them, they sought him*: not for the sake of eternal life, but fearing to end the vapor too soon. They sought him, then—not, surely, those whom he had killed, but those who feared to be killed by their example. But Scripture spoke of them as though those who were being killed sought God, because they were one people, and it is spoken of as of one body. *And they turned back, and came early to God. And they remembered that God is their helper, and the Most High God is their redeemer*[^85]. But all this for the sake of acquiring temporal goods, and avoiding temporal evils. For those who sought God for the sake of earthly benefits were surely not seeking God, but those things. And now too God is worshiped with servile fear, not with free love. So, then, God is not worshiped: for that is worshiped which is loved. Hence, since God is found greater and better than all things, he is to be loved more than all things, that he may be worshiped.

**21.** [vv. 36-37.] Finally, let us see here what follows: *And they loved him,* he says, *with their mouth, and with their tongue they lied to him. But their heart was not right with him, nor were they held faithful in his testament.* He found one thing on their tongue, another in their heart, he to whom the secrets of men are bare, and who saw without any obstacle what they rather loved. The heart, then, is right with God, when for the sake of God it seeks God. For it desired one thing from the Lord, this it will seek, that it may dwell in the house of the Lord forever, and contemplate his delight[^86]. To whom the heart of the faithful says: I shall be satisfied, not with the fleshpots of the Egyptians, nor with the melons and gourds, and garlic and onions, which the perverse and embittering generation preferred even to the heavenly bread[^87], nor with the visible manna and the winged birds themselves; but I shall be satisfied, when your glory shall be made manifest[^88]. For this is the inheritance of the New Testament, in which those men were not held faithful, whose faith, however, even then when it was veiled, was in the chosen, and now, when it has been revealed, is not in many who are called: *For many are called, but few are chosen*[^89]. Such, then, was the perverse and embittering generation, even when it seemed to seek God; loving with the mouth and lying with the tongue; but in the heart not right with God, where it rather loved those things for the sake of which it sought God's help.

**22.** [vv. 38-39.] *But he is merciful, and will be propitious to their sins, and will not destroy them. And he will abound, that he may turn away his anger, and will not kindle all his anger. And he remembered that they are flesh; a spirit that goes, and does not return.* In these words many promise themselves impunity for iniquity from the divine mercy, even if they persist in being such as this *perverse and embittering generation* is described, *which did not direct its heart, and whose spirit was not entrusted to God*: with whom it is not expedient to agree. For if, to speak according to them, God perhaps will not destroy even the wicked, without doubt he will not destroy the good. Why, then, do we not rather choose that in which there is no doubt? For those who lie with their tongue, while their heart has something else, surely suppose and wish, when he threatens eternal punishment to such men, that God too is a liar. But although they themselves by lying do not deceive him, he by speaking the truth does not deceive. These words, then, of the divine utterances, with which the perverse generation flatters itself, let it not pervert, as it does its own heart; because even with that perverted, these stand right. For first they can be understood according to what is written in the Gospel: *That you may be like your Father who is in heaven; who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^90]. For who does not see with how great a patience of mercy he spares the wicked! but before the judgment. So, then, he spared that nation, so as not to kindle all his anger to root it out and finish it utterly; which appears evidently in his words, and in the intercession of his servant Moses for their sins, where God says: *Let me destroy them, and I will make of you a great nation*[^91]: that one intercedes, more ready to be destroyed for their sake than that they should be; knowing that with the Merciful One he was doing this, who, since he would in no way destroy Moses himself, would spare even those very men for Moses' sake. For let us see how much he spared, and still spares. For he brought them into the land of promise, and preserved that nation until, by killing Christ, they bound themselves to the greatest crime of all: and that he scattered them, rooted out of that kingdom, through all the kingdoms of the nations, he surely did not destroy; but the same people remains, preserved by the succession of offspring, as Cain, having received a sign, lest anyone kill him—that is, utterly destroy him[^92]. Behold how that is fulfilled which was said: *But he is merciful, and will be propitious to their sins, and will not destroy them; and he will abound, that he may turn away his anger, and will not kindle all his anger.* For if all his anger—that is, as much as they deserve—were kindled against them, none of that nation would be left. So God, to whom mercy and judgment are sung[^93], both in this world by mercy *makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil,* and at the end of the world by judgment, having separated them from his eternal light, punishes the wicked with eternal darkness.

**23.** Then, lest we seem to do violence to the divine words, and where it was said: *He will not destroy them,* we should say: But afterward he will destroy them: let us, from this very present psalm, note a most usual manner of speaking of Scripture, by which this question may be much more diligently and truly resolved. For surely, speaking of these very men a little after, when he had recalled what the Egyptians had suffered for their sake, recalling the last plague: *And he struck,* he says, *every firstborn in the land of Egypt, the first fruits of their labor in the tents of Ham. And he took away his people like sheep, and led them through like a flock in the desert. And he led them in hope, and they did not fear: and the sea covered their enemies. And he brought them into the mountain of his sanctification; the mountain which his right hand acquired. And he cast out the nations from before them, and by lot distributed to them the land with a measuring line of distribution*[^94]. If anyone, raising a question against us on these words, should say: How does he recall that all these things were bestowed on them, since they themselves were not brought into the land of promise, who were freed from Egypt, for they died? what shall we answer, except that they are so called, because it was the same people through the succession of children? So, then, when we hear—especially because they are words of future time: *And he will be propitious to their sins, and will not destroy them; and he will abound, that he may turn away his anger, and will not kindle all his anger*—let us understand this fulfilled in those of whom the Apostle says: *So then, even at this time a remnant has been saved according to the election of grace*[^95]. Whence he also says: *Has God cast off his people? God forbid. For I too am an Israelite, of the race of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews*[^96]. These, then, Scripture foresaw, who from that people were to believe in Christ, and to receive the remission of sins, even up to that greatest sin by which, raving, they slew the physician himself. Hence it was altogether said: *But he is merciful, and will be propitious to their sins, and will not destroy them; and he will abound, that he may turn away his anger*; because he forgave even that, that his only Son was killed by them: *and will not kindle all his anger;* because *a remnant has been saved*[^97].

**24.** *And he remembered that they are flesh; a spirit that goes, and does not return.* Therefore by calling and pitying them through his grace he himself recalled them, because through themselves they could not return. For how does flesh return—a spirit walking and not returning, when the weight of evil deserts forces it into the depths and far reaches of evils—except through the election of grace? Which is not rendered to merits as a wage; but is given freely as a gift, that the wicked man may be justified, and the lost sheep return; yet not by its own strength, but carried back on the shoulders of the shepherd[^98], which could lose itself while it wandered of its own will, but could not find itself, nor would it be found at all, unless it were sought by the mercy of the shepherd. For neither does that son fail to belong to this sheep, who, having returned into himself, said: *I will arise, and go to my father*[^99]. By a hidden calling and inspiration, then, he too was sought and raised up, by none but him who gives life to all things; and found, by whom, except by him who went forth to save and seek what had perished[^100]? *For he was dead, and lived again; he had perished, and was found*[^101]. For that other question too, no light one, is thus resolved, which is written in Proverbs, when Scripture was speaking of the way of iniquity: *All who walk in it shall not return*[^102]. For it was so said, as though all the wicked were to be despaired of: but Scripture rather commended grace; because by himself man can walk in it, but he cannot by himself return, unless recalled by grace.

**25.** [vv. 40-51.] These perverse and embittering ones, then: *How often they embittered him in the desert, and provoked him to anger in the waterless place! And they turned, and tempted God, and embittered the Holy One of Israel.* He repeats the same unbelief of theirs which he had already recalled above; but the cause of repeating is that there may be recalled also the plagues which he inflicted on the Egyptians for their sake: all which they surely ought to have remembered, and not to have become ungrateful. Finally, what follows? *They did not remember his hand, in the day in which he redeemed them from the hand of the one troubling them.* And he begins to tell what he did to the Egyptians: *He set his signs in Egypt, and his portents in the field of Tanis. And he turned their rivers into blood, and their showers, that they might not drink;* or rather, *the springings of waters,* as some better understand what is written in Greek, which in Latin we call welling springs, waters bubbling up from below. For the Egyptians dug, and found blood instead of water. *He sent among them the dog-fly, and it devoured them; and the frog, and it destroyed them. And he gave their fruit to the blight, and their labors to the locust. And he killed their vines with hail, and their mulberry trees with frost. And he gave over their cattle to the hail, and their possession to fire. He sent upon them the anger of his indignation; indignation, and anger, and tribulation, a sending in by evil angels. He made a way for the path of his anger, and did not spare from death their souls, and shut up their cattle in death. And he struck every firstborn in the land of Egypt, the first fruits of their labors in the tents of Ham*[^103].

**26.** All these punishments of the Egyptians can be expounded by an allegorical interpretation, as each one may wish to understand them, and to compare them with the things to which they are to be referred. Which we too will try to do; doing it the more fitly, the more we have been divinely helped. For to this the words of this psalm constrain us, where it was said: *I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter propositions from the beginning*[^104]. For on this account some things are said here which we do not read happened to the Egyptians at all, since all their plagues are most diligently woven together in order in Exodus; so that, since we are certain that what is not said there is not said in vain in the Psalm, and we cannot interpret it except figuratively, we may at the same time understand also the rest, which it is established did happen, to have been done or written for the sake of some figured signification. For Scripture does this in many places of the prophetic utterances. It says something which is not found in that deed which it seems to recall, rather is found to be otherwise; so that hence it may be understood not to say this which can be supposed, but rather that which ought more to be heeded: as is that: *He shall have dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^105]. Which it is established was not fulfilled in the kingdom of that Solomon, of whom this psalm might be supposed to speak, whereas it was speaking of Christ the Lord. In the plagues of the Egyptians, then, which are in the book called Exodus, where Scripture especially took care that all the things by which they were afflicted should be told in order, there is not found what this psalm has: *And he gave their fruit to the blight.* That too which, when he had said: *And he gave over their cattle to the hail,* he added: *And their possession to fire*[^106]: of cattle killed by hail we read in Exodus; but that their possession was burned by fire, we do not read at all. Although voices and fires came with the hail, as thunders are wont to come with lightnings; yet it is not written that anything was given over to fire to be burned. Finally, the soft things which the hail could not harm are said not to have been struck, that is, harmed by hard blows; which afterward the locust devoured[^107]. Likewise what is here said: *And their mulberry trees with frost,* is not in Exodus: for frost differs much from hail; for on clear winter nights the earth grows white with frost.

**27.** What these things signify, then, let the expounder say as he can; let the reader and hearer judge as is just. Water turned into blood seems to me to signify thinking carnally about the causes of things. The dog-fly is canine manners, which do not even see their parents when they are born. The frog is most talkative vanity. The blight harms secretly; which some have interpreted as rust, others as a scorching wind: which evil to what vice is it more aptly compared, than to that which appears most difficultly, such as trusting much in oneself? For it is a harmful breath which works this latently in the fruits; as in conduct the hidden pride, when anyone thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing[^108]. The locust is malice harming with the mouth, namely, by unfaithful testimony. The hail is iniquity taking away the goods of others; whence are born thefts, robberies, and plunderings: but the ravager himself is more laid waste by his own vice. The frost signifies the vice by which love of one's neighbor freezes over in the darkness of folly, as in nocturnal cold. But the fire, if not that one is here recalled which was in the hail from the lightning clouds—since here he said: *He gave over their possession to fire,* where he surely signifies it burned, which that fire is not read to have done—seems to me to signify the savagery of wrath, by which even murder can be committed. By the death of cattle is figured, as I judge, the loss of chastity. For the concupiscence by which offspring arises we have in common with cattle. To have this tamed and ordered, then, is the virtue of chastity. The death of the firstborn is the loss of that very righteousness by which each one is sociable to the human race. But whether the figured significations of these things are so, or are better understood otherwise; whom does it not move that the Egyptians are struck with ten plagues, and the tablets by which the people of God is ruled are written with ten precepts? Concerning the comparing of these with each other—that is, plagues and precepts—since we have spoken elsewhere, there is no need to burden the exposition of this psalm with them: only we admonish that here too, even if not in the same order, yet ten plagues of the Egyptians are recalled; while in place of three that are in Exodus[^109], and are not here—that is, the gnats, the boils, the darkness—three others are recalled which are not there, that is, the blight, the frost, and the fire, not of the lightnings, but that to which it is said his possession was given over, which is not read there.

**28.** But it is sufficiently expressed here that by the judgment of God these things were done among them through evil angels, in this malignant world, as in Egypt and in the field of Tanis, where we ought to be humble, until there comes the world where we may deserve to be exalted out of this humility. For Egypt too in the Hebrew tongue signifies Darkness or Tribulations, in which tongue Tanis, as I have recalled, is understood to be "humble commandment." Concerning evil angels, then, in this psalm, when he was speaking of these very plagues, there is interposed something not to be passed over cursorily: *He sent upon them,* he says, *the anger of his indignation; indignation and anger, and tribulation, a sending in, through evil angels.* But that the devil and his angels are so evil, that surely eternal fire is prepared for them, no faithful person is ignorant: but that a sending-in is sent through them upon any whom the Lord God judges worthy of this punishment, seems hard to those who are less fit to consider how the supreme justice of God uses even the evil well. As far as concerns their substance, who else but he himself made them? But them as evil he did not make: yet he uses them, since he is good, well—that is, fittingly and justly; as, on the contrary, the wicked use his good creatures ill. God uses evil angels, then, not only to punish the wicked, as in all these things of which the Psalm speaks, as in King Ahab, whom a spirit of deceit, by the will of God, seduced that he might fall in battle[^110]; but also to test and manifest the good, as he did in Job. But as for that corporeal material of visible elements, I think that both good and evil angels can use them, as far as each has power; just as good and evil men too use such things, as far as they can according to the measure of human weakness. For we too use earth, and water, and air, and fire, not only in the necessary matters of our sustenance, but also in many superfluous and playful things, and in marvelously skilled works. For innumerable things called "mechanical" are devised by treating these elements with art. But over these the angels have far greater power, both good and evil, though surely greater the good; but as far as by God's nod and order it is commanded or permitted, as also with us. For in these too we cannot do all that we will. But in a most faithful book we read that the devil was able even to send fire from heaven, to consume so great a number of a holy man's cattle by a marvelous and dreadful onrush[^111]; which no faithful person would perhaps dare to attribute to the devil, unless it were read on the authority of holy Scripture. But that man, righteous by God's gift, and strong, and piously skilled, did not say, The Lord gave, the devil took away; but, *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away*[^112]; knowing most excellently that the devil, even what he could do with those elements, would yet not do to the servant of God except with his Lord willing and permitting; he confounded the malice of the devil, since he knew who would use those things to prove himself. But in the children of unbelief, as in his own bondservants, he works[^113], as men do in their own cattle; and there too as far as is permitted by the just judgment of God. But it is one thing when his power even to handle his own, as it pleases him, is prohibited by a greater power; another when power is given him even over those who are estranged from him. As a man does what he wills with his own cattle, according to human capacity; and surely does not do it, if he is prohibited by a greater power: but to do anything with another's cattle, he awaits power to be given him by the one whose it is. There, then, the power that had been, is prohibited; here, that which had not been, is granted.

**29.** Since these things are so, if God inflicted those plagues on the Egyptians through evil angels, shall we dare to say that the water too was turned into blood through those same angels, and the frogs made through those same angels, the like of which even Pharaoh's magicians could do by their sorceries; so that evil angels stood on both sides, here afflicting them, there deceiving them, according to the judgment and dispensation of the most just and most omnipotent God, justly using even the malice of the wicked? I do not dare to say it. For whence were Pharaoh's magicians least able to make gnats[^114]? was it because the evil angels themselves were not permitted this? Or—what is more truly to be said—is the cause hidden, and exceeds the strength of our inquiry? For if we suppose that God did those things through evil angels because punishments were inflicted, not benefits bestowed, as though God inflicts punishments on no one through good angels, but through those as the executioners of the heavenly host; it will follow that we believe Sodom too was overthrown by evil angels, and that evil angels are seen to have received Abraham and Lot into their hospitality: which far be it that we should think against the most open Scriptures[^115]. It is clear, then, that these things can be done to men through good and evil angels: what or when it ought to be done, is hidden from me; but from him who does it, it is not hidden, and from him to whom he wishes to reveal it. Yet, as far as the divine Scripture moderates our purpose, we read that punishments are inflicted on the wicked both through good angels, as on the Sodomites, and through evil angels, as on the Egyptians: but that the righteous are tempted and tested with bodily punishments through good angels, does not occur to me.

**30.** But as for the present passage of this psalm: if we do not dare to attribute to evil angels those things that were marvelously done concerning the creatures, we have what we can without doubt attribute to them; the deaths of cattle, the deaths of the firstborn, and especially that on which all things were bound up, the hardening of their heart, that they should refuse to let the people of God go. For this most iniquitous and most malignant obstinacy, when God is said to work[^116], he works not by instigating and inspiring, but by deserting, so that those evil angels may work in the children of unbelief[^117] what God duly and justly permits. For that too which the prophet Isaiah says: *O Lord, behold you are angry, and we have sinned; therefore we have erred, and have become like all that are unclean*[^118], is understood by this reason: something went before, on account of which God, most justly angry, withdrew his light from them, so that the blindness of the human mind might stumble into sins which by no shuffling can be defended not to be sins, by deviating and erring from the path of righteousness. And what is written in another psalm of these very Egyptians, that God turned their heart to hate his people, and to deal craftily with his servants[^119], is well believed that God did through those evil angels; so that the now vicious minds of the children of unbelief were stirred up, through those angels to whom the same vices are dear, into hatred of the people of God, and those marvels followed to terrify and correct the good. Those evils of conduct too, which we said are signified by these bodily plagues—on account of which it was foretold: *I will open my mouth in parables*[^120]—are most fittingly believed to be exercised through evil angels upon those who are subjected to them by divine equity. For neither, when there happens what the Apostle says: *God gave them over to the desires of their heart, that they might do what is not fitting*[^121], do the evil angels not there busy themselves and rejoice, as in the material of their work; to whom most justly is subjected human viciousness, except those whom grace frees. And who is fit for these things[^122]? Hence, when he had said: *He sent upon them the anger of his indignation; indignation, and anger, and tribulation, a sending in through evil angels,* what is that which he added: *He made a way for the path of his anger*? whose mental edge, indeed, suffices to penetrate, so as to understand and grasp the meaning lying hidden in so great a depth? For there was a path of God's anger, by which he punished the impiety of the Egyptians by a hidden equity; but he made a way for that same path, so that, leading them as it were out of hiding through evil angels into manifest crimes, he might most evidently take vengeance on the most evident impious ones. From this power of evil angels nothing frees a man but the grace of God, of which the Apostle says: *Who rescued us from the power of darkness, and translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^123]: of which thing this people bore a figure, when it was rescued from the power of the Egyptians, and translated into the kingdom of the land of promise flowing with milk and honey, which signifies the sweetness of grace.

**31.** [vv. 52-53.] There follows, then, after the recalling of the plagues of the Egyptians, the Psalm, and it says: *And he took away his people like sheep, and led them through like a flock in the desert. And he led them in hope, and they did not fear, and the sea covered their enemies.* This is done the better, the more inwardly, when, rescued from the power of darkness, we are translated in mind into the kingdom of God, and according to the spiritual pastures are made the sheep of God, walking in this world as in a desert, since to no one is our faith conspicuous; whence the Apostle says: *Your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^124]. But we are led in hope, since *in hope we have been saved*: nor ought we to fear; *for if God is for us, who is against us*[^125]? And the sea covered our enemies; it abolished them in baptism by the remission of sins.

**32.** [v. 54.] Then it follows: *And he brought them into the mountain of his sanctification.* How much better, into the holy Church? *The mountain which his right hand acquired.* How much more sublime is the Church which Christ acquired, of whom it was said: *And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed*[^126]? *And he cast out the nations from before them*: from before his faithful too; for in a manner the nations are the malignant spirits of gentile errors. *And by lot he divided to them the land with a measuring line of distribution.* In us too *one and the same Spirit works all things, dividing to each his own as he wills*[^127].

**33.** [vv. 55-58.] *And he made the tribes of Israel to dwell in their tents.* In the tents, he says, of the nations he made the tribes of Israel to dwell. Which I think is better discussed spiritually thus, that to the heavenly glory, from which the sinning angels were cast out and thrown down, we are carried up through the grace of Christ. For that *perverse and embittering generation,* since it did not put off the tunic of oldness for these bodily benefits, *tempted* still *and embittered the Most High God, and did not keep his testimonies: and they turned away, and did not keep the covenant, as their fathers did*: for by a certain pact and pleasure they had said: *All that the Lord our God has spoken, we will do and we will hear*[^128]. It is surely to be noted that he says, *as their fathers:* for though throughout the whole text of the Psalm he seemed to speak as of the same men, yet it appears that now it is said of those who were already in the land of promise, and that those are called their fathers who embittered him in the wilderness.

**34.** *They were turned,* he says, *into a perverse bow*: or, as other codices have, *into a crooked bow.* But what this is appears more in what follows, where he says: *And they provoked him to anger on their hills.* He signifies that they leaped forth into idolatry. The bow, then, is crooked, not for the name of the Lord, but against the name of the Lord, who said to that same people: *You shall not have other gods besides me*[^129]. But by the bow he signifies the intention of the mind. Finally, expressing this very thing more plainly: *And by their graven images,* he says, *they provoked him to jealousy.*

**35.** [vv. 59-60.] *God heard, and despised*: that is, he took note, and took vengeance. *And he reduced Israel exceedingly to nothing.* For with God despising them, what did those remain who, with God helping, were what they were? But surely he recalls that deed which was done, when they were conquered by the foreigners in the time of Eli the priest, and the Ark of the Lord was captured, and they were laid waste with great slaughter[^130]. This is what he says: *And he cast off the tabernacle of Shiloh, his tabernacle where he dwelt among men.* He elegantly expounded why he cast off his tabernacle, when he said, *where he dwelt among men.* Since, then, they were not worthy among whom he should dwell, why should he not cast off the tabernacle, which surely he had instituted not for his own sake, but for their sake, whom he now judged unworthy for him to dwell among?

**36.** [v. 61.] *And he gave over their strength into captivity, and their beauty into the hands of the enemy.* The Ark itself, from which they seemed to themselves unconquerable, and over which they applauded themselves, he calls their strength and their beauty. Finally, afterward too, when they were living ill and glorying in the temple of the Lord, he terrifies them through the prophet, saying: *See what I did to Shiloh, where my tabernacle was*[^131].

**37.** [vv. 62-63.] *And he shut up his people with the sword, and despised his inheritance. Fire devoured their young men*: that is, anger. *And their virgins were not lamented*: because there was no leisure for this either, in the fear of the enemy.

**38.** [v. 64.] *Their priests fell by the sword, and their widows were not wept for.* For the sons of Eli fell by the sword, the wife of one of whom, being widowed, and soon dying in childbirth, could not, on account of that same disturbance, be mourned with the honor of a funeral[^132].

**39.** [v. 65.] *And the Lord was awakened as one sleeping.* For he seems to sleep, when he gives his people into the hands of those who hate them, where it is said to them: *Where is your God*[^133]? *He was awakened,* then, *as one sleeping, as a mighty man overcome by wine.* No one would dare to say this of God, except his Spirit. For he said, as it seems to the impious who insult, that he sleeps long as if drunk, when he does not come to aid as quickly as men suppose.

**40.** [v. 66.] *And he struck his enemies in the hinder parts*: those, surely, who rejoiced that they were able to take his Ark captive; for they were struck in their seats[^134]. Which seems to me a sign of that punishment by which everyone will be tormented, if he has looked back to the hinder parts; which, like the Apostle, he ought to esteem as dung[^135]. For those who so receive the testament of God that they do not strip off their old vanity, are like the hostile peoples who set the Ark of the testament, when captured, beside their own idols. And those old things indeed fall even with them unwilling, because *all flesh is grass, and the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass has withered, and the flower has fallen*: but *the Ark of the Lord remains forever*[^136]; namely, the secret of the testament, the kingdom of heaven, where is the eternal *Word* of God. But those who loved the hinder parts will most justly be tormented by them: for *he gave them everlasting reproach.*

**41.** [vv. 67-68.] *And he cast off,* he says, *the tabernacle of Joseph, and did not choose the tribe of Ephraim. And he chose the tribe of Judah.* He did not say: He cast off the tabernacle of Reuben, who was Jacob's firstborn; nor those who follow, and precede Judah in birth, so that, those being cast off and not chosen, the tribe of Judah might be chosen. For it could rightly be said that those were cast off, because even in the blessing of Jacob, by which he blessed his sons, their sins are recalled and gravely detested[^137]; although among them the tribe of Levi deserved to be the priestly tribe, whence Moses too came[^138]. Nor did he say: He cast off the tabernacle of Benjamin; or: He did not choose the tribe of Benjamin, from which a king had already begun to be; for from there Saul had been chosen[^139]: whence, from the very nearness of time, when that one was cast off and reprobated, and David chosen[^140], this could fittingly be said; yet it was not said: but he named especially those who by more illustrious merits seemed to excel. For Joseph fed his father and his brothers in Egypt, and, impiously sold, was most justly exalted by the merit of piety, chastity, and wisdom[^141]; and Ephraim was preferred to his elder brother by the blessing of his grandfather Jacob[^142]: and yet God *cast off the tabernacle of Joseph, and did not choose the tribe of Ephraim.* Where, by these names of illustrious merit, what else do we understand than that whole people, seeking from the Lord earthly rewards with an ancient cupidity, was cast off and reprobated; but that the tribe of Judah was chosen, not for the merits of Judah himself? For far greater are the merits of Joseph; but through the tribe of Judah, since from it Christ arose according to the flesh, Scripture testifies that the new people of Christ was preferred to that old people, the Lord opening his mouth in parables. From there too what follows: *Mount Zion which he loved,* we better understand as the Church of Christ, worshiping God not for the carnal benefits of the present time, but beholding with the eyes of faith the future and eternal rewards afar off: for Zion too is interpreted "watchtower."

**42.** [v. 69.] Finally it follows: *And he built his sanctification like that of unicorns*: or, as some interpreters made a new word, *his sanctuary.* By unicorns are rightly understood those whose firm hope is raised up unto that one thing, of which another psalm says: *One thing I have asked of the Lord; this I will seek*[^143]. But the sanctuary of God is, according to the apostle Peter, understood to be *a holy people, and a royal priesthood*[^144]. But as for what follows: *In the land which he founded forever*: which the Greek codices have, whether it be said by us *forever,* or *for the age,* is in the power of the Latin interpreters, since it signifies both; and therefore this is found in the Latin codices, that in others. Some even have it in the plural, that is, *for the ages;* which in the Greek codices we had, we did not find. But which of the faithful would doubt that the Church, even though by some departing and others coming, it passes mortally from this life, is yet founded forever?

**43.** [vv. 70-71.] *And he chose David his servant.* The tribe of Judah, then, for David's sake; but David for Christ's sake: the tribe of Judah, therefore, for Christ's sake, at whose passing the blind men cried out: *Have mercy on us, Son of David;* and straightway by his mercy they received their sight[^145], because what they cried out was true. This therefore the Apostle says not in passing, but commends attentively, writing to Timothy: *Be mindful that Christ Jesus rose from the dead, of the seed of David, according to my Gospel, in which I labor even unto chains, as an evildoer; but the word of God is not bound*[^146]. The Savior himself, then, made according to the flesh of the seed of David, is figured in this place by the name of David, the Lord opening his mouth in parables. Nor let it move us that when he had said: *And he chose David,* by which name he signified Christ, he added, *his servant;* not, his son: rather, from this let us recognize, not the substance of the Only-begotten coeternal with the Father, but the form of a servant assumed from the seed of David.

**44.** *And he took him from the flocks of sheep; from following the ewes that bore young he took him, to feed Jacob his servant, and Israel his inheritance.* That David indeed, from whose seed is the flesh of Christ, was translated from the pastoral office over cattle to the kingdom over men: but our David, Jesus himself, from men to men, from the Jews to the Gentiles; yet, according to the parable, he was taken and translated from sheep to sheep. For there are not now in that land the Churches of Judea which were in Christ, which existed of the circumcised in the recent passion and resurrection of our Lord; of whom the Apostle says: *But I was unknown by face to the Churches of Judea which are in Christ: only they heard that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith which he once laid waste; and in me they glorified the Lord*[^147]. Now those Churches of the circumcised peoples have passed away from there; and through this, in the Judea which is now on earth, Christ is not now: he has been taken away from there; now he feeds the flocks of the Gentiles. Yet from following the ewes that bore young he was taken from there. For those former ones were such as those of whom it is said in the Song of Songs to the one Church which consists of many—that is, to the one flock whose members are many flocks; of such, then, it is said: *Your teeth,* that is, those through whom you speak, or through whom you bring others into your body, as it were by eating; signifying this, then, *your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes coming up from the washing, all of which bear twins, and there is none barren among them*[^148]. For they then laid down, as it were fleeces, the burdens of the world, when they laid the prices of their sold goods at the feet of the apostles[^149], coming up from that washing, of which the apostle Peter admonishes them, anxious that they had shed Christ's blood, and says: *Do penance, and let each of you be baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, and your sins will be forgiven you*[^150]. And they bore twins, namely, the works of the two precepts of the twofold love, the love of God, and the love of neighbor: whence there was none barren among them. From following these ewes that bore young our David was taken, and now feeds other flocks among the Gentiles, and those too Jacob and Israel: for so it was said: *To feed Jacob his servant, and Israel his inheritance.* For not because these sheep are from the Gentiles are they therefore estranged from that seed which is Jacob and Israel. For it is the seed of Abraham, the seed of the promise, of which the Lord said to him: *In Isaac shall your seed be called*[^151]. Which the Apostle, expounding: *Not,* he says, *the children of the flesh, but the children of the promise are reckoned as seed*[^152]. For from the Gentiles were the faithful to whom he said: *But if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed, heirs according to the promise*[^153]. But as for what he says: *Jacob his servant, and Israel his inheritance,* Scripture in its usual manner repeated the same statement. Unless perhaps someone should wish so to distinguish, that at this time Jacob may serve; but then there may be the eternal inheritance of God, when he will see God face to face, whence Israel received its name[^154].

**45.** [v. 72.] *And he fed them,* he says, *in the innocence of his heart.* What is more innocent than he, who had no sin at all, not only by which he might be conquered, but even which he might conquer? *And in the understanding of his hands he led them*: or, as some codices have, *in the understandings of his hands.* Another might suppose it could have been more fittingly said: In the innocence of the hands and the understanding of the heart: but he who knew better than others what he was saying, preferred to join innocence to the heart, and understanding to the hands. The former, as I judge, because many seem to themselves innocent who do not do evils, when they fear lest they suffer if they do them; but would wish to do them, if they could with impunity. Such men can seem to have innocence of the hands, yet not of the heart. And what at last, or of what kind, is that innocence, if it is not of the heart, where man was made to the image of God? But as for what he says: *In the understanding,* or *understandings, of his hands he led them,* he seems to me to have called it the understanding which he himself works in believers; therefore *of his hands:* for to work pertains to the hands: but as the hands of God can be understood, since Christ is so man, that he is also God. This surely that David, of whose seed he is, could not do in the people over whom as a man he reigned; but he does this, to whom the faithful soul can rightly say: *Make me to understand, and I will search out your law*[^155]. Accordingly, lest we err from him, while we trust in our own understanding as though it were from ourselves, let us by believing submit ourselves to his hands. Let him work it in us, that in the understanding of his hands he may lead us, rescued from error, and bring us where we can no longer err. This is the fruit of the people of God, attending to the law of God, and inclining their ear to the words of his mouth, that they may direct their heart in him, and have their spirit entrusted to him, lest they be changed into a perverse and embittering generation; but, all these things having been announced to them, not only for the present life, but also for the eternal, and not only for receiving the rewards of good works, but also in the very doing of good works, let them put their hope in God.
