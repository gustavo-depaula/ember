# Exposition of Psalm 146

**1.** The delights of our spirit are the divine songs, where even weeping is not without joy. To the faithful man and pilgrim in this world, there is no more pleasant remembrance than that of the city from which he is a pilgrim: but the remembrance of the city in pilgrimage is not without grief and sighing. Yet the sure hope of our return consoles and exhorts us, sad even in our pilgrimage. Let the words of God seize your heart, and let your possessor claim for himself his own possession, that is, your minds, lest they be turned away to something else. Let each one of you be wholly here, that he may not be here: that is, let him be wholly in the word of God, which sounds on earth that he may be exalted by it, and let him not be on earth. For God is with us for this, that we too may be with him. For he who, that he might be with us, came down to us, brings it about that we, that we may be with him, ascend to him. Meanwhile he himself did not disdain our pilgrimage; for nowhere is he a pilgrim, who founded all things.

**2.** [v. 2.] Behold, the Psalm sounds: it is the voice of someone, which, if you wish, is yours, exhorting his soul to praise God, and saying to himself: *Praise the Lord, O my soul*[^1]. For sometimes in the tribulations and temptations of the present life, whether you will or not, the soul is troubled: whose perturbation he addresses in another psalm, saying: *Why are you sad, O my soul? and why do you trouble me*[^2]? But that he may take away from it that same perturbation, he suggests joy not yet from the reality, but from hope, and says to it, troubled and anxious, sad and mourning: *Hope in the Lord, for still I will confess to him.* The hope by which he raised himself, he set in confession, as though his soul, which sadness was troubling, said to him: Why do you say to me: *Hope in the Lord*? The conscience of my sins calls me back; I know what I have committed, and you say: *Hope in the Lord*? You have committed it, it is true: yet whence do you hope? *Because I will confess to him.* As God hates one defending his sins, so he lifts up one confessing them. This hope, then, received, which hope cannot be without joy; although we are in difficult circumstances according to this life, and full of storms and tempests: yet, this hope being raised up, the soul, because it rejoices in hope, as the apostle says: *Rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation*[^3]; receives a certain raising up toward God, that it may praise God; and it is said to it: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.*

**3.** But who says it, and to whom does he say it? What do we say, brothers? Does the flesh say: *Praise the Lord, O my soul*? And can the flesh suggest good counsel to its soul? However much the flesh be tamed, and, by strength imparted by the Lord, subjected to our service, so that it serves us altogether as a bondservant; it is enough that it does not hinder. Then, dearly beloved, counsels are surely sought from the better. And if our soul is something good, and our flesh is something good, because he created both who made all things very good[^4]; although, then, both are good in their own kinds, yet the apostle says: *The body indeed is dead on account of sin*[^5]. There is surely also that body, such as is promised us, and we do not yet have, in whose redemption we rejoice in hope, the apostle saying: *Within ourselves we groan, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body. For by hope we have been saved. But hope which is seen is not hope: for what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^6]. Although, then, our body is some good thing; yet as long as it is mortal on account of sin, as long as it is needy, as long as it is corruptible, as long as it is so changeable that it does not stand still in itself even for a point of time, without doubt it is such that we desire its redemption, by which it may at some time not be such. But what will it be at some time? Such as the same apostle says in another place: *This corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^7]. But even when our body has become such, a body now heavenly and spiritual, an angelic body in the fellowship of the angels, not even so will it give counsel to the soul. For the body always, since it is a body, is below the soul, and any cheap soul is found more excellent than the most excellent body.

**4.** Nor let this seem to you as it were wonderful, that even any cheap sinful soul is better than any great and most outstanding body. It is not better in merits, but in nature. There is indeed a sinful soul, defiled with certain filth of desires: yet better is gold, though dirty, than the most purified lead. So let your mind run through all creatures, and you will see that what we say is not incredible; that, however blameworthy the soul, it is yet more praiseworthy than a praiseworthy body. There are two things, soul and body. I blame the soul, I praise the body: I blame the soul, because it is wicked; I praise the body, because it is healthy. Yet in its own kind I praise the soul, or in its own kind I blame the soul; and in its own kind I praise the body, or blame it. If you ask me which is better, what I blamed, or what I praised; you are about to receive a wonderful answer. I surely blamed that one, and I praised this one: yet, asked which is better, I answer that what I blamed is better than what I praised. If you wonder at these two, attend to those two set ready at hand, which I mentioned concerning gold and lead. Behold, I blamed the gold. The gold is not good, it is dirty, it does not so shine, it is not so purified. This lead is excellent, nothing more purified. That one I blamed, this one I praised; and I set before you both, blaming the one, praising the other. After this my blaming and praising, ask me which of these is better; I will answer: The gold is better, even dirty, than the lead purified. Whence better? and why did you blame it? Why did I blame it? Because it is not yet the gold it can be. What can it be? Purified and better. Because it is not yet purified, it was blamed. Why was the lead praised? Because it is now so purified, that it cannot be better. Likewise you say an excellent horse, and a most wicked man; yet you set the blamed man before the praised horse. For if you are asked which of these two is better, you will answer: The man: not in merits, but in nature. In the arts you say an excellent shoemaker, for example, and you reprove some lawyer, that he is ignorant of many laws; you have praised the shoemaker, you have reproved the lawyer: yet ask of these two which is better; the more unskilled lawyer is set before the perfect shoemaker. Let your charity attend. So, many things being praised, and others blamed, when asked we very often set the blamed things before the praised things. The nature of the soul is more excellent than the nature of the body, it far excels; it is a spiritual thing, it is an incorporeal thing, it is near to the substance of God. It is something invisible, it rules the body, moves the members, directs the senses, prepares the thoughts, brings forth the actions, takes in the images of innumerable things; and who at last, dearest brothers, is sufficient for the praises of the soul? And if in the praises of the soul one fails, what praise is there of him who founded the soul? And yet so great is his grace, that this man says: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.* Who can praise God? If he said: Praise yourself, perhaps he would fail: *Praise*, he says, *God.* Try with the affection of devotion, you will fail in his praises. It is more expedient for you to fail in praising God, than to succeed in praising yourself. For when you praise God, and do not unfold what you wish, your thought is stretched into the inner things; that very stretching makes you more capable of him whom you praise.

**5.** Who, then, is it, as I had begun to say, who says: *Praise the Lord, O my soul*? The flesh does not say it. Though the body be angelic, it is lower than the soul; it cannot give counsel to its superior. The soul itself is unhappy, if it awaits counsel from the body. The flesh, well obedient, is the handmaid of the soul; the latter rules, the former is ruled; the latter commands, this one serves: when can the flesh give this counsel to the soul? Who, then, is it who says: *Praise the Lord, O my soul*? We find nothing more in man than flesh and soul: the whole man is this, spirit and flesh. Or does the soul itself perhaps say it to itself, and in a certain way command itself, and exhort and rouse itself? For by certain perturbations from some part of itself it was wavering; but from a certain part, which they call the rational mind, that by which it thinks wisdom, cleaving now to the Lord and sighing toward him, it noticed certain of its lower parts perturbed by worldly motions, and by a certain cupidity of earthly desires going into the outer things, abandoning the inner God: it calls itself back from the outer to the inner, from the lower to the higher, and says: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.* What pleases you in this world? What is it you wish to praise? what is it you wish to love? Wherever you turn yourself by the bodily senses, heaven meets you, the earth meets you: what you love in the earth is earthly; whatever you love also in heaven is bodily. Everywhere you love, and everywhere you praise: how is he to be praised who made these things which you praise? Now, then, you have lived long occupied, and beaten by the diversity of desires, you bear blows, wounded, divided among many loves, everywhere restless, nowhere secure; gather yourself to yourself: whatever pleased you outside, seek what author it has. There is nothing better on earth, for example, than this and that: gold, silver, animals, trees, pleasant places, think of the whole earth. What is better in heaven than the sun, the moon, the stars? Think of the whole heaven. All these together are very good, because God made all things very good. On every side the beauty of the work, which commends to you the craftsman. You marvel at the building, love the builder. Do not be occupied with what was made, and withdraw from him who made it. For these things with which you are occupied, he made below you, because he made you below himself. If you cleave to the higher, you will trample the lower; but if you withdraw from the higher, these things will be turned into punishment for you. For so it was made, my brothers: man received a body as it were into service, but having God as his Lord, having the body as servant, having above himself his Founder, below himself what was founded under him; the rational soul, set in a certain middle place, received a law, to cleave to the higher, to rule the lower. It cannot rule the lower, unless it is ruled by the better. It is drawn by the lower, so it has deserted the better. It cannot rule what it was ruling, because it was unwilling to be ruled by the one by whom it was ruled. Now, then, let it return, let it praise. The soul itself gives counsel to itself from the light of God through the rational mind, whence it conceives a counsel fixed in the eternity of its author. It reads there something to be feared, to be praised, to be loved, to be desired and sought after: it does not yet hold it, does not yet grasp it; it is grazed by a certain flashing, it is not so strong as to abide there. And so it gathers itself to a certain health, and says: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.*

**6.** And what is it, brothers? Do we not praise the Lord? do we not daily sing a hymn? does not our mouth daily sound, after our measure, our heart bring forth the praises of God? And what is it that we praise? Great is what we praise; but what we praise is still weak. When does the praiser fulfill the excellence of the one praised? Behold, a man stands, sings to God somewhat at length, and often the lips are moved to the song, but the thought flies through I know not what desires. So our mind was as it were standing at the praise of God, and our soul through diverse cupidities or cares of business was wavering this way and that. As it were from above the mind itself attends to the one wavering this way and that, and, as it were turned to it, addresses its restlessness of troubles: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.* What is it that you are busy about other things? what is it that the care of earthly and mortal things occupies you? Stand with me, praise the Lord. And as it were the soul itself, weighed down, and not able so to stand as is worthy, answers the mind: *I will praise the Lord in my life.* What is, *in my life*? Because now I am in my death. So first exhort yourself, and say: *Praise the Lord, O my soul.* Your soul answers you: I praise as much as I can, slightly, slenderly, weakly. Why? Because as long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims away from the Lord[^8]. Why do you so praise the Lord, not perfectly, not stably? Question Scripture: Because *the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the sense thinking many things*[^9]. Take from me the body which weighs down the soul, and I praise the Lord; take from me the earthly dwelling pressing down the sense thinking many things, that from many things I may flow together into one, and I praise the Lord: but as long as I am thus, I cannot, I am weighed down. What then? will you be silent, and not praise the Lord perfectly? *I will praise the Lord in my life.*

**7.** What is, *in my life*? *You are my hope* here. *You are my hope* here, we say: but *my portion* not here, but *in the land of the living*[^10]. For this is the land of the dying: hence we pass over: but it matters whither. Since both the evil man is a pilgrim here, and the good man is a pilgrim here. For it is not that the good man passes over, and the evil man remains here; or the evil man passes over, and the good man remains here: both pass over, but not both to one place. There were two; the poor man full of sores lying at the door of the rich man, and the rich man clothed in purple and fine linen, feasting daily splendidly: both were here, both passed over hence, but not to one place; they receive different places, because different merits lead them. The poor man passed over into the bosom of Abraham, and the rich man passed over into the torments of hell. Neighbors in body on earth; the one in the house, the other before the door: after death so separated, that Abraham says: *Between us and you a great chasm has been fixed*[^11]. So then, brothers, since hope here feeds us, but our life is not perfect, except that which is promised; here groaning, here temptations, here straits, here sorrows, here perils: our soul will praise the Lord as he is to be praised, according to what is said in another psalm: *Blessed are those who dwell in your house, forever and ever they will praise you*[^12]; when our whole business will be that very praise. But when this? *In my life.* For now what is it? It could say to you: My death. Whence your death? Because I am a pilgrim away from the Lord. For if to cleave to him is life; to withdraw from him is death. But what consoles you? Hope. You already live in hope; from hope praise, from hope sing. From what is your death, do not sing; from what you live, sing. Your death is from the sorrow of this world, you live in the hope of the world to come. *I will praise*, he says, *the Lord in my life.*

**8.** And how will you praise your Lord? *I will sing to my God as long as I am*[^13]. What kind of praise is that: *I will sing to my God as long as I am*? See, my brothers, what that being will be. Where there will be everlasting praise, there will be everlasting being. Behold, now you are; do you sing to your God as long as you are? Behold, you were singing; you turned yourself to some business, you no longer sing, and you are: behold, you are, and do not sing. Perhaps even, inclined by cupidity to something, you not only do not sing, but even offend his hearing; and yet you are. What will that praise be, where as long as you are you praise? But what does he say, *as long as I am*? Will it perhaps at some time not be? Rather, that very 'long' will be eternal, and therefore it will truly be long. For whatever has an end in time, however long it be, is not long. *I will sing to my God as long as I am.*

**9.** [v. 3.] Meanwhile well, you will praise the Lord in your life, you sing to your God of the future as long as you are. Well: whatever is to be presumed here, hope it from him. Let not hope desert us in this pilgrimage and temptation, in these wickednesses and snares of the enemy, with the temptations of the world clamoring around us on every side, set on every side in labors and anguishes. What, then, shall we do? Hear what follows: *Do not trust in princes*[^14]. Brothers, we receive here a great matter; it is a divine voice, and it sounds to us from above. For now by I know not what weakness of the human soul, when it has been troubled, here it despairs of the Lord, and wishes to presume on man. Let it be said to a man set in some affliction: There is a certain great man, through whom you can be freed: he smiles, he rejoices, he is raised up. But if it is said to him: God frees you: as it were he grows cold with despair. The help of a mortal is promised, and you rejoice: an immortal is promised, and you are sad! It is promised to you that one to be freed with you may free you, and you exult as at some great help: there is promised that liberator who needs no liberator, and as it were at a fable you despair. Woe to such thoughts; they are far on pilgrimage: truly a wretched and great death is in them. Draw near, begin to desire, begin to seek and to know him by whom you were made. For he will not desert his own work, if he is not deserted by his work. Turn yourself, then, to him to whom you say: *I will praise the Lord in my life, I will sing to my God as long as I am.* For this man, now filled with such great spirit, admonishes us, and says as it were to those far removed, and far on pilgrimage, and not only unwilling to praise God, but not yet even hoping in God: *Do not trust in princes, and in the sons of men, in whom there is no salvation.* In one Son of man there is salvation; and in him not because he is the son of man, but because he is the Son of God; not on account of that which he took from you, but on account of that which he kept in himself. So in no man is there salvation: for in him too there is therefore salvation, because he is God, *who is over all, God blessed forever.* Of Christ it was said: *From whom is Christ according to the flesh*[^15]. From whom? From the Jews, from the fathers *Christ according to the flesh*: but is the whole of Christ that which is according to the flesh? No: for not according to the flesh is he *over all, God blessed forever.* Therefore in him is salvation, because *salvation is the Lord's.* For another psalm says: *Salvation is the Lord's, and upon your people your blessing*[^16]. And men without cause arrogate to themselves that they give salvation. Let them give it to themselves. Answer the proud man: You boast, because you say that you give me salvation; give it to yourself. See whether you have it. Considering well your own frailty, you see that you do not yet have it. So do not admonish me to wait for it from you, but wait with me. *Do not trust in princes, and in the sons of men, in whom there is no salvation.* Behold, I know not what princes come forth, I know not whence, and they say: I baptize, and what I give, that is holy: and if you have received from another, you have received nothing; if you have received from me, you have received something. O man, O prince, do you wish to be among the sons of men, and among the princes in whom there is no salvation? Do I, then, have salvation, because you give it to me? Is it yours, what you give? Or rather, do you even give it? must this be said, that you give it? Let the pipe too say that it too gives water; let the channel too say that it itself flows; let the herald too say that he too frees. In the water I attend to the fountain, in the voice of the herald I recognize the judge. You will plainly not be the author of my salvation: he will be of whom I am secure; of you I am uncertain. If you are not arrogant, not I alone am uncertain of you, but you too are uncertain of yourself. From him, then, is salvation for me, *who is over all;* because *salvation is the Lord's.* You are among the sons of men, you are among the princes: but I hear the voice of the Psalm: *Do not trust in princes, and in the sons of men, in whom there is no salvation.*

**10.** [v. 4.] According to the crowd of men, what are these sons of men? Do you wish to know what they are? *His spirit will go out, and he will return to his earth*[^17]. Behold, all that he speaks, not knowing how long he will speak; he threatens, not knowing how long he will live. Suddenly his spirit will go out, and he will return to his earth. Will his spirit go out when he wishes? It will go out, both when he does not wish it will go out, and when he does not know it he will return to his earth. The spirit going out, the flesh will return to the earth. But because it was flesh that so spoke (for, Presume on me, and I will give it to you, would be said only by those of whom it was said: *That they are flesh*[^18]): *His spirit will go out, and he will return to his earth: in that day all his thoughts will perish.* Where is the haughtiness? where is the pride? where is the boasting? But perhaps he will have passed over to a good place, to the just: if indeed he has passed over. For he who so spoke, I know not where he has passed over. For pride was speaking; and I know not where such men pass over, except that I look upon another psalm, and see that their passing is evil. *I saw the impious exalted above the cedars of Lebanon, and I passed by, and behold he was not; and I sought him, and his place was not found*[^19]. This devout man who passed by and did not find the impious, came there where the impious is not. So then, brothers, let us all hear; brothers beloved of God, let us all hear: in whatever tribulation, in whatever desire of a divine gift, let us not trust in princes, nor in the sons of men in whom there is no salvation. All this is mortal, transitory, and perishable. *His spirit will go out, and he will return to his earth: in that day all his thoughts will perish.*

**11.** [v. 5.] What, then, do we do, if we must not hope in the sons of men, if we must not hope in princes? what do we do? *Blessed is he whose helper is the God of Jacob*[^20]. Not this man or that man, not this angel or that angel; but: *Blessed is he whose helper is the God of Jacob*: because to Jacob himself too he was such a helper, that from Jacob he made Israel[^21]. A great help, Israel now seeing God. So this man, set here and a pilgrim, not yet seeing God, if you have the God of Jacob as a helper, from Jacob you will be Israel, and you will be seeing God; and all labor and all groaning will perish, the biting cares will pass, happy praises will succeed. *Blessed is he whose helper is the God of Jacob*: of this Jacob. Why is he blessed, meanwhile still groaning in this life? *His hope is in the Lord his God.* Therefore blessed, because *his hope is in the Lord his God.* In whom his hope is, he himself will be his reality. Brothers, did I perhaps err, because I said our reality will be God? What if I said our inheritance will be him? *You are my hope, my portion in the land of the living*[^22]. You will be my portion. You will be a possession, and you will possess; you will be a possession of God, and your possession will be God: you will be his possession, that you may be cultivated by him; and he himself will be your possession, that you may cultivate him. For both you cultivate God, and you are cultivated by God. It is rightly said: I cultivate God: but how are you cultivated by God? We find in the apostle: *You are God's tillage, God's building*[^23]. And the Lord: *I am*, he says, *the vine, you are the branches, and my Father is the husbandman*[^24]. So God cultivates you, that you may be fruitful; and you cultivate God, that you may be fruitful. It is good for you that God cultivates you; it is good for you that you cultivate God. If the cultivator God withdraws from a man, the man becomes deserted; if the cultivator man withdraws from God, the man himself becomes deserted. Neither does God grow when you approach, nor diminish when you depart. So he himself will be our possession, that he may feed us; and we will be his possession, that he may rule us.

**12.** [v. 6.] *His hope is in the Lord his God.* Who is this Lord his God? Attend, brothers. For many have many gods, and call them their lords and their gods. But the apostle says: *Even if there are those that are called gods, whether in heaven, or on earth; as there are many gods, and many lords: yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things*[^25]. So let him himself be your hope, the Lord your God; in him be your hope. In his own lord and god is the hope even of him who worships Saturn; in his own lord and god is the hope of him who worships Mars, who worships Neptune, who worships Mercury: I add more, of him who worships the belly, of whom it was said: *Whose god is the belly*[^26]. So that one is the god of that man, and that one of that man. Who is the god of this blessed man? Because *his hope is in the Lord his God.* But who is this? *Who made heaven and earth, the sea and all that is in them.* My brothers, we have a great God! let us bless his holy name, because he deigned to make us his possession. You do not yet see God, you cannot fully love what you do not yet see. The things you see, he himself made. You marvel at the world, why not at the craftsman of the world? You look up at the heaven, and you shudder; you think of the whole earth, and you tremble; the greatness of the sea, when do you occupy your thought with it? Look upon the innumerableness of the stars; look upon so many kinds of seeds, so many diversities of animals, whatever swims in the waters, creeps on the earth, flies in the air, circles in the heaven: all these, how great, how excellent, how beautiful, how stupendous! Behold, he who made all these is your God. Set there your hope, that you may be blessed: *His hope is in the Lord his God.* Whom? *Who made heaven and earth, the sea and all that is in them.* We have a great God!

**13.** Attend, brothers, to the great God, good, making such things. What, then, did God think from this, to make (if indeed it should be said of God: He thought) *heaven, earth, sea, and all that is in them*? Perhaps this man was about to say: I see indeed all these great things: heaven and earth, the sea God made: when does God count me among the things he made? and do I truly belong to his care, and does God now think of me, or does he know whether I am alive? What is this you say? Let no evil thought creep upon you into your heart; be of those of whom we were speaking a little before: *I will praise the Lord in my life, I will sing to my God as long as I am.* But this man addresses others, I know not whom, certain lukewarm ones, whom he exhorts, and as it were fears lest they despair of themselves, because they are not in God's reckoning. For such are the thoughts of many. But for this reason they desert God, and go on through any sins, because they do not believe God cares what they do. Hear the divine words, do not despair of yourself. He who cared to make you, does he not care to remake you? Is he not your God who made heaven, and earth, and sea? If he said only these things, perhaps you would answer: God who made heaven, and earth, and sea, is a great God; but does he think of me? He himself made you, it would be said to you. How? am I heaven, or am I earth, or am I sea? And indeed it is plain, I am neither heaven, nor earth, nor sea; but I am in the earth. At least this you grant me, that you are in the earth. Hear that God made not only heaven and earth and sea: *For he made* heaven, and earth, and sea, and all that is in them. If, then, *all that is in them*, you too. I say little, you: the sparrow, the locust, the little worm; none of these did he not make, and there is care to him for all. There is not care according to precept; for precept he gave to man alone. For the psalm says: *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord; according to the multitude of your mercy, O God*[^27]. *According to the multitude*, he says, *of your mercy; according to* this *you will save men and beasts.* And the apostle: *Is God concerned about oxen?* Here: *God is not concerned about oxen;* here: *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord*: are these contrary? For what does the apostle say? *Is it about oxen that it pertains to God?* Where the precept is: *You shall not muzzle the ox that treads out the grain*[^28]; there did God not think about oxen? And so he wished certain oxen to be signified. For God does not care to admonish you what to do with oxen; human nature itself has this. Man was so made, that he knows how to look after his beasts: nor did he receive precepts from God about this, but it was instilled into his mind by God, that he might be able to do it even without a precept; God made him such. But as he rules cattle, he is to be ruled by another; from him by whom he is ruled, he received a precept. As to the tenor of precept, then, *God is not concerned about oxen*: as to the providence of the universe, by which he created all things and rules the world: *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord.*

**14.** Let your charity attend. Here perhaps someone may say to me: It is from the New Testament that *it does not pertain to God about oxen: Men and beasts you will save, O Lord*, is from the Old Testament. There are those who slander, and say that these two Testaments do not accord with each other. Lest perhaps he say one thing in the Old, another in the New, and demand of me a sentence from the New such as this is: *Men and beasts you will save, O Lord;* what do I do? Nothing is so much at the head of the New Testament as the Gospel. In the Gospel I find that all these things pertain to God, there will be no one now to contradict. For will the apostle be contrary to the Gospel? Let us hear the Lord himself, the prince and master of the apostles. *Look*, he says, *at the birds of the heaven, that they neither sow, nor reap, nor gather into barns; and your heavenly Father feeds them*[^29]. So even apart from man, these animals belong to the care of God, that they may be fed, not that they may receive a law. As to giving a law, then, God is not concerned about oxen; but as to creating, feeding, governing and ruling, all things pertain to God. *Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing* (the Lord Jesus Christ says), *and one of them will not fall to the ground without the will of your Father? How much more are you worth than they*[^30]? Do not, then, say: I do not belong to God. Your soul belongs to God, your body belongs to God; because God made both your soul and your body. You say perhaps: God does not count me in the great multitude. There comes in there a wonderful thing from the Gospel: *The hairs of your head are all numbered*[^31].

**15.** [v. 7.] So God is mine, and in him is my hope, *who made heaven and earth, the sea and all that is in them.* But as to what pertains to me, what does he do with me? *Who keeps the truth forever*[^32]. He commended God both to be loved, and to be feared. *Who keeps the truth forever.* What truth forever? what, and in whom does he keep the truth? *Doing judgment for those who receive injury*[^33]. He avenges those who receive injury, my brothers; he does judgment for them. For whom? For those who receive injury, punishing all who do injury. If, then, he will favor those who receive injury and will punish those who do injury, see now of whose number you wish to be. See, attend whether you wish to be among those who receive injury, or among those who do injury. For at once the apostolic voice comes forth to you, and says to you: *It is now altogether a fault, that you have lawsuits among you. Why do you not rather suffer injury*[^34]? He rebukes men, because they do not suffer injury. He does not exhort you to suffer trouble, but to suffer injury: for not every trouble is injury. For whatever you suffer by right is not injury. Lest perhaps you should say: I too am counted among those who suffer injury; for I suffered that in that place, and that for that cause. See whether you have suffered injury. Robbers suffer many things, but not injury: the criminal, sorcerers, burglars, adulterers, corrupters, all suffer many evils, but there is no injury. It is one thing to suffer injury; another to suffer tribulation, or punishment, or trouble, or torment. Consider where you are, see what you did, see why you suffer; and there you see what you suffer. Right and injury are contraries. For right is what is just. For not everything that is called right is right. What if someone enacts an unjust right? Nor is it to be called right, if it is unjust. So that is true right, which is also just. See what you did, not what you suffer. If you have done right, you suffer injury: if you have done injury, you suffer right.

**16.** Why did I say these things, brothers? That the heretics may not exalt themselves, when perhaps they suffer something from the commands of earthly princes; let them not count themselves among those who receive injury, and say: Behold, the Psalm consoles me; for I worship God, *who will do judgment for those who receive injury.* Rightly I ask whether you receive injury. If you have done right, it is injury that you suffer. Is it right to blow upon Christ? is it right with rebellious pride to set up an altar? is it right, when the persecutors spared the tunic of Christ[^35], to tear apart the Church of Christ? But if this is not right, whatever you suffer for it, is right. You are not, then, of those who suffer injury. I read something more plain from the Gospel: *Blessed*, he says, *are those who suffer persecution.* Wait: why do you hasten? why do you say: I am? Wait, I say; I will read the whole. You have heard: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution;* you had now begun to arrogate something to yourself. If you permit, I read the whole; see what follows. *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for justice*[^36]. Now say: I am. If you dare to say: I am; let us recall the things I said above: or, lest it be long, this one thing I ask: If you condemned one man, whose case you did not know, would you dare to say that you held justice? or, if you suffered something for this matter, would you call it injury? You raise yourself up to the rash tribunal of your heart, whence you may be cast down headlong; and you dare to bring a sentence about a man whose case you do not know! If you did this about one man, you would be unjust: you do this about the whole world, and are you just? Dearest brothers, and what is it that suffers injury, except the Catholic Church which endures such great things? Among so many scandals of heretics it groans; it sees, by evil persuasions and frauds, the weak snatched from its bosom, the little ones dragged off through I know not what secrets of evil caves, rebaptized, Christ blown upon in them, slain in them not that mortal thing by which they are men, but that by which they are to live forever. A man is persuaded to say: I am not a Christian, and it is called justice. You are about to go to the bishop, he says to him, see that you do not say you are a Christian: for if you say you are a Christian, you will not be received; but that you may be received, say you are not. What do you admonish, Christian? what do you teach? Surely you suffer persecution: how much truer a persecutor you! When the emperors persecuted the Christians, by threatening they compelled what you effect by persuading. You persuade a Christian to deny that he is a Christian: what you do by persuading, the persecutor did not do by killing. Under you lives a man who denies that he is a Christian. He denies, and lives? He has already lost life; a corpse speaks to you. He who was struck by the sword of the persecutor, fell, and lives: he to whom you speak stands, and has fallen. Doing these things, whatever you suffer, will it be injury? I do not wish you to flatter yourself: if all the things you do are unjust, whatever you suffer will be just. But for whom does he do judgment, *who keeps the truth forever*? *For those who receive injury.*

**17.** [v. 8.] Now you go on, and say with those good, and as it were sharp and subtle, reasonings of yours, that you feed; and say: Can a hungry man feed? that is, can a sinner give what is holy? Can a hungry man feed? can a sick man heal? can a bound man loose? These are as it were great and subtle things, by which they deceive the unskilled. Let this psalm shut their mouth: *Who gives food to the hungry.* Behold, I expect nothing from you; God *gives food to the hungry.* To which hungry? To all. What is, to all? To all animals, to all men he himself gives food; and does he keep no food for his beloved? If they have another hunger, they have also another food. Let us first seek their hunger, and we will find their food: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice, for they will be satisfied*[^37]. We ought to be hungry for God: before the door of his sight let us beg in prayers; he himself *gives food to the hungry.* Why is it that you, heretic, boast that you loose, you raise up, you enlighten? As though you have now been freed, and you stand, and you are light? Far from it. Attend to what is above: *Do not trust in princes, and in the sons of men, in whom there is no salvation.* They do not themselves give salvation. So let the heretics withdraw from the midst. *The Lord looses the fettered; the Lord raises up the cast-down; the Lord makes the blind wise;* that is, those who are blind he makes wise. Excellently by this sentence he has expounded to us all the earlier ones: lest perhaps, when he had said: *The Lord looses the fettered*, we should refer it to those fettered who are perhaps, on account of some offense, bound with iron by their masters; because he said: *He raises up the cast-down*, there might occur to us someone, either stumbling and falling, or slipped from a horse. There is another fall, there are other fetters; as there is another darkness and another light. In that he said: *He makes the blind wise;* he was unwilling to say: He enlightens the blind, lest you understand this too carnally; as he was enlightened by the Lord, whose eyes he anointed with clay made of spittle, and made him whole[^38]: lest you should hope for any such thing, when he speaks of spiritual things, he showed a certain light of wisdom, by which the blind are enlightened. So as the blind are enlightened by the light of wisdom, so the fettered are loosed, so the cast-down are raised up. Whence, then, are we fettered? whence cast-down? Our body was an ornament to us: we sinned, and from it we received fetters. What are our fetters? Mortality itself. Hear the apostle Paul, because he too was still fettered in this pilgrimage. How many places he traversed fettered! Those fetters were not heavy for him; with those very fetters he preached the Gospel to the whole world: the spirit of love seized the fetters, and went about as much as it could. But yet what does he say? *Having a desire to be dissolved, and to be with Christ*[^39]. What is, *to be dissolved*? From the fetters of mortality. And yet his mercy still wished to be in fetters, on account of others fettered, to whom he might minister: *To remain*, he says, *in the flesh, is necessary for your sake*[^40]. *The Lord*, then, *looses the fettered*, that is, from mortal makes them immortal. *The Lord raises up the cast-down.* Why were they cast down? Because they were raised up. Why are they raised up? Because they were humbled. Adam fell and was cast down: he fell, Christ came down. Why did he come down who did not fall, except that he might be lifted up who fell? *The Lord makes the blind wise; the Lord loves the just.* Therefore he does judgment for those who receive injury.

**18.** [v. 9.] And who are these just? now in what sense just? As you have: *The Lord guards the proselytes.* Proselytes are newcomers. The whole Church of the Gentiles is a proselyte. For it is a newcomer to the fathers, not born of their flesh, but a daughter by imitating. Yet the Lord guards her, not some man. *The orphan and the widow he will take up.* Let no one think it is as it were the orphan on account of an inheritance, or the widow on account of I know not what business of hers. Indeed God helps these too, and in all the offices of the human race he does a good work, who provides for the orphan, who does not desert the widow: but in a certain way we are all orphans, the Father being absent, not dead. For an orphan, according to men, comes to be when his father is dead. And if you seek the truth, brothers, because the soul does not die, our parents live; and rather the fathers being absent are they orphans, who are orphans: if they were evil, they live in punishments; if they were good, they live in rest: all things are whole to the Creator. Yet we, as long as we are in this body, and dwell in the place of pilgrimage, our Father is absent, to whom we cry: *Our Father, who are in heaven*[^41]. So the Church is a widow, as it were with the bridegroom absent, the husband absent. He will come who now protects her, not seen, but desired. For with great desire we are carried away, and we desire by love him whom we do not see. We will cling to his embraces, seen, who are now detained by faith, unseen. So what did he wish to be understood by *both the orphan and the widow*, brothers? Those destitute of all aid and help. The soul destitute in the world, let it hope for the help of God himself. Whatever you have here, you have gold; have you presumed on it? Now you are not a proselyte, you are not an orphan, you are not counted among the widows. You have a friend: if you presume on him, and abandon God, you are not destitute. You have all these things; do you not presume on them, do you not grow proud on them? You are an orphan of God, and a widow of God. So he takes up the destitute; this he said: he takes up the orphan, he takes up the widow too.

**19.** *And the way of sinners he will destroy*[^42]. What is the way of sinners? To mock these things we say. Who is an orphan? who a widow? What kingdom of heaven? and what punishment of hell? These are fables of the Christians. As far as I see, so far will I live: *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*[^43]. See lest such men persuade you of something: lest they enter through the ear into the heart, let them find thorns in your ears; let him who has begun so to enter, pricked, withdraw. *For evil conversations corrupt good morals*[^44]. But perhaps here you are about to say: Why, then, are they happy? Behold, they neither worship God, and daily commit all evils; they abound in these things in which I, needy, labor. Do not be jealous over sinners. What they receive, you see; what is kept for them, you do not see? And whence, he says, do I see what is not seen? Faith altogether has eyes; and greater eyes, and more powerful and stronger. These eyes have deceived no one; let these eyes be ever toward the Lord, that he himself may pluck your feet from these snares[^45]. The way of sinners pleases you, because it is broad, and many walk in it; you see its breadth, you do not see its end. Behold, where it ends, there is a precipice; where it ends, there is a certain depth of an abyss: those rejoicing, wandering at large in this way, at that end are plunged. But you cannot extend your eyes, to see that very end: believe him who sees. And who is the man who sees? Perhaps no man; but your Lord came to you, that you might believe God. For will you not believe the Lord your God, who says: *Broad and spacious is the way which leads to destruction, and many there are who go in by it*[^46]? This way the Lord will destroy; because it is the way of sinners.

**20.** [v. 10.] And when the way of sinners has been destroyed, what remains for us? *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which was prepared for you from the foundation of the world*[^47]. To this the Psalm concludes: *And the way of sinners he will destroy.* And what of you? *The Lord will reign forever*[^48]. Rejoice, because he will reign for you; rejoice, because you will be his kingdom. For see what follows. You are surely a citizen of Zion, not of Babylon; that is, not of the perishing city of this world, but of Zion laboring and on pilgrimage for a time, but to reign forever. You have heard, then, the end; thence you are. *The Lord will reign forever, your God, O Zion.* O Zion, your God will reign forever: will your God reign without you? *From generation to generation.* He said it twice, because he could not say it always. Nor think that, the words being finished, eternity is finished. Eternity in the word consists of four syllables, in itself it is without end. It could not be commended to you except thus: *Your God will reign from generation to generation.* He said little: if he said it all day, it would be too narrow; if he said it all his life, would he not at some time fall silent? Love eternity; you will reign with no end, if Christ is your end, with whom you will reign forever and ever. Amen.
