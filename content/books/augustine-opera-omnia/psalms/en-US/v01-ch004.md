# Exposition of Psalm 4

**1.** [v 1.] *Christ is the end of the Law unto righteousness for everyone who believes*[^1]: for here "end" signifies perfection, not destruction. Now whether every canticle is a psalm, or rather every psalm a canticle; or whether there are certain canticles that cannot be called psalms, and certain psalms that cannot be called canticles, may be asked. But the Scriptures must be observed, lest perhaps a canticle indicate gladness: while those are called psalms which are sung to the psaltery; and that the prophet David used this in a great mystery, the history makes plain. But this is not the place to discuss the matter; for it requires a lengthy inquiry and a long disputation. For now, meanwhile, we must expect either the words of the Lord as man after the resurrection, or those of a man believing and hoping in him within the Church.

**2.** [v 2.] *When I called, the God of my righteousness heard me*: when I called, he says, God heard me, from whom is my righteousness. *In tribulation you have enlarged me*: from the straits of sorrow you have led me into the breadth of joys; for tribulation and anguish are upon every soul of man who works evil. But he who says: *We rejoice in tribulations, knowing that tribulation works patience*, down to where he says: *Because the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^2], does not have straits of heart, although they are brought upon him from without by persecutors. Now the change of person, that from the third, where he says *he heard*, he passed at once to the second, where he says *you have enlarged me*, if it was not done for the sake of variety and pleasantness, it is strange why he first wished, as it were, to indicate to men that he had been heard, and afterward to address his hearer. Unless perhaps, when he had indicated how he was heard in the very enlarging of his heart, he preferred to speak with God; so that in this way too he might show what it is to be enlarged in heart, that is, now to have God poured into the heart, with whom he converses inwardly. This is rightly taken of the person of one who, believing in Christ, has been enlightened: but of the Lord as man himself, whom the Wisdom of God took up, I do not see how this can fit; for he was never deserted by her. But just as that prayer of his is rather an indication of our weakness, so also of this sudden enlarging of heart the same Lord can speak on behalf of his faithful, whose person he took upon himself even when he says: *I was hungry, and you did not feed me; I was thirsty, and you gave me no drink*[^3], and the rest. Therefore here too he can say, *you have enlarged me*, on behalf of one of his least ones conversing with God, whose love he has poured out in his heart through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us. *Have mercy on me, and hear my prayer*: why does he ask again, when he has already indicated that he was heard and enlarged? Is it on account of us, of whom it is said: *But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^4]; or that in him who has believed there may be perfected what has been begun?

**3.** [v 3.] *Sons of men, how long will you be heavy of heart?* At least until the coming, he says, of the Son of God will your error last; why, then, are you any longer heavy of heart? When will you have an end of deceptions, if you do not have it when the truth is present? *Why do you love vanity, and seek a lie?* Why do you wish to be blessed by the lowest things? Truth alone makes blessed, from which all things are true. For it is the vanity of those who deal in vanity, and all is vanity. What abundance does a man have in all his labor by which he labors under the sun? Why, then, are you held by the love of temporal things? Why do you pursue as the highest the things that are the lowest, which is vanity and a lie? For you long that those things should remain with you, all of which pass away like a shadow.

**4.** [v 4.] *And know that the Lord has made his holy one wonderful*: whom, but him whom he raised from the dead, and placed in heaven at his right hand? The human race, then, is rebuked, that it may at last turn to him from the love of this world. But if anyone is troubled by the conjunction added, in that he says, *And know*, it is easy for him to observe in the Scriptures that this kind of expression is familiar to that language in which the Prophets spoke. For you often find a beginning thus: And the Lord said to him: And the word of the Lord came to him. This linking of the conjunction, when no sentence has preceded to which the following may be attached, perhaps wonderfully suggests that the bringing forth of truth in the voice is joined with that vision which takes place in the heart. Although here it might be said that the preceding sentence, *Why do you love vanity, and seek a lie?*, is so put as if it were said: Do not love vanity, and seek a lie; and this being so put, by a most correct expression there follows: *And know that the Lord has made his holy one wonderful.* But the interposed *diapsalma* forbids this from being joined with the preceding: for whether it is a Hebrew word, as some hold, by which "let it be done" is signified; or a Greek one, by which an interval of psalm-singing is signified, so that the psalma is what is sung, but the diapsalma is a silence interposed in the singing; just as sympsalma is called the joining of voices in singing, so diapsalma is the disjoining of them, where a kind of rest of broken continuity is shown: whether, then, it is that, or this, or something else, this at least is probable, that the sense is not rightly continued and joined where a diapsalma is interposed.

**5.** *The Lord will hear me when I cry out to him*: here I believe we are admonished that with great intention of heart, that is, with an inner and incorporeal cry, we should implore the help of God; since, just as we should give thanks for the enlightenment in this life, so we should pray for rest after this life. Therefore, whether from the person of a faithful one preaching the gospel, or from that of the Lord himself, it is to be taken as if it were said: The Lord will hear you, when you cry out to him.

**6.** [v 5.] *Be angry, and do not sin*[^5]: for it occurred to him: Who is worthy to be heard, or how does a sinner not cry out in vain to the Lord? Therefore *Be angry*, he says, *and do not sin*; which can be understood in two ways: either: Even if you are angry, do not sin; that is: Even if a movement of the soul arises, which now, because of the punishment of sin, is not in our power, at least let reason and mind not consent to it, the mind that has been inwardly regenerated according to God, so that with the mind we may serve the law of God, even if with the flesh we still serve the law of sin: or: Do penance; that is, be angry with yourselves over past sins, and cease to sin any further. *The things you say in your hearts*: "say" is understood; so that the full sentence is: The things you say, say in your hearts; that is, do not be the people of whom it was said: *They honor me with their lips, but their heart is far from me*[^6]. *On your beds be pierced with remorse*: this is what was already said, *in your hearts*; for these are the beds of which the Lord too admonishes, that we should pray within with closed doors. *Be pierced with remorse*, however, is referred either to the grief of penance, so that the soul, punishing itself, may be pierced, lest, condemned in God's judgment, it be tormented; or to an arousing, so that we may awaken to behold the light of Christ, as though goads had been applied. Some, however, say that it is better read not *be pierced with remorse*, but *be opened*; since in the Greek Psalter there is *katantēsēte*, which is referred to that enlarging of the heart, so that the outpouring of love through the Holy Spirit may be received.

**7.** [vv 6.7.] *Sacrifice the sacrifice of righteousness, and hope in the Lord.* He says the same in another psalm: *A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit*[^7]. Therefore it is not absurdly taken here that the sacrifice of righteousness is the very thing that is done through penance: for what is more righteous than that each one should be angry at his own sins rather than at another's, and, punishing himself, offer himself to God? Or is the sacrifice of righteousness the righteous works done after penance? For the interposed *diapsalma* too perhaps not absurdly suggests also the passage from the old life to the new life: so that, the old man being extinguished or weakened through penance, the sacrifice of righteousness may be offered to God according to the regeneration of the new man, when the soul itself, now washed, offers itself, and lays itself upon the altar of faith, to be taken hold of by the divine fire, that is, by the Holy Spirit; so that this is the sense: *Sacrifice the sacrifice of righteousness, and hope in the Lord*, that is: Live rightly, and hope for the gift of the Holy Spirit, that the truth, in which you have believed, may enlighten you.

**8.** Yet *Hope in the Lord* is still said obscurely. And what things are hoped for, but good things? But because each one wishes to obtain from God the good that he loves, and those are not easily found who love the inner goods, that is, those pertaining to the inner man, which alone are to be loved, while the rest are to be used for necessity, not enjoyed for joy; when he had said, *Hope in the Lord*, he wonderfully added: *Many say: Who shows us good things?* This saying, and this question, is the daily one of all the foolish and the wicked; whether of those who desire the peace and tranquillity of the worldly life, and, because of the perversity of the human race, do not find it, who even, being blind, dare to accuse the order of things, when, entangled in their own deserts, they think the times are worse than the past were; or of those doubting or despairing of the very future life that is promised to us, who often say: Who knows whether these things are true, or who has come from the dead to announce them? Magnificently, then, and briefly, but to those who see within, he shows what good things are to be sought, answering the question of those who say: *Who shows us good things? The light of your countenance is sealed upon us, Lord*, he says. This light is the whole and true good of man, which is beheld not with the eyes, but with the mind. He said *sealed upon us*, as a coin is sealed with the king's image: for man was made to the image and likeness of God, which by sinning he corrupted: his good, therefore, is true and eternal, if by being reborn he is sealed. And to this I believe pertains what some prudently understand, that saying which the Lord, on seeing Caesar's coin, said: *Render to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's*[^8]; as if he said: Just as Caesar exacts from you the impress of his image, so also does God; that just as the coin is rendered to him, so to God is rendered the soul illumined and sealed with the light of his countenance. *You have put gladness in my heart.* Gladness, then, is not to be sought without, by those who, still heavy of heart, love vanity and seek a lie, but within, where the light of God's countenance is sealed: for in the inner man Christ dwells, as the Apostle says; for to him it pertains to see the truth, since he said: *I am the truth*[^9]. And when he spoke in the Apostle who said: *Do you wish to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^10], it was certainly not without that he spoke to him; but in the very heart, that is, in that bed where prayer is to be made.

**9.** [vv 8.9.] But men pursuing temporal things, who are certainly many, have known to say nothing else but *Who shows us good things?* since they cannot see the true and certain goods within themselves. And so, consequently, of these it is most correctly said, in what he adds: *From the time of their grain, wine, and oil they have been multiplied.* For it is not idle that *their* is added: for there is also a grain of God, since there is the living bread that came down from heaven; there is also a wine of God, for *They will be made drunk*, he says, *with the abundance of your house*[^11]; there is also an oil of God, of which it is said: *You have anointed my head with oil*[^12]. But those many who say: *Who shows us good things?* and do not see that the kingdom of heaven is within them, *from the time of their grain, wine, and oil have been multiplied.* For multiplication does not always signify abundance, and does it not for the most part signify smallness: since the soul, given over to temporal pleasures, always burns with desire and cannot be satisfied, and, distended with manifold and toilsome thought, is not permitted to see the simple good; such as is that soul of which it is said: *For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that thinks on many things*[^13]. Such a soul, by the passing and succession of temporal goods, that is, from the time of its grain, wine, and oil, filled with innumerable phantasms, is so multiplied that it cannot do what is commanded: *Think of the Lord in goodness, and seek him in simplicity of heart*[^14]; for this multiplicity is vehemently opposed to that simplicity. And therefore, leaving these who are many, multiplied, namely, by desire of temporal things, and who say, *Who shows us good things?*, which are to be sought not with the outer eyes, but within with simplicity of heart, the faithful man exults and says: *In peace, in the selfsame, I will fall asleep and take my rest.* For rightly is there hoped for from such men a complete estrangement of mind from mortal things and a forgetting of the miseries of this world, which is fittingly and prophetically signified by the name of falling asleep and of sleep, where the highest peace can be interrupted by no tumult. But this is not yet held in this life, but is to be hoped for after this life. This too the very words show, which are of future time: for it was not said, either: I fell asleep and took my rest; or: I fall asleep and take my rest; but: *I will fall asleep and take my rest.* Then this corruptible will put on incorruption, and this mortal will put on immortality; then death will be swallowed up in victory. This is why it is said: *But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^15].

**10.** [v 10.] For which reason he fittingly links the last verse, and says: *Because you, Lord, have made me dwell singularly in hope.* Here he did not say: You will make; but: *You have made.* In whom, then, this hope already is, there will assuredly also be what is hoped for. And he well says, *singularly*: for it can be referred against those many who, multiplied from the time of their grain, wine, and oil, say: *Who shows us good things?* for this multiplicity perishes, and singularity is held in the saints, of whom it is said in the Acts of the Apostles: *But of the multitude of believers the soul was one and the heart one*[^16]. Singular, then, and simple, that is, set apart from the multitude and throng of things being born and dying, lovers of eternity and unity we ought to be; if we long to cleave to the one God and our Lord.
