# Exposition of Psalm 42

**1.** This psalm is short: it so satisfies the minds of the hearers, that it is not troublesome to the stomachs of the fasting. Let our soul be fed from it, which he who sings in this psalm says is sad: sad, I believe, from some fasting of his, or rather from some hunger of his. For fasting is of the will, hunger of necessity. The Church hungers, the body of Christ hungers, and that man spread everywhere, whose head is above, the members below: his voice in all the Psalms, whether singing or groaning, or rejoicing in hope, or sighing in the reality, we ought to have most known and most familiar, as our own. We must not, then, dwell long, that we may insinuate to you who speaks: let each one be in the body of Christ, and he will speak here.

**2.** [v. 1] You all know, however, who advance, and who groan for that heavenly city, who know their pilgrimage, who hold to the way, who in the desire of that most stable land have fixed hope like an anchor: you know, then, that this kind of men, this good seed, this wheat of Christ, groans among the tares; and this until the time of harvest comes, that is, even to the end of the world, as the truth which does not deceive expounds. Groaning, then, among the tares, that is, among evil men, among the deceitful and seducers, whether turbulent with anger, or venomous with treacheries; observing that he is together with them as in one field throughout the whole world, receiving one rain, blown upon alike, and alike nourished with them amid adversities, having alike these common gifts of God, granted in common to the evil and the good by him who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust: seeing, then, the seed of Abraham, the holy seed, how many things it now has in common with the evil, from whom one day it is to be separated, born alike, allotted the same condition of the human race, alike bearing mortal bodies, using together the light, the fountains, the fruits, the prosperities and adversities of the world, whether famine, or abundance, whether peace, or war, whether health, or pestilence; seeing how many things it has in common with the evil, with whom yet it has not a common cause, he breaks forth into this voice: *Judge me, O God, and discern my cause from the unholy nation*[^1]. *Judge,* he says, *me, O God*: I do not fear your judgment, because I know your mercy. *Judge me, O God, and discern my cause from the unholy nation.* Now meanwhile in this pilgrimage you do not yet discern my place, because I live together with the tares even to the time of harvest; you do not yet discern my rain, you do not yet discern my light: discern my cause. Let there be a difference between him who believes in you, and him who does not believe in you. The weakness is equal, but the conscience unequal: the labor equal, but the desire unequal. The desire of the ungodly shall perish: but about the desire of the just, unless he who promised were certain, we ought to doubt. The end of our desire is the promiser himself. He will give himself, because he gave himself: he will give himself, immortal, to the immortal, because he gave himself, mortal, to the mortal. *Judge me, O God, and discern my cause from the unholy nation. From the iniquitous and deceitful man deliver me*: this is, *from the unholy nation. From the man,* from a certain kind of man, because a certain man and a certain man, and of these two one shall be taken, and one shall be left.

**3.** [v. 2] And since patience is needed for bearing, even to the harvest, a certain, if it may be said, undiscerned discernment; for they are together, and therefore are not yet discerned; but the tares are tares, and the wheat is wheat, and therefore they are already discerned; since, then, fortitude is needed, to be implored from him who commanded that we be strong, and unless he himself makes us strong, we shall not be what he commanded, from him who said, *He who perseveres to the end, he shall be saved*[^2]; lest the soul itself, by arrogating fortitude to itself, be weakened, he at once adds: *Because you are, O God, my strength; why have you cast me off, and why do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me?*[^3] He seeks the cause of his sadness. *Why,* he says, *do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me?* I walk sad, the enemy afflicts me with daily temptations, putting in either what we may evilly love, or what we may evilly fear: and the soul fighting against both, even if not captured, yet imperiled, is drawn together by sadness, and says to God: *Why?* Let it, then, ask of him, and hear why. For it seeks in the psalm the cause of its sadness, saying: *Why have you cast me off, and why do I go sad?* Let it hear from Isaiah, let the reading which was just now recited succor it: *A spirit,* he says, *shall go forth from me, and every breath I have made: on account of a little sin I saddened him, and turned my face away from him; and he was saddened, and went sad in his ways*[^4]. Why, then, did you ask: *Why have you cast me off, and why do I go sad?* You have heard: *on account of sin.* The cause of your sadness is sin, let the cause of your gladness be justice. You wished to sin, and were unwilling to labor; so that it was too little for you that you should be unjust, unless you wished him too to be unjust, by whom you were unwilling to be punished in yourself. Look back at a better voice in another psalm: *It is good for me that you have humbled me, that I may learn your justifications*[^5]. Lifted up, I had learned my iniquities, humbled let me learn your justifications. *Why do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me?* You complain of the enemy; truly he afflicts, but you gave him place. And now you have something to do: choose counsel, admit the king, shut out the tyrant.

**4.** [v. 3] But that he may do this, what he says, what he supplicates, what he prays, attend. Pray what you hear, pray when you hear; let this be the voice of us all: *Send forth your light and your truth; they have led me forth and led me through to your holy mountain, and to your tabernacles*[^6]. Because that very light of yours and truth of yours: these two names, one thing. For what else is the light of God, but the truth of God? or what is the truth of God, but the light of God? And both of these one Christ. *I am the light of the world: he who believes in me will not walk in darkness*[^7]. *I am the way, the truth, and the life*[^8]. He himself the light, he himself the truth. Let him come, then, and deliver us, discerning now our cause from the unholy nation; let him deliver us from the iniquitous and deceitful man: let him separate the wheat from the tares: because he himself will send his angels in the time of harvest, to gather out of his kingdom all scandals, and to cast them into the burning fire, but to gather his wheat into the granary[^9]. Let him send forth his light and his truth; because these have now led us forth and led us through to his holy mountain and to his tabernacles. We have a pledge, we hope for the reward. His holy mountain, his holy Church. That is the mountain which grew from the smallest stone, according to the vision of Daniel, shattering the kingdoms of the lands; and growing so much that it filled the whole face of the earth[^10]. On this mountain he says he was heard, who says: *With my voice I cried to the Lord, and he heard me from his holy mountain*[^11]. Whoever was outside this mountain, let him not hope to be heard unto eternal life. For many are heard unto many things. Let them not applaud themselves that they are heard: the demons were heard, that they might be sent into the swine[^12]. Let us covet to be heard unto eternal life, on account of the desire by which we say: *Send forth your light and your truth.* That light requires the eye of the heart: *For blessed,* he says, *are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^13]. Now we are on his mountain, that is, in his Church, and in his tabernacle. The tabernacle is of those on pilgrimage, the house of those dwelling together: there is also the tabernacle of those on pilgrimage and warring. When you hear tabernacle, understand war, beware the enemy. But what will the house be? *Blessed are they who dwell in your house, forever and ever they shall praise you*[^14].

**5.** [v. 4] Now, then, led to the tabernacle, and set on his holy mountain, what hope do we bear? *And I will go in to the altar of God*[^15]. For there is a certain sublime invisible altar, to which the unjust does not draw near. To that altar he alone draws near, who draws near secure to this one: there he will find his life, who in this one discerns his cause. *And I will go in to the altar of God*: from his holy mountain, and from his tabernacle, from his holy Church, I will go in to the sublime altar of God. What sacrifice is there? He himself who enters is taken up as a holocaust. *I will go in to the altar of God.* What is it that he says, *to the altar of God*? *To God who gladdens my youth.* Youth signifies newness: as though he said: To God who gladdens my newness. He gladdens my newness, who saddened my oldness. For I go now sad in oldness, but then I shall stand rejoicing in newness. *I will confess to you on the harp, O God, my God.* What is it to confess on the harp, and to confess on the psaltery? For not always on the harp, nor always on the psaltery. These two instruments of musicians have between them a distinct and discerned principle, worthy of consideration and of commendation to memory. Both these are carried and touched by the hands, and signify certain bodily works of ours. Both are good, if one knows how to make music on the psaltery, if one knows how to play the harp. But because that instrument is called a psaltery, which has the sounding-box on the upper part; that is, the drum and hollow wood on which the strings, resting, resound: but the harp has that same hollow and sonorous wood on the lower part: our works are to be discerned, when they are on the psaltery, when on the harp, yet both pleasing to God and sweet to his hearing. When, then, from the precepts of God we do something, obeying and hearkening to his commands to fulfill his precepts; where we act and do not suffer, it is the psaltery. For so the angels too act: for they suffer nothing. But when we suffer something of tribulations, temptations, scandals on this earth; because we suffer only from the lower part, that is, from the fact that we are mortal, from the fact that we owe something of tribulations to our first cause, and because we suffer many things from those who are not from above, it is the harp. For the sweet sound comes from the lower part: we suffer and make music, or rather we sing and play the harp. When the Apostle said that he evangelized and preached the Gospel throughout the whole world by the precept of God; because he said he had received that Gospel not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ, the strings sounded from above: but when he said, *We glory in tribulations; knowing that tribulation works patience, patience trial, trial hope*[^16]; the harp sounded from the lower part indeed, but yet sweetly. For all patience is sweet to God. But if in those very tribulations you fail, you have broken the harp. Why, then, did he just now say, *I will confess to you on the harp*? On account of what he had said: *Why do I go sad, while the enemy afflicts me?* For he was suffering something from the lower affliction, and yet in that very thing he wished to please God, and was eager to give thanks to God, strong in tribulations: and because he could not be without tribulation, he owed patience to God: *I will confess to you on the harp, O God, my God.*

**6.** [v. 5] And again to his soul, that from that lower sounding wood it may take a sound: *Why are you sad,* he says, *my soul, and why do you trouble me?*[^17] I am in tribulations, in languors, in sorrows, why do you trouble me, O soul? Who says it? to whom does he say it? To the soul he says it, we all know; for it is plain, to it the speech is directed. *Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?* The person speaking is sought. Does the flesh speak to the soul, when the flesh does not speak without the soul? For it is more proper that the soul address the flesh, than that the flesh address the soul. But because he did not say: Why are you sad, my flesh, but said: *Why are you sad, my soul?* (For if he addressed the flesh, perhaps he would not say: *Why are you sad;* but: Why do you grieve? For the grief of the soul is called sadness: but the trouble that happens in the body can be called grief, it cannot be called sadness. But from the grief of the body the soul is for the most part saddened. Yet it matters what grieves, and what is saddened. For the flesh grieves, the soul is sad; and this voice is open: *Why are you sad, my soul?*) The soul, then, does not address the flesh; because he did not say: Why are you sad, my flesh? nor the flesh the soul; because it is absurd that the lower should address the higher. Therefore we understand that we have something where the image of God is, namely the mind and reason. That very mind was calling upon the light of God and the truth of God. It is that by which we grasp the just and unjust; it is that by which we discern the true from the false; it is that which is called the understanding, which understanding the beasts lack; which understanding whoever neglects in himself, and sets after other things, and so casts away as though he did not have it, hears from the psalm: *Do not be like the horse and mule, in which there is no understanding*[^18]. Our understanding, then, addresses our soul. It is withered in tribulations, wearied in anguish, drawn together in temptations, sick in labors. The mind raises it, taking truth from above, and says: *Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?*

**7.** See whether this address is not in that conflict of the Apostle, prefiguring in himself certain ones, and perhaps us, and saying: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man, but I see another law in my members,* that is, certain carnal motions: and in a certain struggle and as it were despair, he calls upon the grace of God: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^19]. Such ones thus fighting the Lord himself too deigned to prefigure in himself, when he said: *My soul is sad even unto death*[^20]. For he knew that to which he had come. He dreaded the passion who had said: *I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again; no one takes it from me, but I myself lay it down of myself, and take it up again*[^21]? But he who said, *My soul is sad even unto death,* figured certain of his members in himself. For very often the mind now believes well and knows well that a man will be, according to his faith, in the bosom of Abraham: it believes this, and yet, when some crisis of death comes, it is troubled by a certain familiarity with this world; it raises its hearing to that inner voice of God, it hears the reasonable song within. For so from above in silence something sounds, not to the ears, but to the minds; so that whoever hears that melody is affected with weariness toward bodily noise, and this whole human life is to him a certain tumult, hindering the hearing of a certain supernal sound too delightful, and incomparable, and ineffable. And truly when it so happens from some perturbation, a man suffers violence, addressing his soul: *Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?* Or is it perhaps because a purified life is hard to find, when he judges who knows how to judge purely and clearly? Because, even if a life is now approvable among men, so that men do not justly have anything now to reprove: the examination proceeds from his eyes, the rule proceeds making things even without deceit, and finds in a man certain things which God reproves, which men did not see to be reproved, nor he himself within who is to be judged. Fearing these things the soul is perhaps troubled: the mind addresses it, as if saying: Why do you fear about sins, because you cannot avoid them all? *Hope in the Lord, because I will confess to him.* Certain things the present address heals, the rest a faithful confession purges. Plainly fear, if you call yourself just; if you do not have that voice from another psalm: *Enter not into judgment with your servant.* Why: *Enter not into judgment with your servant*? Your mercy I need. For if you exhibit judgment without mercy, where shall I go? *If you mark iniquities, O Lord, Lord, who shall stand?* *Enter not into judgment with your servant, because in your sight shall no living man be justified*[^22]. Therefore if no living man shall be justified in your sight, because whoever lives here, however justly he lives, woe to him if God enter into judgment with him. For from another prophet he thus rebukes the arrogant and proud: *Why do you wish to contend in judgment with me? All of you have forsaken me, says the Lord*[^23]. Do not, then, contend in judgment: give effort to be just; and however much you are, confess yourself a sinner; always hope for mercy: and in this humble confession secure address your soul troubling you, and tumultuous against you. *Why are you sad, my soul, and why do you trouble me?* Perhaps you wished to hope in yourself: *Hope in the Lord,* do not in yourself. For what are you in yourself? what are you of yourself? Let him be health in you, who took up wounds for you. *Hope,* he says, *in the Lord, because I will confess to him.* What will you confess to him? *The salvation of my countenance, my God.* You are the salvation of my countenance, you will heal me. Sick, I speak to you: I recognize the physician, I do not boast myself sound. What is it, I recognize the physician, I do not boast myself sound? What is said in another psalm: *I said, Lord, have mercy on me, heal my soul, for I have sinned against you*[^24].

**8.** This voice, brothers, is safe: but be vigilant in good works. Touch the psaltery, by obeying the precepts: touch the harp, by enduring sufferings. *Break your bread to the hungry*[^25], you heard from Isaiah: do not think fasting suffices. Fasting chastises you, it does not refresh another. Your straits will be fruitful, if you furnish breadth to another. Behold, you have defrauded your soul: to whom will you give what you took from yourself? where will you put what you denied yourself? How many poor can the meal we have omitted today fatten! So fast, that while another eats you may rejoice to have dined, on account of your prayers, that you may be heard. For there he says: *While you are still speaking I will say: Behold, I am here; if you break your bread from the heart to the hungry*[^26]: because for the most part it is done by the sad and the murmuring, that they may be rid of the weariness of the one asking, not that they may refresh the bowels of the needy: *But God loves a cheerful giver*[^27]. If you give bread sad, you have lost both the bread and the merit. Therefore do it from the heart: that he who sees within, while you are still speaking, may say: *Behold, I am here.* How quickly the prayers of those who do good are received: and this is the justice of man in this life, fasting, almsgiving, prayer. Do you wish your prayer to fly to God? Make it two wings, fasting and almsgiving. Let the light of God and the truth of God find us such, that they may find us secure, when they come to free us from death, who has already come to undergo death for us. Amen.
