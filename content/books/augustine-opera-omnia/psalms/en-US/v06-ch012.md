# Exposition of Psalm 133

**1.** [v. 1.] The psalm is brief; but very well known and famous. *Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one*[^1]. So sweet is this sound, that even those who do not know the Psalter sing this very verse. It is as sweet as the love is sweet which makes brothers dwell as one. And this indeed, brothers, does not need interpretation or exposition—how good and how pleasant it is to dwell as one; but the things that follow have something to be opened to those who knock. Yet, that the understanding of the whole fabric of the Psalm may come down to us from this verse, let us first consider this again and again: whether it was said of all Christians: *How good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one*; or whether there are certain definite and perfect ones who dwell as one, and this blessing does not belong to all, but to certain ones, from whom, however, it comes down to the rest.

**2.** For these words of the Psalter, this sweet sound, this gentle melody, as much in song as in understanding, has even given birth to monasteries. By this sound were roused the brothers who longed to dwell as one; this verse was their trumpet. It sounded throughout the whole world, and those who were divided were gathered together. The cry of God, the cry of the Holy Spirit, the prophetic cry was not heard in Judea; and it was heard in the whole world. Against this sound they were deaf, among whom it was sung; and those were found with open ears, of whom it was said: *Those to whom it was not announced of him, will see; and those who have not heard, will understand*[^2]. Yet, dearly beloved, if we consider, from that wall of the circumcision this blessing was first born. For did all the Jews perish? And whence the apostles, the sons of the prophets, the *sons of those who have been shaken out*[^3]? As we speak to those who already know. Whence those five hundred who saw the Lord after the resurrection, whom the apostle Paul mentions[^4]? Whence those hundred and twenty who were together in one place after the resurrection of the Lord and his ascension into heaven[^5], upon whom, set in one place, the Holy Spirit came on the day of Pentecost, sent from heaven[^6]; so sent, as he was promised? All these were from there: and they themselves first dwelt as one, who sold all that they had, and laid the prices of their goods at the feet of the apostles[^7], as we read in the Acts of the Apostles: *And it was distributed to each one as anyone had need; and no one said anything was his own, but all things were common to them*[^8]. And what is, *as one*? *And they had*, he says, *one soul and one heart toward God*[^9]. So they first heard: *Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one.* They first heard, but did not alone hear. For this love and unity of brothers did not come as far as them only. For this exultation of love came also to those who followed, and a vow to God. For something is vowed to God, and it was said: *Vow, and render to the Lord your God*[^10]. But it is better not to vow, than to vow and not render[^11]. But the mind ought to be eager, both to vow and to render; lest, when it thinks itself less fit to render, it be slow to vow. Plainly it will never render, if it thinks it will render from its own resources.

**3.** From the voice of this psalm the Monks too are named, that no one may insult you Catholics over this name. When you begin rightly to insult the heretics over the Circellions, that they may be saved by being shamed; they insult you over the monks. First, if they are to be compared, you see; if there is need of your words, then you labor. There is no need, except that you admonish each one to attend; let him only attend, and compare. What need is there of your words? Let the drunken be compared with the sober, the headlong with the considerate, the raging with the simple, the wandering with the gathered. But yet they are accustomed to say: What does the name of monks mean? How much better do we say: What does the name of Circellions mean? But they are not called Circellions, they say. Perhaps we call them by a corrupted sound of the name. Shall we tell you their whole name? Perhaps they are called Circumcellions, not Circellions. Plainly, if they are so called, let them explain what they are. For they are called Circumcellions, because they wander around the cells: for they are accustomed to go this way and that, having nowhere a seat; and to do what you know, and what they know, whether they will or not.

**4.** Yet, dearly beloved, there are also those who are false monks; and we know such: but the devout brotherhood has not perished on account of those who profess what they are not. For there are false monks just as there are also false clergy, and false faithful. All the kinds, my brothers—the three which we once commended to you, and, as I think, not once—have their good ones, have their bad ones. For of those three kinds it was said: *Two in the field; one will be taken, and one left: and two in the bed; one will be taken, and one left: and two women at the mill; one will be taken, and one left*[^12]. In the field are those who govern the Church. Whence the apostle says (see whether he was not in the field): *I planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase*[^13]. But in the bed he wished those to be understood who loved quiet; for by the bed he wished quiet to be understood; not mingling themselves with crowds, not with the tumult of the human race, serving God in leisure: and yet from there too one will be taken, and one left. There are upright ones there, and there are reprobate ones there. Do not be terrified when reprobate ones are found there; for some too lie hidden, who are not found out except at the end. Likewise two women at the mill he named from the name of the feminine gender; for he wished the common people to be understood. Why at the mill? Because they are engaged in this world, where the mill is understood; because this world turns like a millstone: woe to those whom it crushes. So there the good faithful are engaged, that one of them is consumed, the other taken. For lovers of the world, defrauders, dissemblers, do certain actions of the world. But others are there as the apostle says: *And let those who use this world be as not using it; for the figure of this world passes away: I wish you to be without anxiety*[^14]. Do you hear who will be taken from the mill? For surely many sins seem to belong to the rich. For when they do more, and administer more, and a larger household depends on them, it is difficult that they not contract more sins; and of them it was said that *it is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle than for a rich man into the kingdom of heaven*[^15]. And when the disciples were saddened for those of whom they now despaired, the Lord consoled them, saying: *The things that are impossible for men are easy for God*[^16]. But how does God make that easy? Hear the apostle, if you do not neglect what he commanded: *Command*, he says, *the rich of this world not to be proud-minded*[^17]. For you find a poor man growing proud, a rich man humble; a Christian considering well that all those things pass by and flow away, that he brought nothing into this world, can take nothing away from the world; thinking how that rich man who burned in the flame among the dead longed for a drop to be dripped on him from the finger of the one who had coveted the crumbs from his table[^18]. Thinking these things, they do what the apostle says: *Neither to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who provides us all things abundantly to enjoy. Let them be rich*, he says, *in good works, let them give readily, share, lay up.* And what gain from it for them? *Let them lay up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life*[^19]. Behold who will be taken from the mill. But such a rich man as that one was who was clothed in purple and fine linen, and feasted splendidly daily, and despised the poor man lying at his gate, will be left. Because from the mill one will be taken, and one left.

**5.** So Ezekiel too speaks of three persons, in whom not absurdly we understand these three kinds: *When the Lord sends the sword into the land, even if Noah, Daniel, and Job be in their midst, they will not free sons or daughters; but they themselves alone will be saved*[^20]. They were freed long ago, but in these three names he signified certain three kinds. Noah signifies the rulers of the Church, because he steered the ark in the flood[^21]. But Daniel chose the quiet life, to serve God in celibacy, that is, not seeking a wife[^22]. He was a holy man, leading his life in heavenly desires; tested in many things, and found pure gold. How quiet he was, who even among the lions was secure! So in the name of Daniel, who is also called *a man of desires*[^23]—but surely of chaste and holy ones—are signified the servants of God, of whom it is said: *Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one.* In the name of Job is signified that one woman from the mill who will be taken. For he had a wife, he had sons, he had many riches; and he had so many things in this world, that the devil objected this to him, that not for nothing, but on account of the things he had received, did he worship God. The adversary objected this to the holy man: and in his temptations it was proved how, for nothing, Job worshipped God, and not on account of the things he had received, but on account of him who had given. And so, all things being lost in a sudden affliction and temptation, his inheritance lost, his heirs lost, his wife alone left to him, not for consolation, but for temptation, he said what you know: *The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done: blessed be the name of the Lord*[^24]. There was fulfilled in him what we daily sing, if we also accord with it in our ways: *I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall ever be in my mouth*[^25]. So in these three names were signified three kinds of men, and again in those three in the Gospel which I have mentioned.

**6.** What, then, do those say who insult us over the name of monks? Perhaps they will say: Ours are not called Circumcellions: you call them so by an insulting name; for we do not call them so. Let them say what they call them, and you will hear. They call them Agonistics. We too acknowledge it as an honorable name, if the reality also agreed. But meanwhile let your holiness see this: those who say to us: Show where the name of monks is written, let them show where the name of Agonistics is written. We call them so, they say, on account of the contest (agon). For they contend; and the apostle says: *I have fought the good fight*[^26]. Because there are those who contend against the devil, and prevail, they are called the agonistic soldiers of Christ. Would, then, that they were soldiers of Christ, and not soldiers of the devil, by whom, thanks be to God, more is feared than the roaring of a lion. These too dare to insult us, because the brothers, when they see men, say: Thanks be to God. What is, they say, 'Thanks be to God'? Are you so deaf that you do not know what 'Thanks be to God' is? He who says: Thanks be to God, gives thanks to God. See whether a brother ought not to give thanks to God, when he sees his brother. For is there not occasion for rejoicing, when those who dwell in Christ see one another? And yet you laugh at our 'Thanks be to God': men weep over your 'Praises to God.' But you have surely given a reason for the name, why you call them Agonistics. So be it, as you call them; so be it, we wholly favor it. May the Lord grant that they contend against the devil, and not against Christ, whose Church they persecute. Yet because they contend, you say Agonistics: and you found whence you might name them, because the apostle said: *I have fought the good fight.* Why, then, should we too not name them monks, since the Psalm says: *Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one*? For 'monos' means one: and not one in just any way; for in a crowd too there is one, but one along with many can be called 'one,' but cannot be called 'monos,' that is, alone: for 'monos' is one alone. Those, then, who so live as one, that they make one man, so that there is truly in them what is written, *one soul and one heart*[^27]; many bodies, but not many souls; many bodies, but not many hearts; are rightly called 'monos,' that is, one alone. Whence too that one man was healed at the pool. Let those who insult the name of monks answer us and explain why that man who was found having been thirty-eight years in his weakness answered the Lord thus: *When the water is stirred, I have no one to put me down; another goes down before me*[^28]. One went down, now another did not go down. One alone was healed; he figured the unity of the Church. Deservedly do those insult the name of unity, who have cut themselves off from unity. Deservedly does the name of monks displease them, because they are unwilling to dwell as one with the brothers; but, following Donatus, they abandoned Christ. These things about 'one,' and 'one alone,' your charity has heard; now let us rejoice with the Psalm, that we may see what follows. It is brief; it can be run through by us, as far as the Lord prompts. For I think that from what has been said, the things that follow can be clear, although they seem obscure.

**7.** [v. 2.] *Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell as one.* He who said 'Behold' was pointing it out. We too see it, brothers, and we bless God; and we pray that we too may say: Behold. And to what it is like, let the Psalm say. *Like the ointment on the head, which ran down onto the beard, the beard of Aaron; which ran down onto the hem of his garment*[^29]. What was *Aaron*? A priest. Who is the priest, except the one priest, who entered into the holy of holies? Who is this priest, except he who was both victim and priest? except he who, when he found in the world nothing clean to offer, offered himself? On his head is the ointment, because the whole Christ is with the Church: but the ointment comes from the head. Our head is Christ; crucified and buried, raised up, he ascended into heaven; and the Holy Spirit came from the head. To where? To the beard. The beard signifies the strong; the beard signifies the young, the vigorous, the eager, the brisk. Therefore when we describe such men: He is a bearded man, we say. So that first ointment ran down onto the apostles, ran down onto those who sustained the first onslaughts of the world: so the Holy Spirit ran down onto them. For those too who first began to dwell as one suffered persecution: but because the ointment had run down onto the beard, they suffered, but were not conquered. For the head, having already suffered, had gone before, from which the ointment ran down. With such an example going before, who could conquer the beard?

**8.** Of that beard was holy Stephen. And this is not to be conquered, that love be not conquered by enemies. For they seemed to themselves to have conquered, who persecuted the saints. They struck, they were struck; they killed, they were killed. Who would not think that those conquered, these were conquered? But because love was not conquered, therefore the ointment ran down onto the beard. Attend to Stephen. Love raged in him: it raged against them, when they heard; it prayed for them, when they stoned. For what did he say, when they heard? *Stiff-necked, and uncircumcised in heart and ears, you have always resisted the Holy Spirit*[^30]. See the beard. Did he flatter? did he fear? When they heard those things which were said against them: for Stephen as it were raged; he raged with his mouth, with his heart he loved; and love itself was not conquered in him: for they, shrinking from the word, like darkness fleeing from light, began to run to stones, to stone Stephen. As before the words of Stephen stoned them, so afterward their stones stoned Stephen. When ought Stephen to have been more angry, when he was being stoned, or when he was being heard? Behold, he was made gentle while being stoned, and raged while being heard. Why did he rage while being heard? Because he wished to change those by whom he was being heard. When the stones came upon him, love was not conquered: because the ointment had run down from the head onto the beard, and he had heard from the head itself: *Love your enemies, and pray for those who persecute you*[^31]. He had heard from the head itself hanging on the cross, and saying: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*[^32]. So then the ointment had run down from the head onto the beard; because he too, when he was being stoned, on bent knee said: *Lord, do not lay this sin to their charge*[^33].

**9.** So they were as it were the beard. For many were strong, and suffered many persecutions. But if the ointment had not run down from the beard too, we would now have no monasteries. But because it ran down also onto the hem of the garment; for he says thus: *Which ran down onto the hem of his garment*: the Church followed, from the garment of the Lord she gave birth to monasteries. For the priestly garment signifies the Church. It is that garment of which the apostle says: *That he might present to himself a glorious Church, having neither spot nor wrinkle*[^34]. It is cleansed, that it may not have a spot; it is stretched out, that it may not have a wrinkle. Where does the fuller stretch it out, except on the wood? We see daily that tunics are as it were crucified by the fullers: they are crucified, that they may not have a wrinkle. What, then, is the hem of the garment? my brothers, in the hem of the garment what are we to receive? The hem is the end of the garment. What are we to receive in the end of the garment? Or is it because at the end of the times the Church was to have brothers dwelling as one? or do we understand in the hem perfection, because in the hem the garment is finished; and those perfect, who know how to dwell as one? They are perfect who fulfill the law. But how is the law of Christ fulfilled by those who, as brothers, dwell as one? Hear the apostle: *Bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^35]. This is the hem of the garment. How, my brothers, do we understand which hem he means, to which the ointment could run down? I do not think that he wished the hem of the garment to be understood as at the sides. For there are hems at the sides. But the ointment could run down from the beard to the hem which is at the head, where the opening for the head is. Such are those who dwell as one: that just as through those openings the head of a man enters, that he may clothe himself; so through fraternal concord Christ enters, who is our head, that he may be clothed, that the Church may cleave to him.

**10.** [v. 3.] What else does he say? *Like the dew of Hermon, which ran down onto the mountains of Zion*[^36]. He wished this to be understood, my brothers, that it is the grace of God that brothers dwell as one; not from their own strength, not from their own merits, but from his gift, but from his grace, like dew from heaven. For the earth does not rain for itself, or rather, whatever it brings forth withers, unless rain comes down from above. He says in a certain place in a psalm: *A voluntary rain you will set apart, O God, for your inheritance*[^37]. Why did he say *voluntary*? Because not by our merits, but by his will. For what good did we sinners deserve? what good did we, the wicked, deserve? From Adam, Adam; and upon Adam many sins are born. Whoever is born, is born Adam, condemned from one condemned, and he has added, by living badly, upon Adam. For what good did Adam deserve? And yet, merciful, he loved; and the bridegroom loved, not the beautiful, but that he might make her beautiful. So he called the dew of Hermon the grace of God.

**11.** But you ought to know what Hermon is. It is a certain mountain far separated from Jerusalem, that is, from Zion. And so it is strange that he says thus: *Like the dew of Hermon, which ran down onto the mountains of Zion*; when Mount Hermon is far separated from Jerusalem: for it is said to be beyond the Jordan. So let us seek something from the interpretation of Hermon. For it is Hebrew, and we have the interpretation from those who knew that tongue. 'Hermon' is said to be interpreted 'Light lifted up.' For the dew is from Christ: for there is no light lifted up, except Christ. How was he lifted up? First on the cross; afterward in heaven. He was lifted up on the cross, while he was being humbled; but his humiliation could not but be lofty. The dispensation of the man was becoming less and less, which was signified in John; the dispensation of God in our Lord Jesus Christ was growing: which is shown even by their birthdays. For he was born, as the Church hands down, on the eighth day before the Kalends of July, when the days now begin to diminish: but the Lord was born on the eighth day before the Kalends of January, when the days now begin to grow. Hear John himself confessing: *He must increase, but I must diminish*[^38]. And their sufferings indicate this. The Lord was lifted up on the cross; John was diminished by the cutting off of his head. So the light lifted up is Christ: thence is the dew of Hermon. But whoever you are who wish to dwell as one, desire this dew, be rained on from it. Otherwise you will not be able to hold what you profess, nor will you be able to dare to profess it, unless he thunders; nor will you be able to remain, unless his rich nourishment does not fail you: for that very nourishment runs down onto the mountains of Zion.

**12.** Now *the mountains of Zion*, the great in Zion. What is Zion? The Church. And who are the mountains there? The great. What the mountains signify, this the beard signifies, this the hem of the garment signifies. The beard is understood only in the perfect. So they do not dwell as one, except those in whom the love of Christ has been made perfect. For those in whom the love of Christ is not perfect, even when they are in one place, are hateful, are troublesome, are turbulent, by their anxiety they disturb the rest, and seek what to say about them; just as in the yoke a restless beast not only does not pull, but even breaks with its kicks what is yoked. But if it has the dew of Hermon, *which runs down onto the mountains of Zion*, it is quiet, peaceable, humble, patient, and pours out a prayer instead of a murmur. For murmurers are all magnificently described in a certain place in the Scriptures: *The heart of a fool is like the wheel of a cart*[^39]. What is: *The heart of a fool is like the wheel of a cart*? It carries hay, and murmurs: for the wheel of a cart cannot rest from creaking. So are many brothers; they do not dwell as one except in body. But who are those who dwell as one? Those of whom it was said: *And they had one soul and one heart toward God: and no one said that anything was his own, but all things were common to them*[^40]. They are designated, they are described, who belong to the beard, who belong to the hem of the garment, who are counted among the mountains of Zion. And if there are other murmurers there, let them remember the saying of the Lord: *One will be taken, and one left*[^41].

**13.** *Because there the Lord commanded the blessing*[^42]. Where did he command it? Among the brothers who dwell as one. There he commanded the blessing, there they bless the Lord who dwell in concord. For in discord you do not bless the Lord. In vain you say that your tongue sounds a blessing of the Lord, if you do not sound it with the heart: with the mouth you bless, and with the heart you curse. *With their mouth they blessed, and with their heart they cursed*[^43]. Are these not our words? Certain men were signified. You bless the Lord, when you pray; and you go on in your prayer, and you curse your enemy. Is this what you heard from the Lord himself: *Love your enemies*[^44]? But if you do it, and love your enemy, and pray for him, *there the Lord commanded* his *blessing*; there you will have *life forever*, that is, unto eternity. For many, loving this life, curse their enemies: for what, except for this life, except for worldly conveniences? Where has your enemy straitened you, that you are forced to curse? On earth you are straitened; migrate, dwell in heaven. How, you will say, do I dwell in heaven, a man clothed in flesh, given over to the flesh? Go before in heart, that you may follow in body. Do not hear it as a deaf man: Lift up your hearts. Have your heart on high, and no one will straiten you in heaven. And therefore another psalm follows well.
