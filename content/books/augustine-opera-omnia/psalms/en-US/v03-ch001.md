# Exposition of Psalm 51

**1.** The psalm we have undertaken to speak about to Your Charity is brief, but its title is somewhat laborious. So bear with us patiently until we untangle it as best we can, so far as the Lord helps us. For these matters are not to be passed over lightly, since it has pleased the brethren that what we say should be taken down not only by ear and heart but also by pen, so that we must keep in mind not only the listener but the reader as well. The occasion of this psalm arose from a certain event that we have had recited to you from the book of Kings. For Saul was a king chosen by the Lord not to endure, but given according to the hard and evil heart of the people for their chastisement, not for their benefit, according to that saying of the holy Scriptures which says of God: *He makes a hypocrite to reign because of the perversity of the people*[^1]. Since, then, Saul was such, he persecuted David, in whom God prefigured the kingdom of eternal salvation, and whom God had chosen to endure in his seed; for our king, the king of the ages, with whom we shall reign forever, was to come from the seed of this same David according to the flesh. When, therefore, God had chosen David, and chosen him beforehand, and predestined him for the kingdom, he did not will that David himself should hold the kingdom before he had first freed him from his persecutors, so that in this very thing he might prefigure us, that is, his body, of which body Christ is the head. For if our head himself was unwilling to reign in heaven without first completing his labor on earth, nor to raise up the body that he had taken on below except by the way of tribulation, what do the members dare to hope, that they could be more blessed than their head? *If they have called the master of the house Beelzebub, how much more those of his household?*[^2] Let us not, then, hope for a softer way: let us go where he went before us, let us follow where he led. For if we stray from his footstep, we perish. You see, then, what was prefigured in David; you see also what was prefigured in Saul: an evil kingdom in Saul, a good kingdom in David; death in Saul, and life in David. For nothing persecutes us except death, over which we shall triumph in the end, saying: *O death, where is your contention? O death, where is your sting?*[^3] What do I mean when I say that nothing persecutes us except death? Because if we were not mortal, there would be nothing for the enemy to do to us. For does he do anything to the angels? Therefore even death itself, from which we suffer persecution most of all, whose contention is ended in the end when we rise from the dead, will be ended in us, as it has been ended in our head, if we are found righteous. For he who died was the slayer of death, and death died in him more than he died in death.

**2.** Then again, if we attend to the name itself, it is not without mystery. For Saul means "petition," that is, "appetite." For why should we doubt that we ourselves brought this death upon ourselves? For death was born from the sin of man. Rightly, then, did man himself crave death for himself, and so "petition" is the name of death. For as it is written: *God did not make death, nor does he rejoice in the destruction of the living. For he created all things that they might exist, and he made the nations of the world capable of healing.*[^4] And as though you were asking whence death comes, it says: *But the ungodly summoned it with their hands and their words, and reckoning it a friend, they wasted away*[^5]. So by craving it they wasted away and rushed into death, reckoning it a friend to them, just as the people reckoned a friend and asked for an enemy as king. For the people wrung from the Lord the right to have a king, and Saul was given, as they were given into their own hands who summoned death with their hands and words; and death itself was figured in Saul. This is why the seventeenth psalm has its title thus: *On the day when the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul*[^6]. It said first, all his enemies; and afterward, *from the hand of Saul*, because the last enemy to be destroyed is death. What is *and from the hand of Saul*? Because he rescued us from hell and freed us from the hand of death.

**3.** When, therefore, Saul was persecuting the holy man David, David took refuge where he thought it safe: passing by way of a certain priest, Ahimelech, he received bread from him. There he figured the person not only of a king but also of a priest, because he ate the loaves of the presence, *which*, as the Lord says in the Gospel, *it was not lawful to eat except for the priests alone*[^7]. Afterward Saul began to seek him, and was angry with his own men because none would betray him to him. So it is recorded in the book of Kings. Now there was there a certain Doeg, an Edomite, the chief of Saul's shepherds, when David came to Ahimelech the priest. He, being present when Saul was angry with his men because none would betray David to him, betrayed where he had seen him. Saul at once sent and produced the priest and all his men, and ordered them killed. None of King Saul's men dared, even at the king's command, to lay a hand upon the priests of the Lord; but the one who had betrayed him—like Judas, who did not depart from his purpose and persevered to the end in bearing fruit from that root (and what fruit but such as an evil tree bears?)—this Doeg killed with his own hand, at the king's command, the priest and all his men; then the city of the priests too was destroyed. We find, then, that this Doeg was an enemy of king David and of Ahimelech the priest. Doeg is one man, but Doeg is a kind of men; just as David is the body both of the king and of the priest, as it were one man and two persons, yet one kind of men. So in this time and in this age let us look at these two kinds, that it may be to our benefit either to sing or to hear sung. Now let us look at Doeg, now let us look at the body of the king and priest, now let us look at the body of the men set against the king and priest.

**4.** First, notice how mystical the names themselves are. Doeg means "Motion"; Edomite means "Earthly." Now see what kind of men this Doeg, "Motion," signifies: not, then, one who endures forever, but one destined to be moved on. "Earthly": what fruits do you expect from an earthly man? The heavenly man will be forever. There is, then, an earthly kingdom—to put it briefly and convey it quickly—today in this age, where there is also the heavenly kingdom. Both kingdoms have their citizens as sojourners, the earthly kingdom and the heavenly kingdom, the kingdom to be uprooted and the kingdom to be planted forever. For now, in this age, the citizens of both kingdoms are mingled together: the body of the earthly kingdom and the body of the heavenly kingdom are intermixed. The heavenly kingdom groans among the citizens of the earthly kingdom, and sometimes—for this too must not be passed over in silence—the earthly kingdom in a way conscripts the citizens of the kingdom of heaven, and the heavenly kingdom conscripts the citizens of the earthly kingdom. We will demonstrate both to you from the Scripture of God. Daniel and the three young men were set over the king's affairs in Babylon; Joseph in Egypt was set as second to the king to administer the commonwealth, the commonwealth from which that people of God was to be freed: in that very commonwealth Joseph was, as it were, performing conscripted service, as did those three young men, as did Daniel. It is clear, then, that the earthly kingdom had pressed into its own works—that is, the works of its kingdom, not its evil deeds—citizens of the kingdom of heaven. And how does the kingdom of heaven also press into service, for a time in this age, the citizens of the earthly kingdom? Does not the Apostle speak of these, that they did not preach the Gospel chastely, but, desiring earthly things, preached the kingdom of heaven; seeking their own and yet proclaiming Christ? And that you may know that even these were taken up, like hired hands, for the work of the kingdom of heaven, the Apostle, rejoicing over them, says: *There are those who proclaim Christ out of envy and rivalry, not chastely, supposing to stir up affliction for my bonds. What then? Only that in every way, whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is proclaimed; and in this I rejoice, yes, and I shall rejoice*[^8]. Of such people Christ too speaks, saying: *The Pharisees and the scribes have sat on the seat of Moses. What they say, do; but what they do, do not do; for they speak and do not act*[^9]. What they say belongs to David; what they do belongs to Doeg. Hear me through them, do not imitate them. These two kinds of men are on the earth today. Of these two kinds of men this psalm sings.

**5.** [vv. 1-2] Now the title of the psalm is: *Unto the end, the understanding of David, when Doeg the Edomite came and told Saul: David has come into the house of Abimelech*[^10]—though we read that he came into the house of Ahimelech. And perhaps it is not absurd to think that the titles were varied because of the similarity of the name and the difference of a single syllable, or rather of a single letter. Yet when we examined the codices of the Psalms, we found Abimelech rather than Ahimelech. And since in another place you have a most evident psalm that suggests not a difference of name but an altogether different name—since David changed his countenance before king Achish, not before king Abimelech, and he let him go, and he departed, while the title of the psalm is written thus: *When he changed his countenance before Abimelech*[^11]—this very change of name makes us intent upon a mystery, lest you pursue the matter as a matter of history and despise the sacred veils. The name Abimelech, examined in that psalm, is found to mean "my father's kingdom." But how did David leave his father's kingdom and depart, except as Christ left the kingdom of the Jews and passed over to the Gentiles? Hence perhaps the prophetic spirit too, in assigning the title to this psalm, willed that it should say not Ahimelech but Abimelech, because when David came to his father's kingdom, then was he betrayed; that is, when our Lord Jesus Christ came to the kingdom of the Jews, established by his Father—of which he says: *The kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a nation producing its fruit and its righteousness*[^12]—then was he betrayed to the death that Saul signifies. Yet he was not killed, just as neither was Isaac, when he too prefigured the Lord's passion; and yet the figure was not enacted without blood, either there in the ram or here in the priest Ahimelech. For they ought not to have been killed who were not then to rise again; but Jesus, freeing their life from the peril of death—yet with blood shed—rather signified the resurrection, which was thus figured in them because it was reserved for the true Lord. More could be said on this, had we undertaken in this discourse to treat the mysteries of those events.

**6.** Now let us hear of those two kinds of men, since we have come out of that title, though with rather more labor and perhaps more talk, yet as the Lord granted. Attend to the two kinds of men: one of those who labor, the other of those among whom there is labor; one thinking of earth, the other of heaven; one casting the heart into the depths, the other joining the heart to the angels; one hoping in earthly things in which this world abounds, the other relying beforehand on the heavenly things that God, who does not lie, has promised. But these kinds of men are mixed together. We find, even now, a citizen of Jerusalem, a citizen of the kingdom of heaven, administering something on earth: for instance, he wears the purple, he is a magistrate, he is an aedile, he is a proconsul, he is an emperor, he governs the earthly commonwealth; but he keeps his heart above, if he is a Christian, if he is faithful, if he is devout, if he despises the things he is among, and hopes in the One in whom he is not yet. Of this kind was that holy woman Esther, who, being the king's wife, came to the danger of pleading for her fellow citizens; and when she prayed before God, where she could not lie, she said in her prayer that those royal ornaments had been to her like the rag of a menstruous woman. Let us not, then, despair of the citizens of the kingdom of heaven when we see them transacting some business of Babylon, something earthly in the earthly commonwealth; nor, on the other hand, let us at once congratulate all the men we see transacting heavenly business, because even the sons of pestilence sometimes sit on the seat of Moses, of whom it is said: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do; for they speak and do not act*[^13]. Those men, amid earthly affairs, lift their heart to heaven; these, amid heavenly words, drag their heart down to earth. But the time of the winnowing-fan will come, when each will be most carefully distinguished, lest any grain pass into the heap of chaff to be burned, lest a single stalk pass into the mass to be stored in the barn. As long, then, as it is now mixed together, let us hear here our own voice, that is, the voice of the citizens of the kingdom of heaven (for this we ought to aim at, to bear with the wicked here rather than be borne with by the good); and let us join ourselves to this voice, by ear and tongue and heart and deed. If we do this, it is we who speak here in the things we hear. Let it be said, then, first concerning the evil body of the earthly kingdom.

**7.** [v. 3] *Why do you glory in malice, you who are mighty?*[^14] Attend, my brethren, to the glory of malignity, the glory of evil men. What is the glory? *Why do you glory in malice, you who are mighty?* That is, you who are mighty in malice, why do you glory? It is necessary to be mighty, but in goodness, not in malice. Is it some great thing to glory in malice? To build a house is for the few; any ignorant person can tear one down. To sow grain, to cultivate the crop, to wait until it ripens, and to rejoice in the fruit for which one labored, is for the few; any person can set fire to a whole field with a single spark. To take up an infant, to nurse the newborn, to rear it, to bring it to youth, is a great office; any person can kill it in a single point of time. So the things that are done for destruction are done most easily. *Let him who glories, glory in the Lord*[^15]: let him who glories, glory in goodness. You glory because you are mighty in evil. What are you going to do, O mighty one, what are you going to do, you who boast so much? You are going to kill a man: this a scorpion does too, this a single fever does, this an evil mushroom does. Has all your power been reduced to this, that it is matched with an evil mushroom? This, then, is what the good citizens of Jerusalem do, who glory not in malice but in goodness: first, that they glory not in themselves but in the Lord; then, that the things they do for building up they do zealously, and do such things as have power to endure; while what they do toward destruction, they do for the discipline of those making progress, not for the oppression of the innocent. To this power, then, is that earthly body compared; why should it not hear from these words: *Why do you glory in malice, you who are mighty?*

**8.** [v. 4] *All the day long your tongue has plotted injustice in iniquity.*[^16] *In iniquity all the day long*, that is, all the time, without weariness, without interval, without pause. And when you do not do evil, you think it; so that when some evil is absent from your hands, it is not absent from your heart: either you do evil, or, while you cannot do it, you speak evil, that is, you curse; or, when you cannot do even this, you will and think evil. *All the day long*, then, that is, without intermission. We look for a punishment for this man. Is the punishment too small for him? You threaten him: do you threaten him with the very thing into which you wish to cast him? Leave him to himself. To rage greatly, you would set wild beasts upon him: he is worse to himself than the wild beasts. For a beast can tear his body; he himself cannot leave his own heart whole. Within, he rages against himself, and you seek outward blows for him? Rather, pray to God for him, that he may be freed from himself. Nevertheless, my brethren, in this psalm there is no prayer for the wicked, or against the wicked, but a prophecy of what will befall the wicked. So do not think that the psalm says anything out of ill will: for it is said in the spirit of prophecy.

**9.** What, then, follows? All your power, and all the thought of iniquity all the day long, and the meditation of malignity on your tongue without intermission—what has it accomplished? what has it done? *Like a sharpened razor you have wrought deceit.*[^17] See what the wicked do to the saints: they shave off the hair. What do I mean by this? If they are such citizens of Jerusalem as hear the voice of their Lord, their king, who says: *Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul*[^18]; who hear the voice that was just now recited from the Gospel: *What does it profit a man if he gains the whole world but suffers the loss of himself?*[^19]—they despise all present goods, and even life itself. And what will Doeg's razor do to a man who meditates here on earth the kingdom of heaven, and who will be in the kingdom of heaven, having God with him and abiding with God? What will that razor do? It will shave off the hair, it will make him bald. And this pertains to Christ, who was crucified in the place of Calvary. It also produces the son of Korah, which means "Baldness." For these hairs signify the superfluous things of temporal affairs. These hairs were indeed not made superfluously by God on the human body, but for some adornment; yet because they are cut off without sensation, those who cleave with their heart to the Lord hold these earthly things as if they were hairs. But sometimes some good is to be done even with the hairs, when you break bread for the hungry, when you bring the needy without a bed into your house, when, if you see one naked, you clothe him; finally, when the martyrs themselves, imitating the Lord, shedding their blood for the Church, hearing that voice—as Christ *laid down his soul for us*, so *we too ought to lay down our souls for the brethren*[^20]—in a way did us good with their hairs, that is, with those things that the razor can lop off or shave. And because some good can be done even with the hairs, that woman who was a sinner signified this too, when, weeping over the Lord's feet, she wiped with her hair the feet she had bathed with her tears. What did she signify? That when you have had mercy on someone, you ought also to come to his aid, if you can. For when you have mercy, you pour out tears, as it were; when you come to aid, you wipe with your hair. And if this is so for anyone, how much more for the feet of the Lord? Who are the feet of the Lord? The holy evangelists, of whom it is said: *How beautiful are the feet of those who proclaim peace, who proclaim good things!*[^21] So let Doeg sharpen his tongue like a razor; let him sharpen his deceit as much as he can: he will take away superfluous temporal things; will he take away necessary eternal things?

**10.** [v. 5] *You have loved malice above kindness.*[^22] Kindness was before you—you ought to have loved it. For you were not going to spend anything, nor to fetch from a far voyage the thing you would love. Kindness before you, iniquity before you: compare, and choose. But perhaps you have an eye to see malignity, and have no eye to see kindness. Woe to the iniquitous heart! What is worse, it turns itself away so as not to see what it is able to see. For what was said of such men in another place? *He refused to understand so as to do good.*[^23] For it did not say, "he was not able," but *he refused*, it says, *to understand so as to do good*; he closed his eyes against the present light. And what follows? *He plotted iniquity on his bed*[^24], that is, in the inner secret of his heart. Something like this is brought against this Doeg the Edomite, the malign body, the body of the dead one, the earthly, not-enduring, not-heavenly one. *You have loved malice above kindness.* For do you wish to know how the wicked man sees both, and yet chooses the former, and turns himself away from the latter? Why does he cry out when he suffers some injustice? Why does he then exaggerate iniquity as much as he can, and praise kindness, reproaching the one who has wrought malignity above kindness upon him? Let it be a rule for him to live by, then: he will be judged out of himself. Further, if he were to do what is written: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^25]; and: *Whatever good you wish men to do to you, do also to them*[^26]—he has within himself the means of knowing that what he does not wish done to himself he ought not to do to another. *You have loved malice above kindness.* Unjustly, disorderly, perversely you wish to raise water above oil: the water will sink, the oil will rise to the top. You wish to set light beneath darkness: the darkness will be put to flight, the light will remain. You wish to place earth above heaven: by its own weight the earth will fall to its place. You, then, will be sunk by loving malice above kindness. For malice will never overcome kindness. *You have loved malice above kindness: iniquity more than to speak equity.*[^27] Equity is before you, iniquity is before you: you have one tongue, you turn it where you will; why, then, to iniquity rather, and not to equity? You do not give foods of bitterness to your belly, and do you give foods of iniquity to your malign tongue? As you choose what you will eat, so choose what you will speak. You set iniquity, then, before equity, and you set malice before kindness: you indeed set them first, but what can be above except kindness and equity? But by setting yourself, as it were, above the things that must of necessity go downward, you will not make them be above good things, but you will be sunk with them into evils.

**11.** [v. 6] For this reason there follows in the psalm: *You have loved all words of drowning.*[^28] Rescue yourself, then, if you can, from drowning. You flee shipwreck, and you embrace lead! If you do not wish to be sunk, lay hold of a plank, be carried by the wood, let the cross bring you through. But now, because you are Doeg the Edomite, moved and earthly, what do you do? *You have loved all words of drowning, a deceitful tongue.* This came first; the words of drowning followed the deceitful tongue. What is a deceitful tongue? A deceitful tongue is the minister of falsehood, of those who bear one thing in their heart and bring forth another with their mouth. But in these is overthrow, in these is drowning.

**12.** [v. 7] *Therefore God will destroy you unto the end*[^29]: even if you now seem to flourish like the grass in the field before the sun's heat. For *all flesh is grass, and the splendor of man is like the flower of grass: the grass has withered, and the flower has fallen; but the word of the Lord abides forever*[^30]. See where you should hold fast: to that which abides forever. For if you hold fast to the grass and to the flower of grass, since the grass will wither and the flower will fall, *God will destroy you unto the end*: if not now, certainly in the end he will destroy you, when that winnowing-fan comes and the heap of chaff is separated from the mass. Is not the mass for the barns, and the chaff for the fire? Will not this whole Doeg stand at the left hand, when the Lord is about to say: *Go into the eternal fire that is prepared for the devil and his angels?*[^31] *God*, then, *will destroy you unto the end: he will pluck you up and remove you from your dwelling.*[^32] So now this Doeg the Edomite is in the dwelling: but the servant does not abide in the house forever. He too works some good, even if not by his own deeds, at least by the words of God, so that in the Church, when he seeks his own, he may at least say the things that are Christ's. But *he will remove you from your dwelling*. Amen I say to you, they have received their reward. *And your root from the land of the living.* Therefore in the land of the living we ought to have our root. There let our root be. The root is hidden: the fruits can be seen, the root cannot be seen. Our root is our love, our fruits are our works: your works must proceed from love, and then your root is in the land of the living. Hence this Doeg will be uprooted, and will not be able to remain there at all, because he did not fix his root deep there; but like those seeds on the rock, even though they put out a root, yet because they have no moisture, as soon as the sun rises they at once wither. But those who fix their root deeper, what do they hear from the Apostle? *I bend my knees for you to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you may be rooted and grounded in love.*[^33] And because the root is now there: *That you may be able*, he says, *to comprehend what is the height, the breadth, the length, and the depth; to know also the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God*[^34]. Of such fruits is so great a root worthy, so simple, so fruitful, founded so deep in shoots. But this man's root is uprooted from the land of the living.

**13.** [v. 8] *And the righteous will see, and will fear, and will laugh over him.*[^35] When will they fear? when will they laugh? Let us understand, then, and distinguish, profitably enough, these two times of fearing and of laughing. For as long as we are in this age, it is not yet time to laugh, lest afterward we weep. We read what is reserved in the end for this Doeg; we read it, and because we understand and believe, we see, but we fear. So this was said: *The righteous will see, and will fear.* As long as we see what will befall the wicked in the end, why do we fear? Because the Apostle said: *Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling*[^36]; because it is said in the psalm: *Serve the Lord in fear, and exult in him with trembling*[^37]. Why with fear? *Therefore let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall*[^38]. Why with trembling? Because he says elsewhere: *Brethren, if a man be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual instruct such a one in the spirit of gentleness, looking to yourself lest you too be tempted*[^39]. So the righteous who exist now, who live by faith, see what will befall this Doeg in such a way that they nonetheless also fear for themselves: for they know what they are today; what they will be tomorrow, they do not know. So now *the righteous will see, and will fear*. But when will they laugh? When iniquity has passed, when it has flown away, as the uncertain time has now in great part flown away, when the darkness of this age has been put to flight, in which we now walk only by the lamp of the Scriptures, and therefore we fear as if in the night. For we walk by prophecy, of which the apostle Peter says: *We have the more sure prophetic word, to which you do well to attend, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts*[^40]. As long, then, as we walk by the lamp, we must live with fear. But when our day comes, that is, the manifestation of Christ, of which the same Apostle says: *When Christ your life appears, then you too will appear with him in glory*[^41]—then the righteous will laugh over this Doeg. For there is then no place for coming to his aid: not as now, when you see a man living unjustly and wish to labor with him to correct him; because he who is unjust may be converted and become just, just as the just man, turned away, may become unjust. So neither presume about yourself nor despair about him; and give your effort as much as you can, if you are kind, if you do not love malice above kindness, to correct a man walking and straying in an evil way and lead him to the good way. But when the time of judgment comes, there will be no place for correction, but only for damnation; and there will be repentance there, but fruitless, because too late. Do you wish it to be fruitful? Let it not be late: correct yourself today. You are the accused, he is the judge: correct your guilt, and you will rejoice before the judge. For today he exhorts you, lest he judge you; and the one who is to be your judge is himself today your advocate. Then, therefore, brethren, there remains the time of laughing. For that very mockery of the unrighteous which the righteous will exercise, the book of Wisdom signified. For in his own people, into whose souls wisdom transfers itself, it will do what it said: *I called, and you did not listen; I spoke, and you did not attend to my words; and I too will laugh over your destruction*[^42]. This the righteous will do then over this Doeg. But now let us see and fear, lest we be that which we speak against him; and if we were this, let us cease to be it, so that, fearing now, we may afterward laugh.

**14.** [v. 9] But what will they say then who will laugh? *And they will laugh over him, and say: Behold the man who did not make God his helper.*[^43] See the earthly body. "As much as you have, so much will you be." This is the proverb of the greedy, the rapacious, those who oppress the innocent, who seize others' property, who deny what was entrusted to them. What sort of proverb is this? "As much as you have, so much will you be"; that is, as much money as you have, as much as you have acquired, so much the more powerful you will be. *Behold the man who did not make God his helper, but hoped in the abundance of his riches.* Let not perhaps the wicked poor man say: I am not of this body. For he heard the Prophet say: *He hoped in the abundance of his riches*; and at once, if he is poor, he looks at his rags, glances at a rich man beside him among the people of God dressed more finely, and says in his heart: He means that man; surely not me? Do not exempt yourself from it, do not set yourself apart, unless you see and fear, so that afterward you may laugh. For what does it profit you if you lack means and burn with greed? When our Lord Jesus Christ had said to that rich man who went away sorrowful: *Go, sell all you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me*[^44], and had foretold great despair for the rich, so as to say that a camel could more easily pass through the eye of a needle than a rich man enter the kingdom of heaven, did not the disciples at once grow sorrowful, saying among themselves: *Who then can be saved?*[^45] So when they said: *Who then can be saved?*—were they attending to the few rich? was so great a multitude of the poor hidden from them? Could they not have said to themselves: If it is difficult, indeed impossible, for the rich to enter the kingdom of heaven, as it is impossible for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle, let all the poor enter the kingdom of heaven, and let only the rich be excluded? For how many are the rich? But of the poor there are innumerable thousands. For we are not going to inspect tunics in the kingdom of heaven; rather, each one's garment will be reckoned the brightness of righteousness: the poor, then, will be equal to the angels of God; clothed in the robes of immortality, they will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. Why should we be anxious or labor about the few rich? Not so did the Apostles think; but when the Lord had said this: *It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven*, they, saying among themselves: *Who then can be saved?*—what were they attending to? Not means, but greed. For they saw that even the poor themselves, though they had no money, nonetheless had avarice. And that you may know that it is not money in the rich man but avarice that is condemned, notice what I say: you look at that rich man standing beside you; and perhaps in him there is money and there is no avarice, while in you there is no money and there is avarice. The poor man, full of sores, wretched, licked by dogs, having no help, no food, perhaps not even clothing itself, was carried by the angels into the bosom of Abraham. Come now, poor man, do you rejoice now? Surely you need not also wish for the sores? Is not your health your patrimony? It is not the merit of poverty in this Lazarus, but of piety. For you see who was carried up; you do not see whither he was carried up. Who was carried up by the angels? A poor man, wretched, full of sores. Whither was he carried up? Into the bosom of Abraham. Read the Scriptures, and you will find that Abraham was rich. So that you may know that riches are not blamed: Abraham had much gold, silver, cattle, household; he was rich, and into his bosom the poor Lazarus was carried up. The poor man into the bosom of the rich: or rather, were not both rich toward God, both poor in regard to greed?

**15.** What, then, does Scripture blame in this Doeg? It did not say: Behold the man who was rich; but: *Behold the man who did not make God his helper, but hoped in the abundance of his riches.*[^46] Not because he had riches, but because he hoped in them, and did not hope in God, is he condemned, is he punished, is he moved from his dwelling, like that earthly motion, like the dust that the wind drives from the face of the earth; therefore his root is uprooted from the land of the living. Surely such men are not like the rich of whom the apostle Paul speaks: *Charge the rich of this world not to be proud-minded*, like Doeg; *nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches*, as he himself *hoped in the abundance of his riches; but in the living God*[^47], not as this man *who did not make God his helper*? Finally, what did he charge them? *Let them be rich in good works; let them give readily, share with others*[^48]. And what then, if they give readily, if they share with one who has not? Will they enter through the eye of the needle? They will enter, surely: for on their behalf the camel himself has already entered. For he entered first, whom no one would have loaded, like a camel, with the burden of suffering, had he not laid himself down upon the earth. Because he too said this: *What is impossible for men is easy for God*[^49]. Let this Doeg, then, be condemned; let the righteous now fear concerning him, let the righteous afterward laugh over him. For deservedly is he condemned *who did not make God his helper*, like you—you who perhaps have money, but rely on God, not on money. *And he hoped in the abundance of his riches*: he has been made like those who, when they said, "Blessed is the people that has these things," that is, these earthly things, this man who insults this Doeg at once replied: *Blessed is the people whose Lord is its God.*[^50] For the things in which they called the people blessed, the psalm enumerates. For they spoke like alien sons, like this Doeg the Edomite, that is, the earthly one: *Their mouth has spoken vanity; their right hand is a right hand of iniquity. Their sons are like new plants established in their youth; their daughters adorned and decked out like the likeness of a temple; their storehouses full, overflowing from this into that; their sheep fruitful, multiplying in their goings forth; their oxen fat: there is no breach of the wall, no going out, no cry in their streets*[^51]. They seem to have great happiness of earthly peace. But he who is earthly is also moved, that is, like the dust that the wind drives from the face of the earth. Finally, what is reproached in them? Not that they had these things—for the good too have these things—but what? Attend to this, lest you reproach the rich indiscriminately, and again lest you presume on poverty and want. For if one must not presume on riches, how much more must one not presume on poverty, but on the living God? In what, then, are these men noted? Because *they called the people blessed that has these things*. Therefore they are alien sons, therefore *their mouth has spoken vanity, and their right hand is a right hand of iniquity*. But you, what? *Blessed is the people whose Lord is its God.*

**16.** [v. 10] So, when this man is condemned who *hoped in the abundance of his riches and prevailed in his vanity*[^52]—for what is vainer than one who thinks a coin worth more than God?—when this man is condemned who said *the people that has these things is blessed*, you who say: *Blessed is the people whose Lord is its God*, what do you think of yourself? what do you hope for yourself? *But I*—now hear that body—*but I am like a fruitful olive tree in the house of God.*[^53] It is not one man who speaks, but that fruitful olive tree, from which the proud branches have been lopped off and the humble wild olive has been grafted in. *Like a fruitful olive tree in the house of God, I have hoped in the mercy of God.* What of that other? *In the abundance of his riches*: therefore his root will be plucked up from the land of the living. *But I*, because I am *like a fruitful olive tree in the house of God*, whose root is nourished, not uprooted, *I have hoped in the mercy of God.* But perhaps only for now? For here too men sometimes err. They worship God, indeed, and are no longer like this Doeg; but although they rely on God, yet it is for temporal things, so that they say to themselves: I worship my God, who will make me rich on earth, who will give me sons, who will give me a wife. Such things, indeed, none but God gives, but God does not wish to be loved on account of such things. For this is why he often gives these things even to the wicked, so that the good may learn to seek something else from him. How, then, do you say: *I have hoped in the mercy of God?* Perhaps to obtain temporal things? No—*forever, and unto the age of the age.*[^54] What he said, *forever*, he wished to repeat by adding *unto the age of the age*, so that by repeating it he might confirm how grounded he was in the love of the kingdom of heaven and in the hope of eternal happiness.

**17.** [v. 11] *I will confess to you forever, because you have acted.*[^55] What have you *acted*? You have condemned Doeg, you have crowned David. *I will confess to you forever, because you have acted.* A great confession, *because you have acted*. What have you *acted*, except these very things said above, that, like a fruitful olive tree in the house of God, I should hope in the mercy of God, forever and unto the age of the age? You have acted: the ungodly cannot justify himself. But who is it that justifies? *To him who believes*, it says, *in the one who justifies the ungodly*[^56]. *For what have you that you did not receive? And if you have received it, why do you glory as though you had not received it*[^57], as though you had it of yourself? Far be it that I should glory thus, says this man who is set against Doeg, who bears with Doeg on earth until that man removes from his dwelling and is uprooted from the land of the living. I do not glory as though I had not received, but I glory in God. And *I will confess to you because you have acted*, that is, because you have done it, not from my merits but from your mercy. For what have I done? If you recall, *I was formerly a blasphemer and a persecutor and insolent.*[^58] But you, what did you do? *But I obtained mercy, because I acted in ignorance*[^59]. *I will confess to you forever, because you have acted.*

**18.** *And I will await your name, because it is good.*[^60] The age is bitter, but your name is good. And even if there are certain sweet things in the age, yet they are digested with bitterness. Your name is set first, not only in greatness but also in goodness. For the unrighteous have told me of their delights, but not as your law, O Lord. For if there were nothing sweet to the martyrs, they would not have borne with even mind such great bitternesses of tribulations. Their bitterness was felt by anyone, but their sweetness no one could easily taste. The name of God, then, is good to those who love God above all delights. *I will await your name, because it is good.* And to whom do you prove that it is good? Give me a palate to which it is good. Praise honey as much as you can, exaggerate its sweetness with what words you can: a man who does not know what honey is, unless he has tasted it, does not know what you are saying. Therefore the psalm, inviting you rather to the experience, what does it say? *Taste and see that the Lord is sweet*[^61]. You do not wish to taste, and you say: It is good? What is good? If you have tasted, let it be found in your fruit, not in words alone, as though only in leaves, lest by the Lord's curse you deserve to wither like that fig tree. *Taste*, it says, *and see that the Lord is sweet. Taste and see*: then you will see, if you have tasted. But to a man who does not taste, how do you prove it? By praising the goodness of God's name, whatever you say, they are words: tasting is another thing. The words of his praise even the ungodly hear; but they do not taste how good it is—only the saints do. Accordingly this man, feeling the sweetness of God's name, and wishing to unfold it, and wishing to show it, and finding none to whom—for to the saints there is no need that he should show it, since they too taste and know; while the ungodly cannot feel what they do not wish to taste—what, then, does he do with the goodness of God's name? He at once withdrew himself from the crowds of the ungodly: *And I will await*, he says, *your name, because it is good in the sight of your saints.* Your name is good, but not in the sight of the ungodly: I know how sweet it is, but to those who have tasted.
