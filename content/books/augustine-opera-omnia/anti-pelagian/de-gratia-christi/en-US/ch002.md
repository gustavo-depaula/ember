# Book I: The Grace of Christ

Most sincere brethren, Albina, Pinianus, and Melania, we have dictated these things for you

**1. 1.** How greatly we rejoice over your welfare, both bodily and above all spiritual, most sincere brethren, beloved of God, Albina, Pinianus, and Melania, we leave for you to consider and to believe, since we cannot put it into words, so that we may rather now speak of those matters about which you consulted us. For since the bearer was in haste, and we were in the midst of our occupations, which at Carthage are far more pressing than anywhere else, we have dictated these things as best we could, so far as God has deemed it worthy to grant.

## Pelagius conceals himself by the ambiguity of his words.

**2. 2.** You wrote to me that you had dealt with Pelagius, urging that he condemn in writing whatever was said against him, and that he said, in your hearing: "Let him be anathema who either thinks or says that the grace of God, by which *Christ came into this world to save sinners*[^1], is not necessary, not only at every hour and at every moment, but also for our every single act; and let those who try to take this away suffer eternal punishment."

Whoever hears these words, and does not know his meaning, which he has expressed plainly enough in his books, supposes that he holds entirely what the truth holds—not in those writings which he says were foisted upon him uncorrected, or which he wholly disowns, but in those which he mentions in his letter sent to Rome. But whoever notices what he says more openly in them ought to hold these words also suspect. For even if he places the grace of God, by which *Christ came into the world to save sinners*, in the mere remission of sins, he can fit these words to that meaning by saying that it is therefore necessary at every hour, at every moment, and for our every single act, so that, always retaining in memory and recalling that our sins have been forgiven us, we may sin no further—aided not by any supply of strength, but by the powers of our own will, as we recall through each act what has been bestowed upon us by the remission of sins. Likewise, since they are accustomed to say that Christ furnished us help toward not sinning in that, by living justly and teaching justly, he left us an example, they can also fit these words to this, so as to say that grace of this kind is necessary to us at every moment and for our every single act—that is, that in our whole manner of life we may behold the example of the Lord's manner of life. But your faith perceives how this confession is to be distinguished from the confession of grace, which is the matter in question; and yet it can be concealed by the ambiguity of his words.

## Pelagius is recently shown to think nothing other than what he seemed to condemn.

**3. 3.** But what is surprising in this? For although Pelagius himself, in the episcopal proceedings[^2], without any objection condemned those who say that the grace of God and his help are not given for our individual acts, but lie in free choice, or in the law and teaching—whereby we supposed that all his evasions on this matter had been exhausted—and although he also condemned those who teach that the grace of God is given according to our merits: nevertheless, in the books which he published *In Defense of Free Choice*, which he mentions in the letter he sent to Rome, he is shown to think nothing other than what he seemed to have condemned. For he places the grace of God and the help by which we are aided toward not sinning either in nature and free choice, or in the law and teaching: namely, so that God is believed to help a person to *turn away from evil and do good*[^3] by revealing and showing what ought to be done, but not also by cooperating and breathing in love, so that he may do what he has come to know ought to be done[^4].

## Pelagius is recently shown to think nothing other than what he seemed to condemn.

**3. 4.** For when he establishes and distinguishes three things by which he says the divine commandments are fulfilled—possibility, will, and action: possibility, namely, by which a person can be just; will, by which he wills to be just; action, by which he is just—of these three he confesses that the first, that is, possibility, is given by the Creator of nature and is not in our power, but that we have it even if we are unwilling; whereas the other two, that is, will and action, he asserts to be ours, and so attributes them to us that he contends they are from us alone. Finally, he holds that the grace of God assists not these two, which he wants to be entirely ours—that is, will and action—but that which is not in our power and which is from God in us, namely, possibility: as though those things which are ours, that is, will and action, were so strong for turning away from evil and doing good that they need no divine help, while that which is from God in us—namely, possibility—were so weak that it must always be aided by the help of grace.

## Now hear his very words.

**4. 5.** But lest anyone perhaps say that we either do not rightly understand what he says, or with ill will twist into another sense what was not so meant, now hear his very words. "We," he says, "distinguish these three things in this way, and set them out as in a fixed order, dividing them. In the first place we put being able, in the second willing, in the third being. Being able we locate in nature, willing in choice, being in effect. That first, namely, being able, pertains properly to God, who conferred it on his creature; but the other two, that is, willing and being, are to be referred to the human person, since they descend from the fountainhead of choice. Therefore in the will and in good work the praise belongs to the human person—nay, both to the human person and to God, who gave the possibility of that very will and work, and who always aids that possibility by the help of his grace. But that a person can will the good and accomplish it belongs to God alone. So that one can exist even if these two have not existed; but these cannot exist without that. And so I am free to have neither a good will nor action, but in no way can I fail to have the possibility of good: it is in me even if I am unwilling, nor does nature ever admit any respite from it in this regard.

Some examples will make this meaning clearer to us. That we can see with our eyes is not ours, but that we see well or badly, this is ours; that we can speak is God's, but that we speak well or badly is ours. And to embrace all things in general: that we can do, say, or think every good thing belongs to him who gave this capacity, who aids this capacity; but that we speak well or badly is ours, because we can also turn all these things to evil. Hence—and this must be repeated often because of your slander—when we say that a person can be without sin, and by confessing the possibility he has received we praise God, who has bestowed this capacity on us, there is here no occasion for praising the human person, where the cause of God alone is being treated: for the discussion is not of willing, nor of being, but only of that which can be"[^5].

## The Apostle foresaw so long beforehand the future adversaries of the grace of God.

**5. 6.** Here, then, is the whole teaching of Pelagius in his third book *In Defense of Free Choice*, set out carefully in these very words, in which he took such care to distinguish with such subtlety those three things—the one being being able, the second being willing, the third being being, that is, possibility, will, and action—that whenever we read or hear him confessing the help of divine grace by which we turn away from evil and do good[^6], whether he places it in the law and teaching or wherever else, we may know what he is saying, and may not err by understanding him otherwise than he thinks. For we ought to know that he believes neither our will nor our action to be aided by divine help, but only the possibility of will and work, which alone, among these three, he affirms that we have from God—as though this were the weak thing, which God himself placed in nature, while the other two, which he wished to be ours, were so firm and strong and self-sufficient that they need no help from him; and therefore he does not help us to will, does not help us to act, but only helps us to be able to will and to act. The Apostle, on the contrary, says: *Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling*[^7]*.*

And that they might know that they are divinely aided not only in that they can work (for this they had already received in nature and in teaching), but also in that they do work, he did not say: For it is God who works in you the being able, as though they themselves already had both the willing and the working through themselves, and did not need his help in these two; but he said: *For it is God who works in you both to will and to accomplish*[^8]; or, as it reads in other codices, and especially the Greek ones, *both to will and to work*. See whether the Apostle did not, by the Holy Spirit, foresee so long beforehand the future adversaries of the grace of God, and say that God works in us these two things—that is, both to will and to work—which this man so wanted to be ours, as if they were not aided by the help of divine grace.

## What he said, Pelagius did not want to be understood according to sound doctrine.

**6. 7.** Nor let Pelagius here deceive the unwary and the simple, or even himself, in that, having said, "Therefore in the will and in good work the praise belongs to the human person," he as it were corrected himself and added, "nay, both to the human person and to God." For he did not say this because he wanted it understood, according to sound doctrine, that God works in us both to will and to work; but why he said it he shows plainly enough by at once adding, "who gave the possibility of that very will and work." That he places this possibility in nature is clear from his words above. But lest he seem to have said nothing about grace, he added, "and who always aids that very possibility by the help of his grace"; he did not say: That very will, or, That very working—which, if he said it, would not seem to be at variance with apostolic doctrine—but he said, "that very possibility," namely that one of the three which he located in nature, "he always aids by the help of his grace"; so that in will and action the praise belongs to both God and the human person not because a person wills in such a way that God nevertheless breathes into his will the ardor of love, and works in such a way that God nevertheless cooperates, without whose help what is the human person?—but he added God to this praise only because, unless there were the nature in which he founded us, by which we could will and act, we would neither will nor act.

## Pelagius places the grace of God in the law and teaching.

**6. 8.** But that he confesses this natural possibility to be aided by the grace of God—it is not here plain either what grace he means, or to what extent he holds that nature is aided by it; but, as can be understood from other passages where he speaks more clearly, he does not want anything else to be understood than the law and teaching, by which the natural possibility is aided.

**7. 8.** For in a certain passage he says: "Here the most ignorant of men suppose that we do injury to divine grace, because we say that it in no way perfects holiness in us without our will—as if God had commanded something of his grace, and did not also supply the help of his grace to those whom he commanded, so that what people are bidden to do by free choice they might more easily be able to fulfill through grace"[^9]. And as though about to explain what grace he means, he went on to add, saying: "Which we confess to be, not, as you suppose, in the law only, but also in the help of God." Who would not here desire that he show what he wants understood by grace? For on this account especially we ought to expect of him that he say this, which he does say, that he confesses grace to be not in the law only. But while we are held in suspense by this expectation, see what he added: "For God aids us," he says, "by his teaching and revelation, while he opens the eyes of our heart; while he shows us things to come, lest we be taken up with things present; while he lays bare the snares of the devil; while he illumines us by the manifold and ineffable gift of heavenly grace." Then, concluding his thought as if by a kind of release: "Does the one who says these things," he says, "seem to you to deny grace? Or does he confess both the free choice of the human person and the grace of God?" In all these things he did not depart from commending the law and teaching, diligently inculcating that this is the grace that aids, and carrying out what he had proposed when he said, "but we confess it to be in the help of God." Finally, he thought God's help was to be conveyed in manifold ways, by mentioning teaching and revelation, and the opening of the eyes of the heart, and the showing of things to come, and the uncovering of the devil's snares, and the illumination by the manifold and ineffable gift of heavenly grace: to this end, surely, that we might learn the divine precepts and promises. This, then, is to place the grace of God in the law and teaching.

## Law is one thing, grace another.

**8. 9.** From this, then, it appears that he confesses that grace by which God shows and reveals what we ought to do, not that by which he grants and aids us to do it—since the knowledge of the law, if the assistance of grace is lacking, avails rather to bring about the transgression of the commandment. *For where there is no law,* says the Apostle, *there is no transgression*[^10]; and: *I should not have known desire, if the law had not said, You shall not covet*[^11].

And on this account, law is one thing and grace another to such a degree that the law not only is of no profit, but even does the greatest harm, unless grace aids; and this is shown to be the usefulness of the law, that, by making them guilty of transgression, it compels those whom it makes guilty to flee for refuge to grace, to be set free and to be aided so that they may overcome evil desires. For it commands rather than helps; it teaches that there is a disease, but does not heal it; nay, by it rather is increased what is not healed, so that the medicine of grace may be sought more attentively and anxiously. For *the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life*[^12]. *For if a law had been given that could give life*, *then righteousness would indeed be by the law*[^13]*.* Yet insofar as the law does help, he adds and says: *But Scripture consigned all things under sin, that the promise might be given by faith in Jesus Christ to those who believe. So the law,* he says, *was our schoolmaster in Christ Jesus*[^14].

This very thing, then, is useful to the proud—to be more strictly and more manifestly confined under sin, lest they presume to do righteousness by free choice, as if by their own powers; but that *every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may become guilty before God, since by the law no flesh will be justified before him. For through the law comes the knowledge of sin: but now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from law, attested by the Law and the Prophets*[^15].

How, then, is it manifested apart from law, if it is attested through the law? It is therefore not manifested apart from law, but it is a righteousness apart from law, because it is the righteousness of God—that is, the one that comes to us not from the law, but from God: not the one that is feared by being known when he commands, but the one that is held fast by being loved when he gives, so that *he who boasts may boast in the Lord*[^16].

## In vain does Pelagius think he is hidden.

**9. 10.** What, then, is the point of this man reckoning the law and teaching to be the grace by which we are helped to work righteousness—grace which, however much it helps, helps to this end, that grace be sought? For no one can fulfill the law through the law. *For the fullness of the law is love*[^17]. But the *love of God* has been *poured out in our hearts*, not through the law, but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^18]. Accordingly, grace is shown through the law, so that the law may be fulfilled through grace. For what does it profit Pelagius that, in different words, he says the same thing, so that it may not be understood that he places in the law and teaching the grace by which he asserts the possibility of nature to be aided? But, as I judge, he is afraid of being understood for this reason: because he condemned those who say that the grace of God and his help are not given for individual acts, but lie in free choice, or in the law and teaching; and yet he thinks he is hidden, while in various phrasings he keeps turning over the meaning of the law and teaching.

## What grace Pelagius ought to confess, if he wants to be a Christian.

**10. 11.** And indeed in another passage too, after he had long asserted that a good will is brought about in us not by the help of God, but from our own selves, he set against himself a question from the Apostle's epistle, and said: "And how," he says, "will that saying of the Apostle stand: *For it is God who works in you both to will and to accomplish*[^19]*?*" Then, in order as it were to resolve this objection, which he saw to be vehemently contrary to his teaching, he went on to add: "He works in us to will what is good, to will what is holy, while he kindles us—given over to earthly desires and, after the manner of dumb animals, loving only things present—by the greatness of future glory and the promise of rewards; while by the revelation of wisdom he rouses to the desire of God a will that is stupefied; while he persuades us (which elsewhere you do not fear to deny) of everything that is good"[^20]. What could be plainer—that by the grace by which God works in us to will what is good he means nothing other than the law and teaching? For in the law and teaching of the holy Scriptures the greatness of future glory and of rewards is promised. To teaching belongs also that wisdom is revealed; to teaching belongs the persuading of everything that is good. And if there seems to be some difference between teaching and persuading, or rather exhorting, this too is nevertheless included under the generality of teaching, which is contained in any words or writings; for the holy Scriptures both teach and exhort, and in teaching and exhorting there can also be the working of a human being.

But we want him at some point to confess that grace by which the greatness of future glory is not only promised, but also believed and hoped for; and wisdom not only revealed, but also loved; and everything that is good not only urged, but also brought home as persuasion. *For not all have faith*[^21] who hear through the Scriptures the Lord promising the kingdom of heaven; nor are all persuaded to whom it is urged, that they should come to him who says: *Come to me, all you who labor*[^22]. But whose faith it is, and who are persuaded to come to him, he himself sufficiently showed when he said: *No one comes to me unless the Father who sent me draws him*[^23]; and a little later, when he was speaking of those who did not believe: *I told you,* he says, *that no one can come to me unless it has been granted him by my Father*[^24]. This is the grace Pelagius ought to confess, if he wants not only to be called, but also to be, a Christian.

## Not even the greatest revelations profit a person without grace.

**11. 12.** But what shall I say of the revelation of wisdom? For no one would easily hope to be able in this life to attain to the greatness of the revelations of the apostle Paul; and surely, in them, what else is to be believed to have been revealed to him, except what pertained to wisdom? And yet he says: *So that I might not be exalted by the greatness of my revelations, there was given me a thorn in my flesh, an angel of Satan to buffet me. Concerning this I three times asked the Lord that it might depart from me; and he said to me, My grace is sufficient for you; for power is made perfect in weakness*[^25]. Without doubt, if there had already been in the Apostle the highest love, to which nothing could be added—the love that could in no way be puffed up—would an angel of Satan be necessary, by whose buffeting the elation that could arise in the greatness of revelations might be checked? And what else is elation but a being puffed up? And surely it was most truly said of love: *Love is not jealous, is not puffed up*[^26]. This love, then, was assuredly being increased from day to day even in so great an Apostle, so long as his inner self was being renewed from day to day[^27], to be perfected without doubt where it could no longer be puffed up. But then his mind was still where it might be puffed up by the greatness of revelation, until it should be filled with the solid building of love: for he had not yet, by arriving, laid hold of that toward which he was running by making progress.

## Whoever does not confess himself weak is not perfected.

**12. 13.** And so, to him who was unwilling to endure the trouble by which his elation was being held in check, before there was in him the final and highest perfection of love, it is most rightly said: *My grace is sufficient for you; for power is made perfect in weakness: in weakness*[^28], namely—not, as he supposes, "of the flesh only," but both of the flesh and of the mind: because the mind too was weak in comparison with that highest perfection, and to it, lest it be exalted, the thorn in the flesh, the angel of Satan[^29], was accordingly understood to have been given—although, in comparison with carnal or animal persons, who do not yet perceive *the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^30], it was most firm.

Wherefore, if *power is made perfect in weakness*[^31], whoever does not confess himself weak is not perfected. But this grace by which *power is made perfect in weakness*[^32] leads to the highest perfection and glorification those who are predestined and called according to his purpose[^33]. By this grace it is brought about not only that we may know the things to be done, but also that we may do the things known; and not only that we may believe the things to be loved, but also that we may love the things believed.

## Thus God teaches the predestined, at once granting them both to know what they should do and to do what they know.

**13. 14.** If this grace is to be called teaching, let it surely be called so in such a way that God is believed to infuse it more deeply and inwardly, with ineffable sweetness, not only through those who plant and water from without, but also through himself who, hidden, supplies his own increase[^34], so that he not only shows the truth, but also imparts love. For thus God teaches those who are called according to his purpose[^35], at once granting both to know what they should do and to do what they know.

Hence the Apostle speaks thus to the Thessalonians: *But concerning brotherly love you have no need for anyone to write to you; for you yourselves have been taught by God to love one another.* And to prove that they had been taught by God, he added: *For indeed you do this toward all the brethren in all Macedonia*[^36]. As though this were the most certain sign that you have been taught by God, if you have done what you have been taught. In this way all who are *called according to his purpose* are, as it is written in the Prophets, *taught by God*[^37].

But whoever indeed knows what ought to be done, and does not do it, has not yet learned from God according to grace, but according to the law; not according to the spirit, but according to the letter. Although many seem to do what the law commands, out of fear of punishment, not out of love of righteousness—which the Apostle calls his own righteousness which is from the law, as though it were commanded, not given.

But if it is given, it is called not our righteousness, but God's: because it becomes ours in such a way that it is in us from God. For he says: *That I may be found in him, not having my own righteousness, which is from the law, but that which is through faith in Jesus, the righteousness from God*[^38].

So great, then, is the distance between law and grace that, although it is not doubted that the law is from God, yet the righteousness which is from the law is not from God; but the righteousness which is brought to fulfillment through grace is from God. Because the righteousness which comes about on account of the law's curse is called righteousness from the law; the righteousness which is given through the gift of grace is called righteousness from God: so that the commandment may be not terrifying but sweet, as is prayed in the Psalm: *You are gracious, O Lord, and in your graciousness teach me your righteousness*[^39]:

that is, that I may not be servilely compelled by dread of punishment to be under the law, but may with free love delight to be with the law. For the one who acts willingly makes the precept free. And in this way whoever learns does, entirely, whatever he has learned ought to be done.

## Whoever does not come, has assuredly not learned either.

**14. 15.** Of this manner of teaching the Lord too says: *Everyone who has heard from my Father and has learned comes to me*[^40]. Of the one, then, who has not come, it is not rightly said: He has indeed heard and learned that he should come, but he is unwilling to do what he has learned. It is plainly not rightly said of this manner of teaching by which God teaches through grace. For if, as the Truth says, *everyone who has learned comes*, then whoever does not come has assuredly not learned either. But who does not see that a person both comes and does not come by the choice of the will? Yet this choice can be alone, if he does not come; but it cannot but be aided, if he comes—and aided in such a way that he not only knows what is to be done, but also does what he has come to know. And on this account, when God teaches, not through the letter of the law but through the grace of the Spirit, he teaches in such a way that what each one has learned he not only sees by knowing, but also desires by willing, and brings to fulfillment by doing. And in this divine manner of teaching the will itself and the working itself are aided, not the natural possibility of willing and working alone.

For if only our being able were aided by this grace, the Lord would say: Everyone who has heard from the Father and has learned can come to me. But he did not say it thus; rather: *Everyone who has heard,* he says, *from the Father and has learned comes to me.* Pelagius places being able to come in nature, or even, as he has just begun to say, in grace, however he conceives of it, "by which the possibility itself," as he says, "is aided"[^41]; but coming is now in will and work. It does not, however, follow that one who can come also does come, unless he wills it and does it. But everyone who has learned from the Father not only can come, but does come: where now there is both the advancement of the possibility, and the disposition of the will, and the effect of the action.

## Examples by which Pelagius teaches more plainly what he thinks.

**15. 16.** What, then, do his examples mean, except that they have indeed made his meaning clearer to us, as he promised—not that we should think as they say, but that we may more plainly and openly recognize what he himself thought? "That we can see with our eyes," he says, "is not ours: but that we see well or badly, this is ours"[^42]. Let the Psalm answer him, where it is said to God: *Turn away my eyes, that they may not see vanity*[^43]. And even if this is said of the eyes of the mind, from there surely it proceeds to these eyes of the flesh, to see either well or badly: not in the way that those who gaze with sound eyes are said to see well, and the bleary-eyed badly; but to see well so as to give aid, to see badly so as to lust. For although through these outer eyes there is seen both the poor person to whom aid is given and the woman who is desired, nevertheless from the inner self there proceeds, toward seeing well or badly, either mercy or lust. Why, then, is it said to God: *Turn away my eyes, that they may not see vanity*? Why is that sought which pertains to our power, if God does not aid the will?

## I seem to myself to see what he feared.

**16. 17.** "That we can speak," he says, "is God's: but that we speak well or badly is ours"[^44]. This is not the teaching of him who speaks well. *For it is not you who speak,* he says, *but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^45]. "And to embrace all things in general," he says, "that we can do, say, or think every good thing belongs to him who gave this capacity, who aids this capacity"[^46]. See, here too he repeats the earlier meaning, that of those three—that is, possibility, will, action—only the possibility is aided. Then, adding so as to complete what he intends: "But that we do, or speak, or think well," he says, "is ours"[^47]. He has forgotten what he had, as it were, corrected above, where, having said, "Therefore in the will and in good work the praise belongs to the human person," he added and said, "nay, both to the human person and to God, who gave the possibility of that very will and work"[^48]. Why, even in these examples, did he not recall this, so as at least at the end of them to say: That we can do, say, or think every good thing belongs to him who gave this capacity, who aids this capacity; but that we do, or speak, or think well is both ours and his? He did not say this; but, unless I am mistaken, I seem to myself to see what he feared.

## If we are praised together with God in good works, it does not follow that we are blamed together with God in evil works.

**17. 18.** For when he wanted to show why it is ours: "Because," he says, "all these things we can also turn to evil"[^49]. This, then, is what he feared, so that he would not say, it is both ours and God's, lest it be answered to him: If what we do, speak, think well is therefore both ours and God's, because he gave us this capacity, then also what we do, speak, think evil is both ours and God's, because he gave that capacity for both—and thus, God forbid, just as we are praised together with God in good works, so we are blamed together with him in evil ones. For that possibility which he gave makes us able to do good things as much as evil ones.

## Two trees, the good and the bad, are two persons, the good and the bad.

**18. 19.** Of this possibility Pelagius speaks thus in the first book *In Defense of Free Choice*: "We have," he says, "the possibility of either side implanted in us by God, like, so to speak, a certain fruitful and fertile root, which, according to the human will, may bring forth and bear diverse things, and which can, at the choice of its own cultivator, either be bright with the flower of virtues or bristle with the thorns of vices"[^50]. Here, not seeing what he says, he establishes one and the same root of goods and evils, against evangelical truth and apostolic doctrine. For the Lord says that neither can a good tree bear bad fruits, nor a bad one good fruits[^51]; and when the apostle Paul says that desire is the root of all evils[^52], he surely admonishes us to understand that love is the root of all goods. Hence, if two trees, the good and the bad, are two persons, the good and the bad, what is the good person but one of good will, that is, a tree of a good root? And what is the bad person but one of bad will, that is, a tree of a bad root? For the fruits of these roots and trees are the deeds, words, and thoughts which proceed, the good from a good will and the evil from an evil one.

## A person makes a good tree when he receives the grace of God.

**19. 20.** But a person makes a good tree when he receives the grace of God. For he does not make himself good out of evil by himself; but from him and through him and in him who is always good—and it is necessary that he be aided by the same grace, without which he can do nothing good, not only that the tree may be good, but also that it may bear good fruits. For he himself cooperates in the fruit in good trees, who both waters and cultivates from without through any minister, and through himself gives the increase from within[^53].

But a person makes a bad tree when he makes himself bad, when he falls away from the unchangeable good: for from that falling-away is the origin of the bad will. And this falling-away does not initiate any other, bad nature, but corrupts the nature which was created good. But once the corruption is healed, no evil remains; because the corruption was indeed present in the nature, but the corruption was not the nature.

## Love the root of goods is one thing, desire the root of evils another.

**20. 21.** That possibility, then, is not, as this man supposes, one and the same root of goods and evils. For love the root of goods is one thing, desire the root of evils another[^54]; and they differ from each other as much as virtue and vice. But that possibility is plainly capable of either root; because a person can have not only love, by which he is a good tree, but can also have desire, by which he is a bad tree. But the desire of a person, which is a vice, has as its author a human being, or a deceiver of the human being, not the Creator of the human being. For it is itself *the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the ambition of the world, which is not from the Father, but is from the world*[^55]. And who does not know that Scripture is accustomed to call by the name "world" those by whom this world is inhabited?

## Love is in us from God, not from ourselves.

**21. 22.** But love, which is a virtue, is in us from God, not from ourselves, as Scripture testifies, which says: *Love is from God; and everyone who loves is born of God and knows God, because God is love*[^56]. According to this love it is better understood what is said: *He who is born of God does not sin*[^57]; and that *he cannot sin*[^58]. Because the love according to which he is born of God *does not deal wrongly, nor think evil*[^59]. When, therefore, a person sins, he sins not according to love, but according to desire, according to which he is not born of God: since that possibility, as has been said, is capable of either root. When, then, Scripture says: *Love is from God*[^60]; or, what is more: *God is love*[^61]; when the apostle John cries out most openly: *See what kind of love the Father has given us, that we should be called, and should be, children of God*[^62]: why does this man, hearing *God is love,* still contend that of those three we have only the possibility from God, but have a good will and good action from ourselves? As though a good will were something other than love, which Scripture cries out is in us from God, and given by the Father that we might be his children.

## Pelagius contradicts himself.

**22. 23.** But perhaps preceding merits of ours brought it about that it was given to us—as this man thinks of the grace of God in that book which he wrote to the holy virgin, which he also mentioned in the letter he sent to Rome[^63]. For there, having inserted the testimony of the apostle James, where he says: *Be subject to God; but resist the devil, and he will flee from you*[^64], he adds and says: "He shows how we ought to resist the devil—namely, if we are subject to God, and by doing his will earn the divine grace, and more easily resist the wicked spirit by the help of the Holy Spirit"[^65].

See with how truthful a heart he condemned, in the Palestinian ecclesiastical judgment, those who say that the grace of God is given according to our merits! Or do we still doubt that he thinks this and preaches it most openly? How, then, was that confession in the episcopal examination truthful? Or had he perhaps already written this book, where he says most openly that grace is given according to our merits—which in the Eastern synod he condemned without any objection? Let him confess, then, that he once held it thus, but no longer holds it, so that we might rejoice most openly at his correction. But as it is, when among other things this too had been charged against him, he answered: "Whether these are Caelestius's, let those see to it who say they are Caelestius's: I, for my part, never held it thus, but I anathematize those who hold it thus." How did he never hold it thus, if he had previously composed this book? Or how does he anathematize those who hold it thus, if he composed this book afterward?

## What freedom can avail toward grace.

**22. 24.** But lest he perhaps answer that he meant here, "by doing the will of God we earn the divine grace," in the sense in which grace is added to the faithful who live devoutly, by which they may resist the tempter bravely, while they had nevertheless also first received grace, that they might do the will of God—lest, then, he perhaps answer thus, hear his other words on this matter. "He who runs to the Lord," he says, "and desires to be ruled by him—that is, suspends his own will upon his will; who, by clinging to him continually, becomes, according to the Apostle, one spirit with him[^66]—accomplishes this by nothing other than the freedom of choice"[^67]. See how great a thing he said is accomplished by nothing other than the freedom of choice; and on this account he reckons that we cling to God without the help of God; for this is what "by nothing other than the freedom of choice" means: so that when we have clung to him without his help, then, now that we have clung, we may even earn to be aided.

**23. 24.** For he goes on and says, "He who uses it well"—that is, who uses the freedom of choice well—"so hands himself over wholly to God," he says, "and mortifies all his own will, that he can say with the Apostle: *I live, yet now not I, but Christ lives in me*[^68]: and he places his heart in the hand of God, that he may turn it wherever he wills"[^69]. Truly a great help of divine grace, that God himself may turn our heart wherever he wills. But this so great a help, as this man foolishly supposes, we earn then, when, without any help, by nothing other than the freedom of choice, we run to the Lord, desire to be ruled by him, suspend our will upon his will, and by clinging to him continually are made one spirit with him[^70].

These so vast goods, namely, we accomplish, according to this man, by nothing other than the freedom of choice, so that by these preceding merits we so obtain his grace that he turns our heart wherever he wills. How, then, is it grace, if it is not given freely? How is it grace, if it is repaid as a debt? How does the Apostle speak truly: *Not from yourselves, but it is the gift of God; not from works, lest anyone be exalted*[^71]: and again: *But if by grace,* he says, *then no longer from works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^72]? How, I say, is this true, if such great works precede that make for us the merit of obtaining grace, so that it is not given to us gratuitously, but repaid as a debt? Is it, then, that in order to arrive at the help of God, one runs to God without the help of God; and that, in order to be aided by God by clinging to God, we cling to God without being aided by God? What greater, or what comparable, will grace itself be able to bestow on a person, if without it he could already be made one spirit with the Lord[^73], by nothing other than the freedom of choice?

## That king of the Assyrians.

**24. 25.** But I would wish that this man would say whether that king of the Assyrians, whose bed the holy woman Esther abhorred[^74], when *he was seated on the throne of his kingdom, and was clothed in all the robe of his splendor, all variegated with gold and precious stones, and was very terrible; and, lifting up his face inflamed with glory, he looked upon her like a bull in the onrush of his indignation; and the queen was afraid, and her color changed through faintness, and she sank down upon the head of the maid who went before her*[^75]:

I would wish, then, that this man would tell us whether that king had already run to the Lord, and had desired to be ruled by him, and had suspended his will upon his will, and, by clinging to him continually, had been made one spirit with him[^76], by nothing other than the freedom of choice; whether he had handed himself over wholly to God, and had mortified all his own will, and had placed his heart in the hand of God. I think it is not folly, but madness, in anyone who thinks these things of that king, such as he then was: and yet God turned and changed his indignation into gentleness[^77]. And who does not see that it is far greater to turn and change indignation from its opposite into gentleness, than to make a heart not yet seized by either disposition, but standing midway between the two, incline toward something? Let them read, then, and understand, let them perceive and confess, that not by law and teaching sounding from without, but by an inner and hidden, marvelous and ineffable power, God works in the hearts of human beings not only true revelations, but good wills as well.

## The grace of God toward us is to be proclaimed not only for the possibility of a good will and a good work, but also for the good will and working itself.

**25. 26.** Let Pelagius, then, now cease both to deceive himself and others by disputing against the grace of God. Not for one of those three things—that is, for the possibility of a good will and work—but also for the good will and working itself, is the grace of God toward us to be proclaimed. For he defines that possibility as availing for both: and yet our sins are not on that account to be attributed to God, just as, on account of the same possibility, he wants our good works to be attributed to him. Let not the help of divine grace be commended only because it aids the natural possibility. Let him cease to say, "That we can do, say, or think every good thing belongs to him who gave this capacity, who aids this capacity: but that we do, or speak, or think well is ours"[^78]. Let him cease, I say, to say these things. For God not only gave and aids our being able, but also *works in us to will and to work*[^79]. Not because we do not will, or we do not act: but because without his help we neither will anything good nor do it.

For how is it said, "That we can act well is God's; but that we act is ours"[^80], when the Apostle says that he prays to God for those to whom he was writing, that they may do nothing evil, and that they may do what is good? For he does not say: "*We pray* that you may be able to do nothing evil"; but: *that you may do nothing evil,* nor: "That you may be able to do good"; but: *that you may do good*[^81]. Since of those of whom it is written: *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are children of God*[^82], surely, that they may do what is good, they are led by him who is good. How does Pelagius say, "That we can speak well is God's: that we speak well is ours," when the Lord says: *It is the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^83]*?*

For he does not say: "It is not you who have given yourselves to be able to speak well," but he says: *It is not you who speak*. Nor does he say: "It is *the Spirit of your Father* who gives you, or has given you, to be able to speak well"; but he says: *who speaks in you,* not signifying the advancement of the possibility, but expressing the effect of the cooperation. How does the puffed-up asserter of free choice say, "That we can think well is God's; but that we think well is ours"? To whom the humble preacher of grace answers: *Not that we are competent to think anything of ourselves, as if from our own selves; but our sufficiency is from God*[^84]. For he does not say: "To be able to think"; but, *to think.*

## What good merits could we then have, when we did not love God?

**26. 27.** Let Pelagius also openly confess this grace of God, manifest in the divine words, and let him not cover over with most shameless shame that he has so long thought against it, but disclose it with most salutary sorrow; so that the holy Church may not be troubled by his stubborn obstinacy, but may rejoice at his truthful correction. Let him distinguish knowledge and love, as they are to be distinguished. Because *knowledge puffs up, love builds up*[^85]. And then knowledge does not puff up, when love builds up. And although each is a gift of God, but the one lesser and the other greater, let him not so exalt our righteousness above the praise of our justifier that he attributes the lesser of these two to divine help, but usurps the greater for human choice. And if he agrees that we receive love by the grace of God, let him not think it to be as though any good merits of ours preceded. For what good merits could we then have, when we did not love God?

For in order that we might receive the love by which we should love, we were loved when we did not yet have it. This the apostle John says most openly: *Not that we have loved God, but that he loved us*[^86]*.* And elsewhere: *Let us love,* he says, *because he first loved us*[^87]. Most excellently and most truly. For we would have nothing whereby to love him, unless we received this from him, when he first loved us. But what good would we do, unless we loved? Or how do we not do good, if we love?

For although the commandment of God sometimes seems to be done not by those who love, but by those who fear, nevertheless, where there is no love, no good work is reckoned, nor is it rightly called a good work: because *everything that is not from faith is sin*[^88]; and *faith works through love*[^89]. And on this account, let whoever wants to confess truly so confess the grace of God, by which *the love of God* is poured out *in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^90], that he does not doubt that absolutely nothing good—nothing that pertains to godliness and true righteousness—can be done without it. Not as this man does, who, when he says, "grace is given for this reason, that what is commanded by God may more easily be fulfilled"[^91], shows sufficiently what he thinks of it—namely, that even without it, although less easily, what is divinely commanded can nevertheless be done.

## Without the help of the Holy Spirit, although less easily, the wicked spirit is nevertheless resisted, says Pelagius.

**27. 28.** Indeed, in the book to the holy virgin, which I also mentioned above[^92], when he says, "we earn the divine grace, and more easily resist the wicked spirit by the help of the Holy Spirit"[^93], he surely signifies what he holds. For why did he insert this word, that is, "more easily"? Was the sense not complete: "that we may resist the wicked spirit by the help of the Holy Spirit"? But who does not understand how great a damage he did by this addition? Wanting, surely, to have it believed that the powers of nature are so great—which by exalting he casts down—that even without the help of the Holy Spirit, although less easily, the wicked spirit is nevertheless in some way resisted.

## We have a free choice strong and firm for not sinning, says Pelagius.

**28. 29.** Likewise in the first book *In Defense of Free Choice*: "But although we have in us," he says, "a free choice for not sinning so strong, so firm, which the Creator implanted generally in human nature, again, out of his inestimable kindness, we are fortified by his daily help"[^94]. What need is there of this help, if free choice is so strong, so firm, for not sinning? But here too he wants it understood that the help is for this, that what he thinks can nevertheless be done apart from grace, although less easily, may be done more easily through grace.

## What people are bidden to do by free choice they do more easily through grace, says Pelagius.

**29. 30.** Likewise in the same book, in another passage: "So that what people are bidden to do by free choice," he says, "they may be able to fulfill more easily through grace." Take away "more easily," and the sense is not only complete, but even sound, if it is put thus: "So that what people are bidden to do by free choice they may be able to fulfill through grace." But when "more easily" is added, it is suggested, by a tacit implication, that the fulfillment of a good work can come about even without the grace of God. This meaning is refuted by him who says: *Without me you can do nothing*[^95].

## Nowhere have I found Pelagius and Caelestius confessing the grace of God in the way it ought to be confessed.

**30. 31.** Let him amend all these things, lest, if human weakness has erred in the depth of great matters, there be added to this error also a diabolical falsehood or animosity, whether by denying what he thought or by defending what he wrongly thought, once he has come to know, through the plain truth set before him, that he ought not to have thought it. For that grace by which we are justified—that is, by which *the love of God* is poured out *in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^96]—in the writings of Pelagius and Caelestius, whatever I have been able to read, I have nowhere found them confessing in the way it ought to be confessed[^97]. Indeed, I have never noticed them acknowledging, as they ought to be acknowledged, the children of the promise, of whom the Apostle says: *It is not the children of the flesh who are children of God, but the children of the promise are reckoned as the offspring*[^98]. For what God promises, we do not do through choice or nature, but he himself does through grace.

## What Pelagius says in the letter to Innocent.

**30. 32.** For, to say nothing for the moment of Caelestius's little works, or of his pamphlets which he submitted in the ecclesiastical proceedings—all of which, together with other letters we have judged necessary, we have taken care to send to you, so that, having carefully inspected them all, you may observe that he does not place the grace of God, by which we are aided either to turn away from evil or to do good[^99], apart from the natural choice of the will, anywhere except in the law and teaching; so that he asserts that even prayers themselves are necessary for this, that it may be shown to a person what he should desire and love—to leave these things aside for the moment, then: Pelagius himself recently sent both a letter and a statement of his faith to Rome, writing to Pope Innocent of blessed memory, whom he did not know to be dead. In this letter, then, he says there are two things of which people are trying to defame him: one, that he denies infants the sacrament of Baptism, and promises to some the kingdom of heaven apart from the redemption of Christ; the other, that he so says a person can avoid sin as to exclude the help of God, and so trusts in free choice as to repudiate the assistance of grace[^100]. But concerning the Baptism of infants—though he concedes it ought to be given to them—how perversely he thinks against the Christian and Catholic truth, this is not the place to discuss more carefully: for now what we have undertaken concerning the assistance of grace must be carried through. So let us see what he answers here too to what he has set forth. For, to leave aside his spiteful complaints about his enemies, when it came to the point he spoke thus:

## The words of Pelagius do not suffice to resolve the question.

**31. 33.** "See," he says, "let this letter clear me before Your Blessedness, in which we say, purely and simply, that we have a free choice, whole and entire, for sinning and for not sinning—which in all good works is always aided by divine help"[^101]. You surely discern, by the understanding the Lord has given you, that these words of his do not suffice to resolve the question. For we still ask by what help he says free choice is aided, lest perhaps, as he is wont, he wants the law and teaching to be understood. For if you ask why he said *always*, he will be able to answer, Because it is written: *And on his law he will meditate day and night*[^102]. Then, when he had inserted certain things about the condition of the human person and his natural possibility for sinning and for not sinning, he added, saying: "This power of free choice we say is in all generally—in Christians, Jews, and Gentiles. In all there is free choice equally through nature, but in Christians alone is it aided by grace"[^103]. Again we ask, by what grace? And again he will be able to answer, By the Christian law and teaching.

## Pelagius says that grace is given to Christians according to their merits.

**31. 34.** Then, whatever grace he means, he says it is given to Christians themselves according to their merits—although he already condemned, in Palestine, as I mentioned above, by that splendid clearing of himself, those who say this. For these are his words: "In those," he says, "the good of their condition is naked and unarmed"; he means in those who are not Christians. Then, weaving in the rest: "But in those," he says, "who belong to Christ, it is fortified by the help of Christ." You see that it is still uncertain by what help, according to what we have said above. But he goes on still about those who are not Christians, and says: "They are therefore to be judged and condemned, because, although they have free choice through which they could come to faith and earn the grace of God, they make bad use of the freedom granted them. But these are to be rewarded, who, by using free choice well, merit the grace of the Lord, and keep his commandments"[^104].

Surely it is manifest that he says grace is given according to merits—whatever grace, and of whatever kind, he means, though he does not openly express it. For when he says that those are to be rewarded who use free choice well, and on that account merit the grace of the Lord, he confesses that a debt is repaid to them. Where, then, is that saying of the Apostle: *Justified freely by his grace*[^105]? Where also is that: *By grace you have been saved*[^106]? And lest they should suppose it was through works, he added, *through faith.* And again, lest they should think that faith itself was to be attributed to themselves without the grace of God: *And this,* he says, *not from yourselves, but it is the gift of God*[^107]. Surely, then, that from which begins everything that we are said to receive by merit, we receive without merit—that is, faith itself. Or if it is denied that it is given, what is that which is said: *As God has apportioned to each a measure of faith*[^108]?

But if it is said to be given in such a way that it is repaid to merits, not bestowed, what is that which he says again: *To you it has been granted for Christ's sake not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for him*[^109]? For he testified that both are granted—both that one believes in Christ and that one suffers for Christ.

But these men so attach faith to free choice that grace seems to be repaid to faith—not gratuitous, but owed: and on this account it is now not even grace, because unless it is gratuitous, it is not grace.

## What Pelagius says in the book of his faith.

**32. 35.** But from this letter Pelagius wants the reader to pass to the book of his faith, of which he made mention to you: in which he discussed many things about which he was not being questioned. But let us look at the very things on account of which we are dealing with them. For when he had brought his disputation—as long as he wished—from the unity of the Trinity all the way to the resurrection of the flesh, which no one was asking of him, he says: "And we hold one Baptism, which we say is to be celebrated with the same words of the sacrament in infants as also in adults"[^110].

This, certainly, you said you also heard from him in person: but what does it profit that he says the sacrament of Baptism is celebrated in infants with the same words as in adults, when it is the reality, not the words alone, that we ask of him? More to the point is what he answered, as you write, when you questioned him with his own mouth: that "infants receive Baptism for the remission of sins." For here too he did not say, in the words of the remission of sins; but he confessed that they are baptized for that very remission: and yet if you asked him what sin is believed to be remitted to them, he would contend that they have no sin at all.

## The gift of knowledge and the gift of love.

**33. 36.** Who would believe that under this seemingly manifest confession a contrary meaning lay hidden, had not Caelestius disclosed it? He, in his pamphlet which he submitted at Rome in the ecclesiastical proceedings, confessed that "infants too are baptized for the remission of sins," and denied that "any of them has original sin"[^111]. But now let us attend not to the Baptism of infants, but rather to the assistance of grace—even in the statement of his faith which Pelagius sent to Rome, what he thought. "We so confess free choice," he says, "that we say we always need the help of God"[^112]. See, again we ask by what help he confesses we need: and again we find it ambiguous, since he can answer that he means the Christian law or teaching, by which that natural possibility is aided. But we look in their confession for that grace of which the Apostle says: *For God did not give us a spirit of fear, but of power and love and self-control*[^113]. But it does not follow that one who has the gift of knowledge, by which he knows what he should do, has also that of love, so that he may do it.

## Pelagius is always silent about true grace.

**34. 37.** Those books or writings of his too, which he mentions in the same letter that he sent to Pope Innocent of holy memory, I have read—except for one letter, which he says he sent, a brief one, to the holy bishop Constantius: nor have I been able to find anywhere that he confesses this grace, by which not only the natural possibility of will and action—which he says we have even if we neither will nor do good—but the will and action itself is aided by the supply of the Holy Spirit.

## The letter of Pelagius to Paulinus.

**35. 38.** "Let them read," he says, "that letter which we wrote to the holy man, bishop Paulinus, nearly twelve years ago, which, in perhaps three hundred lines, confesses nothing other than the grace and help of God, and that we can do absolutely nothing good without God"[^114]. This letter, then, I have read, and I found that through almost the whole of it he dwells on nothing but the faculty and possibility of nature, and almost there alone places the grace of God: but Christian grace he touches on with such brevity, with the mere mention of the name, that he seems to have done nothing but fear to be silent about it. Whether, however, he wants it understood as in the remission of sins, or also in the teaching of Christ, where there is too the example of his manner of life—which he does in some passages of his little works—or whether he believes some help for acting well to be joined to nature and teaching through the inspiration of a most ardent and most luminous love, does not appear at all.

## The letter of Pelagius to Constantius.

**36. 39.** "Let them read also," he says, "the one to the holy bishop Constantius, where briefly indeed, but plainly, I joined the grace and help of God to the free choice of the human person"[^115]. This letter, as I said above, I have not read: but if it is not unlike the others which he mentions as known to me, it too does not have what we seek.

## The letter of Pelagius to Demetrias.

**37. 40.** "Let them read also," he says, "the one we composed in the East to the holy virgin of Christ, Demetrias, and they will find that we so praise the nature of the human person that we always add the help of God's grace." This one, indeed, I have read, and it had nearly persuaded me that he confesses that grace which is in question—although in many passages of his little work he seemed to contradict himself: but when other things came into my hands, which he composed later and more fully, I saw how he was able even there to name grace, hiding under an ambiguous generality what he thought, and yet by the word "grace" breaking the ill will and avoiding offense. For in its very beginning, where he says, "Let us sweat over the work set before us, nor distrust the mediocrity of our talent, which we believe to be aided both by the faith of the mother and the merit of the virgin"[^116], he had seemed to me to confess the grace by which we are aided to do something, and I had not noticed that he could have placed this in the mere revelation of teaching.

## Certain words of the same letter.

**37. 41.** Likewise in the same work, in another passage: "But if even without God," he says, "people show what kind they were made by God, see what Christians can do, whose nature has been restored for the better through Christ, and who are aided also by the help of divine grace"[^117]. By "nature restored for the better" he wants the remission of sins to be understood: which he showed sufficiently in another passage in this very book, where he says, "Even those who by long practice of sinning have in a way grown hardened can be restored through penance"[^118]. But the help of divine grace he can place here too in the revelation of teaching.

## Other words of the same letter.

**38. 42.** Likewise elsewhere in the same letter: "For if even before the law," he says, "as we have said, and long before the coming of our Lord and Savior, certain people are reported to have lived justly and holily, how much more must it be believed that we can do this after the illumination of his coming—we who have been restored through the grace of Christ and reborn into a better person, we who, expiated and cleansed by his blood, and stirred by his example to the perfection of righteousness, ought to be better than those who lived before the law"[^119]? See how here too, in different words indeed, but nevertheless in the remission of sins and in the example of Christ, he has placed the assistance of grace.

Then he adds and says: "Better even than they were under the law, as the Apostle says: *Sin will not have dominion over you; for you are not under law, but under grace*[^120]. And since," he says, "we have spoken sufficiently, as I think, on this point, let us now form the perfect virgin, who, always kindled by both, may bear witness by the holiness of her conduct to the good of nature and of grace alike"[^121]. And in these words you ought to notice that he so wanted to conclude what he was saying that we should understand the good of nature to be that which we received when we were created, but the good of grace to be when we behold the example of Christ: as though, for this reason, sin was not pardoned to those who were or are under the law, because they either did not have the example of Christ, or do not believe it.

## Certain words of Pelagius in his book In Defense of Free Choice.

**39. 43.** But that he holds this view, his other words also show—not in this book, but in the third book *In Defense of Free Choice*, where, speaking to the one against whom he disputes, since that one had set down the words of the Apostle saying: *It is not what I will that I do*; and: *I see another law in my members at war with the law of my mind*[^122]*,* and the rest that is said there, he answered and said: "For what you desire to understand of the Apostle, all ecclesiastical men assert that he said in the person of a sinner still placed under the law, who was held, as it were, by a certain necessity of sinning through excessive habit of vices, and who, although he sought the good by will, was nevertheless by habit hurled into evil. But in the person of one man," he says, "he designates the people still sinning under the old law; whom he says is to be freed from this evil of habit through Christ, who first remits to those who believe in him all their sins through Baptism, then by imitation of himself stirs them to perfect holiness, and overcomes the habit of vices by the example of virtues"[^123].

See how he wants those who sin under the law to be aided, so that, justified through the grace of Christ, they may be freed—as though the law alone did not suffice for them, on account of the excessive habit of sinning, unless there be added that of Christ—not the inspiration of love through the Holy Spirit, but the example of his virtue, to be beheld and imitated in the evangelical teaching. And certainly here there was the greatest occasion to express what grace he meant, where the Apostle so concluded the very passage to which he responds that he said: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^124]. Since this man places this not in the help of his virtue, but in the example of his imitation, what more ought we to hope of him, wherever he mentions the name of grace under an ambiguous generality?

## Certain other words of the letter of Pelagius to Demetrias.

**40. 44.** Likewise in the same book to the holy virgin, on which we have already discoursed above, where he says: "Let us be subject to God, and by doing his will earn the divine grace, and more easily resist the wicked spirit by the help of the Holy Spirit"[^125]. In these words of his it is certainly manifest that he so wants us to be aided by the grace of the Holy Spirit, not because without it, even by the mere possibility of nature, we cannot resist the tempter, but that we may resist more easily. And yet this assistance, of whatever sort and however small, he, it is credible, places in this: that knowledge is added to us by the Spirit revealing through teaching, which we either cannot, or can only with difficulty, have through nature. These are the things which I have been able to notice in the book he wrote to the virgin of Christ, where he seems to confess grace: and what they are like, you surely discern.

## In no way does Pelagius depart from the ambiguity of words.

**41. 45.** "Let them read also," he says, "my recent little work, which we were recently compelled to publish *In Defense of Free Choice*; and they will recognize how unjustly they have been eager to defame us with the denial of grace, we who through almost the whole text of the work itself confess perfectly and entirely both free choice and grace"[^126]. There are four books of this work, and these I have read, and from them I have taken what I proposed to treat and examine, and as best I could I have dealt with it, before we came to these letters of his which were sent to Rome. But in these four books also, whatever he seems to say for the grace by which we are aided to turn away from evil and do good, he says in such a way that in no way does he depart from the ambiguity of words, which he can so expound to his disciples that they believe there is no assistance of grace by which the possibility of nature is aided, except in the law and teaching: so that he supposes even prayers themselves, as he most openly affirms in his writings, are to be employed for nothing else than that teaching may be opened to us even by divine revelation—not that the mind of a person may be aided, so that what he has learned ought to be done, he may also bring to fulfillment by love and action.

For he does not at all withdraw from that most manifest teaching of his, where he establishes those three things—possibility, will, action: and he says only the possibility is always aided by divine help; but the will and action he reckons need no help of God. And the help itself, by which he holds the natural possibility to be aided, he places in the law and teaching, which he confesses is revealed to us even by the Holy Spirit, on account of which he also concedes that we should pray. But this assistance of law and teaching, he holds, existed even in prophetic times; whereas the assistance of grace, which is properly called grace, he reckons to be in the example of Christ—which, nonetheless, you perceive to pertain to teaching, which is preached to us in the gospel: so that, namely, the way having been shown along which we ought to walk, we may now, by the powers of free choice, needing no help of another, suffice for ourselves, lest we fail on the way[^127]; though he contends that even the way itself can be found by nature alone, but more easily if grace aids.

## Would that the Pelagians attended, in the writings of Catholic men, to what is to be thought about the assistance of divine grace!

**42. 46.** This, then, as far as my understanding could reach, I have been able to grasp in the writings of Pelagius when he names grace. And you see that those who think these things, *ignorant of the righteousness of God, want to establish their own*[^128], and are far from that *which* is in us *from God*[^129], not from ourselves—which they ought to have noticed and acknowledged most of all in the holy canonical Scriptures.

But because they read them according to their own opinion, they assuredly do not behold even the plain things in them. Would, then, that at least in the writings of Catholic men, by whom they do not doubt that the Scriptures have been rightly understood, they would not negligently attend to what is to be thought about the assistance of divine grace, nor pass it by out of excessive love of their own opinion. For this very Pelagius, in that very recent little work of his, by the mention of which he defends himself—that is, in the third book *In Defense of Free Choice*—hear how he praises the holy Ambrose:

## Why Pelagius commends Ambrose.

**43. 47.** "The blessed bishop Ambrose," he says, "in whose books especially the Roman faith shines forth, who stood out as a certain splendid flower among Latin writers, whose faith and most pure understanding in the Scriptures not even an enemy has dared to censure"[^130]. See with what and how great praises he proclaims a man—however holy and learned, yet in no way to be compared to the authority of canonical Scripture. And this man he so commends for this reason: because he thinks he can use him in a certain passage of his books as a witness by whom to prove that a person can be without sin: which is not now the matter at issue, but the matter at issue is the assistance of grace, by which we are aided to not sinning and to living justly.

## Ambrose says that the Lord cooperates even with our wills.

**44. 48.** Let him, then, hear that venerable prelate saying and teaching, in the second book of his Exposition of the Gospel according to Luke, that the Lord cooperates even with our wills. "You surely see," he says, "that everywhere the power of the Lord cooperates with human endeavors; so that no one can build without the Lord, no one keep watch without the Lord, no one begin anything without the Lord. And therefore, according to the Apostle: *Whether you eat or drink, do all things to the glory of God*"[^131]. You see how the holy Ambrose took away, by these words, even that which people are wont to say—We begin, and God brings to completion—saying, "that no one even begins anything without God." Likewise in the sixth book of the same work, when he was dealing with those two debtors of one moneylender: "According to human reckoning," he says, "the one who owed more perhaps offended more: but through the mercy of the Lord the case is changed, so that he loves the more who owed the more—if, however, he obtains grace"[^132]. See how openly the Catholic teacher proclaims that even the very love by which one loves the more pertains to the gift of grace.

## Ambrose says that penance itself comes to be performed by the mercy and help of the Lord.

**45. 49.** Finally, that penance itself, which without doubt the will performs, comes to be performed by the mercy and help of the Lord, the blessed Ambrose says in the ninth book of the same work, speaking thus: "Good are the tears that wash away guilt. Indeed, those whom Jesus looks upon weep. Peter denied the first time, and did not weep, because the Lord had not looked upon him: he denied the second time, did not weep, because the Lord had still not looked upon him: he denied the third time too, Jesus looked upon him, and he wept most bitterly"[^133]. Let these men read the Gospel, and see that the Lord Jesus was then within, when he was being heard by the chief priests: but that the apostle Peter was without and below in the courtyard with the servants by the fire, now sitting, now standing, as is shown by the most truthful and most concordant narrative of the Evangelists. Hence it cannot be said that the Lord looked upon him visibly, with bodily eyes, by admonishing him.

And therefore what is written there: *The Lord looked upon him*[^134], was done within, was done in the mind, was done in the will. The Lord secretly came to his aid by mercy, touched his heart, called back his memory, by his grace visited the inner Peter, and moved and drew forth the disposition of the inner self even to outward tears. See how God is present, by aiding, to our wills and actions: see how he *works in us both to will and to work*[^135].

## Let Pelagius imitate so great a man!

**45. 50.** Likewise in the same book the same holy Ambrose: "For if Peter fell," he says, "who said: *Even if others are made to stumble, I will not be made to stumble*[^136], who else may rightly presume upon himself? Indeed David, because he had said: *I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever*, confesses that his boasting harmed him, saying: *You turned away your face, and I was confounded*[^137]"[^138]. Let this man hear so great a man teaching, let him imitate the one believing, whose doctrine and faith he praised. Let him hear humbly, let him imitate faithfully: let him not presume stubbornly upon himself, lest he perish. Why does Pelagius wish to be drowned in that sea from which Peter was freed by the rock?

## God calls those whom he deems worthy and makes religious whom he wills, says Ambrose.

**46. 51.** Let him hear the same prelate of God saying, likewise in the sixth book of the same work: "But why they did not receive him, the Evangelist himself related, saying: *Because his face was that of one going to Jerusalem*[^139]*.* But the disciples were eager that he be received within Samaria. But God calls those whom he deems worthy, and makes religious whom he wills"[^140]. O the understanding of the man of God, drawn from the very fountain of the grace of God! "God," he says, "calls those whom he deems worthy, and makes religious whom he wills."

See whether this is not that prophetic saying: *I will have mercy on whom I have had mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I have been merciful*[^141]*,* and that apostolic one: *So it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^142]. As also a man of his own in our own times says, "Whom he deems worthy he calls, and whom he wills he makes religious." Surely no one will dare to say that he is not yet religious "who runs to the Lord, and desires to be ruled by him, and suspends his will upon his will, and who, by clinging to him continually, becomes, according to the Apostle, one spirit with him[^143]"?

But this whole so great work of a religious person, Pelagius says is "accomplished by nothing other than the freedom of choice." But, on the contrary, the blessed Ambrose, praised so excellently by his own mouth, says: "The Lord God calls whom he deems worthy, and makes religious whom he wills." Therefore, that one may run to the Lord, and desire to be ruled by him, and suspend his will upon his will, and, by clinging to him continually, become, according to the Apostle, one spirit with him, God makes religious whom he wills: and this whole thing no one but a religious person does. Wherefore, unless it be done by God that he do this, who does this?

## How the controversy over the assistance of grace between Pelagius and Catholics is to be resolved.

**47. 52.** But because this question, where the choice of the will and the grace of God are disputed, is so difficult to sort out that, when free choice is defended, the grace of God seems to be denied, and when the grace of God is asserted, free choice is thought to be taken away: Pelagius can so wrap himself in the hiding places of this obscurity that he says he even agrees with the things which we have set down as written by the holy Ambrose, and proclaims that he too thinks these things, and has always thought them; and so tries to expound them one by one that they may be believed to suit even his opinion. Wherefore, as far as concerns this question about divine grace and assistance, attend to those three things which he distinguished most openly: being able, willing, being—that is, possibility, will, action. If, then, he agrees with us that not the possibility alone is aided in a person, even if he neither wills nor acts well, but the will and action itself—that is, that we may will well and act well, which are not in a person except when he wills well and acts well—if, as I said, he agrees that the will and action itself is divinely aided, and so aided that without that help we will and do nothing good, and that this is the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord[^144], in which he makes us just by his own righteousness, not ours, so that this is our true righteousness which is in us from him[^145]; then nothing, as I judge, will be left of controversy between us about the assistance of the grace of God.

## The praises of Zechariah and Elizabeth in the writings of Ambrose.

**48. 53.** But as for that on account of which he so praised the holy Ambrose—namely, because he found in his writings, from the praises of Zechariah and Elizabeth, that a person can be in this life without sin—although it is not to be denied, if God wills this, with whom all things are possible[^146], let him nevertheless consider more carefully in what way it was said. For it was said, as it seems to me, according to a certain manner of life among human beings, probable and praiseworthy, which no one could justly call into a complaint of accusation and incrimination. Zechariah and his wife are reported to have had this in the sight of God[^147] for this reason: because in it they deceived no one by any dissembling, but as they appeared to people, so they were known to the eyes of God. But this was not said according to that perfection of righteousness in which we shall live truly and in every way unspotted and perfect. For the apostle Paul too said that, *according to the righteousness which is from the law*, he had been *blameless*[^148]; in which law Zechariah too lived blamelessly: but this righteousness the Apostle counted as dung and loss[^149], in comparison with the righteousness which we hope for, and for which we ought now to hunger and thirst[^150], so that we may at some time be satisfied in vision with that which is now in faith, so long as *the just lives by faith*[^151].

## Other words of Ambrose.

**49. 54.** Let him hear, finally, that same venerable prelate, when he was expounding the prophet Isaiah, saying *that no one in this world can be without sin.* Where no one can say that he said "in this world" as if in the love of this world. For he was speaking of the Apostle, who said: *Our citizenship is in heaven*[^152]. The said bishop, then, explaining this meaning: "The Apostle," he says, "says that many perfect ones are with him, still placed in this world, who, if you look to true perfection, could not be perfect. For he himself said: *We see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face: now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I have been known*[^153]. Thus they are both unspotted in this world and will be unspotted in the kingdom of God: although surely, if you examine more minutely, no one can be unspotted, because no one is without sin"[^154]. That testimony of the holy Ambrose, then, which Pelagius uses for his own opinion[^155], was either said according to a certain manner—probable indeed, but not minutely examined—or else, if that holy and humble man supposed that Zechariah and Elizabeth had the highest and in every way perfect righteousness, to which nothing could now be added, he assuredly corrected his opinion by examining it more minutely.

## To be unspotted from the beginning is impossible for human nature, says Ambrose.

**50. 55.** Let this man surely attend, in that very passage from which he took this Ambrosian testimony that pleased him, that he also said this: that to be unspotted from the beginning is impossible for human nature[^156]; where the venerable Ambrose testifies that that natural possibility—which Pelagius is unwilling faithfully to regard as corrupted by sin, and therefore boastfully exalts—is entirely feeble and weak: without doubt against this man's will, but not against apostolic truth, where it is read: *We too were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^157].

For it is itself, through the sin of the first man, which came from his free choice, a corrupted and condemned nature; to which divine grace alone comes to aid, through the Mediator of God and human beings[^158] and the omnipotent physician. Concerning his assistance for our justification—by which God *works all things for good for those who love* him[^159], whom he first loved[^160], granting them that he be loved by them—since we have long been discoursing of it, let us henceforth begin, as far as the Lord may help, to treat also of the sin which, *through one man,* together with death, *entered the world, and so passed to all human beings*[^161], against those who have broken out more openly into the error contrary to this truth—those things which will seem sufficient to set forth.
