# Book II: Original Sin

## The Pelagians think that carnal generation is not held liable to the sin of the first man.

**1. 1.** Now then, carefully notice how cautiously you ought to listen, concerning the Baptism of infants, to men of this kind, who neither openly dare to deny to that age the bath of regeneration and remission of sins—lest Christian ears could not bear this—and yet persist in upholding and defending their opinion, by which they think that carnal generation is not held liable to the sin of the first man, although they seem to grant them Baptism for the remission of sins. Surely you yourselves wrote that, in your presence, you heard from Pelagius, when he read to you from his pamphlet, which he also asserted he had sent to Rome, that they say "infants ought to be baptized with the same words of the sacrament as adults too"[^1]. Who, after these words, would suppose that any question should be raised with them on this matter? Or, if one did, to whom would he not seem most slanderous, were not their manifest words read, where they deny that infants contract original sin, and contend that all are born without any defect?

## In this error Caelestius was more outspoken than Pelagius.

**2. 2.** And Caelestius indeed showed himself more outspoken in this error, to such a degree that not even in the episcopal judgment at Carthage was he willing to condemn those who say "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression"[^2]. And in the city of Rome, in his pamphlet which he gave to the most blessed Pope Zosimus, he asserted this more expressly: "that original sin binds none of the little ones." For from the ecclesiastical proceedings at Carthage we have copied out these words of his.

## The words of Caelestius in the Carthaginian judgment.

**3. 3.** "Bishop Aurelius said: Let what follows be read. And it was read: That the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race. And when it had been read, Caelestius said: I said that I was in doubt about the transmission of sin, yet in such a way that I would agree with whomever God has given the grace of skill; because I have heard differing things from those who are surely established as presbyters in the Catholic Church. The deacon Paulinus said: Tell us their names. Caelestius said: The holy presbyter Rufinus, who stayed at Rome with the holy Pammachius: I heard him saying that there is no transmission of sin. The deacon Paulinus said: Is there any other? Caelestius said: I have heard several say it too. The deacon Paulinus said: Tell their names. Caelestius said: Is one priest not enough for you?" And after some things, in another place: "Bishop Aurelius said: Let the rest of the pamphlet be read. And it was read: That infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression: down to the end of the lesser pamphlet inserted above."

## The hesitation of Caelestius.

**4. 3.** "Bishop Aurelius said: Did you at some time teach, Caelestius, as the deacon Paulinus says, that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression? Caelestius said: Let him explain what he said: Before the transgression. The deacon Paulinus said: You deny that you taught this. It is one of two things: either let him deny that he taught it, or let him now condemn it. Caelestius said: I have already said, let him explain in what sense he said: Before the transgression. The deacon Paulinus said: Deny that you taught those things. Bishop Aurelius said: I ask what I have gathered from this objection: I say, Adam, established in paradise, is said to have been made beforehand incorruptible, but afterward, through the transgression of the precept, was made corruptible.

Do you say this, brother Paulinus? The deacon Paulinus said: This, lord. Bishop Aurelius said: Whether the state of the infants to be baptized today is certainly such as it was before the transgression of Adam, or whether it certainly draws the guilt of the transgression from that same origin of sin from which one is born; this is what the deacon Paulinus wants to hear. The deacon Paulinus said: Whether he taught this, let him not deny it? Caelestius said: I have already spoken about the transmission of sin, that I have heard several of those established within the Catholic Church demolish it, and others also build it up: although this is a matter of question, not of heresy. I have always said that infants need Baptism, and ought to be baptized: what else does he seek?"

## The words of Caelestius in his statement of faith which he published at Rome.

**4. 4.** Surely you see that Caelestius so granted Baptism to the little ones that he was unwilling to confess that the sin of the first man, which is washed away by the bath of regeneration, passes into them—although he did not dare to deny it either: and on account of this hesitation of his, he did not condemn those who say "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression"[^3].

**5. 5.** But in the pamphlet which he published at Rome, which was submitted there in the ecclesiastical proceedings, he speaks of this matter in such a way as to show that he believes that about which he had here said he was in doubt. For these are his words: "But infants," he says, "ought to be baptized for the remission of sins, according to the rule of the universal Church, and according to the meaning of the Gospel, we confess: because the Lord established that the kingdom of heaven can be conferred on none but the baptized[^4]; and since the powers of nature do not have this, it must be conferred through the freedom of grace"[^5]. If he said nothing further about this matter, who would not believe that he confessed that original sins are remitted in Baptism to infants too, by saying that they ought to be baptized for the remission of sins? Hence, then, comes also that which you wrote, that Pelagius answered you thus, "that infants are baptized with the same words of the sacrament as adults too"[^6]; and that you rejoiced, having heard what you desired, and yet still preferred to consult us about his words.

## Caelestius neither feared nor blushed to profess what Pelagius feared or blushed at.

**6. 6.** Notice, then, what Caelestius said most openly, and there you will see what Pelagius hid from you. For Caelestius adds and says: "But that infants are to be baptized for the remission of sins, we have not said on this account, that we might seem to affirm sin from transmission: which is far alien from the Catholic understanding. For sin is not born with a person, since it is afterward practiced by the person: because it is shown to be a fault not of nature, but of will. And therefore it is fitting to confess this, lest we seem to make different kinds of Baptism, and it is necessary to guard against this, lest, through the occasion of the mystery, to the injury of the Creator, an evil be said to be transmitted to a person through nature before it is done by the person"[^7]. This meaning of his Pelagius either feared or blushed to disclose to you, which his disciple neither feared nor blushed to profess openly, without any windings of obscurity, before the Apostolic See.

## Zosimus preferred gradually to bind Caelestius by his own questions and the other's answers.

**6. 7.** But the very merciful prelate of the said See, when he saw him being carried headlong with such great presumption, as though raving, until—if it could come to pass—he should come to his senses, preferred gradually to bind him by his own questions and the other's answers, rather than, by striking with a severe sentence, to push him into that precipice toward which he already seemed to be leaning. But I did not say: He had openly fallen, but, he seemed to be leaning; because above, in that same pamphlet of his, about to speak of questions of this kind, he had said beforehand: "If perhaps, as happens to human beings, some error of ignorance has crept in, let it be corrected by your judgment"[^8].

## Caelestius was not yet believed to be loosed from the bonds of excommunication.

**7. 8.** Holding to this preliminary statement of his, the venerable Pope Zosimus dealt with the man, whom the wind of false doctrine had puffed up, that he should condemn those things which had been objected against him by the deacon Paulinus, and that he should give his assent to the letters of the Apostolic See which had flowed from his predecessor of holy memory[^9].

But he was indeed unwilling to condemn the objections of the deacon: yet he did not dare to resist the letters of the blessed Pope Innocent[^10]; nay, "he promised that he would condemn all that that See condemned"[^11]; and so, like a frenzied man, that he might rest, as though gently soothed, he was nevertheless not yet believed to be loosed from the bonds of excommunication. But, with an interval of two months interposed, until a reply should be written back from Africa[^12], room for coming to his senses was granted him under a certain medicinal mildness of the sentence.

Since, in truth, if, having laid aside the vanity of obstinacy, he were willing to attend to what he had promised, and to read carefully those same letters to which he had answered that he would consent, he would be healed. But after the rescripts had been directed from the African council of bishops, what followed—so that a sentence was most justly pronounced against him—read it all, for we have sent it all.

## Pelagius is not right to say that he ought not to be held by the same sentence as Caelestius.

**8. 9.** Hence Pelagius too, if he considers himself and his writings without deceit, is not right to say that he ought not to be held by the same sentence. For he deceived the Palestinian judgment; therefore he there seems to have been cleared: but the Roman Church, where you know him to be most well known, he could by no means deceive everywhere; although he attempted this too in some fashion: but, as I said, he by no means prevailed.

For the most blessed Pope Zosimus recalled what his predecessor, worthy of imitation, had thought of those very proceedings. He also attended to what the faith of the Romans, to be proclaimed in the Lord[^13], thought of him—whose harmonious zeal, against his error, on behalf of Catholic truth, he saw burning in concord: among whom that man had long lived, and from whom his teachings could not be hidden; who knew Caelestius to be his disciple in such a way that they could bear most faithful and most firm testimony on this matter. What, then, the holy Pope Innocent judged about the proceedings of the Palestinian synod, by which Pelagius boasts that he was absolved—although you can both read it in his letters which he wrote back to us, and the African synod, in answer to the venerable Pope Zosimus, mentioned it (which we have sent to Your Charity with the other materials)—nevertheless it does not seem that it should be passed over even in this work.

## The rescript of Innocent concerning the ecclesiastical proceedings in the East against Pelagius.

**9. 10.** For when we, in the letter which five bishops wrote to him[^14], had made mention of those same Palestinian proceedings—the report of which had already reached us—saying that in the East, where he lives, ecclesiastical proceedings had taken place by which he is thought to have been cleared, and when he wrote back to this, among other things: "Although there are," he says, "some things placed in those very proceedings which, when objected, he in part suppressed by evading, in part confounded with utter obscurity by twisting many words upon himself; some he cleared by reasoning more false than true, as for the time could appear, by denying some things, by turning others with a false interpretation. But would that—which is rather to be wished—he would now turn himself from that error of his to the true way of the Catholic faith, and desire and will to be cleared, considering the daily grace of God and recognizing his assistance, so that he may be seen truly, and approved by all, to be corrected by manifest reason; not by the judgment of the proceedings, but by a heart turned to the Catholic faith.

Hence we can neither approve nor blame their judgment, since we do not know whether the proceedings are true; or, if they are true, it is rather established that he evaded than that he cleared himself with the whole truth"[^15]. You surely see in these words how the most blessed Pope Innocent seems to speak not as of something unknown. You see what sentence he passed on his clearing. You see what his successor, the holy Pope Zosimus, ought to have recalled, as he did recall, so that in this he might confirm, with all hesitation removed, the judgment of his predecessor.

## Pelagius deceived the Palestinian judges.

**10. 11.** Now then, attend carefully to that from which it is shown that Pelagius deceived the Palestinian judges—to pass over other things—in this very question about the Baptism of infants: lest perhaps, in what we said—that Pelagius hid from you that meaning in which Caelestius was more outspoken, although he himself thinks no otherwise—we should seem rather to slander or suspect than to have grasped anything certain. For surely it has already become clear enough above that Caelestius was unwilling to condemn what is said, "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression"; because he saw that, if he condemned these things, he would confirm that the passage of sin from Adam into infants takes place. But when these things had been objected to Pelagius—that he too thought them together with Caelestius—he condemned them without any retraction. And although I know that you have read this, nevertheless, because this is written not for you alone, lest the reader be reluctant to go back to the proceedings themselves, or, if he does not have them, even with labor to seek them out, we transcribe from there the very words, which are written below.

## The very words of the Palestinian Synod.

**11. 12.** "The Synod said: Now, since Pelagius has anathematized the uncertain foolish talk[^16], rightly answering that a person, with the help and grace of God, can be  
that is, without sin, let him answer to the other chapters also. Another chapter in the teaching of Caelestius, the disciple of Pelagius, from those things which at Carthage were heard and noted by the holy Aurelius, bishop of Carthage, with other bishops: That Adam was made mortal, who, whether he sinned or did not sin, would die. That the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race. That the law leads to the kingdom just as the Gospel also does. That before the coming of Christ there were people without sin. That infants newly born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression. That neither does the whole race of human beings die through the death or transgression of Adam, nor does the whole race of human beings rise again through the resurrection of Christ.

That the holy bishop Augustine answered, against his disciples in Sicily, to Hilary on the chapters appended[^17], writing a book in which these things are contained: That a person can be without sin, if he wills: That infants, even if they are not baptized, have eternal life: That baptized rich persons, unless they renounce all things, if they appear to do anything good, it is not reckoned to them, nor can they have the kingdom of God[^18]. Pelagius said: As to a person's being able to be without sin, it was said above. But as to its having been the case that there were people without sin before the coming of the Lord, we too say that before the coming of Christ certain ones lived holily and justly, according to the tradition of the holy Scriptures: but the rest were not said by me, even according to their testimony, for which I ought not to make satisfaction; yet, for the satisfaction of the holy Synod, I anathematize those who so hold, or have at some time held"[^19].

## There is no difference between Pelagius and Caelestius as to the doctrine of original sin.

**12. 13.** See—to leave out other things—you see that Pelagius anathematized those who hold "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression"[^20]. What else, then, could the bishop-judges here understand, except that he confessed that sin passes from Adam into the little ones? In order not to confess this, Caelestius was unwilling to condemn these things, which this man condemned. Accordingly, if I show that he too thinks nothing else of the little ones than that they are born without any contagion of any defect, what will be the difference between this man and Caelestius in this question, except that the latter was more open, the former more secretive; the latter more obstinate, the former more mendacious; or at least the latter more outspoken, this one more cunning?

For the latter was unwilling, even in the Church of Carthage, to condemn what he afterward confessed at Rome that he held, and professed that *he was prepared to be corrected, if an error had crept upon him as upon a human being*[^21]*.* But this man both condemned that teaching as contrary to the truth, lest he himself be condemned by the Catholic judges; and reserved it for himself to be defended afterward, either condemning it mendaciously, or interpreting it cunningly.

## The words of Pelagius from his work In Defense of Free Choice.

**13. 14.** But I see that it is now most justly demanded of me that I not delay to demonstrate what I promised: whether he too thinks what Caelestius thinks. In the first book of his more recent work, which he wrote *In Defense of Free Choice*, of which work he made mention in the letter he sent to Rome: "Every good and evil," he says, "by which we are either praiseworthy or blameworthy, does not arise with us, but is done by us: for we are born capable of either thing, not full of it, and just as without virtue, so also we are begotten without vice: and before the action of our own will, there is in a person only that which God created"[^22].

Surely you discern in these words of Pelagius that the teaching of both is contained—about the little ones being born without any contagion of any defect from Adam. It is no wonder, then, that Caelestius was unwilling to condemn those who say, "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression": but it is a great wonder with what effrontery Pelagius condemned these things.

For if, as he says, "evil does not arise with us, and we are begotten without vice, and before the action of our own will there is in a person only that which God created," then assuredly the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, because it made no passage into the offspring. For either sin is not an evil, or sin is not a vice, or God created sin. But this man says, "Evil does not arise with us, and we are begotten without vice, and there is in those being born only that which God created." And on this account, since, according to this opinion of his, he thinks it most true that "the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, not the human race," why did Pelagius condemn this, except that the Catholic judges might be deceived?

Similarly this too can be said: "If evil does not arise with us, and we are begotten without vice, and there is in a person being born only that which God created"; then without doubt "infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression," in whom there was no evil or vice, and there was in him only that which God had created. And yet Pelagius anathematized "those who hold or have at some time held that little ones newly come from birth are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression"[^23]—that is, without any evil, without any vice, having only that which God created. Why, then, did Pelagius condemn this too, except that the Catholic synod might be deceived, lest the new heretic be condemned?

## So shameless a lie of Pelagius in an ecclesiastical judgment and on so great a question.

**14. 15.** But as you know—and as I also set down in that book which I wrote about the Palestinian proceedings to our venerable old man Aurelius—I rejoiced that, by that answer of Pelagius, this whole question was exhausted, and he seemed to me to have confessed most openly that there is original sin in the little ones, by saying anathema to those who would believe that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not also the human race, and would suppose that the little ones are in that state in which that first man was before the transgression. But afterward, when I had read his four books—from the first of which I copied out the words mentioned a little before—and had found the man still thinking against the Catholic faith concerning the little ones, I first began to marvel at so shameless a lie in an ecclesiastical judgment and on so great a question.

For if he had already written those same books beforehand, how did he say that he anathematizes those who at some time so held? But if he promised that work afterward, how did he anathematize those who so hold? Unless perhaps he is going to say, ridiculously, that he condemned those who at some time so held, and those who so hold; but that about future time—that is, those who were going to hold it thus—he could prejudge neither himself nor others; and that therefore he did not lie, because he was afterward caught holding it thus.

But he does not say this—not only because it is ridiculous, but because it cannot be true. For in those same books he both speaks against the passage of sin from Adam into the little ones, and boasts of the proceedings of the Palestinian synod, where he was thought to have truly condemned those who so hold, and by deceit stole his own absolution.

## By expounding otherwise words objected for another purpose, Pelagius does not show that he did not deceive the judges.

**15. 16.** For what does it pertain to the matter we are now dealing with, that he answers his disciples "that he condemned those objections for this reason: because he too says that that first sin harmed not only the first man, but also the human race, not by propagation, but by example[^24]"—that is, not that those who were propagated from him drew any vice from him, but that all who afterward sinned imitated him as the first sinner? Or that he says, "infants are therefore not in that state in which Adam was before the transgression, because they cannot yet receive a precept, but he could; and they do not yet use the choice of the rational will, which, unless he had used it, no precept would have been given him"[^25]?

What does this pertain to the matter—that, by so expounding the words objected to him, he thinks he rightly condemned what is said, "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, and not the human race; and that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before sin"[^26]; and yet, these things condemned, does not mendaciously hold what is found in his little works composed afterward, "that the little ones are born without any evil, without any vice, and that there is in them only that which God created"[^27], not the wound which the enemy inflicted?

## Therefore you are not absolved.

**16. 17.** Does he, by saying these things—by expounding otherwise words objected for another purpose—bring it about that he shows he did not deceive the judges? He by no means achieves this: for he deceived the judges all the more secretly, the more cunningly he expounds these things.

For the Catholic bishops, when they heard a man anathematizing those who say, "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone, not the human race," supposed that he held nothing other than what the Catholic Church is accustomed to proclaim: whence it truly baptizes the little ones for the remission of sins—not of those which they committed by imitating, on account of the example of the first sinner, but of those which they drew by being born, on account of the vice of their origin.

And when they heard him anathematizing those who say, "that infants who are born are in that state in which Adam was before the transgression," they believed that he said nothing other than that those who think the little ones have drawn no sin from Adam, and accordingly are in that state in which he was before sin. For this would be objected to him, and nothing else, since this was the matter in question. Accordingly, when this man so expounds this as to say that infants are therefore not in that state in which Adam was before sin, because they are not yet in the same firmness of mind or body, not because any guilt of propagation has passed into them, let it be answered to him: When those things to be condemned were objected to you, the Catholic bishops did not understand them thus; therefore, when you condemned them, they believed you to be Catholic.

On this account, then, what they supposed you held was to be absolved: but what you held was to be condemned. Therefore you are not absolved, you who held things to be condemned: but that was absolved which you ought to have held. But in order that you might be thought absolved, you were believed to hold things to be praised, since the judges did not understand that you were concealing things to be condemned. Rightly were you judged the associate of Caelestius, of whom you show yourself a manifest partaker. And although in the judgment you covered up your books, nevertheless after the judgment you published them.

## Many who followed the Pelagians were provided for, because they saw them associated with Catholic communion.

**17. 18.** Since these things are so, you surely perceive that, against the authors of so wicked an error, the episcopal councils, and the Apostolic See, and the whole Roman Church, and the Roman Empire[^28]—which, by God's favor, is Christian—were most rightly moved, until they should come to their senses from the snares of the devil. For who knows whether God may perhaps give them repentance, to recognize and confess and proclaim even the truth, and truly to condemn the condemnable falsehood? But however these men may wish to be, we cannot doubt that, by the mercy of the Lord, provision was made for many who followed them for this reason: because they saw them associated with Catholic communion.

## Pelagius attempted to deceive even the episcopal judgment of the Apostolic See.

**17. 19.** But how Pelagius tried to creep in so as to deceive even the episcopal judgment of the Apostolic See, in this very question about the Baptism of infants, attend carefully. For in the letter which he sent to Rome to Pope Innocent of blessed memory—since they did not find him in the body, and it was given to the holy Pope Zosimus, and from there directed to us—he says "that he is defamed by people, as denying infants the sacrament of Baptism, and promising to some the kingdom of heaven apart from the redemption of Christ"[^29]. But these things are not objected to them as he put it. For they neither deny infants the sacrament of Baptism, nor promise to any the kingdom of heaven apart from the redemption of Christ. And so that of which he complains he is defamed, he so set forth that he could easily answer the charge objected, with his teaching kept safe.

**18. 19.** But it is objected to them that they are unwilling to confess that unbaptized infants are liable to the condemnation of the first man, and that original sin, to be purged by regeneration, has passed into them; since they contend that infants are to be baptized only for receiving the kingdom of heaven: as though, apart from the kingdom of heaven, they could have anything but eternal death, who without participation in the body and blood of the Lord cannot have eternal life. See what is objected to them about the Baptism of infants: not what he himself so set forth that he could, according to his teachings, answer his own proposition, as if it were the objection of an adversary.

## Other ambiguous words of Pelagius.

**18. 20.** Then notice how he answers, and see that the hiding places of ambiguity prepare refuges for falsehood, by pouring darkness over the truth; so that even we, when we first read these things, almost rejoiced at them as correct or corrected. But his fuller disputations in his books, where, however much he covers himself, he is for the most part compelled to disclose himself, made these things too suspect to us, so that, looking more attentively, we found them ambiguous. For when he had said "that he had never heard even any impious heretic say of the little ones" what he set forth, he then added and said: "For who is so ignorant of evangelical reading that he would not only try to affirm this, but could even lightly say it, or even think it? Then who is so impious as to want the little ones to be excluded from the kingdom of heaven, while he forbids them to be baptized and to be reborn in Christ"[^30]?

## The question is about the purging of original sin in the little ones.

**19. 21.** In vain he says these things: he does not thereby clear himself. That the little ones cannot enter the kingdom of heaven without Baptism, not even they themselves have ever denied. But that is not the question: the question is about the purging of original sin in the little ones. Let him clear himself of this, who is unwilling to confess that the bath of regeneration has, in the little ones, something to purge. And therefore let us attend to the rest of what he is going to say.

For after interposing the testimony from the Gospel, that *unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, no one* can *enter the kingdom of heaven*[^31]—about which, as we said, no question arises for them—he went on to add, saying: "Who is so impious as to forbid to a little one of any age the common redemption of the human race"[^32]? And this too is ambiguous, what redemption he means: whether from evil to good, or from good to better. For Caelestius too, at Carthage, in his pamphlet confessed the redemption of the little ones, and yet was unwilling to confess that sin had passed from Adam into them.

## Other ambiguous words of Pelagius.

**20. 22.** But notice what this man then adds: "And forbid," he says, "the one who is born to an uncertain life to be reborn to a perpetual and certain one"[^33]? That is, Who is so impious as to forbid to be reborn to a perpetual and certain life the one who is born to an uncertain one? When we first read these words, we believed that by "uncertain life" he meant this temporal life: although it seemed to us that he ought rather to have called it mortal than uncertain, since it ends in certain death. Nevertheless, as long as it is lived, since it is not doubted to be uncertain at every moment, we thought he meant nothing other than that he preferred to call this mortal life uncertain. And on this account, although he openly was unwilling to confess the eternal death of the little ones who depart from this life without the sacrament of Baptism, nevertheless a seemingly certain reasoning consoled our anxiety about that. For we said: If perpetual life, as he seems to confess, cannot belong to any but those who have been baptized, then assuredly perpetual death will follow those who die unbaptized. Which can happen, by no justice of God, to those by whom no sins have been committed in this life, except on account of original sin.

## Little ones dying without Baptism—where they do not go, I know; where they go, I do not know.

**21. 23.** But afterward there were not lacking brethren who admonished us that Pelagius could have said this for this reason: because on this question he is reported to have been accustomed to answer those who asked, saying, "Little ones dying without Baptism—where they do not go, I know; where they go, I do not know"; that is, I know that they do not go into the kingdom of heaven; but where they go, he said, or says, that he does not know, because he did not dare to say that those go into perpetual death whom he both held to have committed nothing evil here, and did not agree to have drawn original sin. And so these words of his too, sent to Rome as a great clearing of himself, are so ambiguous that they can offer hiding places to their teaching, from which the heretical meaning may leap out to lie in wait, when, no one being present who could answer, someone is found weak, as if in a wilderness.

## Other words of Pelagius.

**21. 24.** But now, in the statement of his Faith, which he sent to Rome with that same letter to the same Pope Innocent, to whom he had also written a letter, by covering himself he laid himself bare much more evidently, saying: "We hold one Baptism, which we say is to be celebrated with the same words of the sacrament in infants as also in adults"[^34]. He did not even say: With the same sacrament; which indeed, if he had said it, would still be ambiguous: but, "with the same," he says, "words of the sacrament"; as though to infants the remission of sins were spoken by the sound of words, not effected by the reality of things. He nevertheless seemed for a time to say something that suited the Catholic faith: but he did not prevail to deceive that See to the end.

For after the rescripts of the African council—into which province indeed that pestiferous doctrine had arrived by creeping, but had not so widely occupied it or so deeply pervaded it—other things of his also, in the city of Rome, where he had spent a very long time, and where he had earlier been engaged in these discourses and disputes, were brought forth and laid open by the care of the faithful brethren; which Pope Zosimus appended, as things to be execrated, to his letters which he composed to be carried throughout the Catholic world, as you can read. There Pelagius, as if expounding the Letter of the apostle Paul to the Romans, argues and says: "If the sin of Adam harmed even those who did not sin, then the righteousness of Christ profits even those who do not believe"[^35]. And other things of this kind, all of which, with the Lord's help, have been refuted and dissolved in the books which we wrote on the Baptism of infants[^36].

And these things, indeed, in his so-called expositions, he did not dare to object in his own person; but he said this there, where he was most well known to many, and could not hide what he thought and said: which, in those books of which I mentioned something above from the first[^37], he carries on not dissemblingly, but most openly, with all the force of disputing that he can, that human nature in the little ones may be believed in no way corrupted from propagation; and in arrogating salvation to it, he begrudges it the Savior.

## Let Pelagius and Caelestius be brought back to a place of repentance, or else be wholly condemned.

**22. 25.** Since these things are so, and since it is now established that a pestiferous teaching and a heretical error has arisen—which, with the Lord's help, the Church now more openly shuns—these two, namely Pelagius and Caelestius, being either brought back to a place of repentance, or, if they refuse this, wholly condemned—who are either reported, or even proved, to be the authors of this perversity; or at least, if they are not the authors, but learned this from others, are nevertheless touted as its asserters and teachers, through whom it might creep more widely and grow, both by their words and writings, and by fitting evidence, and by the report arising and increasing from all these:

what remains, except that every Catholic, by the powers which he takes from the Lord, refute this plague, and vigilantly resist it; so that, while one contends for the truth by the necessity of answering, without zeal for contention, the unlearned may be instructed, and so that what the enemy contrived for ruin may be turned to the usefulness of the Church, according to that saying of the Apostle: *There must be heresies too, that those who are approved may be made manifest among you*[^38]?

## Wishing cunningly to avoid the odium of heresy, they assert that this question is apart from peril to the faith.

**23. 26.** Wherefore, after the many things which, by writing, we have been able to discuss against this error hostile to the grace of God, which through Jesus Christ our Lord he bestows on small and great, now it is fitting to examine this: that, wishing cunningly to avoid the odium of heresy, they assert "that this question is apart from peril to the faith"; so that, namely, if they are convicted of having gone astray in it, they may seem to have erred not criminally, but as it were civilly. For thus Caelestius spoke at Carthage in the ecclesiastical proceedings: "I have already," he says, "spoken about the transmission of sin, that I have heard several of those established within the Catholic Church demolish it, and others also build it up; although this is a matter of question, not of heresy. I have always said that infants need Baptism, and ought to be baptized: what else does he seek?" He said this as if he wished to signify that it would then have been to be judged a heresy, if he denied that infants ought to be baptized: but now, since he confesses that they are to be baptized, even if he gives as the reason for their Baptism not that which the truth holds, but one that does not pertain to the faith, he reckons that he does not err, and therefore is not to be judged a heretic.

Likewise in the pamphlet which he gave at Rome, when he had set forth his faith, as much as it pleased him to say, from the Trinity of one deity all the way to the resurrection of the dead, of what kind it will be—about which no one had questioned him, and concerning which no question was being raised with him—where his discourse came to what was at issue: "But if any questions," he says, "apart from the faith have arisen, about which there is contention among many; I have not, as the author of some teaching, established this by definitive authority, but those things which I have received from the fountain of the Prophets and Apostles I offer to be approved by the judgment of your Apostolate; so that if perhaps, as happens to human beings, some error of ignorance has crept in, it may be corrected by your judgment"[^39]. Surely you perceive that he did this with this preliminary statement set forth: so that, if any error appeared in him, he might seem to have erred not in the faith, but in questions which are apart from the faith, where, even if the error is to be corrected, it is nevertheless not corrected as a heresy, and one who has not been corrected is said to err in such a way that he is nevertheless not judged a heretic.

## Questions in which, with the faith kept safe, one is either ignorant or errs are in a far different case.

**23. 27.** But this opinion greatly deceives him. These questions, which he reckons to be apart from the faith, are in a far different case from those in which, with the faith kept safe by which we are Christians, one is either ignorant of what is true, and a definitive judgment is suspended, or, by a human and weak conjecture, surmises otherwise than it is. As when it is asked of what kind, or where, is the paradise where God placed the man whom he formed from dust[^40]; though the Christian faith does not doubt that that paradise exists: or when it is asked where Elijah or Enoch now is, whether there, or somewhere else; whom, however, we do not doubt to be alive in the bodies in which they were born: or when it is asked whether the Apostle was caught up to the third heaven in the body or out of the body[^41]; although this is a shameless inquiry of those who want to know what he himself, to whom this was granted, testifies—with the faith surely kept safe—that he does not know:

or how many heavens there are, to the third of which he records that he was caught up: or whether the elements of this visible world are four or more: what makes the sun or moon fail, by those eclipses which astrologers are accustomed to predict by a fixed reckoning of times: why the ancient people lived so long as holy Scripture testifies; and whether, in proportion to a longer lifespan, they began to beget children at a late puberty: how Methuselah was able to live[^42], who was not in the ark, and who, as the years are reckoned in very many codices both Greek and Latin, is found to have survived the flood; or whether one ought rather to believe the fewer, which are very rarely found, in which the number of years is so written that he is shown to have died before the flood. For who would not perceive that, in these and similar varied and innumerable questions—pertaining either to the most obscure works of God or to the most hidden recesses of the Scriptures, which it is difficult to embrace and define by any fixed kind—many things are unknown with the Christian faith kept safe, and one errs somewhere without any crime of heretical teaching?

## But here the Christian faith stands.

**24. 28.** But in the cause of the two men, by one of whom we were sold under sin, by the other are redeemed from sins; by one we were cast headlong into death, by the other are freed unto life; since the one ruined us in himself by doing his own will, not the will of him by whom he was made; the other saved us in himself by doing not his own will, but the will of him by whom he was sent[^43]: in the cause, then, of these two men the Christian faith properly stands. *For there is one God, and one mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus*[^44]. Since *there is no other name under heaven given to human beings, in which we must be saved*[^45]: and in him God has appointed *faith for all, raising him from the dead*[^46].

And so without this faith—that is, without faith in the one mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus; without faith, I say, in his resurrection, which God has appointed for all, and which surely cannot be truly believed without his incarnation and death—without faith, then, in the incarnation and death and resurrection of Christ, the Christian truth does not doubt that not even the ancient just could, in order to be just, be cleansed from sins and justified by the grace of God: whether among those just ones whom holy Scripture mentions, or among those whom it does not indeed mention, but who are nevertheless to be believed to have existed, whether before the flood, or from then until the giving of the law, or in the time of the law itself, not only among the children of Israel, such as the Prophets were, but also outside that same people, such as Job was. For their hearts too were cleansed by the same faith in the mediator, and *love was poured out* in them *through the Holy Spirit*[^47], who *breathes where he wills*[^48], not following merits, but even making the merits themselves. For the grace of God will in no way be grace, unless it be gratuitous in every way.

## Even in the time of the law, the people of God were under grace.

**24. 29.** Although, then, *death* reigned *from Adam until Moses*[^49], because not even the law given through Moses could conquer it—for it was not given as something that could give life[^50], but as something that should show the dead, for whose giving of life grace was necessary, to be not only laid low by the propagation and domination of sin, but also convicted by the added transgression of the law itself; not that anyone should perish who even then understood this in the mercy of God, but that, destined to punishment through the reign of death, and made manifest even to himself through the transgression of the law, he should seek the help of God, so that *where sin abounded, grace might superabound*[^51], which alone frees *from the body of this death*[^52].

**25. 29.** Although, then, not even the law given through Moses could take away from any person the reign of death; nevertheless, even in the time of the law, there were people of God—not under the law that terrifies, convicts, punishes, but under the grace that delights, heals, frees. There were those who would say: *I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother nourished me in the womb*[^53]; and: *There is no peace in my bones because of the face of my sins*[^54]; and: *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me*[^55]; and: *Strengthen me with a princely spirit*[^56]; and: *Take not your Holy Spirit from me*[^57]. There were those who would say: *I believed, therefore I have spoken*[^58].

For they too were cleansed by the same faith by which we are. Hence the Apostle also says: *But having the same spirit of faith, according to what is written, I believed, therefore I have spoken; we too believe, therefore we also speak*[^59]. From that same faith it was said: *Behold, a virgin will conceive in the womb, and will bear a son; and they will call his name Emmanuel, which is interpreted, God with us*[^60]. From that same faith it was said of him: *And he himself, like a bridegroom coming forth from his chamber: he rejoiced as a giant to run the way; from the height of heaven is his going forth, and his circuit even to the height of heaven; and there is no one who can hide himself from his heat*[^61]. From that same faith it was said to him: *Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, a rod of uprightness, the rod of your kingdom: you have loved righteousness, and hated iniquity, therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness above your fellows*[^62].

By the same spirit of faith these future things were seen by them, by which they are believed by us to have been done. For those who could prophesy these things to us with faithful love were not themselves without a share in them. And whence is it that the apostle Peter says: *Why do you tempt God, by laying upon the neck of the disciples a yoke which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear; but through the grace of the Lord Jesus we believe that we are saved, even as they were*[^63]:

except because they too were saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, not through the law of Moses, through which there came not healing, but knowledge of sin, as the Apostle teaches, saying: *For through the law comes the knowledge of sin*[^64]? *But now, apart from the law, the righteousness of God has been manifested, attested by the Law and the Prophets*[^65].

If, then, it has now been manifested, it existed even then, but hidden. Its hiddenness was signified by the veil of the temple, which, to signify its revelation, was torn at the death of Christ[^66]. And even then, therefore, this grace of the one mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus, was in the people of God: but it lay hidden within, as the rain in the fleece, which God sets apart for his inheritance not as owed, but as freely given[^67]; but now, as if that fleece had been dried—that is, the Jewish people having been rejected—it is seen lying open in all the nations, as on the threshing floor[^68].

## How Pelagius divides the ages.

**26. 30.** Let us not, then, like Pelagius and his disciples, divide the ages[^69], saying that just people first lived by nature, then under the law, thirdly under grace. By nature, namely, from Adam through so long an age, in which the law had not yet been given. "For then," they say, "the Creator was known with reason as guide, and how one ought to live was carried written in their hearts, not by the law of the letter, but of nature. But when morals had been corrupted," they say, "when nature, now discolored, began not to suffice, the law was added to it, by which, as by a file, the rust having been rubbed away, it might be restored to its former brightness. But after the excessive habit of sinning prevailed, as they argue, which the law had little power to heal: Christ came, and, as to a most desperate disease, the physician himself came to the aid, not through his disciples, but through himself."

## The Pelagians try to exclude the ancient just from the grace of the Mediator.

**26. 31.** Arguing these things, they try to exclude the ancient just from the grace of the mediator, as though *the man Christ Jesus* had not been the *Mediator* of *God* and of those *human beings*[^70]; because, not having yet taken flesh from the womb of the Virgin, he was not yet a human being when those just ones lived. But if this were so, the Apostle would by no means say: *Through a human being came death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead: for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive*[^71]. Since those ancient just, according to the empty talk of these men, nature sufficing for them, did not need the man Christ as mediator, through whom they might be reconciled to God: nor will they be made alive in him, to whose body and members—according to that by which he was made a human being for the sake of human beings—they are shown not to pertain. But if, as the Truth says through his Apostles, *as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive*; since *through* that *human being came death, and through* this *human being the resurrection of the dead*,

who, being a Christian, would dare to doubt that even those just ones, who in the more recent ages of the human race pleased God, are therefore going to come into the resurrection of eternal life, not of eternal death, because they will be made alive in Christ; and that they are made alive in Christ because they pertain to the body of Christ; and that they pertain to the body of Christ because for them too *Christ is the head*[^72]; that Christ is the head for them too because *there is one Mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus*[^73]? Which he would not have been for them, unless they had believed in his resurrection through his grace. And how would this have come about, if they had not known that he was going to come in the flesh, nor had lived justly and devoutly from this faith? For if the incarnation of Christ did not profit them because it had not yet taken place, then neither does the judgment of Christ over the living and the dead profit us, because it has not yet taken place. But if we, through faith in this judgment not yet made, but to come, shall stand at the right hand of Christ; then assuredly they, through faith in his incarnation then not yet made, but to come, are members of Christ.

## The humanity of Christ was revealed to the ancient just.

**27. 32.** For it is not to be thought that to the ancient just only the divinity of Christ, which always existed, was revealed and profited, and not also his humanity, which did not yet exist. For that which the Lord Jesus said: *Abraham longed to see my day, and he saw it, and was glad*[^74]; if by his day he wanted his time to be understood, he assuredly bore witness to Abraham that he had been imbued with faith in his incarnation. For according to this he has a time: but his divinity exceeds all time, because through it all times were made. And even if anyone supposed it should be taken of the everlasting day, which is bounded by no tomorrow, preceded by no yesterday—that is, of eternity itself, in which he is coeternal with the Father—how would Abraham truly long for it, unless he knew the future mortality of him whose eternity he sought? Or if anyone constricts the meaning of these words to this, so as to say that nothing is to be understood in what the Lord said, He sought my day, except, He sought me, who am the abiding day—that is, the unfailing light: as when we speak of the life of the Son, of which the Gospel speaks, saying: *So he gave to the Son also to have life in himself*[^75]: we understand not the Son to be one thing and his life another, but the same Son himself to be the life, who said: *I am the way, the truth, and the life*[^76]: and of whom it is said: *He is the true God and life eternal*[^77]: so that Abraham desired to see this divinity of his equal to the Father, his incarnation by no means foreknown, as some philosophers too sought him, who learned nothing of his flesh: will anyone rightly understand otherwise that too which Abraham bids, that his servant place his hand under his thigh, and swear by the God of heaven[^78], except that Abraham knew in what flesh, propagated from that thigh, the God of heaven would come?

## He is Mediator, therefore, by that whereby he is a human being.

**28. 33.** Of this flesh and blood, when he blessed Abraham himself, Melchizedek too brought forth a testimony most well known to faithful Christians[^79], so that so long afterward it was said to Christ in the Psalms—what had not yet been done, but was still to come, and yet the same one faith of the fathers, which is ours, sang: *You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek*[^80]. For to those who find death in Adam, Christ profits by this: that he is the mediator unto life. But he is not mediator by that whereby he is equal to the Father: for by this he is as far distant from us as the Father is; and how will there be a middle, where the very distance is the same? Therefore the Apostle does not say: *One mediator between God and human beings,* Christ Jesus; but, *the man Christ Jesus*[^81]. He is Mediator, then, by that whereby he is a human being: lower than the Father by that whereby he is nearer to us; higher than us by that whereby he is nearer to the Father. Which is said more openly thus: lower than the Father, because in the form of a servant[^82]; higher than us, because without the stain of sin.

## All human beings need the second Adam as a physician.

**29. 34.** Wherefore whoever contends that human nature, at whatever age, does not need a physician, the second Adam, because it was not corrupted in the first Adam, is convicted as an enemy of the grace of God—not in some question in which one can doubt or err with the faith kept safe, but in the very rule of faith by which we are Christians. But what kind of thing is it, that by these men the nature of human beings which existed before the law is praised, as though still corrupted by less evil morals; and they do not consider that human beings were then submerged in such great, as it were intolerable, sins, that—except for one man of God and his wife, and three sons and as many daughters-in-law—by the just judgment of God, just as afterward the small land of Sodom was destroyed by fire, so the whole world was destroyed by the flood[^83]? From the time, therefore, when *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^84], assuredly the whole mass of perdition became the possession of the destroyer. And so no one, absolutely no one, has been freed from there, or is freed, or will be freed, except by the grace of the Redeemer.

## Abraham received the sign of circumcision.

**30. 35.** And before Abraham, indeed, whether the just, or their little ones, were marked by any bodily and visible sacrament, Scripture does not express. Abraham himself, however, received the sign of circumcision, the seal of the righteousness of faith[^85]. And he so received it that thenceforth he was bidden to circumcise all the little ones of his house too, freshest from their mothers' wombs, on the eighth day of their birth; so that even these, who could not yet believe with the heart unto righteousness[^86], might nevertheless take the seal of the righteousness of faith. This was commanded under such great terror that God said that that soul would perish from his people whose foreskin had not been circumcised on the eighth day[^87]. If the justice of so horrible a penalty be sought, will not all the argumentation of these men about free choice and the praiseworthy soundness and purity of nature, however clever it be, struck back and broken, fly to pieces? For what evil, I ask, did a little one commit by his own will, that, with another being negligent and not circumcising him, he himself should be condemned, with so severe a condemnation that that soul should perish from his people? For the terror of temporal death was not struck in; since of the just, when they died, it was then rather said: *And he was gathered to his people*[^88]; or, *he was gathered to his fathers*[^89]: since thenceforth no temptation is feared for a person which would separate him from his people, if his people is itself the people of God.

## Rightly is an uncircumcised infant punished with that perdition, because he belongs to the mass of perdition.

**31. 36.** What, then, does so great a condemnation mean, for nothing admitted of one's own will? For it is not, as some suppose, following the Platonists, that this is repaid to the soul of each infant, which by its own will it committed before this life, when, before this body, it had a free choice of living either well or badly: since the apostle Paul says most openly that those not yet born have done nothing good or evil[^90]. Whence, then, is an infant rightly punished with that perdition, except because he belongs to the mass of perdition, and is justly understood, as born from Adam, to be condemned by the obligation of the ancient debt, unless he has been freed from there, not according to debt, but according to grace? And what grace, except the grace *of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^91]? Whom, assuredly, among the other sacraments of the ancients, the very circumcision of the foreskin also prophesied. For the eighth day, in the recurrences of the weeks, is the Lord's day, on which the Lord rose: and *the rock was Christ*[^92]; whence the knife of circumcision is of stone[^93]; and the flesh of the foreskin, the body of sin.

## The one Christ, without vice, came to heal vices.

**32. 37.** Accordingly, the sacraments having been changed, after he came who was signified to be going to come—yet the help of the Mediator not changed, who even before he had come in the flesh freed his ancient members by faith in his incarnation[^94]—we too, when we were *dead in trespasses and in the foreskin of* our *flesh*, were made alive together with Christ, *in whom* we were circumcised *with a circumcision not made by hand*[^95], which the circumcision made by hand prefigured, *that the body of sin might be done away with*[^96], with which we are born from Adam. The propagation of the condemned origin condemns us, unless we are cleansed by the likeness of sinful flesh, in which was sent, without sin, the one who nevertheless from sin would condemn sin[^97]—for he was made sin for us. Whence the Apostle says: *We beseech you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God*: *him who knew no sin he made sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in him*[^98].

God, then, to whom we are reconciled through him, made him sin for us—that is, a sacrifice through which our sins might be remitted: since sacrifices for sins are called sins. And surely he himself was offered up for our sins, having no vice, alone among human beings—such as was sought even then in the beasts, by which it was signified that the one without vice would come to heal vices. On whatever day of his birth, therefore, an infant is baptized in Christ, he is circumcised as on the eighth day: since he is circumcised in him who rose on the third day indeed from when he was crucified, but on the eighth day in the weeks. And he is circumcised *in the stripping off of the body of the flesh*[^99]—that is, so that the debt which the contagion of carnal generation drew, the grace of spiritual regeneration may absolve. *For no one is clean from filth* (with what filth, I ask, except of sin?), *not even the infant whose life is of one day upon the earth*[^100].

## How the Pelagians argue against original sin from the natural good of the human being and of marriage.

**33. 38.** But they argue thus, saying: "Then marriage is an evil, and the human being whom marriage begets is not the work of God?" As though the good of marriage were the disease of concupiscence, in which those who do not know God love their wives—which the Apostle forbids[^101]—and not rather conjugal chastity, by which carnal lust is brought back into the good uses of begetting children in an ordered way: or as though a human being could be anything but the work of God—not only one who is begotten of marriage, but even one who is begotten of fornication or of adultery.

But in this question, where it is asked not for what thing a creator, but for what thing a savior is necessary, one must look not at what good there is in the procreation of nature, but at what evil there is in the sin by which it is certain that nature was corrupted. But both are propagated together, both nature and the vice of nature; of which the one is good, the other evil. The former is taken from the bounty of the Creator, the latter is drawn from the origin of condemnation: of the former the cause is the good will of God most high, of the latter the bad will of the first man: the former indicates God as the founder of the creature, the latter indicates God as the punisher of disobedience: finally, the same Christ himself, for creating the former, is the maker of the human being; for healing the latter, was made a human being[^102].

## The goods proper to marriage.

**34. 39.** Marriage, then, is a good in all things which are proper to marriage. And these are three: the ordering of begetting, the fidelity of chastity, the sacrament of wedlock. On account of the ordering of begetting it is written: *I want the younger to marry, to bear children, to be mothers of households*[^103]. On account of the fidelity of chastity: *The wife does not have power over her own body, but the husband: likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^104]. On account of the sacrament of wedlock: *What God has joined together, let no one separate*[^105].

Of these, in our other little works, which are not unknown to you, we remember having discussed sufficiently, as far as we could with the Lord's help. On account of all these, *marriage is honorable in all, and the bed undefiled*[^106]. For insofar as marriage is good, it makes very much good even out of the evil of lust: since lust is used well not by lust, but by reason. But lust is in that which the Apostle notes—the law of the disobedient members fighting against the law of the mind: but reason, using lust well, is itself in the law of marriage. For if no good could come from evil, God would not even form a human being from an adulterous union.

As, therefore, the condemnable evil of adultery, when even from it a human being is born, is not imputed to God, who surely in the evil work of human beings himself does a good work: so whatever there is shameful in that disobedience of the members, at which those blushed who after sin covered those same members with fig leaves[^107], is not imputed to marriage, on account of which conjugal intercourse is not only licit, but even useful and honorable; but is imputed to the sin of disobedience, whose penalty this followed, that the human being, disobedient to God, should feel his members also disobedient to himself:

blushing at which, because they were moved not at the choice of his will, but at the incitement of lust, as if by their own choice, he judged them shameful, and took care to cover them. For a human being ought not to have been confounded over the work of God, nor were those things in any way to be blushed at as created, which seemed good to the Creator to establish. And so that simple nakedness displeased neither God nor the human being, when there was nothing shameful, because nothing punishable had preceded.

## What kind of marriage would have continued, if the reproach of sin had not intervened . . .

**35. 40.** But there would without doubt be marriage even had sin not preceded, because for no other cause was a helper for the man made not another man, but a woman. And those words of God: *Increase and multiply*[^108], are not a prediction of sins to be condemned, but a blessing of marriages made fruitful. For God, by these ineffable words of his—that is, by the divine reasons living in the truth of his wisdom, through which all things were made—implanted the force of seed in the first human beings. But if nature had not been dishonored by sin, far be it that we should suppose that marriages in paradise would have been such that in them the genital members would be moved for sowing offspring not by the assent of the will, as the foot for walking, the hand for working, the tongue for speaking, but by the heat of lust. Nor, as now happens, would the integrity of virginity be corrupted, for conceiving offspring, by the force of turbid ardor, but it would obey the command of a most tranquil love: and in that way there would be neither pain nor blood of the virgin in intercourse, just as there would be no groaning of the mother in giving birth. These things are not believed, because in this condition of mortality they are untried. For nature, changed for the worse by vice, does not find an example of that first purity. But we speak to the faithful, who have known how to believe the divine words, even with no examples of tried truth applied. For how shall I now show a human being made from dust without any parents, and for him a wife from his own side[^109]? And yet what the eye no longer finds, faith believes.

## . . . cannot be shown, can be believed.

**36. 41.** So, then, both the tranquility of the first marriage without the passion of lust, and the movement of the genitals like that of the other members—not at the incitement of unbridled heat, but at the choice of the will (such marriage as would have continued, if the reproach of sin had not intervened)—now cannot be shown: but, from those things which have been written by divine authority, it can not undeservedly be believed. For thus I do not now find anyone having intercourse without itching lust, just as I do not find anyone giving birth without pain and groaning, anyone being born without future death: and yet, according to the truth of the holy Scriptures, the groaning of one giving birth and the death of a human being would not have been, if sin had not preceded. So too that at which they blushed, who covered those members: because this too is written, in those same holy Letters, to have followed when sin preceded.

For unless an unseemly movement had announced to their eyes—not indeed closed, but in this respect not open, that is, not attentive—that those members were to be noticed, they would have felt nothing in their body shameful and to be veiled—a body which God had surely made wholly praiseworthy: because, unless the misdeed had gone before, which disobedience dared to commit, the disgrace would not follow, which shame would wish to hide.

## Intercourse and lust.

**37. 42.** It is clear, therefore, that this is not to be imputed to marriage, which, even if it did not exist, marriage would nevertheless exist: whose good is not taken away by this evil, but by it even this evil is turned to a good use. But because now, in this condition of mortals, intercourse and lust are carried on together, it comes about that, when lust is censured, even the licit and honorable conjugal intercourse is thought to be censured by those who are unwilling, or do not know how, to distinguish these. Nor do they attend to the fact that that is the good of marriage by which marriages glory—that is, offspring, chastity, the sacrament; but that this is not the good of marriage, but the evil of carnal concupiscence, at which marriages too blush. But because without that evil the good of marriage—that is, the propagation of children—cannot come about, when it comes to a work of this kind, secret places are sought, witnesses are removed, even the presence of the children themselves, if any have already been born from there, is avoided, when through their age they have already begun to perceive these things: and so marriages are allowed to exercise what is licit, in such a way that they do not neglect to conceal what is unseemly. Hence it is that even infants, who cannot sin, are nevertheless not born without the contagion of sin; not from that which is licit, but from that which is unseemly. For from that which is licit, nature is born; from that which is unseemly, the vice. Of the nature being born the author is God, who created the human being, and who joined man and woman by the law of marriage: but of the vice the author is the cunning of the deceiving devil, and the will of the consenting human being.

## Whatever was not yet known was deservedly condemned in the transgressing root.

**38. 43.** In which God did nothing, except that he condemned by just judgment, together with his stock, the human being who sinned by his will: and therefore there, whatever was not yet even born was deservedly condemned in the transgressing root: in which condemned stock carnal generation holds the human being, from which spiritual regeneration alone frees. Therefore, for regenerated parents, if at least they have persevered in the same grace, without doubt this lust, on account of the remission of sins which has taken place in them, will not harm—unless they use it badly, not only in all illicit corruptions, but even in their own spouses, when, not with the will to propagate, they spend effort on creating children, but with the pleasure of wantonness serve to glut their craving.

That, on account of avoiding fornications, husbands and wives should not defraud one another, *except by consent for a time, that* they may be free *for prayers*, the Apostle concedes *by way of indulgence, not by way of command*[^110]. For evidently, while he grants indulgence, he marks a fault. But conjugal intercourse, which the marriage contracts also indicate, taking place for the sake of begetting children, is good through itself outright, not in comparison with fornication: which, although—on account of the body of death, which is not yet renewed by resurrection—it cannot take place without a certain bestial movement, at which human nature blushes; nevertheless the intercourse itself is not sin, where reason uses lust for the good, and is not overcome by it for the evil.

## The remission of sins in this life and in the final regeneration.

**39. 44.** This concupiscence of the flesh would do harm, even merely by being present, unless the remission of sins so profited that the concupiscence which is in them can, in one born, both be present and do harm, but in one reborn can indeed be present, yet not do harm. But it so harms those born that, unless they are reborn, it can profit nothing if they are born of the reborn. For the vice of origin remains in the offspring, so as to make it guilty; even if in the parent the guilt of the same vice has been washed away by the remission of sins, until every vice—by consenting to which one sins—is consumed by the final regeneration:

that is, by the very renewal of the flesh, which is promised to be in his resurrection, where we shall not only do no sins, but shall not even have any vicious desires, by consenting to which we might sin; to which blessed perfection one arrives by the grace of the holy bath now given. For by that regeneration of the spirit, by which it now comes about that all past sins are remitted, by its merit there will come about also the regeneration of the flesh unto eternal life, by which, in the very flesh rising again incorruptibly, the incitements of all sins will be healed. But that salvation has as yet been made in hope, is not held in reality; nor is it possessed through presence, but awaited through patience.

**40. 44.** And on this account, not only all sins, of which now the remission is made in Baptism—which make one guilty, while one consents to vicious desires and sins; but even the vicious desires themselves, by which, if one does not consent, no guilt of sin is contracted, which will not exist in that other life, but in this—all are purged by the same bath of Baptism.

**40. 45.** And so the guilt of that vice of which we are speaking will remain in the carnal offspring of the regenerated, until there too it be washed away by the bath of regeneration. For one regenerated does not regenerate the children of the flesh, but generates; and on this account he passes into them not what he is as regenerated, but what he is as generated.

So, then, whether a guilty unbeliever or an absolved believer, neither begets absolved children, but guilty ones: just as not only the seeds of the wild olive, but even the seeds of the olive, do not generate olives, but wild olives. So, then, the first birth holds a human being in condemnation, from which only the second frees.

The devil, then, holds; Christ frees: the deceiver of Eve holds, the Son of Mary frees: the one who came to the man through his wife holds, the one who was born of a wife who endured no man frees: the one who brought the occasion of lust to the woman holds, the one who was conceived without lust in a woman frees. He could indeed hold all through the one, nor does any free from his dominion but the one whom he could not hold.

Finally, the very sacraments of the Church, which she celebrates with such authority of ancient tradition that, although these men reckon them to be done in the little ones rather in pretense than truly, they nevertheless do not dare to spurn them with open disapproval: these very sacraments, I say, of the holy Church indicate sufficiently that little ones, even those freshest from birth, are freed through the grace of Christ from the servitude of the devil. For besides that they are baptized for the remission of sins, by a mystery not deceptive but faithful, the contrary power is also first exorcized in them and breathed out; to which, by the words of those by whom they are carried, they answer that they renounce it. By all these consecrated and evident signs of hidden realities, they are shown to pass from the worst captor to the best redeemer; who, for our sake having taken on weakness, bound the strong one, that he might snatch away his vessels[^111]: because the weak thing of God is stronger not only than human beings[^112], but even than angels. And so God, freeing the small with the great, shows in both what the Truth spoke through the Apostle. For not only those greater in age, but even the small ones, he *rescues from the power of darkness*, to transfer them *into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^113].

## Why does the goodness of God create what the malice of the devil possesses?

**40. 46.** Nor let anyone marvel and say: "Why, then, does the goodness of God create what the malice of the devil possesses?" For he bestows this on the seeds of his creature out of that goodness by which he also *makes his sun to rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust*[^114]. For by this goodness he also blessed the seeds themselves, or by blessing established them: which blessing of a praiseworthy nature a condemnable fault did not take away.

Which fault, although, through the justice of the punishing God, it had power to make human beings be born with the vice of original sin, did not nevertheless have power that human beings should not be born. Just as in those greater in age, any vices of sins do not take away the human being from the human being; but the good work of God remains, in however great evil works of the impious. For even if a human being, set in honor, and not understanding, is compared to the beasts, and is made like them[^115], he is nevertheless not made so like that he is a beast.

For he is compared through vice, not through nature; not by the vice of a beast, but of his nature. For a human being is of such great excellence in comparison with a beast that the vice of a human being is the nature of a beast: nor yet on that account is the nature of a human being turned into the nature of a beast. And on this account God condemns the human being on account of the vice by which the nature is dishonored, not on account of the nature, which is not taken away by the vice. But far be it that we should suppose the beasts liable to the penalty of condemnation, which it is just should be free from misery, since they cannot be partakers of blessedness either.

What wonder, then, is it, or what injustice, that a human being should be made subject to an unclean spirit, not on account of his nature, but on account of his uncleanness, which, coming not from the divine work but from human will, he contracted in the stain of his origin: since even the unclean spirit itself is good, in that it is a spirit; evil, in that it is unclean? For the former is from the work of God, the latter from its own will. And so a stronger nature—that is, the angelic—holds an inferior nature—that is, the human—made subject by the fellowship of vice. Therefore the Mediator, stronger than the angels, was made weak for the sake of human beings: so the pride of the captor is destroyed by the humility of the Redeemer; so that he who glories over the children of man by angelic strength may be conquered by the Son of God through human weakness taken on.

## Let us make Ambrose answer the slanderous talkativeness of these men concerning original sin too.

**41. 47.** But now, about to conclude this book too, we think it fitting that, as we did concerning grace, so concerning original sin too—in the deleting of which grace itself is more evidently commended—we make Ambrose, the prelate of God, whose most pure faith Pelagius proclaims especially among the ecclesiastical writers of the Latin tongue, answer the slanderous talkativeness of these men. In the work which he wrote *On the Resurrection*, the holy Ambrose says: "I fell in Adam, I was cast out of paradise in Adam, I died in Adam: whom he does not call back unless he find me in Adam, so that, as in him liable to guilt, owing death, so in Christ justified." Likewise, writing against the Novatians, he says: "All human beings are born under sin, whose very origin is in vice, as you have read, David saying: *Behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins did my mother bear me*[^116].

Therefore the flesh of Paul was a body of death, as he himself says: *Who will free me from the body of this death?*[^117] But the flesh of Christ condemned sin, which by being born it did not feel, which by dying it crucified; that in our flesh there might be justification through grace, where before there was defilement through fault"[^118]. Likewise the same man, when he was expounding the prophet Isaiah, speaking of Christ: "Therefore," he says, "as a human being he was tempted in all things, and in the likeness of human beings endured all things: but as one born of the Spirit he abstained from sin[^119]. *For every human being is a liar*[^120]; and no one is without sin, except the one God. It was provided, therefore, that no one born of man and woman—that is, through that commingling of bodies—should be seen to be free of sin. But the one who is free of sin is free also of a conception of this kind."

Likewise, when he was expounding the Gospel according to Luke: "For it was not the union with a man," he says, "that unsealed the secret places of the virginal womb, but the Holy Spirit poured an immaculate seed into the inviolable womb. For alone among those born of woman is the Lord Jesus holy in all things, who, by the newness of his immaculate birth, did not feel the contagions of earthly corruption, and by his heavenly majesty drove them away"[^121].

## Let Pelagius repent of having thought against Ambrose.

**41. 48.** Yet these words of the man of God, whom Pelagius himself praised with so great a proclamation, he contradicts, and says, "that just as without virtue, so we are begotten without vice." What, then, remains, except either that Pelagius condemn this error of his, or that he repent of having so praised Ambrose? But because the blessed Ambrose spoke these things according to the Catholic faith, as a Catholic bishop, it follows that Pelagius, gone astray from this way of faith, has deservedly been condemned, with his disciple Caelestius, by the authority of the Catholic Church, unless he repent of not having praised Ambrose, but having thought against Ambrose. I know that you read insatiably the things which are written for the edification or confirmation of the faith: nevertheless this book, however useful it may be for this, must now at last at some point be ended.
