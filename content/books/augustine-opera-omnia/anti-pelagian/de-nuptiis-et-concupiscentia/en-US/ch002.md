# Book One

**1. 1. 1.** The new heretics, most beloved son Valerius, who contend that the medicine of Christ, by which sins are healed, is not necessary for little ones born of the flesh, most spitefully cry out that we are condemners of marriage and of the divine work by which God creates human beings from males and females, because we say that those who are born of such intercourse contract original sin, of which the Apostle says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed upon all men, in whom all sinned*[^1], and because we do not deny that they, of whatever sort the parents from whom they are born, are still under the devil unless they are reborn in Christ and, rescued by his grace from the power of darkness, are transferred into the kingdom of him who was unwilling to be born from that same intercourse of the two sexes. Because, then, we say this, which is contained in the most ancient and most firm rule of the catholic faith, these assertors of a novel and perverse teaching, who say that there is no sin in little ones that is washed away by the bath of regeneration, slander us, faithlessly or ignorantly, as though we condemned marriage and as though we said that the work of God, that is, the human being who is born of marriage, is the work of the devil. They do not notice that, just as the good of marriage cannot be accused on account of the original evil that is contracted from it, so the evil of adulteries and fornications cannot be excused on account of the natural good that is born from it. For just as sin, whether contracted by little ones from this side or that, is the work of the devil, so the human being, whether born from this side or that, is the work of God. The intention of this book, then, is this: that, as far as the Lord deigns to help us, we may distinguish the evil of carnal desire, on account of which a human being who is born through it contracts original sin, from the goodness of marriage. For this shameful desire, which is shamelessly praised by the shameless, would not exist had the human being not sinned first; but marriage would exist even if no one had sinned, since the sowing of children would take place without this disease in a body of that life, without which it now cannot take place in the body of this death.

**1. 2. 2.** But why I wished to write to you in particular on this matter, there are three chief reasons, which I will briefly explain. One is that, by the gift of Christ, your observance of conjugal chastity is great. Another is that, by your authority, in caring for and pressing the matter, you effectively resisted these profane novelties, which we here resist by disputation. The third is that I have learned that a certain piece of writing composed by them has come into your hands, which, though you laughed at it with most robust faith, it is nevertheless good that we should know how to help by defending what we believe; for the apostle Peter directs that *we be ready to give satisfaction to everyone who asks of us an account of* our *faith* and *our hope*[^2]. And the apostle Paul says, *Let your speech be seasoned with salt, that you may know how you ought to answer everyone*[^3]. These are chiefly the things that compelled me to have with you in this volume such speech as the Lord might deign to grant. For it has never pleased me to thrust some little work of mine to be read upon any illustrious man, distinguished by such eminence as you yourself are—especially one not yet enjoying a leisured dignity, but still occupied with public and indeed military affairs—when he had not asked me for it, doing so not so much diligently as shamelessly. If, then, I have now done anything of the sort, for the reasons I have recalled, graciously forgive me, and turn a kindly mind to what follows.

**1. 3. 3.** That conjugal chastity too is a gift of God, the most blessed Paul shows, where, speaking of this matter, he says, *I wish all men to be as I myself am; but each has his own gift from God, one in this way, another in that*[^4]. See, he said that this too is a gift from God—though lower than that continence in which he wished all to be as himself—nevertheless a gift from God. From this we understand that, when these things are commanded to be done, nothing else is shown than that, in order to receive and have them, our own will too must be present in us. But when they are shown to be gifts of God, we learn from whom they are to be sought if they are not had, and to whom thanks are to be given if they are had, and that our wills avail little to seek, take, and retain these things unless they are helped from on high.

**1. 3. 4.** What, then, do we say, when conjugal chastity is found even in certain irreligious people? Are they to be said to sin in this, that they use a gift of God badly, not referring it to the worship of him from whom they received it? Or perhaps these things are not even to be thought gifts of God when unbelievers do them, according to the saying of the Apostle, *Everything that is not from faith is sin*[^5]? But who would dare to say that a gift of God is sin? For the soul and the body and whatever goods of soul and body are naturally implanted are gifts of God even in sinners, since it is God, not they themselves, who made these things. But of the things that they do it is said, *Everything that is not from faith is sin*[^6]. When, therefore, people without faith do these things that seem to pertain to conjugal chastity, whether seeking to please human beings, either themselves or others, or avoiding human troubles in those matters that they viciously desire, or serving demons, sins are not restrained but other sins are overcome by other sins. Far be it, then, that anyone be truly called chaste who does not keep faith with his wife in marriage for the sake of the true God.

**1. 4. 5.** The union of male and female for the sake of begetting is, then, the natural good of marriage. But one uses this good badly who uses it in a bestial way, so that his intention is on the pleasure of lust, not on the purpose of offspring. And yet in some animals that lack reason, as in most birds, a kind of compact of marriages, as it were, is preserved—a shared diligence in nest-building, the times for hatching the eggs apportioned between them in turn, and the alternating labor of nurturing their young—which makes them seem, when they mate, to be carrying out the business of continuing their kind rather than of satisfying lust. Of these two, the former in the beast is like the human being, the latter in the human being is like the beast. But what I said pertains to the nature of marriage—that male and female be joined in a fellowship for begetting and so not defraud each other, just as every fellowship naturally does not want a fraudulent partner—this evident good, when unbelievers have it, they turn into evil and sin, because they use it without faith. In this way, then, the marriages of the faithful turn even that desire of the flesh, by which *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^7], to the use of righteousness. For they have the intention of begetting those who are to be reborn, so that those who are born of them as children of this age may be reborn as children of God. Therefore those who beget children not with this intention, this will, this end—to transfer them from being members of the first man into being members of Christ—but who as unbelieving parents glory in unbelieving offspring, even if they are of such great observance that, according to the marriage tablets, they have intercourse only for the sake of procreating children, do not have true conjugal chastity in them. For since chastity is a virtue, to which the contrary vice is unchastity, and since all virtues, even those that operate through the body, dwell in the mind, how is the body asserted to be chaste by true reasoning when the mind itself fornicates away from the true God? This fornication that holy psalm denounces where it says, *For behold, those who put themselves far from you shall perish; you have destroyed everyone who fornicates away from you*[^8]. True chastity, then, whether conjugal or widowed or virginal, is not to be spoken of except as that which is given over to true faith. For although, by right judgment, consecrated virginity is preferred to marriage, what sober-minded Christian would not set even Catholic Christian wives who have had but one husband not only before the Vestals but also before heretical virgins? So much does faith avail, of which the Apostle says, *Everything that is not from faith is sin*[^9]; and of which it is likewise written to the Hebrews, *Without faith it is impossible to please God*[^10].

**1. 5. 6.** Since this is so, surely those err who, when carnal lust is blamed, suppose that marriage is being condemned, as though this disease came from marriage and not from sin. Were not those first spouses, whose marriage God blessed, saying, *Increase and multiply*[^11], *naked, and they were not ashamed*[^12]? Why, then, after sin came shame from those members, except because there arose there an unseemly movement that, had human beings not sinned, marriage would without doubt not have had? Or perhaps, as some suppose—because they attend too little carefully to what they read—were the first human beings created blind, like dogs, and (what is more absurd) did they obtain sight, not by growing as dogs do, but by sinning? Far be it to believe this. But what moves those who think this is what is read: *Taking of its fruit she ate; and she gave to her husband with her, and they ate. And the eyes of both were opened, and they recognized that they were naked*[^13]. Hence it is that those who understand too little suppose that their eyes had previously been closed, because divine Scripture testifies that they were then opened. But did Hagar too, the maidservant of Sarah, when her son was thirsting and crying, open her eyes and see the well, have her eyes closed before[^14]? Or did those two disciples after the Lord's resurrection walk on the road with him with their eyes closed, of whom the Gospel says that *in the breaking of bread their eyes were opened, and they recognized him*[^15]? What is written, then, of the first human beings, *The eyes of both were opened*[^16], we ought to understand as their being made attentive to behold and recognize what new thing had occurred in their body, which body certainly lay before their open eyes every day, both naked and known. Otherwise, how could Adam, with the land animals and all the flying creatures brought to him, have given them names with his eyes closed, which he would not do except by distinguishing them, and he could not distinguish except by seeing? How, finally, was the woman herself shown to him, when he said, *This now is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh*[^17]? Finally, if anyone be so contentious as to say that he could do these things not by seeing but by touching, what will he say to what is read there, that the woman saw the tree, of which she was about to take the forbidden food, that it was *beautiful to the eyes to behold*[^18]? So then they *were naked and were not ashamed*[^19]—not because they did not see, but because they felt nothing in the members they saw whereby they might be ashamed. For it was not said, *They were both naked* and did not know it, but, they were not *ashamed*. For because nothing had gone before that was not lawful, nothing had followed that would cause shame.

**1. 6. 7.** There, when the human being had first transgressed God's law, he began to have in his members another law warring against his mind, and he felt the evil of his own disobedience when he found that the disobedience of his flesh had been most fittingly repaid to him. For such an opening of the eyes the serpent too had promised by seducing him—namely, the knowing of something that would better be unknown. Then indeed the human being felt in himself what he had done; then he distinguished evil from good not by lacking it but by suffering it. For it was unjust that he should be obeyed by his servant, that is, by his own body, who had not obeyed his Lord. For what is it that the eyes, lips, tongue, hands, feet, and the bendings of the back, neck, and sides are placed in our power to be moved to their proper tasks, when we have a body free of impediments and sound; but when it comes to begetting children, the members created for this work do not serve the will's command, but it is waited for that lust should move them, as though by its own right, and sometimes it does not act when the mind wills, while sometimes it acts even when the mind is unwilling? Would not human free choice blush at this—that, by despising God who commands, it had lost its own command even over its own members? But where could it be more fittingly shown that human nature was depraved by the deserved penalty of disobedience than in these disobedient parts, from which, by succession, that very nature subsists? For it is on this account that these parts of the body are properly called by the name of "nature." And so, since those first human beings had felt this movement—unseemly because disobedient—in their flesh and had blushed at their nakedness, they covered those same members with fig leaves, so that at least by the choice of those ashamed there might be veiled what was not moved by the choice of those willing; and since what was unseemly pleased them, by covering it what was seemly might be done.

**1. 7. 8.** Because, then, the good of marriage could not perish even with this evil added to it, the imprudent suppose that this is not an evil but pertains to that good. But it is distinguished not only by subtle reasoning but also by the most common natural judgment, which appeared in those first human beings and is still held today by married people. For what they afterward did by propagation is the good of marriage; but what they first covered out of shame is the evil of desire, which everywhere shuns sight and seeks privacy out of shame. Accordingly, marriage glories because it makes something good even out of that evil, and it blushes because it cannot take place without it. As if someone with an injured foot were to reach some good even by limping—the arrival there is not bad on account of the evil of the limping, nor is the limping good on account of the good of the arrival—so neither ought we to condemn marriage on account of the evil of lust, nor to praise lust on account of the good of marriage.

**1. 8. 9.** This indeed is the disease of which the Apostle, speaking even to the married faithful, says, *For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from fornication, that each of you know how to possess his own vessel in sanctification and honor, not in the disease of desire like the gentiles, who do not know God*[^20]. Therefore the married believer should not only not use another's vessel, which those do who covet others' wives, but should not even know how to possess his own in the disease of carnal desire. This is not to be taken as though the Apostle forbade conjugal—that is, lawful and honorable—intercourse, but so that this intercourse, which would have had nothing of diseased lust joined to it had the choice of freedom not perished in it through preceding sin, but which now has it joined to it, should be a matter not of will but of necessity—without which, however, in the procreating of children the fruit of the will itself cannot be attained. This will, in the marriages of the faithful, is not determined by the end that children may be born who will pass away in this age, but that they may be reborn to remain in Christ. If this comes about, the reward from the marriage will be one of full happiness; but if it does not come about, there will be peace for spouses of good will. With this intention of heart, whoever possesses his vessel, that is, his spouse, without doubt possesses it not *in the disease of desire, like the gentiles who do not know God*, but *in sanctification and honor*[^21], like the faithful who hope in God. For a man uses that evil of desire, and is not overcome by it, when he reins in and restrains it as it seethes with disordered and unseemly movements and relaxes and applies it only with a view to offspring, so that he may carnally beget those who are to be spiritually reborn, not subject the spirit to the flesh in vile servitude. No Christian ought to doubt that the holy fathers from Abraham, and before Abraham, to whom God gives testimony that they pleased him, used their wives in this way, since to some of them, individually, it was even granted to have several wives, where there was a reason of multiplying offspring, not an appetite for varying pleasure.

**1. 9. 10.** For if that multitude of wives had not displeased the God of our fathers, who is also ours, for the reason that lust might more abundantly flaunt itself, then the holy women too would each have served several husbands. But if any woman did so, what would compel her but the baseness of desire, to have several husbands, since by that license she would not have more children? Yet that it pertains more to the good of marriage to have not one man and many women, but one man and one woman, is sufficiently indicated by that first union of spouses, made by God himself, so that marriages might take their beginning there, where a more honorable example might be observed. But as the human race advanced, good women were joined to certain good men, several to one man. From this it appears that the former sought rather the modesty of dignity, and the latter was permitted by the nature of fruitfulness. For rule too can more naturally belong to one over many than to many over one. Nor can it be doubted that, by the natural order, men have rule over women rather than women over men. Observing this, the Apostle says, *The head of the woman is the man*[^22]; and, *Wives, be subject to your husbands*[^23]; and the apostle Peter says, *As Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord*[^24]. Although this is so—that the nature of ruling principles loves singularity, while we more easily see plurality among the subject—nevertheless several women would never be lawfully joined to one man unless more children were born from it. Hence if one woman lies with several men, since there is for her from this no multiplying of offspring but only a frequenting of lust, she cannot be a wife, but a harlot.

**1. 10. 11.** Since indeed not only fruitfulness, whose fruit is in offspring, nor only chastity, whose bond is faith, but also a certain sacrament of marriage is commended to the married faithful (whence the Apostle says, *Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church*[^25]), the reality of this sacrament is, without doubt, that the male and female joined in marriage should persevere inseparably as long as they live, and that it not be lawful, except for the cause of fornication, for one spouse to be separated from the other. For this is preserved in Christ and the Church, that the living should never be separated from the living by any divorce. So great is the observance of this sacrament *in the city of our God, in his holy mountain*[^26], that is, in the Church of Christ, among all the married faithful, who without doubt are members of Christ, that, although women marry or wives are taken for the sake of procreating children, it is not lawful to abandon even a barren spouse in order to take another who is fruitful. If anyone does this, he is guilty of adultery—not by the law of this age, where, by an intervening divorce, it is permitted without crime to contract other marriages with others (which the Lord testifies that even holy Moses permitted the Israelites because of their hardness of heart[^27]), but by the law of the Gospel; as is also that woman, if she marries another. To such a degree do the rights of marriage, once entered upon, remain between the living, that those who are separated from each other are spouses to each other rather than to those to whom they have attached themselves. For with others they would not be adulterers unless they remained spouses to each other. Finally, when the husband with whom there was a true marriage has died, a true marriage can be made with the one with whom there was previously adultery. So there remains between the living something conjugal, which neither separation nor union with another can take away. But it remains for the harm of guilt, not for the bond of the covenant—just as the soul of an apostate, withdrawing as it were from marriage with Christ, even with faith lost, does not lose the sacrament of faith that it received in the bath of regeneration. For it would without doubt be restored to one returning, had he lost it in departing. But the one who has withdrawn has this for the heaping up of punishment, not for the merit of reward.

**1. 11. 12.** But for those who have agreed by common consent to abstain forever from the use of carnal desire, far be it that the conjugal bond between them should be broken; rather it will be the firmer, the more they have entered upon those pacts together that are to be kept more dearly and harmoniously—not by the pleasurable bonds of bodies but by the willing affections of minds. For it was not falsely said by the angel to Joseph, *Do not fear to take Mary your wife*[^28]. She is called wife from the first faith of betrothal, which she had neither known nor was to know by intercourse; nor had the appellation "wife" perished or remained false, where there had not been nor was to be any union of flesh. For that virgin was therefore the more holily and wondrously pleasing to her husband, because she was even fruitful without a husband—unlike him in offspring, like him in faith. On account of which faithful marriage both deserved to be called the parents of Christ—and not only she his mother, but also he his father, as being the husband of his mother—both by mind, not by flesh. Yet whether he was a father by mind alone or she a mother also by flesh, both were nonetheless parents of his humility, not of his sublimity; of his weakness, not of his divinity. For the Gospel does not lie, where it is read, *And his father and mother were marveling at the things that were said of him*[^29]. And in another place, *And his parents went every year to Jerusalem*[^30]. Likewise a little later, *And his mother said to him: Son, why have you done so to us? Behold, your father and I were grieving as we sought you*[^31]. But he, to show that he had, besides them, a Father who begot him without a mother, answered them, *Why is it that you sought me? Did you not know that I must be about my Father's business?*[^32] And again, lest by this saying he be thought to have denied those parents, the Evangelist went on and added, *And they did not understand the word that he spoke to them, and he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was subject to them*[^33]. Subject to whom but his parents? And who was subject but Jesus Christ, *who, though he was in the form of God, did not think it robbery to be equal to God*[^34]? Why, then, was he subject to those who were far below the form of God, except because *he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant*[^35], of which form they were the parents? But if she had given birth though he did not sow the seed, surely both would not have been parents even of that form of a servant, unless they were spouses to each other, even without union of flesh. Hence also the series of generations, when the parents of Christ are recalled by the connection of succession, had to be carried down rather to Joseph, as in fact it was, lest in that marriage an injury be done to the male sex, which is certainly the more excellent, since nothing of the truth would be lost, because both Joseph and Mary were of the seed of David, from whom Christ was foretold to come.

**1. 11. 13.** And so every good of marriage was fulfilled in those parents of Christ: offspring, faith, sacrament. We recognize the offspring in the Lord Jesus himself; the faith, because there was no adultery; the sacrament, because there was no divorce.

**1. 12. 13.** Only nuptial intercourse was lacking there, because in the flesh of sin it could not take place without that desire of the flesh which befell from sin—without which he willed to be conceived who was to be without sin, not in the flesh of sin but in the likeness of the flesh of sin, so that hereby too he might teach that all flesh that is born of intercourse is flesh of sin, since only that flesh which was not born thereof was not flesh of sin. And yet conjugal intercourse, which is done with the intention of begetting, is not itself sin, because the good will of the mind leads the bodily pleasure that follows, and does not follow it leading, nor is human choice dragged along by sin subjugating it, when the wound of sin is justly turned to the use of begetting. A certain itching of this wound reigns in the foulnesses of adulteries and fornications and all sorts of debaucheries and uncleannesses, but in the bonds of marriage it serves. There, by such a master, baseness is condemned; here, by such an attendant, honorableness blushes. This lust, then, is not the good of marriage, but the obscenity of those who sin, the necessity of those who beget, the ardor of wantonness, the shame of marriage. Why, then, should those not remain spouses who by consent cease to have intercourse, if Joseph and Mary remained spouses who did not even begin to have intercourse?

**1. 13. 14.** For this propagation of children, which among the holy fathers was a most dutiful service for the begetting and preserving of the people of God, among whom the prophecy of Christ had to go before, now no longer has that necessity. For now there is an abundance of children to be spiritually begotten from all the nations, wherever they may have been born carnally. And what is written, *A time for embracing, and a time for refraining from embracing*[^36], is recognized as distributed through that time and this: for that was the time for embracing, but this is the time for refraining from embracing.

**1. 13. 15.** And so the Apostle too, when he spoke of this, said, *But this I say, brethren: the time is short; it remains that those who have wives be as though not having them, and those who weep as though not weeping, and those who rejoice as though not rejoicing, and those who buy as though not buying, and those who use this world as though they did not use it; for the figure of this world passes away. I wish you to be without anxiety*[^37]. All this, to say something briefly here by way of exposition, I think is to be understood thus. *This*, he says, *I say, brethren: the time is short*—the people of God is no longer to be propagated by carnal generation but is now to be gathered by spiritual regeneration. *It remains*, then, *that those who have wives* not be subjected to carnal desire, *and those who weep* at the sadness of present evil rejoice in the hope of future good, and *those who rejoice* on account of some temporal advantage fear the eternal judgment, *and those who buy* possess by so having that they do not cleave by loving, *and those who use this world* think of themselves as passing through, not remaining; *for the figure of this world passes away. I wish you*, he says, *to be without anxiety*—that is, I wish you to have your heart on high in those things that do not pass you by. Then he adds and says, *He who is without a wife is concerned about the things of the Lord, how he may please the Lord; but he who is joined in marriage is concerned about the things of the world, how he may please his wife*[^38]. And so in a way he expounds what he had said above, *Let those who have wives be as though not having them*. For those who so have wives that they are concerned about the things of the Lord, how they may please the Lord, and are not concerned, in the things of the world, to please their wives, are as though not having them. This is done with the more ease when the wives too are such that their husbands please them not because they are rich, or exalted, or noble by birth, or lovable in the flesh, but because they are faithful, religious, chaste, and good men.

**1. 14. 16.** But in the married, as these things are to be desired and praised, so other things are to be tolerated, lest one fall into damnable disgraces, that is, into fornications or adulteries. On account of avoiding which evil, even those acts of intercourse of spouses that are not done for the cause of begetting but serve victorious desire—in which they are bidden not to defraud each other, lest Satan tempt them because of their incontinence—are indeed not commanded by way of precept, yet are conceded by way of indulgence. For thus it is written, *Let the husband render the debt to his wife, and likewise the wife to her husband. The woman does not have power over her own body, but the man; and likewise the man does not have power over his own body, but the woman. Do not defraud each other, except by consent for a time, that you may have leisure for prayer, and return again to it, lest Satan tempt you because of your incontinence. But this I say by way of indulgence, not by way of precept*[^39]. Where, therefore, indulgence is to be given, by no reasoning will it be denied that there is something of fault. Since, then, intercourse with the intention of begetting, which is properly to be imputed to marriage, is not culpable, what does the Apostle concede by way of indulgence except that spouses, while they do not contain themselves, demand the debt of the flesh from each other not from the will of offspring but from the pleasure of lust? Yet this pleasure does not fall into fault on account of marriage, but receives indulgence on account of marriage. Wherefore from this too marriages are praiseworthy, because even that which does not pertain to themselves they cause to be pardoned on their own account. For not even this intercourse, by which desire is served, is so done that the offspring which marriage requires is impeded.

**1. 15. 17.** But still it is one thing not to have intercourse except with the sole will of begetting, which has no fault; another to seek the pleasure of the flesh in intercourse, but not outside one's spouse, which has venial fault, because, although intercourse is not had for the cause of propagating offspring, yet the propagation of offspring is not for the cause of this lust obstructed, whether by an evil wish or an evil deed. For those who do this, although they are called spouses, are not, nor do they retain any truth of marriage, but hold out an honorable name to veil baseness. They are exposed, however, when they go so far as to expose the children who are born against their will. For they hate to nourish or to have those whom they feared to beget. And so, when dark iniquity rages against its own children, whom it begot unwillingly, it is brought out into the light by clear iniquity, and the hidden baseness is convicted by manifest cruelty. Sometimes this lustful cruelty, or cruel lust, goes so far that it even procures poisons of sterility, and, if these avail nothing, somehow extinguishes and pours out within the womb the offspring conceived—willing its own offspring to perish before it lives, or, if it was already living in the womb, to be killed before it is born. Surely, if both are such, they are not spouses; and if they were such from the beginning, they came together not by marriage but rather by debauchery. But if both are not such, I dare to say: either she is in a way the harlot of her husband, or he the adulterer of his wife.

**1. 16. 18.** Since, therefore, marriages cannot be such as those of the first human beings could have been had sin not gone before, let them be such as those of the holy fathers were, so that the shameful desire of the flesh—which neither existed in paradise before sin nor was permitted to exist after sin, since it is necessary that it exist in the body of this death—be not served, but rather be itself compelled to serve only for the propagating of offspring. Or, since this time, which we have already said is the time for refraining from embracing, does not have the necessity of this office, so great a fertility of children to be spiritually begotten lying at hand everywhere among all the nations, let him rather, who can receive that good of excellent continence, receive it[^40]. But whoever cannot receive it, both *if he takes a wife, does not sin*[^41], and a woman, *if she does not contain herself, let her marry*[^42]. For *it is good for a man not to touch a woman*[^43]. But because *not all receive this word, but those to whom it is given*[^44], it remains *that, on account of fornications, each man have his own wife and each woman have her own husband*[^45]; and so the weakness of incontinence, lest it fall into the ruin of disgraces, is taken up by the honorableness of marriage. For what the Apostle says of women, *I wish therefore the younger to marry*[^46], can be said of men too: I wish the younger to take wives, so that it may pertain to both sexes *to procreate children*, to be fathers and *mothers of households, to give no occasion to the adversary for the sake of reviling*[^47].

**1. 17. 19.** In marriages, nevertheless, let the nuptial goods be loved: offspring, faith, sacrament. But offspring not only that it be born, but also that it be reborn; for it is born to punishment unless it is reborn to life. And faith—not such as even unbelievers have between themselves out of jealousy for the flesh; for what man, however irreligious, wants an adulterous wife? Or what woman, however irreligious, wants an adulterous husband? This good in marriage is indeed natural, yet carnal. But a member of Christ ought to fear adultery in a spouse for the spouse's sake, not for his own, and to hope from Christ for the reward of the faith that he shows to his spouse. And the sacrament, which neither those separated nor those committing adultery lose, let spouses keep harmoniously and chastely. For it alone is what even a barren marriage retains by the right of piety, the hope of fruitfulness, for which it had been joined, being now lost. Let him praise these nuptial goods in marriage who wishes to praise marriage. But carnal desire is not to be imputed to marriage, but to be tolerated. For it is not a good coming from natural marriage, but an evil befalling from ancient sin.

**1. 18. 20.** On account of this, then, it comes about that even from the just and lawful marriages of the children of God, not children of God but children of the age are begotten; for those who beget, even if they are now reborn, do not beget from that whereby they are children of God, but from that whereby they are still children of the age. For there is a saying of the Lord, *The children of this age beget and are begotten*[^48]. And so, from the fact that we are still children of this age, *our outer man is corrupted*[^49], from this both the children of this age are begotten, and they do not become children of God unless they are reborn; but from the fact that we are children of God, *the inner man is renewed from day to day*[^50]. Although the outer man too has been sanctified through the bath of regeneration and has received the hope of future incorruption, on account of which it is deservedly called the temple of God. *Your bodies*, says the Apostle, *are the temple within you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God, and you are not your own; for you were bought at a great price. Glorify, therefore, and bear God in your body*[^51]. All this was said not only on account of present sanctification but chiefly on account of that hope of which he himself says elsewhere, *But we ourselves too, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^52]. If, then, according to the Apostle, the redemption of our body is awaited, surely what is awaited is still hoped for, not yet held. Hence he adds and says, *For in hope we were saved; but hope that is seen is not hope; for what one sees, why does he also hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^53]. Therefore it is not through that which we await, but through that which we tolerate, that carnal children are propagated. Far be it, then, that the faithful man, when he hears from the Apostle, *Love your wives*[^54], should love carnal desire in his wife, which he ought not to direct even in himself, hearing another Apostle, *Do not love the world nor the things that are in the world. Whoever loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him, because all that is in the world is the desire of the flesh and the desire of the eyes and the ambition of the age, which is not from the Father but is from the world. And the world will pass away and its desire; but he who does the will of God remains forever, just as God also remains forever*[^55].

**1. 19. 21.** From this desire of the flesh, then, whatever is born is born for the world, not for God; but it is born for God when it is reborn from water and the Spirit. The guilt of this desire regeneration alone remits, which generation contracts. Therefore let what has been generated be regenerated, because it cannot be otherwise, that what has been contracted be remitted. For that what is remitted in the parent should be contracted in the offspring comes about in wondrous ways indeed, yet it comes about. These things are invisible and, to unbelievers, incredible, yet true; and so that they might have some visible example, divine providence has provided this in certain trees. For why should we not believe that it was established for this purpose, that from the olive the wild olive should be born? Or is it not to be believed that, in some thing that was created for human uses, the Creator foresaw and established something that would serve as an example for the human race? It is a wonder, then, how those freed from the bond of sin through grace nevertheless beget those bound by the same bond, who must be freed in the same way; it is a wonder, we admit. But that the seeds of wild olives lie hidden even in the seeds of olives—when would this be believed, were it not proved by experience? Accordingly, just as a wild olive is begotten from the seed of a wild olive, and from the seed of an olive nothing but a wild olive—though there is the greatest difference between a wild olive and an olive—so from the flesh of a sinner and from the flesh of a just man each begets a sinner, though there is the greatest difference between a sinner and a just man. But a sinner is begotten, as yet no actual one and new in birth, but old in guilt: a human being from the Creator, a captive from the deceiver, a needy one from the Redeemer. But it is asked how the captivity of offspring can be contracted even from parents already redeemed. And because it is not tracked down by easy reasoning nor explained in speech, it is not believed by unbelievers—as if what we said of the wild olive and the olive, that of an unlike kind the offspring is like, were easily found by any reasoning or explained in speech. But this can be discerned by one who is willing to make trial; let it therefore serve as an example, whereby that too may be believed which cannot be discerned.

**1. 20. 22.** For the Christian faith, which the new heretics have begun to attack, does not doubt both that those who are washed in the bath of regeneration are redeemed from the power of the devil, and that those who have not yet been redeemed by such regeneration—even the little children of the redeemed—are held captive under the power of that same devil, unless they too be redeemed by the same grace of Christ. For we do not doubt that to all ages pertains that benefit of God of which the Apostle speaks, *who rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^56]. From this power of darkness, then, of which the devil is prince—that is, from the power of the devil and his angels—whoever denies that little ones are rescued when they are baptized is refuted by the very truth of the Church's sacraments, which no heretical novelty is permitted to take away or change in the Church of Christ, the head ruling and aiding his whole body, the little ones together with the great. In truth, then, not in falsity, the diabolical power is exorcised in little ones, and they renounce it—because through their own powers they cannot, through the hearts and mouths of those who carry them—so that, rescued from the power of darkness, they may be transferred into the kingdom of their Lord. What, then, is there in them, by which they are held in the power of the devil until they are rescued thence through the sacrament of the baptism of Christ? What is it, except sin? For the devil found nothing else by which he could bind human nature to his own right—that nature which the good Author established good. But little ones have committed no sin of their own in their life. There remains, therefore, original sin, through which they are held captive under the power of the devil, unless they be redeemed thence by the bath of regeneration and the blood of Christ, and pass into the kingdom of their Redeemer, the power of their captor being thwarted, and power being given by which they may become, from children of this age, children of God.

**1. 21. 23.** Now, then, if in a manner we should question those nuptial goods, whence sin could be propagated through them into little ones, the work of propagating offspring will answer us: I would be happier in paradise, if sin had not been admitted. For to me pertains that blessing of God, *Increase and multiply*[^57]. For this good work the different members of the different sex were created, which indeed existed before sin but were not shameful. The faith of chastity will answer: If sin had not been, what could have been more secure for me in paradise, where neither my own lust would goad me nor another's lust tempt me? The sacrament of marriage will also answer: Of me it was said in paradise, before sin, *A man shall leave his father and mother and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh*[^58]. *This great sacrament*, the Apostle says, *is in Christ and in the Church*[^59]. What, therefore, is great in Christ and in the Church is least in each and every husband and wife, yet a sacrament of inseparable union. Which of these is in marriage, whereby the bond of sin might pass into posterity? Surely none; and certainly the goodness of marriage would hold itself perfectly by these three goods, by which goods marriages are good even now.

**1. 22. 24.** But further, if that desire of the flesh be questioned, by which those things became shameful that previously had not been shameful, will it not answer that it began to be in the members of the human being after sin? And so it is called, in the words of the Apostle, the law of sin, because it has made the human being subject to itself, since he was unwilling to be subject to his God; and it is that very thing at which the first spouses then blushed, when they covered their shameful parts, and at which all now blush, when they seek out hidden places for intercourse, nor dare to have as witnesses of this work even the children whom they have begotten thereby. Against this natural shame the error of the Cynic philosophers struggled with wondrous shamelessness, since they judged that this act, because it was lawful and honorable, ought to be done openly with one's spouse. Hence deservedly the uncleanness of this shamelessness received a canine name; for from this the Cynics were so named.

**1. 23. 25.** This desire altogether, this law of sin dwelling in the members, which the law of righteousness forbids us to obey—the Apostle saying, *Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, to obey its desires, nor present your members as weapons of iniquity to sin*[^60]—this desire, I say, which is expiated only by the sacrament of regeneration, surely passes the bond of sin by generation into posterity, unless they too be loosed from it by regeneration. For this desire itself is indeed no longer sin in the regenerate, when there is no consent to it for unlawful works, and the members are not given by the queen mind to perpetrate them, so that, if what is written, *You shall not desire*[^61], is not fulfilled, at least what is read elsewhere may be fulfilled, *Go not after your desires*[^62]. But because in a certain manner of speaking it is called sin, both because it was made by sin and because it makes sin if it conquers, its guilt avails in the one generated, which guilt the grace of Christ, through the remission of all sins in the regenerate, does not allow to avail, if he does not obey it as it commands, in a manner, to evil works. But it is called sin in this way, because it was made by sin, although in the regenerate it is no longer itself sin—just as speech is called "tongue," which the tongue makes, and writing is called "hand," which the hand makes. And likewise it is called sin because it makes sin if it conquers—just as cold is called "lazy," not because it is made by the lazy, but because it makes people lazy.

**1. 23. 26.** This wound inflicted on the human race by the devil compels whatever is born through it to be under the devil, as if he plucked the fruit by right from his own bush—not that human nature is from him, which is only from God, but the defect, which is not from God. For human nature is condemned not on account of itself, which is praiseworthy because it is the work of God, but on account of the damnable defect by which it was vitiated. And on account of that for which it is condemned, on account of this it is also subjugated to the damnable devil, because the devil himself too is an unclean spirit—and certainly "spirit" is good, "unclean" is evil; since by nature he is spirit, by defect he is unclean: of which two the former is from God, the latter from himself. He does not, therefore, hold human beings, whether of greater or of infant age, on account of their being human beings, but on account of their being unclean. Whoever, then, wonders that a creature of God is subject to the devil, let him not wonder; for a creature of God is subject to a creature of God, the lesser to the greater, because the human being to the angel—yet not on account of nature but on account of defect, because the unclean to the unclean. This is his fruit from the ancient stock of uncleanness that he planted in the human being—he himself indeed to suffer greater punishments at the last judgment, the more unclean he is; yet even those for whom there will be a more tolerable lot in that condemnation are subject to him as to the prince and author of sin, because there will be no cause of condemnation except sin.

**1. 24. 27.** Wherefore the devil holds those born guilty—not from the good whereby marriages are good, but from the evil of desire, which marriages indeed use well, but of which even marriages blush. For although marriages are honorable in all the goods that properly pertain to them, even if they have an unstained bed not only from fornications and adulteries, which are damnable disgraces, but also from those excesses of intercourse which are not done for the cause of offspring with the will ruling, but for the cause of pleasure with lust conquering—which are venial sins in spouses—nevertheless, when it comes to the work of begetting, even that lawful and honorable intercourse cannot take place without the ardor of lust, so that what belongs to reason may be carried out, not what belongs to lust. This ardor surely, whether it follow or precede the will, nevertheless by a sort of command of its own moves the members that cannot be moved by the will; and so it shows itself to be not the servant of one commanding, but the punishment of a disobedient will, to be moved not by free choice but by some enticing goad, and therefore shameful. From this desire of the flesh, which, though in the regenerate it is no longer reckoned as sin, yet does not befall nature except from sin—from this desire of the flesh, I say, as from a daughter of sin and (when there is consent to it for base things) also the mother of many sins, whatever offspring is born is bound by original sin, unless it be reborn in him whom the Virgin conceived without this desire, and who therefore, when he deigned to be born in the flesh, alone was born without sin.

**1. 25. 28.** But if it is asked how this desire of the flesh remains in the regenerate, in whom the remission of all sins has been made, since through it the carnal offspring is sown and begotten even of a baptized parent—or at any rate, if in a baptized parent it can be present and not be sin, why the very same thing is sin in the offspring—to this it is answered that the desire of the flesh is remitted in baptism, not so that it should not be, but so that it should not be imputed as sin. But although its guilt is now loosed, it nevertheless remains until all our infirmity is healed, as the renewal of the inner man advances from day to day, when the outer man shall have put on incorruption. For it does not remain substantially, like some body or spirit, but it is a certain affection of an evil quality, like a languor. Therefore nothing remains that is not remitted when it comes about, as it is written, *The Lord is gracious toward all our iniquities*[^63]; but until what follows also comes about, *who heals all your languors, who redeems your life from corruption*[^64], carnal desire remains in the body of this death, by whose vicious desires we are bidden not to obey for the perpetrating of unlawful things, lest sin reign in our mortal body. Yet this desire is daily diminished in those who advance and contain themselves, and most of all as old age also comes on. But in those who wickedly serve it, it gains such great strength that often, when the members are now failing with age, and those parts of the body are less able to be applied to that work, it does not cease to rave more basely and more boldly.

**1. 26. 29.** In those, then, who are regenerated in Christ, when they receive the remission of absolutely all sins, it is certainly necessary that the guilt also of this desire—though it still remains—be remitted, so that, as I said, it not be imputed as sin. For just as of those sins which cannot remain, since they pass away when they are committed, the guilt nevertheless remains and, unless it be remitted, will remain forever, so of that desire, when it is remitted, the guilt is taken away. For this is to have no sin: to be guilty of no sin. For if, for example, anyone has committed adultery, even if he never afterward commits it, he is guilty of adultery until the guilt of it is remitted by pardon. He therefore has sin, although that which he committed no longer is, because it passed away with the time in which it was done. For if to cease from sinning were to have no sins, it would suffice that Scripture admonish us of this, *Son, have you sinned? do not do it again*[^65]; but it does not suffice, and so it added, *And pray concerning your former sins, that they may be remitted to you*[^66]. They remain, then, unless they be remitted. But how do they remain, if they are past, except because they passed away in act, but remain in guilt? And so, conversely, it can come about that something remains in act, while it passes away in guilt.

**1. 27. 30.** For the desire of the flesh does something even when there is offered to it neither the heart's assent, where it might reign, nor the members as weapons, as it were, by which what it commands might be fulfilled. But what does it do except those very desires that are evil and base? For if they were good and lawful, the Apostle would not forbid us to obey them, saying, *Let not sin reign in your mortal body, to obey its desires*[^67]. For he does not say, To have its desire, but, *To obey its desires*—so that, since they are greater in some and lesser in others, according as each has advanced in the newness of the inner man, in this we may keep the contest of righteousness and chastity, that we not obey them. Yet we ought to will that those desires not even exist, even if in the body of this death we cannot obtain this. For hence also in another place the same Apostle, speaking as though by the introduction of his own person, instructs us, saying, *For it is not what I will that I do, but what I hate, that I do*[^68]—that is, I desire, because he would not want to do this either, so as to be perfect in every part. *But if what I do not will*, he says, *this I do, I consent to the law that it is good*[^69], because the law too does not want this, which I too do not want. For it does not want me to desire, that law which says, *You shall not desire*[^70], and I do not want to desire. In this, then, the will of the law and mine consent. But because he did not want to desire and yet desired, but, by no means consenting to that same desire, did not serve it, he added and said, *But now it is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^71].

**1. 28. 31.** But a man is much deceived who, consenting to the desire of his flesh and resolving to do what it desires and determining upon it, thinks that it must still be said by him, *It is not I that work it*[^72], even if he hates it, because he consents. For both are present together: he himself hates it, because he knows it to be evil, and he himself does it, because he has determined to do it. And if indeed he adds that too which Scripture forbids, saying, *Nor present your members as weapons of iniquity to sin*[^73], so that what he determines to do in his heart he also completes in his body, and therefore says, *It is not I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^74]—because, when he determines and does it, it displeases him—he errs so greatly that he does not even recognize himself, when, although he wholly is himself, both in heart determining and in body fulfilling, he still thinks that it is not himself.

**1. 29. 31.** Whoever, then, says, *It is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^75], speaks truly if he only desires, but not if he determines upon it by the consent of his heart, or even accomplishes it by the ministry of his body.

**1. 29. 32.** Then the Apostle adds, *For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, good; for to will is present to me, but to accomplish the good I do not find*[^76]. This was said for the reason that the good is then accomplished when there are no evil desires, just as evil is then accomplished when evil desires are obeyed. But when they indeed exist, yet are not obeyed, neither is evil accomplished, because they are not obeyed, nor good, because they exist; but in one part good comes about, because there is no consent to the evil desire, and in one part evil remains, because there is still desire. Therefore the Apostle did not say that to do the good was not present to him, but to accomplish it. For he does much good who does what is written, *Go not after your desires*[^77], but he does not accomplish it, because he does not fulfill what is written, *You shall not desire*[^78]. For to this end the law said, *You shall not desire*[^79], that, finding ourselves lying in this disease, we might seek the medicine of grace, and that in that precept we might know both whither we ought to strive by advancing in this mortality, and whither it can be attained by us in that most blessed immortality; for unless it were at some time to be accomplished, it would never have been commanded.

**1. 30. 33.** Then, repeating, so that he may further commend the foregoing sentence, the Apostle says, *For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do. But if I do what I will not, it is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^80]. And he goes on, *I find, then, a law for me who will to do good; since evil is present to me*[^81]—that is: I find the law to be good for me who will to do what the law wills; since it is not to the law itself, which says, *You shall not desire*, but to me that the evil which I do not will is present, because even unwilling I desire. *For I delight*, he says, *in the law of God according to the inner man, but I see another law in my members warring against the law of my mind and taking me captive under the law of sin which is in my members*[^82]. This delight in the law of God according to the inner man comes to us from the great grace of God. For in it *our inner man is renewed from day to day*[^83], when in it he advances perseveringly. For it is not fear that tortures, but love that takes pleasure. There we are truly free, where we are not delighted unwillingly.

**1. 30. 34.** But that which he says, *I see another law in my members warring against the law of my mind*[^84], is that very desire of which we speak, the law of sin in the flesh of sin. And what he said, *And taking me captive under the law of sin*[^85]—that is, under itself—*which is in my members*, either he said *taking captive* meaning trying to take captive, that is, compelling to consent and fulfill, or rather (which is not in dispute) taking captive according to the flesh, which, unless carnal desire held it—which he calls the law of sin—it certainly would not stir up in it any unlawful desire that the mind ought not to obey. But that he did not say, Taking my flesh captive, but, *taking me captive*, came about so that another sense here might be sought, and that we might take *taking captive* as though he had said, Trying to take captive. But why should he not say, *taking me captive*, where he wished his flesh to be understood? Was it not said of Jesus, when his flesh had not been found in the tomb, *They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him*[^86]? Surely it was not improperly said because it was not said, the Flesh or the Body of my Lord, but, *my Lord*?

**1. 31. 35.** Although a little earlier the Apostle himself made clear enough how he could rightly say of his flesh, *Taking me captive*[^87]. For when he had said, *For I know that there does not dwell in me*[^88], expounding this very thing he added and said, *that is, in my flesh, good*[^89]. This, then, is taken captive under the law of sin, in which *good does not dwell*, that is, the flesh. But he now called "flesh" that in which there is a certain diseased affection of the flesh, not the very conformation of the body, whose members are not to be applied as weapons to sin, that is, to that same desire which holds this carnal part of ours captive. For as regards the bodily substance and nature itself, in faithful men, whether married or continent, it is already the temple of God. And yet, if nothing at all of our flesh were held captive—not indeed under the devil, because there too the remission of sin has been made, so that sin is not imputed, which is properly called the law of sin, but under the law of sin itself, that is, under its own desire—unless our flesh were somewhat held captive, how would it be true that the same Apostle says, *Awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^90]? To this degree, then, the redemption of our body is still awaited, in that it is still in some part captive under the law of sin. Hence too, crying out here, he says, *Unhappy man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^91]. Where what are we to understand, except that *the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul*[^92]? When, therefore, this same body shall be received now incorruptible, there will be full liberation from the body of this death, from which those are not freed who are to rise again for punishment. To the body of this death, then, it is understood to pertain, that another law in the members indeed wars against the law of the mind, while *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^93], even if it does not subjugate the mind, because the spirit too desires against the flesh. And so, although the law of sin itself holds something of the flesh captive, whence it may resist the law of the mind, nevertheless it does not reign in our body, though mortal, if its desires are not obeyed. For even enemies against whom one fights are wont both to be inferior in the contest and, though conquered, to hold something captive; and this, in our flesh, although it be held under the law of sin, is yet in the hope of redemption, because that vicious desire itself will remain in no way at all, but our flesh, healed of that plague and disease and clothed in entire immortality, will remain in eternal blessedness.

**1. 31. 36.** But the Apostle adds and says, *So then I myself with the mind serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*[^94]. This is to be understood thus: *With the mind I serve the law of God* by not consenting to the law of sin; *but with the flesh* I serve *the law of sin* by having the desires of sin, which, although I do not consent to them, I do not yet wholly lack. Finally, let us attend to what he inferred after these things. *There is therefore now*, he says, *no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus*[^95]. Even now, he says, when the law in the members wars against the law of the mind and holds something captive in the body of this death, there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. Hear why. *For the law of the spirit of life*, he says, *in Christ Jesus has freed me from the law of sin and death*[^96]. Freed it how, except that it dissolved its guilt by the remission of all sins, so that, although it still remains and is more and more diminished from day to day, it is nevertheless not imputed as sin?

**1. 32. 37.** And so, as long as this remission of sins does not take place in the offspring, this law of sin is there in such a way that it is even imputed as sin—that is, so that its guilt too is with it, which holds the offspring a debtor of eternal punishment. For this the parent passes into his carnal offspring: what he himself is as carnally born, not what he is as spiritually reborn; because that very thing whereby he was carnally born, although, its guilt being loosed, it does not impede its fruit, nevertheless lies hidden as in the seed of the olive, even if, on account of the remission of sins, it does no harm to the oil—that is, to that life by which, according to Christ, who is named from oil, that is, from chrism, *the just man lives by faith*[^97]. But this, which in the regenerate parent, as in the seed of the olive, is covered over without any guilt, because it has been remitted, is surely held, in the offspring not yet regenerated, as in the wild olive, with guilt, until there too the same grace remits it. For from the time that Adam, from such an olive—in which there was not even seed of the kind from which the bitterness of the wild olive would be born—was turned by sinning into a wild olive (because it was so great a sin, where so great a change of nature for the worse took place), he made the whole human race a wild olive, so that (as we now see in the trees themselves), if the divine grace turns anything thence into an olive, there the defect of the first birth—which was original sin, transmitted and drawn from carnal desire—is remitted, covered over, not imputed; yet from it a wild olive is nonetheless born, unless it too be reborn into an olive by the same grace.

**1. 33. 38.** Blessed, then, is the olive whose *iniquities are remitted* and whose *sins are covered*; blessed, *to whom the Lord has not imputed sin*[^98]. But that which is remitted and covered and not imputed, until the full change into eternal immortality comes about, has a certain hidden power whence a bitter wild olive may be sown, unless there too the same husbandry of God remits, covers, does not impute. But there will be nothing at all vicious even in the carnal seed, when, by the same regeneration which now takes place through the sacred bath, purging and healing all the evils of the human being unto the end, the same flesh, through which the soul was made carnal, shall itself become also spiritual, having no desire of the flesh resisting the law of the mind, sending forth nothing of carnal seed. For thus is to be taken what the same Apostle says, *Christ loved the Church and gave himself for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the bath of water in the word, that he might present to himself the Church glorious, having no spot or wrinkle or anything of the sort*[^99]. Thus, I say, this is to be taken, that by the same bath of regeneration and word of sanctification absolutely all the evils of regenerated human beings are cleansed and healed—not only the sins which are all now remitted in baptism, but also those that are afterward contracted through human ignorance or weakness; not so that baptism be repeated as often as one sins, but because by that very baptism which is once given it comes about that not only beforehand but also afterward pardon of any sins whatsoever is obtained for the faithful. For what would penance profit, either before baptism, unless baptism followed, or afterward, unless it went before? In the Lord's Prayer too, which is our daily cleansing, with what fruit, with what effect would it be said, *Forgive us our debts*[^100], unless it were said by those who are baptized? And likewise the generosity and beneficence of almsgiving, however great, to whom in the end would it profit for the remitting of his sins, if he were not baptized? Finally, that very happiness of the kingdom of heaven, where the Church will have no spot or wrinkle or anything of the sort, where there will be nothing of reproach, nothing of pretense, where there will be not only no guilt but not even any desire of sin—whose will it be except the baptized'?

**1. 34. 39.** And by this not only all sins, but absolutely all the evils of human beings, are taken away by the holiness of the Christian bath, by which Christ cleanses his Church, that he may present her to himself, not in this age but in the age to come, having no spot or wrinkle or anything of the sort. For those who now say that she is such, and yet are in her—since they themselves confess that they have sins—if they speak the truth, then surely, because they are not clean from sins, the Church has in them a spot; but if they speak falsely, because they speak with a double heart, the Church has in them a wrinkle. But if they say that they have these things, not she herself, then let them confess that they are not members of her, nor that they pertain to her body, so that by their own confession too they may be condemned.

**1. 35. 40.** But concerning this desire of the flesh, which we have taken care to distinguish from the nuptial goods by a lengthy disputation on account of the new heretics, who slander us when this is blamed, as though marriage were being blamed—so that, namely, by praising it as a natural good, they may confirm their pestilential teaching, by which they assert that the offspring which is born through it contracts no original sin—concerning this desire of the flesh, then, the blessed Ambrose, bishop of Milan, by whose priestly ministry I received the bath of regeneration, spoke thus briefly, when, expounding the prophet Isaiah, he introduced the carnal nativity of Christ: "Therefore," he says, "he was both tempted in all things as a man and endured all things in the likeness of men, but, as one born of the Spirit, he abstained from sin; for *every man is a liar*[^101], and no one is without sin except the one God. It is therefore established that from man and woman, that is, through that union of bodies, no one is seen to be free of fault. But he who is free of fault is also free of this kind of conception." Did the holy Ambrose, then, by the truth of this sentence of his, condemn the goodness of marriage, and not rather the vanity of these heretics, though they had not yet appeared at that time? This I thought ought to be recalled, because Pelagius so praises Ambrose as to say: "The blessed bishop Ambrose, in whose books especially the Roman faith shines forth, who among Latin writers stood out as a kind of beautiful flower, whose faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures not even an enemy has dared to blame." Let him therefore repent of what he thought against Ambrose, lest he repent of having so praised Ambrose. You have a book troublesome both by the annoyance of its length and by the difficulty of the question—as troublesome for me to dictate as for you to read—in whatever fragments of time it could or shall be able to find you at leisure. This book, indeed, as far as the Lord has deigned to help me, worked out amid my ecclesiastical cares, I would not thrust upon you amid your public ones, had I not heard from a man of God, who knows you more intimately, that you read so gladly that you even spend some night hours watchfully on reading.
