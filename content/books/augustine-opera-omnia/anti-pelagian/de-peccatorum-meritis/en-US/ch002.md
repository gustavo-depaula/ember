# Book I

## Preface.

**1. 1.** Although amid the great and feverish press of cares and weariness that hold us back on account of sinners who forsake the law of God—even though we charge these very cares too against the deserts of sins—I nevertheless did not wish, my dearest Marcellinus, to remain any longer in debt to your zeal, by which you are the more pleasing and agreeable to me; and, to tell the truth, I could not. For I was compelled, whether by that very love by which in the one unchangeable God we are one, being destined to be changed for the better, or by the fear lest in you I should give offense to God, who has given you such a desire that by serving it I serve him who gave it—so compelled, I say, so led and drawn, was I to resolve, with what little strength I have, the questions you laid upon me by writing, that for a little while this cause prevailed in my mind over others, until I should accomplish something to make it clear that I had served your good will, and that of those to whom these matters are a concern, if not adequately, yet obediently.

## Adam, had he not sinned, would not have died.

**2. 2.** Those who say, "Adam was created such that he would have died even without the deserving of any sin, not as the penalty of guilt but by the necessity of nature," surely try to refer what is said in the law, *On the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^1], not to the death of the body but to the death of the soul, which comes about in sin. It was by this death that the Lord signified the unbelievers to be dead, of whom he said, *Let the dead bury their dead*[^2]. What, then, will they answer when we read that God said this to the first man even after the sin, rebuking and condemning him: *You are earth, and into earth you shall go*[^3]?

For it was not according to the soul but, as is plain, according to the body that he was earth, and it was by the death of that same body that he was to go into the earth. For although according to the body he was earth and bore the animal body in which he was created, nevertheless, had he not sinned, he was to be changed into a spiritual body and to pass over, without the danger of death, into that incorruption which is promised to the faithful and the holy. That we have a desire for this we not only feel within ourselves, but also recognize it at the Apostle's prompting, where he says: *For in this we groan, longing to be clothed over with our dwelling which is from heaven, if indeed, being clothed, we are not found naked. For we who are in this dwelling groan, being burdened, in that we do not wish to be unclothed but to be clothed over, that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life*[^4]. Accordingly, had Adam not sinned, he was not to be unclothed of the body but to be clothed over with immortality and incorruption, so that what was mortal might be swallowed up by life—that is, that he might pass from the animal to the spiritual.

## It is one thing to be able to die, another to have to die.

**3. 3.** Nor was there reason to fear that, perhaps, if he lived here longer in an animal body, he might be weighed down by old age and, gradually growing old, come to death. For if God granted to the Israelites that their garments and sandals were not worn out through so many years[^5], what wonder if to an obedient man the same power would grant that, having an animal—that is, mortal—body, he might maintain in it a certain condition in which he would be advanced in years without decay, until the time when God willed him to pass from mortality to immortality without an intervening death? For just as this very flesh which we now have is not invulnerable simply because it is not necessary that it be wounded, so that flesh was not immortal merely because it was not necessary that it die. I think that such a constitution, while still in an animal and mortal body, was granted also to those who were taken from here without death. For neither Enoch nor Elijah withered with old age through so long an age, and yet I do not believe that they were already changed into that spiritual quality of body which is promised in the resurrection, the resurrection that first went before us in the Lord—unless perhaps these men have no need even of the foods that restore by their own consumption, but from the time they were taken up they so live that they have a satiety like that of those forty days in which Elijah lived without food from a cup of water and a cake of bread[^6]; or, if they do have need of these supports, then perhaps they are fed in paradise as Adam was before he deserved on account of sin to go out from there. For he had, as I judge, both refreshment against decline from the fruits of the trees and stability against aging from the tree of life.

## The death of the body too is from sin.

**4. 4.** Besides this, that God in punishing said, *You are earth, and into earth you shall go*[^7]—which, except of the death of the body, I do not know how it can be understood—there are also other testimonies by which it appears most evidently that the human race deserved on account of sin the death not only of the spirit but also of the body. The Apostle says to the Romans: *But if Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness. If, then, the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you*[^8]. I think a statement so clear and open needs no expositor but only a reader. *The body*, he says, *is dead*—not on account of earthly frailty, because it was made from the dust of the earth, but *because of sin.* What more do we seek? And most watchfully he did not say "mortal" but *dead.*

## A body mortal, about to die, dead.

**5. 5.** For before it was changed into that incorruption which is promised in the resurrection of the saints, it could be mortal yet not destined to die, just as this body of ours can, so to speak, be liable to sickness yet not destined to fall sick. For whose flesh is there that cannot fall sick, even if by some chance it should die before it falls sick? So too that body was already mortal; and the change into eternal incorruption would have done away with that mortality, had righteousness, that is, obedience, remained in the man; but that mortal thing did not become dead except on account of sin. But because that change to come in the resurrection will have not only no death—which came about on account of sin—but not even the mortality which the animal body had before sin, he does not say, "He who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your dead bodies," though he had said above *the body is dead*, but he says, *he will give life also to your mortal bodies*, so that they may now be not only not dead but not even mortal, when the animal will rise into the spiritual and this mortal will put on immortality[^9], and what is mortal will be swallowed up by life[^10].

## In what sense the body is dead because of sin.

**6. 6.** It would be strange if anything more transparent than this declaration were sought—unless, perhaps, we must listen to what is alleged against this clarity, namely, that we should understand "the body is dead" here in that sense in which it is said, *Put to death your members which are upon the earth*[^11]. But in this way the body is put to death on account of righteousness, not on account of sin; for it is in order that we may do righteousness that we put to death our members which are upon the earth.

Or, if they think that *because of sin* was added so that we should understand not that sin has been committed but that sin may not be committed—as though he said, "The body indeed is dead so that sin may not be committed"—then what does he mean when, having added *but the spirit is life*, he went on to say, *because of righteousness?* For it would have sufficed to add *the life of the spirit* in such a way that here too "so that sin may not be committed" might be understood, so that we should understand both for the sake of one thing: that the body is dead and that the spirit is life, so that sin may not be committed. And in the same way, even if he had wished to say only *because of righteousness*, that is, "for the doing of righteousness," both could be referred to this: that the body is dead and that the spirit is life for the doing of righteousness. But as it is, he both said the *body is dead because of sin* and that the *spirit is life because of righteousness*, assigning different deserts to different things: to the death of the body indeed the desert of sin, but to the life of the spirit the desert of righteousness.

Wherefore, if, as cannot be doubted, the *spirit is life because of righteousness*, that is, by the desert of righteousness, then surely, when the *body is dead because of sin*, what else ought we or can we understand than "by the desert of sin," unless we try to pervert and twist the most evident sense of Scripture according to our own pleasure? To this the light of the following words also is added.

For when, defining the grace of the present time, he had said that the *body indeed is dead because of sin*—because in it, not yet renewed through the resurrection, the desert of sin remains, that is, the necessity of death—but that the *spirit is life because of righteousness*—because, although we are still burdened with the body of this death, yet, with renewal already begun according to the inner man, we breathe again into the righteousness of faith[^12];

nevertheless, lest human ignorance should hope for nothing concerning the resurrection of the body, that very body which, on account of the desert of sin, he had called dead in the present age, he says will, in the future, on account of the desert of righteousness, be given life—and not merely so that it become living from dead, but also immortal from mortal.

## The life of the spirit prepares the life of the body.

**7. 7.** And so, although I am wary lest by expounding a plain matter I should rather obscure it, attend nonetheless to the light of the Apostle's statement. *But if Christ*, he says, *is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness*[^13]. This was said lest men should think that they have either no benefit or only a small one from the grace of Christ, because they are necessarily going to die in the body. For they ought to consider that the body indeed still bears the desert of sin, in that it is bound to the condition of death, but that the spirit has already begun to live because of the righteousness of faith—the spirit which itself too had been extinguished in man by a certain death of unbelief. "Therefore," he says, "do not think that little has been bestowed on you through the fact that Christ is in you, that, while the body is dead because of sin, your spirit already lives because of righteousness; nor on that account despair even of the life of the body itself. *For if the Spirit of him who raised Christ from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you*[^14]."

Why is the smoke of contention still cast over so great a light? The Apostle cries out: "The body indeed is dead in you because of sin, but your mortal bodies too will be given life because of the righteousness on account of which now the spirit is already life, all of which will be brought to completion through the grace of Christ, that is, through his Spirit dwelling in you"; and still they are going to cry out against it! He says also in what way it comes about that life, by putting death to death, turns it into itself. *Therefore*, he says, *brethren, we are debtors not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh. For if you live according to the flesh, you will die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the flesh, you will live*[^15]. What is this but: "If you live according to death, the whole will die; but if, by living according to life, you put death to death, the whole will live"?

## Paul speaks of the death of the body.

**8. 8.** Likewise, when he says, *By a man came death, and by a man the resurrection of the dead*[^16], of what else can it be understood than of the death of the body, since to say this he was speaking of the resurrection of the body and was urging it with the most insistent and forceful intent? What, then, is this which he says here to the Corinthians: *By a man came death, and by a man the resurrection of the dead; for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^17], but what he also says to the Romans: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death*[^18]?

This death they wish to be understood not of the body but of the soul—as though something else were said to the Corinthians by *by a man came death*, where they are by no means allowed to take it of the death of the soul, because the matter at hand was the resurrection of the body, which is contrary to the death of the body. For this reason, too, only death there is mentioned as brought about by a man, and not also sin, because the matter at hand was not righteousness, which is contrary to sin, but the resurrection of the body, which is contrary to the death of the body.

## Sin passed into all not only by imitation but also by propagation.

**9. 9.** But this apostolic testimony in which he says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death*[^19], you indicated in your letter that they are trying to twist into another, novel opinion; but what that is which they suppose in these words you did not say. As far, however, as I have learned from others, what they hold here is "that this death too, which is mentioned there, is not that of the body—which they refuse to admit that Adam deserved by sinning—but that of the soul, which comes about in sin itself; and that sin itself passed into other men from the first man not by propagation but by imitation." For from this they refuse to believe that even in little ones original sin is loosed through baptism, contending that there is in those being born no sin at all. But if the Apostle had wished to mention that sin which entered into this world not by propagation but by imitation, he would have called its author not Adam but the devil, of whom it is written, *The devil sins from the beginning*[^20]; of whom it is also read in the book of Wisdom, *But by the envy of the devil death entered into the world*[^21]. For since that death came thus from the devil into men, not because they were propagated from him but because they imitated him, he immediately added, *And they imitate him who are of his side*[^22]. Accordingly, when the Apostle was mentioning that sin and death which had passed from one into all by propagation, he set down as its author the one from whom the propagation of the human race took its beginning.

## The distinction between actual and original sin.

**9. 10.** They imitate Adam, as many as transgress the commandment of God through disobedience; but one thing is what is an example to those sinning by their own will, another what is the origin for those born with sin. For the saints too imitate Christ in following after righteousness. Hence the same Apostle says, *Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ*[^23]. But besides this imitation, his grace works our illumination and justification even inwardly, by that work of which the same preacher of his says, *Neither he who plants is anything nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^24].

For by this grace he grafts into his body even baptized little ones, who certainly are not yet able to imitate anyone. As, therefore, that one in whom all are made alive—besides offering himself as an example of righteousness to those who imitate him—also gives to the faithful the most hidden grace of his Spirit, which he pours in secretly even into little ones; so too that one in whom all die—besides being to those who transgress the Lord's command by their own will an example for imitation—has also, by the hidden corruption of his carnal concupiscence, corrupted in himself all who were to come of his stock. Hence, and from nowhere else, the Apostle says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^25]. If I were to say this, they would resist and cry out that I do not speak rightly and do not think rightly. For they would understand in these words no meaning of any man who said them except that very one which they refuse to understand in the Apostle. But because the words are those of him to whose authority and teaching they submit, they charge us with slowness of understanding, when they try to twist into some other thing the words that have been said so plainly. *Through one man*, he says, *sin entered into the world, and through sin death*: this belongs to propagation, not to imitation; for if to imitation, he would have said "through the devil." But—as no one disputes—he means that first man, who was called Adam. *And so*, he says, *it passed into all men.*

## In Adam all sinned.

**10. 11.** Then what follows, *in whom all sinned*—how circumspectly, how precisely, how unambiguously it is said! For if you understand the sin which through one man entered into the world, the sin in which all sinned, then surely it is manifest that there are certain sins proper to each, in which only those sin whose sins they are, and another, this one sin in which all sinned, when all were that one man. But if it is understood not as the sin, but as the one man himself, in which one man all sinned, what could be more manifest than this declaration? For surely we read that those who believe in Christ are justified in him on account of the hidden sharing and inbreathing of spiritual grace, by which *whoever clings to the Lord is one spirit*[^26], although his saints also imitate him. Let something of the kind be read to me concerning those who have imitated his saints—whether anyone has been said to be justified in Paul or in Peter or in any of those whose authority stands out greatly among the people of God; except that we are said to be blessed in Abraham, as it was said to him, *In you all nations will be blessed*[^27], on account of Christ, who was called his seed according to the flesh. This is said more manifestly when the same thing is put thus: *In your seed all nations will be blessed*[^28]. But that anyone has been said by the divine oracles to have sinned or to sin in the devil—though all the wicked and the impious imitate him—I do not know whether anyone may find. Yet though the Apostle said this of the first man—*in whom all sinned*—the propagation of sin is still disputed, and some fog of imitation is set against it.

## A sin older than the law.

**10. 12.** Attend also to what follows. For when he had said, *in whom all sinned*, he went on to add, *for until the law sin was in the world*[^29]—that is, because not even the law could take away sin, the law which entered in that sin might the more abound[^30], whether the natural law, in which everyone who already uses reason begins to add his own sins to original sin, or that very law which was given to the people in writing through Moses. *For if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would indeed be from the law. But Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^31]. *But sin was not reckoned, when there was no law*[^32]. What is *was not reckoned*, but "was unknown and was not thought to be sin"? For it was not held by the Lord God himself as though it were not, since it is written, *Whoever have sinned without the law will perish without the law*[^33].

## One sin common to all.

**11. 13.** *But death reigned*, he says, *from Adam until Moses*[^34], that is, from the first man until that very law which was divinely promulgated, because not even the law could take away the reign of death. For he wishes the reign of death to be understood as when the guilt of sin so dominates in men that it does not allow them to come to eternal life, which is the true life, but drags them also to the second death, which is penally eternal.

This reign of death the grace of the Savior alone destroys in any man, the grace which worked also in the ancient saints—whoever, before Christ came in the flesh, belonged nonetheless to his assisting grace and not to the letter of the law, which could only command and not assist. For this was hidden in the Old Testament by a most just dispensation of the times, which is now revealed in the New.

Therefore in all *death reigned from Adam until Moses* who were not helped by the grace of Christ, so that the reign of death might be destroyed in them; therefore also in those *who did not sin after the likeness of the transgression of Adam*, that is, who had not yet sinned by their own proper will as he did, but had drawn original sin from him—*who is the form of the one to come*[^35], because in him was constituted the form of condemnation for the posterity to come who would be created from his propagation, so that from one all should be born unto condemnation, from which nothing frees but the grace of the Savior.

I know indeed that very many Latin manuscripts have it thus: *Death reigned from Adam until Moses over those who sinned after the likeness of the transgression of Adam*; and even those who read it so refer it to the same understanding, so that they take to have sinned after the likeness of the transgression of Adam those who sinned in him, so as to be created like him—as men from a man, so sinners from a sinner, those destined to die from one destined to die, the condemned from one condemned. But the Greek manuscripts, from which the translation was made into the Latin tongue, have, either all or almost all, what was first set down by me.

## Redemption surpasses original sin.

**11. 14.** *But not*, he says, *as the trespass, so also the gift; for if by the trespass of one many died, much more did the grace of God and the gift, in the grace of the one man Jesus Christ, abound unto many*[^36]: not "more men," that is, many more men—for no more are justified than are condemned—but it *abounded much more.* For Adam from his one trespass begot guilty offspring; but Christ by his grace loosed and forgave even the trespasses of their own will that men had added to the original sin in which they were born, as he says more evidently in what follows.

## The condemnation of all on account of the sin of one.

**12. 15.** But consider more carefully what he says: that on account of the trespass of one many died. For why on account of that one man's, and not rather on account of their own proper trespasses, if imitation and not propagation is to be understood in this place? But attend to what follows: *And the gift is not as it was by the one who sinned; for the judgment indeed was from one unto condemnation, but the grace is from many trespasses unto justification*[^37].

Now let them say where in these words that imitation has a place. *From one*, he says, *unto condemnation*: from which one, if not the one trespass? For he makes this plain when he adds, *but the grace is from many trespasses unto justification.* Why, then, is the judgment from one trespass unto condemnation, but the grace from many trespasses unto justification?

If there is no original trespass, does not the judgment unto condemnation, no less than the grace unto justification, lead men from many trespasses? For grace does not forgive many trespasses without judgment also condemning many trespasses. Or if they are led from one trespass unto condemnation because all the trespasses that are condemned were committed in imitation of that one man, then by the same reasoning they would have to be understood as led also from one trespass unto justification, because all the trespasses that are forgiven to the justified were committed in imitation of that one man. But this, evidently, the Apostle did not understand when he said, *The judgment indeed was from one trespass unto condemnation, but the grace from many trespasses unto justification!* No—rather let us understand the Apostle, and see that the judgment is said to be from one trespass unto condemnation because it would suffice for condemnation even if there were in men no sin but the original one. For although the condemnation is heavier for those who have joined their own trespasses to the original one, and heavier for each in proportion as each has sinned more grievously, nevertheless even that alone which is drawn from the origin not only separates from the kingdom of God—which they themselves confess that little ones who die without having received the grace of Christ cannot enter—but also makes them strangers to salvation and eternal life, which can be none other than the kingdom of God, into which the fellowship of Christ alone leads.

## Not all sins are from Adam, but the forgiveness of all sins is from Christ.

**13. 16.** And by this, from Adam, in whom all sinned, we have derived not all our sins but only the original one; from Christ, however, in whom we are all justified, we obtain the forgiveness not only of that original sin but also of the rest of the sins that we ourselves have added. *Therefore the gift is not as it was by the one who sinned.* For the judgment indeed, from one trespass, that is, the original one, if it is not forgiven, can already lead unto condemnation; but the grace, from many trespasses forgiven—that is, not only that original one but also all the rest—leads on to justification.

## The reign of death and of life.

**13. 17.** *For if, on account of the trespass of one, death reigned through one, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and of righteousness reign in life through the one Jesus Christ*[^38]. Why did death reign through one on account of the trespass of one, except that they were held by the bond of death in that one in whom all sinned, even if they added no sins of their own? Otherwise it was not on account of the trespass of one that death reigned through one, but on account of the many trespasses of many through each one who sins. For if the rest died on account of another man's trespass because, following after him who went before in trespassing, they imitated him, then he too, and much more, died on account of another's trespass—the devil, who in trespassing had so gone before him that he even persuaded him to the trespass. But Adam persuaded nothing to his imitators, and many who are called his imitators have either not heard that he existed and committed any such thing, or do not believe it at all.

How much more rightly, then, as I have already said, would the Apostle have set the devil as the author, of whom alone he would say that sin and death passed through all, if in this place he had wished to speak of imitation and not propagation! For Adam is much more reasonably called an imitator of the devil, whom he had as a persuader to sin, if anyone can imitate even one who has persuaded him to nothing of the kind, or whom he does not know at all. And what is *those who receive the abundance of grace and of righteousness*, except that the grace of forgiveness is given not only for that sin in which all sinned but also for those they have added, and that to those men so great a righteousness is granted that, whereas Adam consented to the one persuading him to sin, these do not yield even to one compelling them?

And what is *much more will they reign in life*, since the reign of death drags far more men into eternal punishments, unless we understand that the very same men are spoken of in both—those who pass from Adam to Christ, that is, from death to life—because they will reign in eternal life without end, more than death reigned in them temporally and with an end?

## Christ's justification is not set forth to be imitated.

**13. 18.** *Therefore, as through the trespass of one it came upon all men unto condemnation, so also through the justification of one it comes upon all men unto justification of life*[^39]. This trespass of one, if we attend to imitation, will be none but the devil's. But because it is plain that it is said of Adam, not of the devil, it remains that not imitation but propagation of sin is to be understood.

**14. 18.** For what he says of Christ, *through the justification of one*, he expressed better than if he had said "through the righteousness of one." For he means that justification by which Christ justifies the ungodly, which he did not set forth to be imitated, but he alone can do it. For the Apostle could rightly say, *Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ*[^40], but he would never say, "Be justified by me, as I too am justified by Christ," since there can be and are and have been many righteous men, and men to be imitated, but there is none *righteous and justifying* save Christ. Hence it is said, *To one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness*[^41].

Whoever, therefore, has dared to say, "I justify you," it follows that he should also say, "Believe in me." And no one of the saints could rightly say this except the Holy One of holy ones. *Believe in God, and believe in me*[^42]; so that, because he himself justifies the ungodly, to one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly faith is reckoned unto righteousness.

## No one is begotten but from Adam, no one is reborn but from Christ.

**15. 19.** For if imitation alone makes men sinners through Adam, why does not imitation alone also make them righteous through Christ? *For as*, he says, *through the trespass of one it came upon all men unto condemnation, so also through the justification of one it comes upon all men unto justification of life*[^43].

Accordingly, those "one" and "one" should have been set down not as Adam and Christ, but as Adam and Abel, since, although many sinners have gone before us in the time of this life, and those who sinned in later time imitated them, these men nonetheless wish that none but Adam be named, in whom all sinned by imitation, because he himself was the first of men to sin. And by this reasoning Abel should have been named, in whom alone all men are likewise justified by imitation, since he himself was the first of men to live righteously. Or if, on account of a certain point of time pertaining to the beginning of the New Testament, Christ is set down, on account of imitation, as the head of the righteous, then Judas his betrayer should have been set down as the head of sinners. Further, if Christ is the one in whom all are justified for this reason—that not his imitation alone makes men righteous, but grace regenerating through the Spirit—then for the same reason Adam too is the one in whom all sinned, because not his imitation alone makes men sinners, but the penalty generating through the flesh.

For this reason, too, *all* and *all* were said. For not the very same all who are begotten through Adam are regenerated through Christ; but this was rightly said, because just as no one's carnal generation is except through Adam, so no one's spiritual generation is except through Christ. For if some could be begotten in the flesh not through Adam and some reborn in the spirit not through Christ, *all* would not clearly be said either in the one case or in the other. But the same who are *all* he afterward calls *many*; for in some matter all may be those who are few; but carnal generation has many, and spiritual generation many too, although this spiritual one not so many as that carnal one. Yet just as the one has all men, so the other has all righteous men, because just as no one is a man apart from the one, so no one is a righteous man apart from the other, and in each there are many. *For as through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, so through the obedience of one man many will be made righteous*[^44].

## A comparison between carnal generation and spiritual generation.

**15. 20.** *But the law entered in, that the trespass might abound*[^45]. This men added to the original sin now by their own will, not through Adam; but this too is loosed and healed through Christ, because *where sin abounded, grace abounded yet more, so that, as sin reigned in death*—even that which men did not draw from Adam but added by their own will—*so also grace might reign through righteousness unto eternal life*[^46]. Yet there is no righteousness apart from Christ, as there are some sins apart from Adam. Therefore, when he had said, *as sin reigned in death*, he did not here add "through one" or "through Adam," because he had spoken above also of that sin which abounded with the law entering in—and this surely belongs not to origin but now to one's own will—but when he had said, *so also grace might reign through righteousness unto eternal life*, he added, *through Jesus Christ our Lord*, because, with the flesh generating, only that is drawn which is original sin, but with the spirit regenerating, there comes forgiveness not only of original sins but also of voluntary ones.

## The condemnation of infants who depart without baptism.

**16. 21.** Accordingly it can be rightly said that little ones who go out of the body without baptism will be in the mildest condemnation of all. But he greatly deceives and is deceived who declares that they will not be in condemnation, since the Apostle says, *The judgment from one trespass unto condemnation*, and a little after, *through the trespass of one upon all men unto condemnation*[^47]. When, therefore, Adam sinned by not obeying God, then his body, although it was animal and mortal, lost the grace by which in every part it obeyed his soul; then there arose that bestial movement, shameful to men, at which he blushed in his nakedness;

then too, by a certain disease conceived from the sudden and pestilent corruption, it came about in them that, the stability of age in which they were created being lost, they should go through the changes of the ages toward death. Although, therefore, they lived many years afterward, yet they began to die on that day on which they received the law of death by which they would grow old into senility. For whatever runs by continual change little by little toward an end that does not perfect but consumes does not stand still even for a point of time, but slips away without interruption. Thus, then, was fulfilled what God had said: *On the day you eat of it, you shall die the death*[^48]. From this disobedience of the flesh, therefore, from this law of sin and death, whoever is generated carnally needs to be regenerated spiritually, so that he may not only be led to the kingdom of God but also be freed from the condemnation of sin.

And so men are born in the flesh subject at once to the sin and the death of the first man, and at once, joined to the righteousness and eternal life of the second man, they are reborn in baptism; so it is written also in *Ecclesiasticus*: *From a woman the beginning of sin was made, and through her we all die*[^49]. But whether it be said "from a woman" or "from Adam," both pertain to the first man, since, as we know, the woman is from the man, and the one flesh is of both. Hence also that which is written, *And the two will be in one flesh*[^50]. *Therefore now they are not two*, says the Lord, *but one flesh*[^51].

## No personal sin is to be attributed to infants.

**17. 22.** Wherefore those who say that little ones are baptized in order that there may be forgiven them what they have contracted as their own in this life, and not what they have derived from Adam, need not be refuted with great effort. For when they themselves have thought about it for a little while, without the zeal for contention, how absurd and unworthy of discussion is what they say, they will at once change their opinion. And if they will not, we must not so despair of human good sense as to fear that they will persuade anyone of it. For they themselves, in order to say this, were driven, unless I am mistaken, by the prejudice of some other opinion of theirs. And therefore, since they necessarily confessed that sins are forgiven to the baptized, and were unwilling to confess that the sin which they admitted is forgiven to infants is drawn from Adam, they were compelled to accuse infancy itself—as though the accuser of infancy became the safer in this, that the accused could not answer him. But these men, as I have said, let us pass over; for there is need neither of discourse nor of proofs by which the innocence of infants may be shown, as far as concerns the life they live in themselves with their recent birth, if human sense does not recognize it without the aid of anyone's argument.

## Baptism is necessary for infants for the forgiveness of original sin.

**18. 23.** But those move us and seem to bring forward something worthy of consideration and discussion who say that little ones, brought forth with their recent life from the wombs of their mothers, receive baptism not for the forgiveness of sin but in order that, having a spiritual procreation, they may be created in Christ and become partakers of his very kingdom of heaven, in the same way sons and heirs of God, but coheirs of Christ[^52]. Yet when these are asked whether those not baptized and not made coheirs of Christ and partakers of the kingdom of heaven have at least the benefit of eternal salvation in the resurrection of the dead, they labor greatly and find no way out. For what Christian could bear it, when it is said that anyone can come to eternal salvation if he is not reborn in Christ[^53]—which he willed to come about through baptism at that time, by then, when such a sacrament had to be instituted for those to be regenerated in the hope of eternal salvation[^54]? Hence the Apostle says, *Not by works of righteousness that we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, through the washing of regeneration*[^55]. Yet he says that this salvation is in hope, while we live here, where he says, *For in hope we were saved. But hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^56]. Who, then, would dare to affirm that little ones can be saved unto eternity without this regeneration, as though Christ had not died for them? For *Christ died for the ungodly*[^57]. But if these, who, as is manifest, have committed nothing ungodly in their own life, are not held bound by any bond of ungodliness even originally, how did he die for them, who died for the ungodly? If they are wounded by no sickness of original sin, how is it that, by the pious fear of their kin who run to him, they are carried to the physician Christ, that is, to receive the sacrament of eternal salvation, and it is not said to them in the Church, "Take these innocents away from here; the physician is not needed by the healthy but by the sick; Christ came not to call the righteous but sinners"[^58]? Never has such a contrivance been said, never is it said, never will it ever be said at all in the Church of Christ.

## Infants are sinners before baptism.

**19. 24.** And lest anyone suppose that little ones ought to be brought to baptism because, just as they are not sinners, so neither are they righteous, then how do certain ones recall that the merit of this age was praised by the Lord, when he said, *Let the little ones come to me; for of such is the kingdom of heaven*[^59]? For if this was said not on account of the likeness of humility, because humility makes one like little ones, but on account of the praiseworthy life of children, then surely they are also righteous. For it could not otherwise rightly be said, *Of such is the kingdom of heaven*, since it can belong to none but the righteous.

But perhaps it is not fitting to say that the Lord praised the life of little ones when he said, *Of such is the kingdom of heaven*, since the true understanding is that he set the likeness of humility in the small age. Yet perhaps this is to be held which I said: that little ones ought for this reason to be baptized, because, just as they are not sinners, so neither are they righteous. But when it had been said, *I came not to call the righteous*, as if it were answered him, "Whom, then, have you come to call?" he immediately added, *but sinners to repentance.* And by this reasoning, just as if they are righteous, so also if they are not sinners, he came not to call them who said, *I came not to call the righteous, but sinners.* And therefore they would seem to rush upon his baptism—the baptism of him who does not call them—not only in vain but even wrongly; far be it from us to think this. The physician, therefore, calls them, the physician who is not needed by the healthy but by the sick, and who came not to call the righteous but sinners to repentance. And therefore, because they are as yet held subject to no sins of their own life, the original sickness in them is healed in the grace of him who saves through the washing of regeneration[^60].

## Infants in baptism are to be called penitents and faithful.

**19. 25.** Someone will say, "How, then, are they too called to repentance? Can such tiny ones repent of anything?" To this it is answered: "If they are not to be called penitents because they do not yet have the sense of repenting, neither are they to be called faithful, because they likewise do not yet have the sense of believing. But if they are rightly called faithful because they in a certain way profess faith through the words of those who carry them, why should they not also be held to be penitents first, when through the words of the same carriers they are shown to renounce the devil and this world? All this is done in hope by the power of the sacrament and of divine grace, which the Lord has given to the Church."

For the rest, who does not know that, if a baptized little one, coming to the years of reason, does not believe and does not abstain from unlawful desires, it will profit him nothing that he received what he received as a little one? Yet if, having received baptism, he departs from this life, the guilt to which he was originally subject being loosed, he will be perfected in that light of truth which, remaining unchangeably in eternity, illumines the justified by the presence of the Creator. For only sins separate between men and God, and these are loosed by his grace, through whom as mediator we are reconciled, when he justifies the ungodly.

## There is no salvation or eternal life outside the kingdom of heaven.

**20. 26.** But these men are frightened by the saying of the Lord, *Unless one is born again, he will not see the kingdom of God*—which, when he explained it, he said, *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^61]—and therefore they try to grant to unbaptized little ones, by the merit of their innocence, salvation and eternal life, but, because they are not baptized, to make them strangers to the kingdom of heaven, by a certain new and astonishing presumption, as though the salvation of eternal life could be eternal apart from the inheritance of Christ, apart from the kingdom of heaven. For they evidently have a place to which to flee and where to hide, because the Lord did not say, *If one is not reborn of water and the Spirit*, "he will not have life," but he said, *he will not enter into the kingdom of God.* For if he had said that, no doubt could here arise. Let doubt, then, now be removed; let us hear the Lord, not the suspicions and conjectures of mortals; let us hear the Lord, I say, saying this not indeed of the sacrament of the holy washing, but of the sacrament of his holy table, to which no one rightly comes but the baptized: *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^62]. What more do we seek? What can be answered to this, unless obstinacy strain its contentious sinews against the steadfastness of perspicuous truth?

## The eucharistic precept embraces infants too.

**20. 27.** Or will anyone dare to say even this, that this sentence does not pertain to little ones, and that they can have life in themselves without partaking of this body and blood, because he did not say, "He who does not eat," as he did of baptism, *He who is not reborn*, but he said, *If you do not eat*, as though addressing those who could hear and understand—which little ones surely cannot?

But whoever says this does not attend to the fact that, unless that sentence holds for all—namely, that without the body and blood of the Son of man they cannot have life—then even the older age cares for it in vain. For if you attend not to the will but to the words of the one speaking, it can seem to have been said only to those to whom the Lord was then speaking, because he did not say "he who does not eat," but, *If you do not eat.* And where is that which in the same place, concerning this very matter, he says: *The bread that I will give is my flesh for the life of the world*[^63]?

For according to this we understand that that sacrament pertains also to us who were not yet in being when he said these things, because we cannot say that we do not pertain to the world for whose life Christ gave his flesh. And who would doubt that by the name "world" he signified the men who by being born come into this world? For, as he says elsewhere, *The sons of this world beget and are begotten*[^64]. And by this reasoning the flesh that was given for the life of the world was given also for the life of little ones; and if they do not eat the flesh of the Son of man, neither will they have life.

## Infants not baptized are to be counted in the number of those who do not believe.

**20. 28.** From this comes also that saying: *The Father loves the Son, and has given all things into his hand. He who believes in the Son has eternal life; but he who is unbelieving toward the Son will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon him*[^65]. In which, then, of these kinds shall we place infants? Among those who believe in the Son, or among those who are unbelieving toward the Son? "In neither," someone says, "because, since they cannot yet believe, neither are they to be reckoned unbelieving." But the ecclesiastical rule does not indicate this, which joins baptized infants to the number of the faithful. Further, if those who are baptized, on account of the power and celebration of so great a sacrament—although in their own heart and mouth they do not perform what pertains to believing and confessing[^66]—are nevertheless counted in the number of believers, then surely those who have lacked the sacrament are to be held among those who do not believe in the Son; and therefore, if they go out of the body empty of this grace, that which has been said will follow them: *They will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon them.* And whence is this, when it is clear that they have no sins of their own, if they are not held subject even to original sin?

## God's justice in dispensing the grace of baptism is inscrutable.

**21. 29.** But he well did not say, *the wrath of God* "will come upon him," but, *remains upon him.* For from this wrath, under which all are in sin[^67], of which the Apostle says, *For we too were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^68], nothing frees but *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^69]. Why this grace comes to one and does not come to another can be a hidden cause; it cannot be an unjust one. For *is there injustice with God? By no means*[^70]. But first our necks must be bowed to the authorities of the holy Scriptures, that through faith each may come to understanding.

For it was not said in vain, *Your judgments are like a great deep*[^71]. As though terrified at the depth of this deep, the Apostle exclaims, *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God*[^72]! For he had set before it a saying of wondrous profundity, saying, *For God has shut up all in unbelief, that he may have mercy on all*[^73]. As though struck by the awe of this profundity, *O the depth*, he says, *of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How inscrutable are his judgments and his ways past finding out! For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that it should be repaid him? For from him and through him and in him are all things; to him be glory unto the ages of ages. Amen*[^74]. We have, therefore, a very small understanding for examining the justice of the judgments of God, for examining the free grace which, with no merits going before, is not unjust, and which moves us not so much when it is given to the unworthy as when it is denied to others equally unworthy.

## The differing lot of infants is veiled by the mystery of God's justice.

**21. 30.** For even these to whom it seems unjust that little ones who go out of the body without the grace of Christ should be deprived not only of the kingdom of God—into which they themselves confess that none can enter unless reborn through baptism—but also of eternal life and salvation: as they ask how it is just that one be loosed from original ungodliness and another not loosed, since the condition of both is the same, let them answer, according to their own opinion, how it is likewise just that to this one baptism be granted, by which he enters into the kingdom of God, and to that one not be granted, since the case of both is equal. For if it moves us why, of these two—since both are equally sinners originally—one is loosed from this bond, to whom baptism is conceded, and another is not loosed, to whom such grace is not conceded, why does it not equally move us that, of two originally innocent, one received baptism by which he could enter into the kingdom of God, and the other did not receive it, so that he could not come to the kingdom of God?

Surely in either case we come back to that exclamation: *O the depth of the riches*[^75]! And then, of these baptized little ones themselves, let it be told me why one is taken away, *lest malice change his understanding*[^76], and another lives who is to be ungodly? Would not both, if both were taken away, enter into the kingdom of heaven? And yet there is no injustice with God. What? Whom would it not move, whom would it not compel to exclaim at such a depth, that some little ones are vexed by an unclean spirit[^77], others suffer nothing of the kind, others even in their mothers' wombs[^78], like Jeremiah, are sanctified, when all, if there is original sin, are equally guilty, and, if there is none, are equally innocent? Whence comes this so great diversity, except that *his judgments are inscrutable and his ways past finding out*[^79]?

## He refutes those who think that souls are thrust into bodies on account of offenses committed elsewhere and are afflicted in them more or less.

**22. 31.** Or is perhaps that opinion now to be held which has been hissed off and repudiated—that souls, having sinned beforehand in a heavenly dwelling, come gradually and little by little to bodies suited to their deserts, and, according to the life lived before, are afflicted more or less by corporeal plagues? Although holy Scripture most openly contradicts this opinion, which, in commending grace, says, *When they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not from works but from him who calls, it was said that the elder will serve the younger*[^80], yet not even those who hold this opinion escape the straits of this question, but, hemmed in and stuck in them, are likewise compelled to exclaim, *O the depth!*

For whence does it come that one man, from his earliest boyhood, is more modest, more gifted, more temperate, in great part a conqueror of his lusts, hating greed, detesting extravagance, and rising up more advanced and apt toward the other virtues, and yet is in a place where the Christian grace cannot be preached to him—*for how will they call upon him in whom they have not believed? Or how will they believe him whom they have not heard? And how will they hear without a preacher?*[^81]—

while another, slow of mind, given over to lusts, covered with shameful deeds and crimes, is so governed that he hears, believes, is baptized, and is taken away, or, if he is kept here, lives praiseworthily? Where did these two contract deserts so different—I do not say so that this one believes and that one does not believe, which is a matter of one's own will, but so that this one hears what he may believe and that one does not hear—for this is not in a man's power—where, I say, did they contract these deserts so different?

If they lived some life in heaven, such that for their acts they were driven down or slipped to the earth and held in corporeal receptacles suited to their previous life, then surely the one who deserved not to be much weighed down by it is to be believed to have lived better before this mortal body, in that he both had a good nature and was pressed by milder concupiscences which he could easily overcome; and yet he did not deserve to have preached to him that grace by which alone he could be freed from the destruction of the second death.

But the other, on account of worse deserts—as they suppose—was entangled in a heavier body and on this account in a dull heart, and, when he was overcome by the most burning desire for the enticements of the flesh and through a most wicked life added worse earthly things to the former sins by which he had deserved to come to this, yet either heard on the cross, *Today you will be with me in paradise*[^82], or clung to some Apostle, by whose preaching he was changed and through the washing of regeneration made whole, so that where sin abounded, grace abounded yet more[^83]. What they may answer to this I do not see at all—those who, wishing to defend the justice of God by human conjectures and being ignorant of the height of grace, have woven improbable fables.

## The example of a certain Christian simpleton.

**22. 32.** For many things could be said about the wondrous callings of men, whether those we read of or those we have experienced, by which the opinion of those is overturned who believe that the souls of men lived certain lives of their own before these bodies, by which they came to these bodies, destined to experience here, according to the diversity of their deserts, different things, whether good or evil. But the care of bringing this work to an end does not allow me to linger longer on these matters. One thing, however, which among many I have found wonderful, I will not pass over in silence.

Who, according to these men who suppose that souls are weighed down more or less by earthly bodies on account of the deserts of a prior life lived in the heavens before this body, would not affirm that those had sinned more wickedly and monstrously before this life who deserved so to lose the light of mind that they were born with a sense close to the beasts—I do not say the slowest of mind, for this is wont to be said of others, but so witless that, with curled hair, they are put forward to provoke laughter and offer to the sensible the diversions of their foolishness,

whom the common people call by a name derived from the Greek, *morions?* Yet a certain one of such men was so Christian that, although by a wonderful foolishness he was most patient of all the injuries done to himself, he nevertheless could so not bear an injury to the name of Christ or to the religion with which he had been imbued, even in his own person, that he would not cease to pursue with stones those sensible men who blasphemed—from whom he heard such things, that he might be provoked—nor in that cause would he spare even his masters.

Such men, therefore, I judge to be predestined and created so that those who can may understand that the grace of God and the Spirit, who breathes where he wills[^84], for this reason passes over no kind of natural gift in the sons of mercy, and likewise passes over every kind of natural gift in the sons of Gehenna, *so that he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^85].

But those who affirm that souls receive their several earthly bodies according to the deserts of a higher life—bodies by which some are weighed down more, others less—and that human natural gifts are varied according to those same deserts, so that some are sharper and others duller, and that the divine grace too for freeing men is dispensed according to the deserts of that higher life: what will they be able to answer concerning this man? How will they attribute to him both a most foul higher life, so that from this he was born a simpleton, and one so well deserving, so that from this in Christ he was by grace preferred to many of the sharpest minds?

## There is no salvation for infants without baptism.

**22. 33.** Let us yield, therefore, and consent to the authority of holy Scripture, which knows neither to be deceived nor to deceive; and, just as we do not believe that the not-yet-born have done anything good or evil for the distinguishing of their deserts, so let us in no way doubt that all are under the sin which through one man entered into the world and passed through all men[^86], from which nothing frees but *the grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ.*

**23. 33.** His healing coming is not needed by the healthy but by the sick, because he came not to call the righteous but sinners[^87]; into whose kingdom none will enter but he who has been reborn of water and the Spirit, nor will he possess salvation and eternal life apart from his kingdom, since he who has not eaten his flesh[^88], and he who is unbelieving toward the Son, will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon him[^89]. From this sin, from this sickness, from this wrath of God, of which they are by nature children—who, even if through their age they have no sin of their own, yet draw the original sin—nothing frees but the *Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world*[^90], none but the Physician, who came not for the healthy but for the sick, none but the Savior, of whom it was said to the human race, *Today a Savior is born to you*[^91]; none but the Redeemer, by whose blood our debt is blotted out.

For who would dare to say that Christ is not the Savior and Redeemer of infants? But whence does he save them, if there is in them no original sickness of sin? Whence does he redeem them, if they are not, through the origin of the first man, sold under sin[^92]? Let no eternal salvation, then, be promised to infants by our own judgment apart from the baptism of Christ—a salvation which the divine Scripture, to be preferred to all human reasonings, does not promise.

## For infants too baptism is salvation, the Eucharist is life.

**24. 34.** The African Christians very well call baptism itself nothing other than salvation, and the sacrament of the body of Christ nothing other than life. From what, except, as I think, from an ancient and apostolic tradition by which they hold it as planted in the Church of Christ that, apart from baptism and partaking of the table of the Lord, no man can come not only to the kingdom of God but not even to salvation and eternal life? For this Scripture too testifies, according to what we said above. For what else do those hold who call baptism by the name of salvation, but what was said: *He saved us through the washing of regeneration*[^93]; and what Peter says: *So baptism too, in a like form, saves you*[^94]? And what else do those too hold who call the sacrament of the Lord's table life, but what was said: *I am the living bread, who came down from heaven*; and, *The bread that I will give is my flesh for the life of the world*; and, *Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of man and drink his blood, you will not have life in you*[^95]? If, then, as so many and so great divine testimonies agree, neither salvation nor eternal life is to be hoped for by anyone without baptism and the body and blood of the Lord, in vain is it promised to little ones without these.

Further, if nothing separates a man from salvation and eternal life except sins, then through these sacraments nothing but the guilt of sin is loosed in little ones; concerning which guilt it is written that no one is clean, *not even if his life has been but of one day*[^96]. Hence too that saying in the Psalms: *For I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother nourished me in the womb*[^97]; for either it is said in the general person of man himself, or, if David says this properly of himself, he was surely born not of fornication but of lawful wedlock. Let us not doubt, then, that the blood was shed even for the baptizing of infants—the blood which, before it was shed, was so given and commended in the sacrament that it was said, *This is my blood, which will be shed for many unto the forgiveness of sins*[^98]. For they deny that those are freed who refuse to confess that they are under sin. For whence are they freed, if they are held bound by no slavery of sin?

## Without baptism infants remain in the darkness of sin.

**24. 35.** *I*, he says, *have come as light into the world, that everyone who believes in me may not remain in darkness*[^99]. By this saying what does he show but that everyone who does not believe in him is in darkness, and that by believing he brings it about that he does not remain in darkness? What do we understand this darkness to be but sins? But whatever else this darkness is understood to be, surely he who does not believe in Christ will remain in it; and it is certainly penal, not, as it were, the nightly darkness necessary for the rest of living things.

**25. 35.** Accordingly, little ones, if through the sacrament which was divinely instituted for this they do not pass over into the number of believers, will surely remain in this darkness.

## No one is illumined apart from baptism.

**25. 36.** Although some believe that they are illumined as soon as they are born, understanding thus what is written: *It was the true light, which illumines every man coming into this world*[^100]. But if this is so, it is much to be wondered at how those illumined by the only Son, who in the beginning was the Word, God with God, are not admitted to the kingdom of God, and are not heirs of God and coheirs of Christ. For that this is not granted to them except through baptism, even those who hold this opinion confess. Then, being already illumined, if they are not yet fit to obtain the kingdom of God, they ought at least gladly to receive baptism itself, by which they become fit for this; yet we see them resist it with great weeping, and we make light of that ignorance in that age, so that we complete in them, even resisting, the sacraments which we know to profit them. For why too does the Apostle say, *Do not be children in your minds*[^101], if their minds were already illumined by that true light which is the Word of God?

## No one of men is illumined except by God.

**25. 37.** And so that which is set down in the Gospel, *It was the true light, which illumines every man coming into this world*[^102], was said for this reason, because no man is illumined except by that light of truth which is God, lest anyone should think that he is illumined by him from whom he hears something in order to learn it—I do not say if it should fall to him to have some great man, but not even an angel, as a teacher. For the word of truth is applied from without by the ministry of a bodily voice; yet *neither he who plants is anything nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^103]. For a man hears either a man or an angel speaking; but in order that he may perceive and know that what is said is true, his mind is sprinkled within by that light which remains eternal, which shines even in the darkness. But just as this sun is not comprehended by the blind, although in a certain way it clothes them with its rays, so it is not comprehended by the darkness of folly[^104].

## The inner man remains in darkness without faith.

**25. 38.** But as to why, when he had said, *which illumines every man*, he added, *coming into this world*—whence this opinion was born, that he illumines the minds of little ones being born at the very moment of their bodily birth from the mother's womb—although in the Greek it is so set down that it can be understood also as the light itself coming into this world, nevertheless, if "a man coming into this world" must be taken, I think either that it was said simply, as many things are found in the Scriptures which, even if removed, would diminish nothing of the sense, or, if it must be believed to have been added for the sake of some distinction, perhaps this was said to distinguish spiritual illumination from that bodily one, which, whether through the luminaries of heaven or through any fires, illumines the eyes of the flesh—so that he called the inner man one coming into this world, because the outer man is corporeal, like this world, as though he said, "He illumines every man coming into a body," according to that which is written: *I obtained a good soul, and I came into an undefiled body*[^105].

Either, then, it was so said, if it was said for the sake of some distinction, *He illumines every man coming into this world*, as though it were said, "He illumines every inner man," because the inner man, when he truly becomes wise, is illumined by none but that which is the true light; or, if he wished to call illumination that reason itself by which the human soul is called rational—a reason which, as yet, as it were, at rest and as if asleep, yet implanted and in a certain way sown, lies hidden in little ones—as though the creation of the inner eye, then we must not deny that it comes to be when the soul is created, and that this is not absurdly understood as when a man comes into the world.

Yet even this eye, although already created, must remain in darkness if it does not believe in him who said, *I have come as light into the world, that everyone who believes in me may not remain in darkness*[^106]. That this comes about in little ones through the sacrament of baptism Mother Church does not doubt, who furnishes them a maternal heart and mouth, that they may be imbued with the sacred mysteries, because they cannot yet believe with their own heart unto righteousness, nor confess with their own mouth unto salvation[^107]. Nor does any of the faithful on that account hesitate to call them faithful—a name which surely comes from believing—although it was not they themselves, but others on their behalf, who answered amid the sacraments.

## The universal Church acknowledges that even infants need the redemption of Christ.

**26. 39.** It will become too long if we argue in like manner about each testimony. Hence I think it more convenient to heap up together the many that may have come to hand, or that will seem to suffice, by which it may appear that the Lord Jesus Christ came in the flesh and, having taken the form of a servant, became obedient unto the death of the cross[^108] for no other cause

than that by this dispensation of most merciful grace he might give life to, save, free, redeem, and illumine all those to whom—as to members set in his body—he is the head for the obtaining of the kingdom of heaven; all those who had first been set in the death of sins, in weaknesses, slavery, captivity, darkness, under the power of the devil, the prince of sinners; and so that he might become the mediator of God and men, through whom, after the enmities of our ungodliness were ended by the peace of that grace[^109], we might be reconciled to God unto eternal life, snatched from the eternal death which hung over such men.

For when this has appeared more abundantly, it will follow that they cannot pertain to that dispensation of Christ which was made through this his humility who do not need life, salvation, liberation, redemption, illumination. And since baptism, by which they are buried together with Christ[^110] so as to be incorporated into him as his members, that is, his faithful, pertains to this dispensation, surely baptism too is not necessary for those who have no need of that benefit of forgiveness and reconciliation which comes about through the mediator. Further, because they concede that little ones are to be baptized—who cannot, without doubt, go against the authority of the universal Church, handed down through the Lord and the Apostles—they must concede that little ones need those benefits of the mediator, so that, washed through the sacrament and the love of the faithful, and thus incorporated into the body of Christ, which is the Church, they may be reconciled to God, that in him they may become living, saved, freed, redeemed, illumined. From what, except from the death, vices, guilt, subjection, darkness of sins? Which, since in that age they have committed none through their own life, original sin remains.

## Gospel testimonies on the necessity of baptism for the saving of infants.

**27. 40.** This reasoning will then be the stronger when I have heaped up the many testimonies I promised. Already above we set down: *I came not to call the righteous, but sinners*[^111]. Likewise, when he had entered Zacchaeus's house: *Today*, he says, *salvation has come to this house, since this man too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of man came to seek and to save what had perished*[^112].

So too of the lost sheep, sought and found when the ninety-nine were left, so too of the coin which had perished out of ten[^113]. Hence, as he says, *it was fitting that repentance and the forgiveness of sins be preached in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem*[^114]. Mark too testifies that at the end of his Gospel the Lord said: *Going into the whole world, preach the Gospel to every creature. He who believes and is baptized will be saved; but he who does not believe will be condemned*[^115].

And who does not know that for infants to believe is to be baptized, and not to believe is not to be baptized? And from the Gospel of John, although we have already set down some passages, attend also to these. John the Baptist says of him: *Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sins of the world*[^116]; and he himself of himself: *Those who are of my sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me; and I give them eternal life, and they will not perish forever*[^117]. Since, therefore, little ones do not begin to be of his sheep except through baptism, surely, if they do not receive this, they will perish; for they will not have the eternal life which he will give to his sheep. Likewise in another place: *I am the way, the truth, and the life; no one comes to the Father but through me*[^118].

## Testimonies from 1 Peter.

**27. 41.** Taking up this teaching, see with how great an attestation the Apostles declare it. Peter in his first letter: *Blessed*, he says, *is God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to the multitude of his mercy has regenerated us into the hope of eternal life through the resurrection of Jesus Christ, into an immortal and undefiled inheritance, flourishing, kept in heaven for you, who by the power of God are guarded through faith unto a salvation prepared to be revealed openly in the last time*[^119].

And a little after: *that you may be found unto the praise and honor of Jesus Christ, whom you did not know, in whom now, not seeing him, you believe; whom, when you see him, you will exult with joy unspeakable and full of glory, receiving the testament of faith, the salvation of your souls*[^120]. Likewise in another place: *But you*, he says, *are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people in adoption, that you may proclaim the virtues of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light*[^121].

And again: *Christ*, he says, *suffered for our sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God*[^122]. Likewise, when he had recalled the eight men saved in Noah's ark: *So too*, he says, *baptism, in a like form, saves you*[^123]. From this salvation, then, and from this light little ones are strangers, and they will remain in perdition and darkness, unless through adoption they are joined to the people of God, holding Christ, who suffered, the just for the unjust, that he might bring them to God[^124].

## Testimonies from 1 John.

**27. 42.** From the letter of John too these passages came to me which seemed necessary to this question. *But if we walk in the light*, he says, *as he too is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son will purify us from every trespass*[^125]. Likewise in another place: *If we receive the testimony of men, the testimony of God is greater, because this is the testimony of God, which is greater, in that he has testified concerning his Son. He who believes in the Son of God has the testimony in himself. He who does not believe God makes him a liar, because he has not believed in the testimony which he testified concerning his Son. And this is the testimony, that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in his Son. He who has the Son has life; he who does not have the Son does not have life*[^126]. Not only the kingdom of heaven, therefore, but not even life will little ones have if they do not have the Son, whom they cannot have except through his baptism. Likewise in another place: *For this*, he says, *the Son of God was manifested, to loose the works of the devil*[^127]. Little ones, then, will not pertain to the grace of the manifestation of the Son of God, if he does not loose in them the works of the devil.

## Testimonies from the letter to the Romans.

**27. 43.** Now attend to the testimonies of the apostle Paul on this matter—surely so much more numerous as he wrote more letters and as he took more diligent care to commend the grace of God against those who gloried in works and who, being ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish their own righteousness, were not subject to the righteousness of God[^128]. In the letter to the Romans: *The righteousness*, he says, *of God upon all who believe; for there is no distinction. For all have sinned and need the glory of God, being justified freely by his grace through the redemption which is in Christ Jesus, whom God set forth as a propitiation through faith in his blood, for the showing of his righteousness on account of the passing over of former sins in the forbearance of God, for the showing of his righteousness in this time, that he may be just and the justifier of him who is of the faith of Jesus*[^129].

Likewise in another place: *To him who works*, he says, *the reward is not reckoned according to grace but according to debt; but to him who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness. As David too speaks of the blessedness of the man to whom God reckons righteousness without works: blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven and whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord has not imputed sin.* Likewise a little after: *But it was not written*, he says, *on his account alone, that it was reckoned to him, but also on our account, to whom it will be reckoned, who believe in him who raised Jesus Christ our Lord from the dead, who was handed over for our trespasses and rose again for our justification*[^130]. And a little after: *For if Christ*, he says, *while we were still weak, in due time died for the ungodly*[^131]. And elsewhere: *We know*, he says, *that the law is spiritual; but I am carnal, sold under sin. For what I do, I do not understand; for what I will, this I do not do, but what I hate, that I do. But if I do what I do not will, I consent to the law, that it is good. But now it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me. For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, good. For to will is present with me, but to accomplish the good I do not find. For I do not the good that I will, but the evil that I do not will, this I do. But if I do what I do not will, it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me. I find, then, the law for me when I will to do good, that evil is present with me. For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man, but I see another law in my members warring against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin which is in my members. Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^132]. Let those say, who can, that men are born otherwise than in the body of this death, so that they can also say that the grace of God through Jesus Christ, by which they are freed from the body of this death, is not necessary for them. Likewise a little after: *For what was impossible for the law, in that it was weakened through the flesh, God, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and concerning sin, condemned sin in the flesh*[^133]. Let those say, who dare, that it was fitting for Christ to be born in the likeness of sinful flesh otherwise than because we were born in sinful flesh.

## Testimonies from the letters to the Corinthians.

**27. 44.** Likewise to the Corinthians: *For I handed on to you among the first things*, he says, *what I also received, that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures*[^134].

Likewise to the same Corinthians in the second letter: *For the love of Christ compels us, judging this, that one died for all, therefore all died. And he died for all, that those who live may no longer live for themselves, but for him who died for them and rose again. Therefore from now on we know no one according to the flesh; and if we knew Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him no longer. If, then, anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; the old things have passed away, behold, they are made new. But all things are from God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation. In what way? Because God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses, and placing in us the word of reconciliation. For Christ, then, we serve as ambassadors, as though God were exhorting through us; we beseech you for Christ, be reconciled to God. Him who knew no sin he made sin for us, that we might be the righteousness of God in him. And as fellow workers we also entreat you not to receive the grace of God in vain. For he says: In an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you. Behold, now is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation*[^135]. If little ones do not pertain to this reconciliation and salvation, who seeks them for the baptism of Christ?

But if they do pertain to it, they are among the dead men for whom he died, and they cannot be reconciled and saved by him unless he forgive and not reckon their trespasses.

## Testimonies from the letter to the Galatians.

**27. 45.** Likewise to the Galatians: *Grace to you and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, who gave himself for our sins, that he might rescue us from the present evil age*[^136]. And in another place: *The law was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, ordained through angels in the hand of a mediator. But a mediator is not of one; yet God is one. Is the law, then, against the promises of God? By no means! For if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness would indeed be from the law. But Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^137].

## Testimonies from the letter to the Ephesians.

**27. 46.** To the Ephesians too: *And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, of that spirit who now works in the sons of unbelief, among whom we all too once conducted ourselves in the desires of our flesh, doing the will of the flesh and of the affections, and we were by nature children of wrath, like the rest; but God, who is rich in mercy, on account of the great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, made us alive together with Christ, by whose grace we have been saved*[^138]. And a little after: *By grace*, he says, *you have been saved through faith, and this not of yourselves, but it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone be lifted up. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared, that we should walk in them*[^139].

And a little after: *You who were*, he says, *at that time without Christ, alienated from the fellowship of Israel and strangers to the covenants and the promise, having no hope and without God in this world; but now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been made near in the blood of Christ. For he himself is our peace, who made both one and, breaking down the dividing wall of the partition, the enmities, in his flesh making void the law of commandments with its decrees, that he might form the two in himself into one new man, making peace, and might reconcile both in one body to God, through the cross slaying the enmities in himself. And coming, he preached peace to you who were far off, and peace to those who were near, because through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father*[^140].

Likewise elsewhere: *as the truth is in Jesus, that you put off, according to your former manner of life, the old man, who is corrupted according to the desires of deception; but be renewed in the spirit of your mind and put on the new man, who was created according to God in righteousness and the holiness of truth*[^141]. And elsewhere: *Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you were sealed unto the day of redemption*[^142].

## Testimonies from the letter to the Colossians.

**27. 47.** To the Colossians too he speaks thus: *Giving thanks to the Father, who has made us fit for a share in the lot of the saints in light, who rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love, in whom we have redemption in the forgiveness of sins*[^143]. And in another place: *And you are*, he says, *filled in him, who is the head of all rule and power, in whom you were also circumcised with a circumcision not made by hand, in the putting off of the body of the flesh, in the circumcision of Christ, buried together with him in baptism, in which you also rose together through faith in the working of God, who raised him from the dead; and you, when you were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, he made alive with him, forgiving us all our trespasses, blotting out the bond of the decree that was against us, which was contrary to us, taking it out of the way and nailing it to the cross, stripping himself of the flesh; the rulers and powers he made an example, triumphing over them confidently in himself*[^144].

## Testimonies from the letters to Timothy.

**27. 48.** And to Timothy: *The saying is human and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first. But for this reason I obtained mercy, that in me first Christ Jesus might show all his long-suffering, for the instruction of those who are to believe in him unto eternal life*[^145]. Likewise he says: *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself a redemption for all*[^146]. In the second letter too to the same man: *Do not, then*, he says, *be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me his prisoner, but labor together for the Gospel according to the power of God, who saves us and calls us with his holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given to us in Christ Jesus before the eternal ages, but is now made manifest through the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has indeed made void death, but has brought to light life and incorruption through the Gospel*[^147].

## Testimonies from the letter to Titus.

**27. 49.** To Titus too: *Awaiting*, he says, *that blessed hope and the manifestation of the glory of the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity and cleanse for himself an abounding people, zealous for good works*[^148]. And in another place: *But when the kindness and humanity of God our Savior shone forth, not by works of righteousness that we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, through the washing of regeneration and the renewal of the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out most richly upon us through Jesus Christ our Savior, so that, justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life*[^149].

## Testimonies from the letter to the Hebrews.

**27. 50.** The letter to the Hebrews too—although it is uncertain to some, yet, since I have read certain men who hold views contrary to this our opinion concerning the baptism of little ones wishing to bring even it forward as a witness for some of their opinions, and since the authority of the Eastern Churches, which have it even among the canonical books, weighs more with me—it must be noticed how many testimonies for us it contains. In its very opening it is read: *In many parts and in many ways God spoke of old to the fathers through the prophets; last, in these days he has spoken to us through the Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom he also made the ages. Who, being the splendor of his glory and the figure of his substance, and bearing all things by the word of his power, having made by himself a purification of sins, sits at the right hand of the Majesty on high*[^150]. And after a few words: *For if the word spoken through angels was made firm, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense of reward, how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation*[^151]?

And in another place: *Therefore, since the children shared in blood and flesh, he too in like manner partook of theirs, that through death he might make void him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and might free those who through fear of death were all their life subject to slavery.* And a little after: *Hence he had*, he says, *to be made like his brethren in all things, that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the things that pertain to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people*[^152]. And elsewhere: *Let us hold*, he says, *our confession; for we do not have a priest who cannot be compassionate toward our weaknesses; for he has been tried in all things according to our likeness, without sin*[^153].

And in another place: *He has*, he says, *a priesthood that does not pass away. Hence he is able to save completely those who come through him to God, always living to make intercession for them. For it was fitting that we should have such a high priest, just, without malice, undefiled, separated from sinners, made higher than the heavens, not having a daily necessity, like the high priests, to offer sacrifice first for his own sins, then for the people; for this he did once, offering himself*[^154]. And in another place: *For Christ did not enter into a sanctuary made by hand, which are likenesses of the true, but into heaven itself, to appear before the face of God for us; not so that he might offer himself often, as the high priest enters into the sanctuary once a year with another's blood. Otherwise he would have had to suffer often since the foundation of the world; but now once, at the end of the ages, he has been manifested for the forgiveness of sins through his own sacrifice. And just as it is appointed for men to die once, and after this the judgment, so Christ too was offered once to bear the sins of many, and a second time he will appear without sins to those who await him unto salvation*[^155].

## Testimonies from the Apocalypse.

**27. 51.** The Apocalypse of John too testifies that these praises are offered to Christ through a new song: *Worthy are you to receive the book and to open its seals, for you were slain and have redeemed us to God by your blood out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation*[^156].

## Testimonies from the Acts of the Apostles.

**27. 52.** Likewise in the *Acts of the Apostles* the apostle Peter said that the Lord Jesus is the author of life, rebuking the Jews because they had killed him, speaking thus: *But you laid a burden upon the holy and just one and denied him and asked that a man, a murderer, should live and be granted to you; for you killed the author of life*[^157]. And in another place: *This is the stone rejected by you the builders, which has become the head of the corner. For there is no other name under heaven given to men, in which we must be saved*[^158]. And elsewhere: *The God of our fathers raised up Jesus, whom you killed by hanging on a tree. Him God exalted by his glory as prince and savior, to give repentance to Israel and the forgiveness of sins in him*[^159]. Likewise in another place: *To him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives the forgiveness of sins through his hand*[^160]. Likewise in the same book the apostle Paul: *Let it be known to you, then*, he says, *men and brethren, that through this man forgiveness of sins is announced to you; from all the things from which you could not be justified in the law of Moses, in him everyone who believes is justified*[^161].

## Implicit testimonies from the Old Testament.

**27. 53.** By so great a mound of testimonies whose exaltation against the truth of God would not be pressed down? And many others indeed can be found, but the care of finishing this work must not be carelessly disregarded. From the books of the Old Testament too I thought it superfluous to bring many attestations of the divine oracles for this opinion, since indeed what is there hidden under the veil, as it were, of earthly promises, is in the preaching of the New Testament revealed. And the Lord himself briefly demonstrated and defined the usefulness of the old books, saying that what had been written of him in the Law and the Prophets and the Psalms had to be fulfilled, and that this very thing was that Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead on the third day, and that repentance and the forgiveness of sins had to be preached in his name through all nations, beginning from Jerusalem[^162]. And Peter says, as I recalled a little before, that to him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives the forgiveness of sins through his hand[^163].

## Explicit testimonies from the Old Testament.

**27. 54.** Nevertheless it is more convenient to bring forth even from the Old Testament itself a few testimonies, which ought to serve either as a supplement or rather as a culmination. The Lord himself, speaking through the prophet in a psalm, says: *To the saints who are in his land he has made wonderful all my wills in them*[^164]—not "their merits," but *my wills.* For what of theirs is there but what follows? *Their infirmities were multiplied*, beyond the measure in which they were infirm. To this too the law entered in, that the trespass might abound[^165]. But what does he add? *Afterward they made haste.*

When their infirmities were multiplied, that is, when the trespass abounded, they sought the physician more eagerly, so that where sin abounded, grace abounded yet more. Finally: *I will not gather their assemblies from bloods*, since by the many bloods of sacrifices—when they were formerly gathered into the tabernacle or the temple—they were convicted as sinners rather than cleansed. "No longer, then," he says, "will I gather their assemblies from bloods"; for one blood was given for many, by which they would be truly cleansed. Finally there follows: *Nor will I be mindful of their names through my lips*, as of the cleansed, as of the renewed. For their names were formerly "sons of the flesh, sons of the age, sons of wrath, sons of the devil, unclean, sinners, ungodly," but afterward "sons of God"—the new name belonging to the new man singing the new song through the New Testament. Let men not be ungrateful to the grace of God, the small with the great[^166], from the lesser to the greater[^167]. The voice of the whole Church is: *I have gone astray like a lost sheep*[^168]; the voice of all the members of Christ is: *We have all gone astray like sheep*[^169], *and he himself was handed over for our sins*[^170].

That whole passage of prophecy in Isaiah, which, when it had been expounded to him by Philip[^171], made that eunuch of Queen Candace believe in him—see how often it commends this very thing and, as it were, drives it home again and again, as to certain proud or contentious men. *A man*, he says, *in affliction and one who knows how to bear infirmities; for which reason his face was turned away, was treated with injury and not greatly esteemed. He bears our infirmities and is in sorrows for us, and we esteemed him to be in sorrows and in affliction and in punishment. But he was wounded on account of our sins and made weak on account of our iniquities. The chastisement of our peace was upon him; by his bruise we are healed. We have all gone astray like sheep, and the Lord handed him over for our sins. And he, because he was ill-treated, did not open his mouth; like a sheep he was led to the slaughter, and like a lamb before its shearer he was without voice, so he did not open his mouth. In his humiliation his judgment was taken away. Who will declare his generation? For his life will be taken from the earth. For the iniquities of my people he was led to death. I will give the wicked for his burial and the rich for his death, because he did no iniquity nor deceit with his mouth. The Lord wills to purify him from affliction. If you give your soul for your trespasses, you will see a seed of the longest life. And the Lord wills to take away his soul from sorrows, to show him light and to form him by understanding, to justify the just one who serves many well, and he himself will bear their sins. Therefore he will inherit many and will divide the spoils of the strong, because his soul was handed over to death and he was reckoned among the wicked, and he himself bore the sins of many and was handed over for their iniquities*[^172]. Attend also to that of the same prophet which, having performed even the office of a reader, he himself recited in the synagogue as fulfilled in himself: *The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, for which reason he has anointed me; he has sent me to preach good news to the humble, to bring refreshment to those who are in the affliction of heart, to proclaim to captives release and to the blind sight*[^173]. Let us all, then, acknowledge him—and let none of those be excepted who wish to cling to his body, to enter through him into his sheepfold, to pertain to the perpetual life and salvation he promised to his own—let us all, I say, acknowledge him *who did no sin and bore our sins in his body upon the tree, that, separated from sins, we might live with righteousness; by whose wounds we have been healed*, when we were weak, *like straying cattle*[^174].

## All men need the forgiveness at least of original sin. No one is saved outside the kingdom of Christ. No one will rise except through Christ.

**28. 55.** Since these things are so, sound faith and sound doctrine have never held that any of those who have come to Christ through baptism is excepted from the grace of the forgiveness of sins, or that there can be for anyone an eternal salvation apart from his kingdom. For this salvation is prepared to be revealed in the last time[^175], that is, in the resurrection of the dead who pertain not to the eternal death, which is called the second death, but to the eternal life which God, who does not lie, promised to his saints and faithful—of which life all who are partakers will not be given life except in Christ, just as in Adam all die[^176]. For just as all who pertain to the generation of the will of the flesh[^177] die only in Adam, in whom all sinned, so of these all who pertain to the regeneration of the will of the spirit are given life only in Christ, in whom all are justified, because just as through one all unto condemnation, so through one all unto justification[^178]. Nor is there for anyone any middle place, such that he could be anywhere but with the devil, who is not with Christ. Hence the Lord himself, wishing to take from the hearts of those who believe wrongly that I-know-not-what middle state which some try to attribute to little ones who are not baptized—so that, as it were, by the merit of innocence they are in eternal life, but, because they are not baptized, they are not with Christ in his kingdom—uttered a definitive sentence to stop these mouths, where he says: *He who is not with me is against me*[^179]. Take, then, any little one you please: if he is already with Christ, why is he baptized?

But if, as the truth holds, he is baptized for this reason, that he may be with Christ, then surely the one not baptized is not with Christ; and because he is not with Christ, he is against Christ; for we ought not and cannot weaken or alter so manifest a sentence of his. Whence, then, is he against Christ, if not from sin? For it is not from body and soul, both of which are creatures of God.

Further, if from sin, what sin in that age but the original and ancient one? For there is one flesh of sin, in which all are born unto condemnation, and there is one flesh in the likeness of the flesh of sin, through which all are freed from condemnation. Nor is "all" so said as though whoever are born in the flesh of sin should be understood to be, all of them, cleansed through the flesh like the flesh of sin—*for faith is not of all*[^180]—but all who pertain to the generation of carnal wedlock are born only in the flesh of sin, and all who pertain to the generation of spiritual wedlock are cleansed only through the flesh like the flesh of sin; that is, those through Adam unto condemnation, these through Christ unto justification. As if we should say, for example, "There is one midwife in this city, who receives all, and there is one teacher of letters here, who teaches all," neither there can "all" be understood except those who are born, nor here "all" except those who learn; yet not all who are born learn letters; but it is clear that there too it was rightly said, "She receives all, apart from whose hands no one is born," and here it was rightly said, "He teaches all, apart from whose instruction no one learns."

## A conclusion from so many testimonies.

**28. 56.** But when all the divine testimonies I have recalled have been considered—whether arguing about each one singly or heaping up many together—or whatever like ones I have not recalled, nothing is found except what the universal Church holds, which must keep watch against all profane novelties[^181]: that every man is separated from God except one who is reconciled to God through the mediator Christ; and that none can be separated except by sins intervening; therefore that none can be reconciled except by the forgiveness of sins through the one grace of the most merciful Savior, through the one victim of the most true priest; and thus that all the sons of the woman who believed the serpent, so as to be corrupted by lust[^182], are not freed from the body of this death except through the Son of the Virgin who believed the angel, so as to conceive without lust[^183].

## The same scope of original sin and of carnal concupiscence.

**29. 57.** The good of marriage, then, is not the heat of concupiscence, but a certain lawful and honorable manner of using that heat for the propagation of offspring, suited not to the satisfying of lust. It is that will, not this pleasure, that is conjugal. That, therefore, which in the members of the body of this death is moved disobediently and tries to draw the whole soul, cast down, to itself, and which neither rises up when the mind wills nor rests when the mind wills—this is the evil of sin, with which every man is born.

But when it is curbed from unlawful corruptions and is permitted only for the ordered propagation of the supplements of the human race, this is the good of marriage, through which by an ordered fellowship a man is born. But no one is reborn in the body of Christ unless he is first born in the body of sin. Now, just as to use a good thing badly is evil, so to use an evil thing well is good. These two, then, good and evil, and the other two, good use and bad use, joined to each other make four differences. He uses a good thing well who dedicates continence to God; he uses a good thing badly who dedicates continence to an idol; he uses an evil thing badly who lets concupiscence loose in adultery; he uses an evil thing well who restrains concupiscence by wedlock.

Just as, then, it is better to use a good thing well than to use an evil thing well, since both are good, so *he who gives his virgin in marriage does well, and he who does not give her does better*[^184]. On this question I have discussed much more fully and much more sufficiently in two books, one *On the Good of Marriage*, the other *On Holy Virginity*, as much as the Lord granted, according to the smallness of my powers.

Let those, therefore, not defend the evil of concupiscence by the good of marriage who exalt the flesh and blood of the transgressor against the flesh and blood of the Redeemer; let them not be lifted up in the pride of another's error, those of whose small age the Lord gave us an example of humility. He alone was born without sin, whom the Virgin conceived without a man's embrace, not by the concupiscence of the flesh but by the obedience of the mind; she alone could bring forth a medicine for our wound, who sent forth the shoot of pious offspring not from the wound of sin.

## How the Pelagians understood the words of John 3:5.

**30. 58.** Now let us examine more diligently, as much as the Lord helps, that very chapter of the Gospel where he says: *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of God*[^185]. Were these men not moved by this sentence, they would judge that little ones should not be baptized at all. "But because he did not say," they say, "unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not have salvation or eternal life, but only said, he will not enter into the kingdom of God, little ones are to be baptized for this reason, that they may be even with Christ in the kingdom of God, where they will not be if they have not been baptized—although even without baptism, if little ones die, they will have salvation and eternal life, since they are bound by no bond of sin." In saying this, these men first never explain by what justice the image of God, having no sin, should be separated from the kingdom of God.

Then let us see whether the Lord Jesus, the one and only good Teacher[^186], in this very Gospel reading did not signify and show that it is only through the forgiveness of sins that the baptized come to the kingdom of God—although for those who understand rightly it ought to have sufficed that it was said, *Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God*; and, *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God.* For why should he be born again, unless to be renewed? Whence renewed, unless from oldness? From what oldness, unless that in which our old man was crucified together with him, *that the body of sin might be made void*[^187]? Or whence does the image of God not enter into the kingdom of God, unless the impediment of sin prevents it? Nevertheless, as we proposed, let us see, intently, as much as we can, and diligently, the whole context itself of the Gospel reading pertaining to the matter at hand.

## The conversation of Jesus with Nicodemus.

**30. 59.** *Now there was a man*, he says, *of the Pharisees, Nicodemus by name, a ruler of the Jews. He came to Jesus by night and said to him: "Rabbi, we know that you have come from God as a teacher; for no one can do these signs that you do, unless God is with him." Jesus answered and said to him: "Amen, amen I say to you, unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." Nicodemus says to him: "How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter again into his mother's womb and be born?"*

*Jesus answered: "Amen, amen I say to you, unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the spirit is spirit. Do not marvel that I said to you: You must be born again. The Spirit breathes where he wills, and you hear his voice; but you do not know whence he comes or whither he goes. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit." Nicodemus answered and said to him: "How can these things be?"*

*Jesus answered and said to him: "Are you a teacher in Israel and do not know these things? Amen, amen I say to you, that we speak what we know and bear witness to what we see, and you do not receive our testimony. If I told you earthly things and you did not believe, how, if I tell you heavenly things, will you believe? No one has ascended into heaven but he who descended from heaven, the Son of man, who is in heaven. And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of man be lifted up, that everyone who believes in him may not perish but have eternal life. For God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son, that everyone who believes in him may not perish but have eternal life.*

*For God did not send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him. He who believes in him is not judged; but he who does not believe is already judged, because he does not believe in the name of the only-begotten Son of God. And this is the judgment, that light came into the world, and men loved darkness more than light; for their works were evil. For everyone who does evil hates the light and does not come to the light, lest his works be reproved; but he who does the truth comes to the light, that his works may be made manifest, because they are done in God*[^188]. Thus far the whole connected discourse pertains to the matter about which we are inquiring; thereafter the narrator turns aside to something else.

## An unraveling of John 3: in what way the spiritual regeneration of man comes about.

**31. 60.** When, therefore, Nicodemus did not understand what was being said, he asked the Lord how these things could come about. Let us see what the Lord answers to this. For surely, if he deigns to answer the question, "How can these things come about?" he is going to say in what way the spiritual regeneration of men coming from carnal generation can come about. And so, having briefly noted the inexperience of one who preferred himself to others in respect of teaching, and having reproved the unbelief of all such men, in that they did not receive the testimony of the truth, he added also that he had told them earthly things and they had not believed, asking, or wondering, how they were going to believe heavenly things.

Yet he goes on and answers—that others will believe, if these do not believe—to that which he was asked, how these things can come about: *No one*, he says, *has ascended into heaven but he who descended from heaven, the Son of man, who is in heaven.* "Thus," he says, "spiritual generation will come about, so that there are heavenly men from earthly ones, which they will not be able to attain unless they are made my members, so that he himself ascends who descended, because no one ascends but he who descended."

Unless, therefore, all who are to be changed and lifted up run together into the unity of Christ, so that Christ, who descended, may himself ascend—reckoning his body, that is, his Church, as nothing other than himself, since of Christ and the Church it is more truly understood, *The two will be in one flesh*[^189], of which he himself said, *Therefore now they are not two, but one flesh*[^190]—they will not be able to ascend at all, because *no one has ascended into heaven but he who descended from heaven, the Son of man, who is in heaven.*

For although he was made the Son of man on earth, he nevertheless judged his divinity, by which, remaining in heaven, he descended to earth, not unworthy of the name "Son of man," just as he deemed his flesh worthy of the name "Son of God"—lest, as it were, two Christs be taken, one God and the other man, but one and the same God and man: God, because *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*; man, because *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^191]. And by this, through the distance of divinity and infirmity, the Son of God remained in heaven and the Son of man walked on earth; but through the unity of the person, by which both substances are one Christ, the Son of God walked on earth, and the same Son of man remained in heaven.

Faith in things more credible, then, comes from things less credible that have been believed. For if the divine substance, far more distant and by an incomparable diversity more sublime, could for our sake so take up the human substance that one person came to be, and so the Son of man, who was on earth through the infirmity of the flesh, was the same one in heaven through the divinity shared with the flesh, how much more credible is it that other men, his holy and faithful ones, become with the man Christ one Christ, so that, with all ascending through this his grace and fellowship, the one Christ himself, who descended from heaven, ascends into heaven! So too the Apostle says: *As in one body we have many members, but all the members of the body, though they are many, are one body, so also is Christ*[^192]. He did not say, "so also are Christ's," that is, the body of Christ or the members of Christ, but *so also is Christ*, calling the head and the body one Christ.

## An unraveling of John 3: little ones too are poisoned by the serpent's bite.

**32. 61.** Great and wondrous is this condescension! And because it cannot come about except through the forgiveness of sins, he goes on and says: *And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of man be lifted up, that everyone who believes in him may not perish but have eternal life.* What then happened in the desert we know: many were dying from the bites of serpents. Then the people, confessing their sins, besought the Lord through Moses that he would take this venom away from them. And so Moses, by the command of the Lord, lifted up a bronze serpent in the desert and warned the people that whoever was bitten by a serpent should look upon that one lifted up; doing this, they were immediately healed[^193].

What is the serpent lifted up but the death of Christ, signified in that manner by which what is effected is signified through what effects it? For from the serpent came death, who persuaded man to the sin by which he would deserve to die. But the Lord did not transfer sin into his flesh, like the serpent's venom, yet he did transfer death, so that there might be in the likeness of sinful flesh a punishment without guilt, whence in the flesh of sin both guilt and punishment might be loosed. As, then, in those days whoever looked upon the lifted-up serpent was both healed of the venom and freed from death, so now whoever is conformed to the likeness of the death of Christ[^194] through faith and his baptism is freed both from sin through justification and from death through resurrection. For this is what he says: *that everyone who believes in him may not perish but have eternal life.* What need is there, then, for a little one to be conformed to the death of Christ through baptism, if he is not at all poisoned by the serpent's bite?

## An unraveling of John 3: the grace of Christ is denied to infants if baptism is denied.

**33. 62.** Then if—as he goes on to say—*God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son, that everyone who believes in him may not perish but have eternal life*, a little one was going to perish and not have eternal life if through the sacrament of baptism he did not believe in the only-begotten Son of God, while meanwhile he came thus, not to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him; especially since he goes on and says: *He who believes in him is not judged; but he who does not believe is already judged, because he does not believe in the name of the only-begotten Son of God.* Where, then, do we place baptized little ones but among the faithful, as the authority of the whole Church everywhere proclaims?

Therefore among those who have believed—for this is acquired for them through the power of the sacrament and the response of those who present them—and by this, those who are not baptized among those who have not believed. Further, if those who are baptized are not judged, then these, because they lack baptism, are judged. But what he adds, *And this is the judgment, that light came into the world, and men loved darkness more than light*—whence does the *light come into the world* if not of his own coming that he speaks? Without the sacrament of his coming, how are little ones said to be in the light? Or how do they too not have it in their love of darkness, who, just as they themselves do not believe, so do not judge that their little ones should be baptized, when they fear the death of the body for them?

But he says that the works of the one who comes to the light are done in God, because he understands that his justification pertains not to his own merits but to the grace of God. *For it is God*, says the Apostle, *who works in us both to will and to work according to good will*[^195]. In this way, then, the spiritual regeneration of all who come from carnal generation to Christ comes about. He himself opened this, he himself showed it, when he was asked how these things could come about; he made human argumentation in this matter free to no one. Let not little ones be made strangers to the grace of the forgiveness of sins; there is no other passing over to Christ, no one can otherwise be reconciled to God and come to God except through Christ.

## The form of baptism indicates that in infants the forgiveness of sin comes about.

**34. 63.** What shall I say of the very form of the sacrament? I would wish that one of those who hold the contrary view would bring me a little one to be baptized. What does my exorcism do in him, if he is not held in the family of the devil? He himself was surely going to answer me on behalf of that same little one whom he carried, because the child could not answer for himself. How, then, was he going to say that the child renounces the devil, of whom there was nothing in him?

How was he to turn to God, from whom he was not turned away? To believe, among other things, in the forgiveness of sins, none of which was granted to him? Indeed, for my part, if I supposed him to hold contrary to these things, I would not allow him even to enter into the sacraments with the little one; but with what face he would bear himself toward men in this, with what mind toward God, I do not know, nor do I wish to say anything more severe.

That a false or deceitful form of baptism, therefore, would be handed on to little ones—in which it would seem to be uttered and done, and yet no forgiveness of sins would come about—some of them have seen that nothing more execrable and detestable could be said or thought. Accordingly, as far as concerns the baptism of little ones, in order that it be necessary for them, they concede that redemption too is needful for them, as is contained in a very brief booklet of one of them, who nevertheless there refused to express more openly the forgiveness of any sin.

But as you yourself indicated to me in your letter, they now confess, as you say, that even in little ones the forgiveness of sins comes about through baptism. And no wonder; for redemption could not be understood in any other way. "Yet not originally," they say, "but in a life now their own, after they were born, they began to have sin."

## Let the Pelagians first agree among themselves.

**34. 64.** Wherefore you see how great a distance there now is between those against whom in this work I have already long and much argued—one of whom too I have read a book containing the things I refuted as I could. Between these men, then, as I had begun to say, who maintain that little ones are altogether pure and free from all sin, both original and their own, and these others, who think that, once born, they have contracted sins of their own, from which they believe they ought to be purified through baptism, you see how great is the difference.

Accordingly these latter, by looking to the Scriptures and the authority of the whole Church and the form of the sacrament itself, had well seen that through baptism the forgiveness of sins comes about in little ones, but that whatever it is that is in them is original, they either will not say or cannot see. The former, however, in human nature itself, which is ready at hand for all to consider, had well seen—which was easy—that that age could in its own life have contracted no sin, but, lest they confess original sin, they say there is no sin at all in little ones. In these matters, then, in which each says something true singly, let them first agree among themselves, and it will follow consequently that they disagree with us in no part. For if the former concede to the latter that to baptized little ones the forgiveness of sins comes about, and the latter concede to the former that little ones—as nature itself cries out in silent infants—have as yet contracted no sin of their own life, both will concede to us that nothing remains but the original sin, which is loosed through baptism in little ones.

## In little ones there is no sin of their own life.

**35. 65.** Or are we indeed going to inquire into this too, and argue about it, and spend time on it, in order to prove and teach how, through their own will—without which there can be no sin of one's own life—infants have committed no evil, who for this reason are called innocent by all? Does not so great a weakness of soul and body, so great an ignorance of things, so utter an incapacity for any precept, no sense or movement of either natural or written law, no use of reason in either direction, proclaim and indicate this by a silence far more attestable than our speech? Let the matter avail something to persuade of itself by its very evidence; for nowhere do I so fail to find what to say as where the thing about which one speaks is more manifest than anything that is said.

## No sin can be imputed to the will of infants.

**35. 66.** Yet I would wish that whoever holds this view would say what sin he has seen or supposed in an infant fresh from the womb—for whose redeeming he confesses that baptism is already necessary—what evil he has committed in this his own life through his own soul or body. If perhaps it be that he cries and is a weariness to his elders, it would be strange if this should be charged to iniquity rather than to unhappiness. Or that he cannot be restrained from his very weeping by any reasoning of his own or by anyone's prohibition?

But this belongs to ignorance, in which he lies most deeply sunk—the ignorance by which, when after a little time he is able, in anger he will even strike his mother and often the very breasts which, when he is hungry, he demands. These things are not only borne but even loved in little ones; and by what affection but the carnal, by which even laughter and jest delight us, seasoned, as it were, by the very absurdity of even sharp-witted men? For if it were felt in the way it is said, they would now be laughed at not as witty but as fools. We see fools too, whom the common people call "simpletons," brought in for the diversion of the sensible and, in the appraisal of slaves, held more precious than the sensible: so much does carnal affection avail, even in those not at all foolish, in the delight taken in another's misfortune. For although another's foolishness is pleasant to a man, yet he himself would not have wished to be such;

and if a father foreknew that his own little son—from whose prattling he, glad, awaits and provokes such things—would be such when he grew up, he would in no way doubt that he should be lamented more pitiably than if dead. But while the hope of growth is present and the light of natural ability is believed to be coming with the advance of age, it comes about that the abuses of little ones, even against their parents, are not only not injurious but even welcome and pleasant.

Which indeed no one of the prudent would approve—that from sayings or doings of this kind they should not only not be restrained, when they can already be restrained, but even be incited to them by a zeal for laughter and the vanity of their elders. For very often that age, already recognizing father and mother, dares not curse either of them unless either by one of them or by both it is permitted or commanded. But these things belong to those little ones who already burst forth into words and can now express the movements of their soul by whatever signs of the tongue. Let us rather look at that most profound ignorance of the newly born, from which, by making progress, they have come to that not-to-last stammering foolishness, as though tending toward knowledge and speech.

## Whence comes the ignorance of infants.

**36. 67.** Let us consider, I say, those shadows of the mind—a mind surely rational—in which they are utterly ignorant even of God, to whose sacraments they offer resistance even when they are baptized; into these shadows I ask whence and when they have been submerged. Did they then contract them here, and in this their own life, through excessive negligence, forget God, but live prudent and religious lives, perhaps in their mothers' wombs? Let those say these things who have dared; let those hear who will; let those believe who can. But I, for my part, think that all whose mind is not clouded by the obstinacy of defending their own opinion cannot hold these views.

Or is there no evil of ignorance, and therefore none to be purified? And what does that voice mean: *Remember not the sins of my youth and of my ignorance*[^196]? For although the sins more deserving of condemnation are those committed by those who know, yet, if there were no sins of ignorance, we would not read what I have recalled: *Remember not the sins of my youth and of my ignorance.* Into those, then, the densest shadows of ignorance, where the soul of an infant fresh from the womb—surely the soul of a man, surely a rational soul—lies not only untaught but even unteachable, why or when or whence has it been thrust? If it is the nature of man so to begin, and this nature is not already corrupted, why was Adam not created such? Why was he capable of a precept and able to impose names on his wife and on all the animals? For of her too he said, *She will be called woman*; and, *Whatever Adam called a living soul, that is its name*[^197].

But this one, not knowing where he is, what he is, by whom he was created, from whom he was begotten, already guilty of a trespass, not yet capable of a precept, so wrapped and pressed in the deep gloom of ignorance that he can neither, as it were, be roused from sleep so as at least to recognize these things when shown, but the time must be awaited in which he gradually digests this I-know-not-what, as it were, drunkenness—not through a single night, as any heaviest one is wont to be, but through several months and years—until which is done, we tolerate in little ones so many things which in their elders we punish, that they cannot at all be numbered: this so great evil of ignorance and infirmity, if little ones already born in this life have contracted it, where, when, how, by some great impiety committed, were they suddenly wrapped in so great a darkness?

## If Adam was not created such as we are born, why was Christ born an infant and weak?

**37. 68.** Someone will say: "If this nature is not pure but the beginnings of a corrupted nature, because Adam was not created such, why did Christ, far more excellent and certainly without any sin, born of a virgin, nevertheless appear begotten in this same infirmity and age?" To this proposition we answer: Adam was not created such because, with no parent's sin going before, he was not created in sinful flesh; we are such because, with his sin going before, we were born in sinful flesh; Christ is such because, in order to condemn sin from sin, he was born in the likeness of sinful flesh[^198].

For the question concerning Adam is not what pertains to the size of the body, because he was not made a little one but with the full mass of his members—for it may be said that beasts too were so created, and yet that it was not brought about by their sin that little offspring are born of them, what sort of thing this is we do not now inquire—but the question is about a certain vigor of that mind and use of reason, by which Adam, being teachable, might both grasp the precept of God and the law of the commandment and easily be able to keep it, if he willed. But now man is born so that he is utterly unable to do this, on account of a horrendous ignorance and infirmity not of the flesh but of the mind—since we all confess that in a little one there lives a rational soul not of another but of the same substance as was in the first man. Although even that so great infirmity of the flesh itself shows, as I think, something penal. For it gives one pause whether, if those first men had not sinned, they were going to have such children as would use neither tongue nor hands nor feet. For perhaps, on account of the capacity of the womb, it would have been necessary for little ones to be born; although, since a rib is a small part of the body[^199], yet not for this reason did God make a small mate for the man, whom he built up into a woman, whence the omnipotence of the Creator could also have made her children, as soon as they were brought forth, grown at once.

## The ignorance and infirmity of the infant.

**38. 69.** But, to omit this, he could certainly have granted what he granted to many beasts too, whose young, although they are little and do not advance in mind even with the increase of body, since they do not have a rational soul, yet even when tiniest both run and recognize their mothers, and are not brought to the suckling of the udders by another's care and aid, but they themselves know them, set in a hidden place of the mother's body, with wonderful ease.

On the contrary, the newborn man has feet not fit for walking, nor hands at least apt for scratching; and unless, by the work of the one nursing, the teat of the udder is put to his motionless lips, he does not feel where they are, and, with the breasts lying beside him, he can rather cry from hunger than suck. Accordingly this infirmity of the body altogether agrees with the infirmity of the mind. Nor would the flesh of Christ have been in the likeness of sinful flesh, unless this were the flesh of sin, by whose weight the rational soul is so weighed down—whether it too be drawn from the parents, or created there, or breathed in from above, which I now defer to inquire into.

## To what extent sin is made void through baptism in little ones.

**39. 70.** In little ones, certainly, the grace of God through the baptism of him who came in the likeness of sinful flesh brings it about that the flesh of sin is made void. But it is made void not so that in the very living flesh the concupiscence sprinkled and inborn is suddenly consumed and is no more, but so that what was present in the one born may not harm the one who has died. For if after baptism he lives and is able to come to an age capable of a precept, there he has something with which to fight and which, with God helping, he may overcome, if he has not received his grace in vain[^200], if he has not wished to be reprobate[^201].

For neither is this granted to grown people in baptism—except perhaps by the ineffable miracle of the most omnipotent Creator—that the law of sin which is in the members, warring against the law of the mind[^202], be utterly extinguished and be no more; but that whatever evil has been done, said, or thought by the man, while with subjected mind he was a slave to that same concupiscence, all be abolished and held as though it had not been done, while concupiscence itself, the bond of guilt being loosed by which through it the devil held the soul, and the barrier destroyed by which it separated man from his Creator, remains in the conflict in which we chastise our body and bring it into subjection[^203], either to be relaxed for lawful and necessary uses or to be checked by the continent man.

But since by the divine Spirit, who knows all things of the human race—whether past or present or future—far better than we, such a human life has been foreknown and foretold that not every living person is justified in the sight of God[^204], it comes about that, through ignorance or infirmity, not exerting against it the whole strength of the will, we yield to that same concupiscence even in some unlawful things, the more and the more often the worse we are, the less and the more rarely the better we are. But since concerning this question—in which it is asked whether a man can be, or is, or has been, or will be without sin in this life, except for him who said, *Behold, the prince of the world comes, and in me he will find nothing*[^205]—we must argue somewhat more diligently, let this be the measure of this volume, so that we may inquire into that matter from another beginning.
