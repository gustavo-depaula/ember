# Book II

## What is to be treated in this book.

**1. 1.** Concerning the baptism of little ones, dearest Marcellinus—that it is given to them not only for the kingdom of God but also for the obtaining of salvation and eternal life, which no one can have without the kingdom of God and without the fellowship of Christ the Savior, into which he redeemed us by his blood[^1]—we have, I think, argued sufficiently in the prior book. But in this one, whether anyone lives in this age, or has lived, or will live, without any sin at all, except the one Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, *who gave himself a redemption for all*[^2], I have undertaken to discuss and unravel with as much skill as he himself grants.

And if a question about the sin or baptism of little ones again and again inserts itself into this discussion by some necessity or opportunity, it will be no wonder, nor to be shirked, that in those places we answer, as we are able, all the things that demand our response.

## Free choice needs the grace of God.

**2. 2.** But the solution of this question concerning a human life without any creeping in or anticipation of sin is most necessary, on account of our daily prayers too. For there are certain men who presume so much upon the free choice of the human will that they suppose we do not even need to be divinely helped toward not sinning, the free choice of the will having once been granted to our nature. From which it follows consequently that we ought not even to pray that we may not enter into temptation[^3]—that is, that we may not be overcome by temptation, either when it deceives and anticipates the unaware, or when it presses and urges the weak. But how harmful, and how destructive and contrary to our salvation, which is in Christ, and how vehemently opposed to the very religion with which we have been imbued and to the piety by which we worship God it is, that we should not ask the Lord for the receiving of such a benefit, and should think it set down in vain in the Lord's Prayer itself, *Lead us not into temptation*[^4], we cannot explain in words.

## Works of mercy obtain the forgiveness of sins.

**3. 3.** But they seem to themselves to speak shrewdly, as though any of us were ignorant of this, that *if we do not will, we do not sin, and God would not command of man what was impossible for the human will.* But this they see less, that for overcoming certain things—whether what are wrongly desired or what are wrongly feared—great and total strength of will is sometimes needed, which he foresaw that we would not perfectly apply in all cases, who willed it to be said truthfully through the Prophet: *Not every living person will be justified in your sight*[^5]. Foreknowing, therefore, that we would be such, the Lord deigned to give and to make effective certain salutary remedies against the guilt and bonds of sins even after baptism, namely the works of mercy, when he says: *Forgive, and you will be forgiven; give, and it will be given to you*[^6]. For who, with any hope of obtaining eternal salvation, would depart from this life, while that sentence stood—that *whoever keeps the whole law but offends in one point is made guilty of all*[^7]—unless a little after there followed: *So speak and so act, as those who are to be judged through the law of liberty. For judgment is without mercy to him who does not show mercy; but mercy triumphs over judgment*[^8]?

## In the baptized it is not concupiscence itself, but only consent to it, that harms.

**4. 4.** Concupiscence, therefore, as a law of sin[^9] remaining in the members of the body of this death, is born with little ones; in baptized little ones it is loosed from guilt, is left for the contest, and pursues with no condemnation those who die before the contest; the little ones not baptized it binds as guilty and, as children of wrath[^10], even if the little ones die, it drags to condemnation.

But in grown people who are baptized, in whom—being now of the use of reason—whatever the mind has consented to that same concupiscence for sinning is a matter of their own will: with all their sins blotted out, with even the guilt loosed by which it held them originally bound, it remains for the contest, going to harm not at all those who do not consent to it for unlawful things, until death is swallowed up in victory[^11] and in perfect peace there exists nothing to be overcome. But those who consent to it for unlawful things it holds guilty, and, unless they are healed through the medicine of repentance and the works of mercy, through the heavenly priest interceding for us[^12], it leads to the second death and condemnation[^13].

For this reason too the Lord, teaching us to pray, warned us among other things to say: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors, and lead us not into temptation, but free us from evil*[^14]. For evil remains in our flesh, not by the nature in which man was divinely created, but by the vice into which he fell by his will, where, his strength being lost, he is not healed with that ease of will by which he was wounded. Of this evil the Apostle says: *I know that there does not dwell in my flesh good*[^15]. To which evil he commanded us not to be obedient, when he says: *Let not sin, then, reign in your mortal body, so as to obey its desires*[^16].

If, then, we have consented to these desires of the concupiscence of the flesh by an unlawful inclination of the will, for the healing of this we say, *Forgive us our debts*[^17], applying a remedy from the work of mercy in that we add, *as we also forgive our debtors*[^18]. But that we may not consent to it, we beseech his help, saying, *And lead us not into temptation*—or, as some manuscripts have it, *Bring us not into temptation*—not that God himself tempts anyone with such a temptation, *for God is no tempter to evils, and he himself tempts no one*[^19], but so that, if perhaps we begin to be tempted by our own concupiscence, we may not be forsaken by his help, that in it we may be able to conquer, lest, enticed, we be drawn away[^20]. Then we add what will be brought to completion in the end, when what is mortal will be swallowed up by life[^21]: *But free us from evil*[^22]. For then there will be no such concupiscence with which we are commanded to contend and to which we are commanded not to consent. Thus, then, this whole thing, set in three benefits, can be briefly asked: "Forgive us the things in which we have been drawn away by concupiscence, help us that we be not drawn away by concupiscence, take concupiscence away from us."

## God gives what he commands.

**5. 5.** For toward sinning we are not helped by God; but we cannot do just things or fulfill in every part the precept of righteousness unless we are helped by God. For just as the eye of the body is not helped by light to depart, closed or turned away, from that same light, but is helped by it to see, and cannot do this at all unless that light has helped, so God, who is the light of the inner man, helps the gaze of our mind, that we may work some good thing not according to our own but according to his righteousness. But if we are turned away from him, it is our own doing, and then we are wise according to the flesh, then we consent to the concupiscence of the flesh for unlawful things[^23].

Those who are turned toward him, then, God helps; those turned away he forsakes. But even that we may be turned toward him, he himself helps—which this light surely does not grant to the eyes of the body. When, therefore, he commands us, saying, *Turn to me, and I will turn to you*[^24], and we say to him, *Turn us, O God of our healings*[^25], and, *O God of hosts, turn us*[^26], what else do we say but "Give what you command"? When he commands by saying, *Understand, then, you who are foolish among the people*[^27], and we say to him, *Give me understanding, that I may learn your commandments*[^28], what else do we say but "Give what you command"? When he commands by saying, *Go not after your concupiscences*[^29], and we say, *We know that no one can be continent unless God grants it*[^30], what else do we say but "Give what you command"? When he commands by saying, *Do righteousness*[^31], and we say, *Teach me your statutes*[^32], what else do we say but "Give what you command"? Likewise, when he says, *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled*[^33], from whom ought we to ask the food and drink of righteousness but from him who promises its fullness to those who hunger and thirst for it?

## Liberty and prayer.

**5. 6.** Let us therefore drive from our ears and minds those who say that, the free choice of the will having once been received, we ought not even to pray that God may help us not to sin. For not even that Pharisee was blinded by such darkness who, although he erred in this—that he thought nothing was to be added to his righteousness and judged himself filled with its fullness—nevertheless gave thanks to God that he was not like the rest of men, unjust, robbers, adulterers, like that tax collector, that he fasted twice on the Sabbath, that he gave tithes of all he possessed. He did not now ask that anything be added to his righteousness, but yet, by giving thanks to God for the things he had, he confessed that he had received all from him.

And yet he was disapproved, both because, as though filled, he no longer asked to receive anything of the nourishments of righteousness, and because he was eager, as though insulting, to prefer himself to the tax collector who hungered and thirsted for it[^34]. What, then, will become of those who, even if they confess that they do not have righteousness, or do not have it fully, yet presume that it is to be had from themselves, not to be besought from their Creator, where his storehouse and his fountain are? Nor, however, is the matter on that account to be dealt with by prayers alone, so that the efficacy of our will too is not applied to living well. For God is called *our helper*[^35], and none can be helped but one who also strives somewhat of his own accord, because God does not work our salvation in us as in senseless stones or as in those in whose nature he did not establish reason and will. But why he helps this one and does not help that one, this one so much and that one so much, this one in that way and that one in this way, lies with him, and the reckoning of so secret an equity and the excellence of his power.

## The first question: whether man can be without sin in this life.

**6. 7.** For those who say that a man can be in this life without sin are not at once to be opposed with incautious rashness. For if we deny that it can be, we shall derogate both from the free choice of man, who in willing this strives after it, and from the power or mercy of God, who by helping brings it about. But one question is whether it can be, another whether it is; another, if it is not, when it can be, why it is not; another, whether one who has never at all had any sin is not only anyone now, but also ever could be or can be.

In this fourfold proposition of questions, if I am asked whether a man can be without sin in this life, I will confess that he can through the grace of God and his free choice—not doubting that free choice itself too pertains to the grace of God, that is, to the gifts of God, not only that it exists but also that it be good, that is, that it be turned to the doing of the Lord's commandments; and thus the grace of God not only shows what is to be done but also helps that what it has shown may be able to be done.

For what do we have that we have not received? Hence Jeremiah too says, *I know, Lord, that man's way is not in himself, nor is it for a man to walk and direct his steps*[^36]. Hence too in the Psalms, when one had said to God, *You have commanded your commandments to be kept exceedingly*[^37], he immediately presumed not upon himself but wished that he might keep them: *O that*, he says, *my ways may be directed to the keeping of your statutes! Then I shall not be confounded, while I look upon all your commandments*[^38]. But who wishes for what he has so in his own power that he needs no help to do it? And from whom he wishes it—since it is not from fortune or fate or any other than God—he shows sufficiently in what follows: *Direct*, he says, *my paths according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^39].

From the slavery of this execrable mistress are freed those to whom the Lord Jesus, when they receive him, has given *the power to become sons of God*[^40]. They had to be freed from this horrendous dominion to whom he says, *If the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^41]. By these and other innumerable testimonies of this kind I cannot doubt either that God has commanded man nothing impossible, or that anything is impossible for God in succoring and helping, so that what he commands may come about. And by this a man can, if he will, be without sin, helped by God.

## The second question: whether anyone in this life is without sin.

**7. 8.** But if it is asked, as I had put in the second place, whether one is, I do not believe that he is. For I rather believe the Scripture which says: *Enter not into judgment with your servant, for not every living person will be justified in your sight*[^42]. And therefore there is need of the mercy of God, which triumphs over judgment, which will not be to him who does not show mercy[^43]. And there is what the Prophet, when he had said, *I said: I will declare against myself my trespass to the Lord, and you forgave the impiety of my heart*[^44], immediately added: *For this every holy one will pray to you in an opportune time*[^45]—not every sinner, then, but every holy one; for it is the voice of the holy: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^46]. Hence in the *Apocalypse* of the same Apostle those *one hundred and forty-four thousand* holy ones *who did not even defile themselves with women—for they remained virgins—and in whose mouth no lie was found, for they are blameless*[^47]; surely they are *blameless* for this reason, that they truly blamed themselves; and therefore *no lie was found in their mouth*, because, if they said they had no sin, they would deceive themselves and the truth would not be in them, and surely there would be a lie where there was no truth, since the just man, when at the beginning of his speech he is his own accuser, surely does not lie[^48].

## Perfection is not acquired at once.

**7. 9.** And by this, in that it is written, *He who is born of God does not sin and cannot sin, because his seed remains in him*[^49], and if anything else is said in that way, they are much deceived by considering the Scriptures too little. For they do not notice that men become sons of God to the extent that they begin to be in newness of spirit and to be renewed in the inner man according to the image of him who created them[^50]. For it is not from the hour in which anyone is baptized that all his old infirmity is consumed; but renewal begins from the forgiveness of all sins and to the extent that anyone is wise in spiritual things—who is now wise—but the rest is done in hope, until it be done also in reality, up to the renewal of the body itself into the better state of immortality and incorruption, with which we shall be clothed in the resurrection of the dead. For the Lord calls even this a regeneration—not, surely, such as comes about through baptism, but that in which what is now begun in spirit will be brought to completion even in the body: *In the regeneration*, he says, *when the Son of man sits on the seat of his majesty, you also will sit upon twelve seats, judging the twelve tribes of Israel*[^51].

For although in baptism the forgiveness of sins is made whole and full, nevertheless, if at once the whole and full change of man into eternal newness also came about—I do not say in the body too, which is surely manifestly still tending toward the old corruption and toward death, to be renewed afterward in the end, when there will truly be a whole newness; but, the body excepted, if in the very soul, which is the inner man, a perfect newness came about in baptism—the Apostle would not say, *Although our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^52]. For surely one who is still being renewed from day to day is not yet wholly renewed; and to the extent that he is not yet renewed, to that extent he is still in oldness.

Accordingly, from the fact that they are still in oldness, although already baptized, from this they are still also sons of the age; but from the fact that they are in newness, that is, from the full and perfect forgiveness of sins, and however much that is which they are wise in spiritually and the conduct suited to it which they perform, they are sons of God[^53]. For inwardly we put off the old man and put on the new, since there we lay aside falsehood and speak the truth, and the rest by which the Apostle explains what it is to put off the old man and to put on the new, *who was created according to God in righteousness and the holiness of truth*[^54]. And that they may do this, he exhorts the already baptized and faithful; and they would not need still to be admonished if this had already been perfectly done in baptism. And yet it was done, just as we have also been saved; for *he saved us through the washing of regeneration*[^55]. But in another place he says in what way this was done: *Not only that*, he says, *but we ourselves too, having the first fruits of the Spirit, even we groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body. For in hope we were saved; but hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he also hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^56].

## Earthly perfection is never total.

**8. 10.** The full adoption of sons, then, will come about in the redemption of our body too. And so we now have the first fruits of the Spirit, whence we have already in reality been made sons of God; but in the rest we are sons of God in hope, just as we are saved, just as we are renewed—but in reality, because we are not yet saved, therefore not yet fully renewed, not yet even sons of God, but sons of the age.

We advance, then, into renewal and a righteous life through that by which we are sons of God, and through this we cannot sin at all, until the whole be transmuted into this, even that by which we are still sons of the age; for through this we can still sin too[^57]. So it comes about both that he who is born of God does not sin, and that, if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us[^58]. That, then, by which we are sons of the flesh and of the age will be consumed, and that by which we are sons of God and reborn in spirit will be brought to completion[^59].

Hence the same John says: *Beloved, we are sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be*[^60]. What is this *we are* and *we shall be*, but that we are in hope, we shall be in reality? For it follows and he says: *We know that, when it appears, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is.* Now, then, we have also already begun to be like him, having the first fruits of the Spirit, and we are still unlike through the remnants of oldness. Accordingly, insofar as we are like, so far are we sons of God by the regenerating Spirit, but insofar as we are unlike, so far are we sons of the flesh and of the age. From the one side, then, we cannot sin; but from the other, if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves[^61], until the whole pass over into adoption and there be no sinner and *you seek his place and do not find it*[^62].

## An objection of the Pelagians: why a just man does not beget a just one.

**9. 11.** In vain, then, do some argue even this, saying: *If a sinner begot a sinner, so that in his little one the guilt of original sin is loosed in the reception of baptism, then a just man too ought to have begotten a just one.* As though anyone begets carnally from the fact that he is just, and not rather from the fact that in his members there is a movement of concupiscence and the law of sin is turned to the use of propagation against the law of the mind[^63].

He begets, then, from that by which he still draws the old among the sons of the age, not from that by which he has advanced into newness among the sons of God. For the sons *of this age beget and are begotten*[^64]. Hence too what is born is such, because *what is born of the flesh is flesh*[^65]. But none are just except the sons of God. And insofar as they are sons of God, they do not beget by the flesh, because they themselves too are born of the Spirit, not of the flesh.

But whoever of them beget, they beget by the flesh from that by which they have not yet changed all the remnants of oldness into perfect newness. Hence whatever son is born of this old and infirm part, it is necessary that he too be old and infirm; therefore it is fitting that he too be renewed in spirit through the forgiveness of sin into another generation.

But if this does not come about in him, a just father will profit him nothing; for he is just by the spirit, by which he did not beget him. But if it does come about, an unjust father too will harm him nothing; for the one made his passage by spiritual grace into the hope of eternal newness, while the other, by a carnal mind, remained wholly in oldness.

## Not even the saints to whom Scripture bears witness were without sin: Noah.

**10. 12.** That testimony, then, in which it is said, *He who is born of God does not sin*[^66], is not contrary to that testimony in which it is said to those already born of God, *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us*[^67]. For as long as a man—although already wholly in hope, and even now in reality renewed in part by spiritual regeneration—nevertheless still bears a body which is corrupted and weighs down the soul[^68], what pertains to what and what is said from what must be distinguished, even in one man.

For, as I judge, the Scripture of God does not easily render so great a testimony of righteousness to anyone as to three of his servants, Noah, Daniel, and Job, whom the prophet Ezekiel says could alone be freed from a certain impending wrath of God[^69], prefiguring surely in those three men three kinds of men to be freed: in Noah, as I judge, the just leaders set over the peoples, on account of the steering of the ark, as of the Church; in Daniel, the just who are continent; in Job, the just who are married—and if there is perhaps some other understanding, into which it is not now necessary to inquire. Yet how greatly these excelled in righteousness, this both by the prophetic testimony and by other divine testimonies appears sufficiently[^70]. Nor on that account would any sober man say that drunkenness is not a sin, which nevertheless crept upon so great a man; for Noah, as we read, was once drunk, although far be it that he was a drunkard[^71].

## Daniel.

**10. 13.** Daniel, after the prayer which he poured out to God, says of himself: *When I was praying and confessing my sins and the sins of my people to the Lord my God*[^72]. For this reason, unless I am mistaken, through the above-mentioned Ezekiel it is said to a certain most proud man: *Are you wiser than Daniel*[^73]? Nor can it here be said—what some argue against the Lord's Prayer—that "although the holy and now perfect Apostles, having no sin at all, prayed it," they say, "yet they said it not for themselves but for the imperfect and as-yet sinners: Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors[^74]; so that by this," they say, "in that they said our, they might show that they were in one body both with those still having sins and with themselves, who now in every part lacked sin." In Daniel, certainly, this cannot be said, who, as I believe, like a prophet foreseeing that this presumption would one day arise, when in his prayer he had often said *we have sinned*, did not expound to us why he said this in such a way that we heard from him, *When I was praying and confessing the sins of my people to the Lord my God*[^75], nor with the distinction still confused, so that it would be uncertain on account of the fellowship of one body, had he said, "When I was confessing our sins to the Lord my God," but altogether so distinctly, as though busying himself about the very distinction concerning himself and commending it most strongly and vehemently, he says, *my sins and the sins of my people.* Who contradicts this evidence, except one whom it pleases more to defend what he holds than to find what ought to be held?

## Job.

**10. 14.** But Job, after so great a testimony of righteousness from God concerning him, let us see what he himself says. *In truth*, he says, *I know that it is so. For how will a man be just before the Lord? For if he should wish to contend with him, he could not obey him*[^76]. And a little after: *Who*, he says, *will oppose his judgment? But if I am just, my mouth will speak impiously*[^77]. Again a little after: *I know*, he says, *that he does not let me go unpunished. Since I am impious, why am I not dead? But if I am purified with snow and cleansed with clean hands, you have steeped me sufficiently in filth*[^78]. Likewise in another of his speeches: *Because you have written against me evils*, he says, *and have clothed me with the sins of my youth, and have set my foot in a snare, you have watched all my works and looked into the roots of my feet, who grow old like a wineskin or like a garment eaten by a moth. For a man born of woman is of short time and full of wrath, and, like a flower when it has bloomed and fallen, he has departed; like a shadow he does not remain. Have you not made the care of this one too come into your judgment? For who will be clean from filth? No one, not even if his life has been but of one day*[^79]. And a little after: *You have numbered*, he says, *all my needs, and nothing of my sins has escaped you; you have sealed my sins in a little bag and noted down if I have committed anything unwillingly*[^80].

Behold, Job too confesses his sins and says that he knows in truth that no one is just before the Lord. And therefore he knows this in truth, because, if we say that we have no sin, the truth itself is not in us[^81]. Accordingly, according to the measure of human conduct, God renders him so great a testimony of righteousness; but he himself, measuring himself by that rule of righteousness which, as he can, he beholds with God, knows in truth that it is so, and adds: *For how will a man be just before the Lord? For if he should wish to contend with him, he could not obey him*[^82]—that is, "if, being about to be judged, he should wish to show that there cannot be found in him anything to be condemned, he could not obey him"; for he loses even that obedience by which he could obey the one commanding that sins must be confessed. Hence he rebukes certain ones, saying, *Why do you wish to contend with me in judgment*[^83]? Guarding against this, he says, *Enter not into judgment with your servant, for not every living person will be justified in your sight*[^84]. Therefore Job too says: *For who will oppose his judgment? But if I am just, my mouth will speak impiously*[^85]; for this is: "If I call myself just against his judgment, where that perfect rule of righteousness convicts me of being unjust, surely *my mouth will speak impiously*, because it will speak against the truth of God."

## Job, "a child of wrath."

**10. 15.** Showing also the very frailty—or rather the condemnation—of carnal generation from the transgression of original sin, when he was dealing with his sins, as though rendering their causes, he said that a man born of woman is of short time and full of wrath[^86]. By what wrath, except that by which all are, as the Apostle says, *by nature*—that is, originally—*children of wrath*[^87], since they are sons of the concupiscence of the flesh and of the age? That the death of man too pertains to this same wrath he consequently shows.

For when he had said, *he is of short time and full of wrath*, he added also: *and, like a flower when it has bloomed and fallen, he has departed; like a shadow he does not remain.* But when he goes on, *Have you not made the care of this one too come into your judgment? For who will be clean from filth? No one, not even if his life has been but of one day*[^88], he surely says this: "You have made the care of a man of short life come into your judgment.

For however short his life may have been, even if it were of one day, he could not be clean from filth, and therefore he will most justly come into your judgment." But that which he says, *You have numbered all my needs, and nothing of my sins has escaped you; you have sealed my sins in a little bag and noted down if I have committed anything unwillingly*[^89], did he not sufficiently make plain that even those sins are justly imputed which are committed not by the enticement of delight but for the sake of avoiding some trouble or pain or death? For these too are said to be committed by a certain necessity, when all things ought to be overcome by the love and delight of righteousness. What he said, *and noted down if I have committed anything unwillingly*[^90], may also seem to pertain to that voice in which it is said, *For I do not the good that I will, but what I hate, this I do*[^91].

## Job reproaches himself.

**10. 16.** What of this, that the Lord himself, who had borne him witness—since even Scripture, that is, the Spirit of God, said that *in all the things that befell him he did not sin with his lips before the Lord*[^92]—nevertheless afterward, when he spoke to him, spoke in rebuke, as Job himself is witness, saying: *Why am I still judged, being admonished and hearing the rebukes of the Lord*[^93]? But no one is justly rebuked except in whom there is something worthy of rebuke.

**11. 16.** And of what kind is that very rebuke which is understood as from the person of the Lord Christ? He enumerates to him the divine works of his power, rebuking him under this sentence, so that he appears to say: Can you do these so great things which I can? But to what does it tend, except that Job may understand—we believe that this too was divinely inspired in him, that he foreknew that Christ would come to the passion—that he may understand, then, with how even a mind he ought to bear what he endured, if Christ, in whom there was no sin at all, though for our sake he had become man[^94], and in whom there is so great a power as God, nevertheless by no means refused the obedience of the passion? Understanding this with a purer intention of heart, Job added to his response: *I heard you before by the hearing of the ear, and now, behold, my eye sees you. Therefore I have reproached myself and pined away, and I have esteemed myself earth and ashes*[^95]. Why did he so displease himself in this so great an understanding?

For the work of God by which he was a man could not rightly displease him, since to God himself it is said, *Despise not the works of your hands*[^96]. But surely, according to that righteousness by which he had known himself just, he reproached himself and pined away and esteemed himself earth and ashes, beholding in his mind the righteousness of Christ, in whom—not only in his divinity, but neither in his soul nor in his flesh—could there be any sin; according to which righteousness, which is from God, the apostle Paul too esteemed that of his own which, according to the righteousness that is from the law, was without complaint, not only as losses but even as dung[^97].

## No one just in all things.

**12. 17.** That illustrious testimony of God, then, by which Job was praised, is not contrary to that testimony in which it is said, *Not every living person will be justified in your sight*[^98], because it does not at all persuade us that in him there was nothing which might be reproved either by himself truly or by the Lord God rightly, although he was already, and not falsely, called just and a true worshiper of God and abstaining from every evil work. For these are the words of God concerning him: *Have you considered my servant Job? For there is no man like him on earth, without complaint, just, a true worshiper of God, abstaining from every evil work*[^99]. By the first words he is praised in comparison with the men who are on earth; accordingly he excelled all who then could be just on earth. He did not, therefore, on that account have no sin at all, because in the advance of righteousness he went before the rest. Then is added *without complaint*, of whose life no one could justly complain; *just*, who had advanced with so great an uprightness of conduct that none could be made equal to him; *a true worshiper of God*, being indeed a true and humble confessor even of his own sins; *abstaining from every evil work*—it would be a wonder if from every evil word and thought too.

How great Job was we do not know; but we know that he was just, we know too that he was great in enduring the horrendous temptations of tribulations; we know that he endured all those things not on account of sins but for the showing of his righteousness. Nevertheless these words by which he is praised by the Lord could be said also of one who delights together in the law of God according to the inner man[^100], but sees another law in his members warring against the law of his mind, especially since he says: *I do not the good that I will, but the evil that I hate, this I do. But if I do the evil that I hate, it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^101]. Behold, this man too, according to the inner man, is a stranger to every evil work, because he himself does not do it, but the evil that dwells in his flesh; and yet, since he has that very thing by which he delights together in the law of God only from the grace of God, still needing liberation he cries: *Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^102].

## Perfect human righteousness is imperfect.

**13. 18.** There are, then, just men on earth; there are great, strong, prudent, continent, patient, pious, merciful men, bearing all temporal evils with an even mind for the sake of righteousness; but if it is true—and it is true—that *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves*[^103], and, *Not every living person will be justified in your sight*[^104], they are not without sin, nor is any one of them so arrogantly mad as to judge that he has no need of the Lord's Prayer for his own sins, whatever they may be.

## The perfection of the priest Zechariah.

**13. 19.** For of Zechariah and Elizabeth, who are often urged upon us in the discussions of this question, what shall we say, except that Scripture evidently testifies that Zechariah was of eminent righteousness among the leaders of the priests pertaining to the offering of the sacrifices of the Old Testament? But we read in the letter which is written to the Hebrews—a testimony I have already set down in the prior book—that Christ alone is the prince of priests[^105], who would not have the necessity, like those who were called princes of priests, of offering sacrifice first daily for their own sins, then for the people. *For it was fitting*, he says, *that we should have such a high priest, just, without malice, undefiled, separated from sinners, made higher than the heavens, not having a daily necessity, like the princes of priests, to offer sacrifice first for their own sins*[^106]. In this number of priests was Zechariah, in it Phinehas, in it Aaron himself, from whom this order took its beginning, and whatever others lived praiseworthily and justly in that priesthood—who nevertheless had the necessity of offering sacrifice first for their own sins, Christ alone existing, whose figure, as the one to come, they bore, who, an undefilable priest, would not have this necessity[^107].

## Zechariah and Elizabeth are compared to the apostle Paul.

**13. 20.** But what praiseworthy thing was said of Zechariah and Elizabeth that is not comprised in what the Apostle, when he had not yet believed in Christ, professed of himself? For he said that he had been, *according to the righteousness that is in the law, without complaint*[^108]. This too is read of them in this way: *Now they were both just before God, walking in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord without complaint*[^109]. Because, then, whatever of righteousness was in them was not feigned toward men, therefore it was said, *before God.*

But what was written of Zechariah and his wife, *in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord*, this he said briefly: *in the law.* For there was not one law for them and another for him before the Gospel, but one and the same, which we read was given through Moses to their fathers, according to which Zechariah too was a priest and in his turn offered sacrifice.

And yet the Apostle, who was then endowed with a like righteousness, goes on and says: *What were gains to me, these I have counted losses for Christ. Yet I also count all things to be loss because of the surpassing knowledge of Christ Jesus our Lord, for whose sake I have considered all things not only as detriments but even esteemed them as dung, that I may gain Christ and be found in him, not having my own righteousness, which is from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God in faith, to know him and the power of his resurrection and the fellowship of his sufferings, being conformed to his death, if by any means I may attain to the resurrection of the dead*[^110].

So far, then, is it from being the case that on account of those words we should believe Zechariah and Elizabeth to have had a perfect righteousness without any sin, that we judge that not even the Apostle himself was perfected in the summit of that same rule—not only in that righteousness of the law, like to theirs, which he reckons among losses and dung in comparison with the most eminent righteousness which is in faith in Christ, but even in the Gospel itself, where he also earned the headship of so great an apostolate—which I would not dare to say, did I not deem it sinful to disbelieve him.

For where he goes on and adds: *Not that I have already received it or am already perfect, but I follow after, that I may lay hold of that for which I was also laid hold of by Christ Jesus. Brethren, I do not consider that I myself have laid hold; but one thing, forgetting the things that are behind and stretching out to the things that are before, I follow after, according to my intent, to the prize of the supernal calling of God in Christ Jesus*[^111], he himself confesses that he has not yet received, is not yet perfect in the fullness of the righteousness which he loved to attain in Christ, but that he still follows after, according to his intent, and, forgetting the things behind, stretches out to the things that are before; so that we may know that to him too pertains that which he says: *Although our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^112], although he was already a perfect traveler, even if he was not yet, by the perfection of the journey itself, one who had arrived.

Finally, he wishes to carry such men along with him as companions in this course, to whom he immediately goes on and says: *As many of us, then, as are perfect, let us be of this mind; and if you are of any other mind in anything, this too God will reveal to you; nevertheless, in that to which we have come, in it let us walk*[^113]. This walking is carried on not by the feet of the body but by the affections of the mind and the conduct of life, so that those can be perfect possessors of righteousness who, by the right journey of faith advancing from day to day in their renewal, have already been made perfect travelers of that same righteousness[^114].

## All must pray that it be forgiven them.

**14. 21.** And so all who in this life have been proclaimed by the testimonies of the divine Scriptures for their good will and acts of righteousness, and all who have been such after them, although not proclaimed and praised by those same testimonies, or are even now, or will be afterward—all are great, all just, all truly praiseworthy, but they are not without some sin, since by the testimonies of the Scriptures, by which we believe their praises[^115], we also believe this: that not every living person is justified in the sight of God, and therefore that he is asked not to enter into judgment with his servants, and that the Lord's Prayer, which he handed on to his disciples, is necessary not only for all the faithful universally but even for each one.

## Perfection in a qualified sense and perfection without qualification.

**15. 22.** *But indeed the Lord says: Be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect*[^116]; *which he would not command*, they say, *if he knew that what he commands could not be done.* It is not now asked whether it can be done, if they take that perfection to mean that anyone is without any sin while he lives this life—for we have already answered above that it can be done—but whether anyone does it, this is what we now ask. But that there is no one who wills as much as the matter demands has been foreknown, as the so great testimonies of the Scriptures which I have recalled above declare.

And yet, when anyone's perfection is spoken of, it must be seen in what respect it is spoken of. For I set down a little before a testimony from the Apostle, where he confesses that in the reception of the righteousness which he desires he is not yet perfect, and yet immediately says: *As many of us, then, as are perfect, let us be of this mind*[^117]; both of which he would not say, unless he were perfect in one respect and not in another. As, for instance, if anyone is already a perfect hearer of wisdom—which those were not yet to whom he said, *I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able, nor indeed are you even now able*[^118]; for to them he also says, *We speak wisdom among the perfect*[^119], surely wishing perfect hearers to be understood—it can happen, then, as I said, that someone is already a perfect hearer of wisdom whose teacher is not yet perfect; one can be a perfect knower of righteousness, not yet a perfect doer; one can be perfect in loving enemies who is not yet perfect in bearing with them.

And of one who is perfect in this, that he loves all men—seeing that he has come even to the love of enemies—it is asked whether he is now perfect also in that very love, that is, whether he loves those whom he loves as much as that unchangeable rule of truth prescribes that they ought to be loved. When, therefore, anyone's perfection is read of in the Scriptures, in what respect it is spoken of must not be carelessly considered, since one is not on that account understood to be wholly without sin because in some respect he is said to be perfect.

Although even in this it can be so said that it is not because there is now nothing in which he may advance, but because he has advanced in the greatest part, that he is held worthy of this name—just as in the doctrine of the law anyone can be called perfect, even though something still lies hidden from him; just as the Apostle called those perfect to whom nevertheless he says: *And if you are of any other mind in anything, this too God will reveal to you; nevertheless, in that to which we have come, in it let us walk*[^120].

## Why God commands what he knows men cannot keep.

**16. 23.** Nor is it to be denied that God commands this—that we ought to be so perfect in doing righteousness that we have no sin at all. For neither will it be sin, if anything is, if it is not divinely commanded that it be not. "Why, then," they say, "does he command what he knows no man will do?" In this way it can also be asked why he commanded those first men, who were only two, what he knew they would not do. For it is not to be said that he commanded for this reason, that one of us might do it if they did not; for this—that they should not take food from that tree—God commanded none but them alone[^121], because, just as he knew what righteousness they would not do, so he also knew what righteousness he himself would do concerning them. In that way, then, he commands all men that they do no sin, although he foreknows that no one will fulfill this—so that whoever impiously and damnably despise his precepts, he himself may do, in their condemnation, what is just; but whoever, advancing obediently and piously in his precepts, yet not fulfilling all that he commanded, have, just as they wish it forgiven them, so forgiven others their sins, he himself may do, in their cleansing, what is good. For how is it forgiven the one who forgives, through the mercy of God, if it is not sin? Or how is it not forbidden, through the justice of God, if it is sin?

## The security of the apostle Paul.

**16. 24.** "But behold," they say, "the Apostle says: I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith; there remains for me a crown of righteousness[^122]; which he would not say if he had any sin." Nay rather, let them answer how he could say these things, for whom there still remained the so great struggle of the very passion which he had said was now hanging over him, so troublesome and great a contest. Or was there still little lacking to the finishing of his course, when that was lacking in which he was going to face a sharper and crueler enemy? But if for this reason he rejoiced in such words, certain and secure, because he who had already revealed that same passion to be imminent had now made him certain and secure of victory in so great a contest to come, he said these things not in the fullest reality but in the firmest hope, and indicated what he presumed would be as though it had been done. If, then, he had added even this to these words, so as to say, "I now have no sin," we would understand that he said this too not of the perfection of a thing done but of a thing to come. For so it pertained to the finishing of his course to have no sin—which these men suppose was already completed in him while he said these things—just as it pertained to the finishing of his course to overcome his adversary in the contest of the passion, which even they themselves must confess was, when he said these things, still to be completed in him.

This whole thing, then, we say was still to be perfected at the time when, already trusting in advance in the promise of God, he so spoke of it all as though it had been accomplished. For to the finishing of his course there pertained also that he forgave their debts to his debtors and so prayed that they be forgiven him[^123]; by which promise of the Lord he was most certain that in that end—which, though still to come, he now by trusting said was fulfilled—he would have no sin. For, to omit other things, I wonder whether, when he said those words by which he seemed to these men to have had no sin, that thorn of the flesh had already been taken from him, concerning the taking away of which he had asked the Lord three times and had received the answer: *My grace is sufficient for you; strength is made perfect in weakness*[^124]. For the perfecting of this so great a man it was necessary that the angel of Satan not be taken from him, by whom he was therefore buffeted, lest he be lifted up by the greatness of the revelations; and does anyone dare even to think or say that anyone set under the burden of this life is clean from all sin whatsoever?

## Even the holiest had sins to be expiated.

**16. 25.** Though there be men excelling in so great a righteousness that God speaks to them from the pillar of cloud, such as *Moses and Aaron among his priests and Samuel among those who call upon his name*[^125]—whose great praises of piety and innocence the truthful Scripture proclaims from his earliest boyhood, from the time when his mother, fulfilling her vow, set him in the temple of God and dedicated him as a servant to the Lord—yet even of such it is written: *You were gracious to them, and taking vengeance on all their doings*[^126]. For on the sons of condemnation he takes vengeance in anger, but on the sons of grace he takes vengeance graciously, while he rebukes him whom he loves and scourges every son whom he receives[^127].

But no vengeance, no rebuke, no scourge of God is owed except to sin, except for him who was prepared for scourges[^128], that he might be tried in all things according to our likeness, without sin, that he might be the holy priest of the holy, interceding even for the holy, who not falsely say even of themselves: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^129]. Hence even those who argue against these things, although they are of chaste life and praiseworthy conduct and do not hesitate to do what the Lord commanded that rich man who sought counsel for the obtaining of eternal life—when he had answered that he had already fulfilled all the commandments of the law, the Lord enjoined that, if he wished to be perfect, he should sell all that he had and give to the poor and transfer his treasure into heaven—yet none of them dares to say that he is without sin[^130]. And this, as we believe, they say with no deceitful mind; but if they lie, by that very thing they begin either to increase or to have sin.

## The third question: why no one in this life is without sin.

**17. 26.** Now, then, let us see what I put in the third place. Since, with the human will helped by divine grace, a man can be without sin in this life, why is he not? I could most easily and most truly answer: Because men do not will it. But if it is asked of me why they do not will it, we go on at length; nevertheless I will say this too briefly, without prejudice to a more diligent inquiry. Men do not will to do what is just, either because it lies hidden whether it is just, or because it does not delight them. For we will each thing the more vehemently the more certainly we know how good it is and the more ardently we delight in it. Ignorance, therefore, and infirmity are vices that hinder the will from being moved to do a good work or to abstain from an evil work.

But that what lay hidden may become known and what did not delight may become sweet belongs to the grace of God, by which he helps the wills of men; and that they are not helped, the cause is likewise in themselves, not in God—whether they are predestined to be condemned on account of the iniquity of pride, or, against their very pride, to be judged and instructed, if they are sons of mercy. Hence Jeremiah, when he had said, *I know, Lord, that man's way is not in himself, nor is it for a man to walk and direct his steps*, immediately added: *Correct me, Lord, but yet in judgment and not in your fury*[^131], as though he said: "I know that it pertains to my correction that I am less helped by you to have my steps perfectly directed; yet do not deal with me in this very thing as though in the fury by which you have determined to condemn the wicked, but as in the judgment by which you teach your own not to be proud." Hence elsewhere it is said: *And your judgments will help me*[^132].

## The cause of all human vices is pride. A good work delights the more, the more God is loved.

**17. 27.** Refer, then, the cause of no human fault to God. For the cause of all human vices is pride. To convict and take this away, such a medicine came from heaven: to man, lifted up through pride, God came down humble through mercy, commending a clear and manifest grace in that very man whom he took up with so great a love beyond his fellows. For he himself too, so joined to the Word of God that by that conjunction he became one Son of God, and the same one Son of man, did not do this by any merits of his own will going before. For it was fitting that he be one; but there would be even two and three and more, if this could come about, not through the proper gift of God, but through the free choice of man. This, then, is especially commended; this, in the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden in Christ, is, as far as I dare to judge, especially taught and learned[^133].

Therefore each of us now knows, now does not know, how to take up, carry out, and fulfill a good work; now delights in it, now does not delight—so that he may know that it is not of his own ability but of the divine gift, either that he knows or that he delights; and thus he may be healed of the vanity of self-exaltation and may know how truly it was said—not of this earth, but spiritually: *The Lord will give sweetness, and our earth will give its fruit*[^134]. But a good work delights the more, the more God is loved, the highest and unchangeable good and the author of all goods of whatever kind. But that God may be loved, his *love is poured out in our hearts*, not through ourselves, but *through the Holy Spirit, who was given to us*[^135].

## Grace and liberty.

**18. 28.** But men labor to find in our will what good is our own, which is not to us from God; and how it can be found I do not know. For apart from what the Apostle says, when he was speaking of the goods of men—*For what do you have that you have not received? But if you have received, why do you boast, as though you had not received*[^136]?—the very reasoning that can be entered into about these matters by such as we are vehemently presses any of us inquiring, lest we so defend grace that we seem to take away free choice, and again, lest we so assert free choice that we be judged ungrateful for the grace of God by a proud impiety.

## Good will is from God.

**18. 29.** For that saying of the Apostle which I recalled some have wished to defend in such a way as to say *that whatever of good will a man has is therefore to be attributed to God, because this too could not be in him if the man himself did not exist; but since, that he be something and be a man, he has it only from God, why should there not be attributed to God the author even whatever of good will is in him, which would not be unless there were something in which it could be?* But in this way it can also be said that an evil will too is to be attributed to God the author, because neither could it be in a man unless there were a man in whom it could be. But that there be a man, God is the author; thus too the author of his evil will, which could not at all be unless it had a man in whom to be, must be referred to God the author. Which it is sinful to say.

## Good will is necessarily from God.

**18. 30.** Wherefore, unless we maintain that not only the choice of the will—which is freely bent this way and that, and is among those natural goods which even an evil man can use badly—but also a good will, which is now among those goods whose use cannot be bad, cannot be to us except from God, I do not know how we may defend what was said: *For what do you have that you have not received*[^137]? For if some free will is to us from God, which can still be either good or evil, but a good will is from ourselves, then what is from ourselves is better than what is from him. But if it is most absurd to say this, we must confess that we obtain even a good will divinely. Although it would be a wonder if the will could so stand in a certain middle that it be neither good nor evil. For either we love righteousness, and it is good, and, if we love it more, it is more good, if less, less good; or, if we do not love it at all, it is not good. But who would doubt to say that a will in no way loving righteousness is not only an evil will but even a most evil one? If, then, the will is either good or evil, and surely we do not have the evil one from God, it remains that we have a good will from God; otherwise I do not know, when we are justified by him, by what other gift of his we ought to rejoice. And from this, I think, it is written: *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^138]. And in the Psalms: *By the Lord the steps of a man are directed, and he will choose his way*[^139]. And what the Apostle says: *For it is God who works in you both to will and to work according to good will*[^140].

## Man's turning to God is from divine mercy.

**18. 31.** Wherefore, since the fact that we turn away from God is our own—and this is the evil will—but the fact that we turn toward God we cannot do unless he himself arouses and helps us—and this is the good will—what do we have that we have not received[^141]? But if we have received, why do we boast as though we had not received? And by this, that *he who boasts may boast in the Lord*[^142], that God has willed to grant this to those to whom he has willed belongs to his mercy, not to their merit; but that he has not willed it for those for whom he has not willed it belongs to truth. For a just penalty is owed to sinners, *since the Lord God loves mercy and truth*[^143]; and, *mercy and truth have met each other*[^144]; and, *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^145]. And who could unfold how often divine Scripture recalls these two joined together? Sometimes even with the names changed, so that grace is put for mercy—whence comes: *And we saw his glory, glory as of the Only-begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth*[^146]—sometimes judgment for truth, as it is: *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^147].

## God works his mercy and justice in a hidden way.

**18. 32.** But why he wills to turn these and to punish those for turning away—since in granting the benefit no one would justly reprove the merciful one, and in exercising vengeance no one would justly reprove the truthful one, just as in those Gospel laborers no one would justly blame him who rendered to some the agreed wage[^148] and lavished on others even what was not agreed—the counsel of a more hidden justice nevertheless lies with him.

## Through grace come the knowledge of the good and the delight in it.

**19. 32.** Let us, as far as is granted, be wise and understand, if we can, that the good Lord God therefore sometimes does not grant even to his holy ones either a certain knowledge of some just work or a victorious delight in it, so that they may know that the light by which their darkness is illumined, and the sweetness by which he gives their earth to yield its fruit, are not from themselves but from him[^149].

**19. 33.** But when we beseech from him his help to do and perfect righteousness, what else do we beseech but that he open what lay hidden and make sweet what did not delight? For by his grace we have learned that this too is to be besought from him, while before it lay hidden; by his grace we have loved it, while before it did not delight—so that *he who boasts* may boast not in himself but *in the Lord*[^150]. For to be lifted up into the pride of one's own will is of men, not of the work of God; for God does not compel or help them to this. There goes before, then, in the will of man a certain appetite of his own power, so that he becomes disobedient through pride. But even if this appetite were not present, it would be nothing troublesome; and when a man willed it, he would without difficulty have refused it; but, by a due and just penalty, there followed such a vice that it was now troublesome to obey righteousness.

Unless this vice be overcome with the help of grace, no one is turned toward righteousness; unless he be healed by the working of grace, no one fully enjoys the peace of righteousness. But by whose grace is he overcome and healed, except his to whom it is said: *Turn us, O God of our healings, and turn away your wrath from us*[^151]? And if he does this, he does it by mercy, so that it is said: *He has not done to us according to our sins, nor repaid us according to our iniquities*[^152]. And for those for whom he does not do it, he does not do it by judgment. And who will say to him, "Why have you done this?"—to him to whom mercy and judgment are sung with the pious mind of the holy[^153]? For this reason he heals even his holy and faithful ones more slowly in certain vices, so that in these the good delights them less than suffices for fulfilling righteousness in every part—whether when it lies hidden or when it is even manifest—so that, as far as pertains to the most perfect rule of his truth, not every living person is justified in his sight[^154].

Nor in this very thing does he wish us to be deserving of condemnation, but humble, commending to us his same grace, lest, having obtained ease in all things, we suppose to be ours what is his—an error much contrary to religion and piety. Nor on that account, however, let us suppose that we must remain in those same vices, but, against pride above all—on account of which we are humbled in them—let us both vigilantly strive and ardently beseech him, understanding at the same time that we have both this striving and this praying by his gift, so that in all things, not looking to ourselves but having our hearts on high, we may give thanks to the Lord our God and, when we boast, may boast in him[^155].

## The fourth question: no one was or can be without sin, Christ alone excepted.

**20. 34.** Now there remains that fourth point, with which unraveled—as much as the Lord helps—this so lengthy discourse too may at last take its end: whether one who has never at all had any sin, or will have any, is not only anyone of men born, but also could ever have been or can be. That there is, was, or will be no such one except the one Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^156], is most certain. On this we have already said many things concerning the baptism of little ones, who, if they have no sin, then not only are there innumerable men without sin, but also have been and will be. Further, if that has been truly established—of which we treated in the second place—that no one is without sin, then surely not even little ones are without sin. From which it is concluded that, even if anyone in this life could have been who was so perfected in virtue that he came to so great a fullness of righteousness as to have no sin at all, it is nevertheless not to be doubted that he was previously a sinner, from which he was turned toward this newness of life.

For in that second place one thing was asked, and in this fourth another is proposed. For in that one it was inquired whether anyone in this life comes, through the grace of God by the zeal of his will, to the perfect life which is wholly without any sin; but in this fourth it is asked whether there is among the sons of men, or could have been, or can be, one who comes not from sin to the most perfect righteousness, but is bound by no sin at all ever. Therefore, if those things are true which we have said so many of concerning little ones, there is no such one among the sons of men, nor was, nor will be, except the one Mediator, in whom the propitiation and justification for us is set[^157], through which, the enmities of sins being ended, we are reconciled to God[^158]. It is therefore not beside the point, as far as seems to suffice for the present cause, to repeat a few things from the very beginning of the human race, by which the reader's mind may be formed against certain things that might disturb it.

## The sin of the first parents was chiefly a sin of disobedience.

**21. 35.** After those first men, one man Adam and from him Eve his wife, having received the precept of God, refused to keep obedience, a just and due penalty followed them. For the Lord had threatened that on the day on which they ate the forbidden food they would die the death[^159]. Accordingly, because they had received the power of using for food every tree that was in paradise—in which God had also planted the tree of life—but he had forbidden them only that one which he called of the knowledge of good and evil, by which name the consequence of experience would be signified, and what good they would feel by keeping and what evil by transgressing the prohibition, they are surely rightly understood to have, before the malign persuasion of the devil, abstained from the forbidden food and used the things granted, and by this both the rest and especially the tree of life[^160].

For what more absurd than that they should be believed to have taken nourishment from the other trees, but not also from that one, which both had been likewise permitted and, by a special usefulness, did not allow the bodies—animal though they were—to be changed through the decay of the ages and to grow old into death, granting this benefit to the human body from its own body, and showing by a mystical signification what is conferred through wisdom, whose figure it bore, upon the rational soul, so that, vivified by its nourishment, it might by no means be turned into the decay and death of wickedness? For deservedly it is said of it: *It is a tree of life to those who embrace it*[^161].

As this tree was in the bodily paradise, so that one was in the spiritual paradise—the one offering vigor to the senses of the outer man, the other to those of the inner man, a vital vigor without any change for the worse by time. They served God, then, by the piety of obedience strongly commended to them, by which alone God is worshiped. How great this is in itself, and how it alone suffices to keep the rational creature under the Creator, could not be more excellently intimated than that they were forbidden a tree that was not evil.

For far be it that the Creator of good things, who made all things *and behold, they were very good*[^162], should plant anything evil even in the fertility of that bodily paradise.

But that it might be shown to man—for whom under such a Lord servitude was most useful—how great was the good of obedience alone, which alone he had required of his servant, for whom it was expedient to obey not on account of that one's dominion but rather on account of the usefulness of the one serving, they were forbidden that tree, by which, had they used it unforbidden, they would have suffered no evil at all, so that what they suffered by using it after the prohibition might be sufficiently understood—namely, that this tree, perilous by a harmful food, did not bring it upon them, but only the obedience violated.

## The state of man before and after sin.

**22. 36.** Before they had violated this, then, they were pleasing to God and God was pleasing to them, and although they bore an animal body, they felt nothing disobedient moved in it against themselves. For the order of justice brought it about that, because their soul had received the body as a servant from the Lord, just as the soul itself obeyed its Lord, so its body should obey it and render to it a servitude suited to that life without any resistance. Hence too they were naked and were not ashamed[^163].

For the rational soul is now ashamed, by a natural shame, that in the flesh, into whose servitude it received the right of power, it cannot, by some infirmity, bring it about that the members are not moved when it does not will and are moved when it does. For this reason these members are in any chaste person deservedly called shameful, in that they are aroused against the mind, their mistress, as it were of their own power, as it pleases them, and the reins of virtue have only this much right over them—that they not allow them to come to unclean and unlawful corruptions.

This disobedience of the flesh, then, which is in the very movement, even if it is not allowed to have its effect, was not in those first men then, when they were naked and were not ashamed. For the rational soul, the mistress of the flesh, had not yet stood out disobedient to its Lord, so that by a reciprocal penalty it would experience its servant the flesh disobedient, with a certain sense of confusion and of its own trouble—a sense which surely it itself, by its own disobedience, did not bring upon God. For it is nothing shameful or troublesome to God if we do not obey him, whose supreme power over us we can in no way diminish; but it is shameful to us that the flesh does not serve our command, because this comes about through the infirmity which by sinning we deserved, and it is called the sin dwelling in our members[^164].

But this sin is such that it is the penalty of sin. Finally, after that transgression was committed and the disobedient soul was turned away from the law of its Lord, its servant—that is, its body—began to have against it a law of disobedience, and those men were ashamed of their nakedness, the movement they had not before felt being noticed in themselves; which noticing was called the opening of the eyes[^165]; for they did not wander among those trees with closed eyes. So too it is written of Hagar: *Her eyes were opened, and she saw a well*[^166]. Then those men covered the shameful parts, which God had made members for them, but they themselves made shameful.

## The corruption of nature through sin, the renewal through Christ.

**23. 37.** From this law of sin is born the flesh of sin, to be expiated through the sacrament of him who came in the likeness of sinful flesh[^167], that the body of sin might be made void—which he also calls the body of this death[^168]—from which nothing frees the wretched man but the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord[^169]. For this law, the beginning of death, passed from them into their posterity in the same way as the toil by which all men toil on the earth, in the same way as the bearing of children with pains in women[^170]. For these things, when they were convicted of sin, they deserved by the sentence of God, which we see fulfilled not in them alone but also in their successors, in some more, in others less, yet in all.

And so, since the first righteousness of those first men was to obey God and not to have in their members this law of concupiscence against the law of their mind[^171], now, after their sin, our flesh of sin being born from them, it is held a great thing by those who obey God not to obey the desires of that same concupiscence[^172] and to crucify in themselves the flesh with its passions and concupiscences, that they may be Jesus Christ's, who figured this on his cross, to whom by his grace he gave the power to become sons of God. For he did not give this to all men, but to as many as received him, that they who had been born in the flesh to the age might be reborn to God in spirit[^173]. For thus it was said of them: *But as many as received him, he gave them the power to become sons of God, who were born not of flesh, not of blood, not of the will of man nor of the will of the flesh, but of God*[^174].

## The flesh of the incarnate Word of God.

**24. 38.** But going on he added: *And the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^175]; as though saying: This indeed was a great thing done in them, that they who had first been born of flesh to the age—though created by God himself—should be born to God of God; but a far more wonderful thing was done, in that, although it belonged to nature for these men to be born of flesh, but to a benefit to be born of God, in order to impart this benefit, he who was naturally born of God deigned mercifully to be born also of flesh.

For this is: *And the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us.* Through this, he says, it came about that we, born of flesh and flesh, afterward, by being born of the Spirit, might be spirit and might dwell in God, because God too, born of God, afterward, by being born of flesh, was made flesh and dwelt among us. For the Word, which was made flesh, was in the beginning, and with God was God[^176].

Nevertheless that very participation of him in our lower things, that ours might be in his higher things, held a certain middle even in the nativity of the flesh, so that we indeed were born in the flesh of sin[^177], but he in the likeness of sinful flesh; we not only of flesh and blood[^178], but also of the will of man and the will of the flesh; but he was born only of flesh and blood, not of the will of man nor of the will of the flesh, but of God. And therefore we are born unto death on account of sin, he unto death for our sake without sin. But just as his lower things, by which he descended to us, are not in every way made equal to our lower things, in which he found us here[^179], so too our higher things, by which we ascend to him, will not be made equal to his higher things, in which we are going to find him there. For we, by his grace, will have been made sons of God; he was always by nature the Son of God; we, once turned, will cling to him, unequal to God; he, never turned away, remains equal to God[^180]; we are partakers of eternal life, he is eternal life. He alone, therefore, even when made man, remaining God, never had any sin, nor took up the flesh of sin, although from the maternal flesh of sin. For what of flesh he took up from there, he surely either cleansed before taking it up, or cleansed by taking it up. Therefore he created a virgin mother conceiving not by the law of the flesh of sin—that is, not by the movement of carnal concupiscence—but by pious faith earning that the holy shoot should come to be in her; whom he chose, of whom he would be created; he chose her of whom he would be created. How much more, then, must the flesh of sin be baptized for the escaping of judgment, if the flesh without sin was baptized for an example of imitation!

## An objection of the Pelagians: the son of a baptized man is already washed.

**25. 39.** But what we answered above against those who say *If a sinner begot a sinner, a just man too ought to have begotten a just one*, this we answer also to those who say that one born of a baptized man ought already to be held as baptized. "For why," they say, "could he not have been baptized in the loins of his father, if, according to the letter that is written to the Hebrews, Levi could be tithed in the loins of Abraham[^181]?" Let those who say this attend that Levi was not tithed afterward, not because he had already been tithed in the loins of Abraham, but because he was so ordained in the honor of the priesthood that he received tithes, not gave them; otherwise neither would the rest of his brothers, who gave to him, be tithed, because they too had already been tithed in the loins of Abraham by Melchizedek[^182].

## The son of a baptized man needs baptism, as the son of a circumcised man needs circumcision.

**25. 40.** But lest anyone say that the sons of Abraham could rightly be tithed, although they had already been tithed in the loins of their father, because tithing was such a thing as had often to be done in each man—just as the Israelites every year, indeed from all their fruits, were wont their whole life to give frequent tithes to the Levites—but that baptism is such a sacrament as is given once, and that, if anyone had already received this when he was in his father, he ought to be reckoned as nothing but baptized when one who had been baptized begot him: whoever says this, that I may not argue long, let him look to circumcision, which was done once and yet was done in individuals individually. As, then, in the time of that sacrament one who was born of a circumcised man had to be circumcised, so now one who is born of a baptized man must be baptized.

## Why the sons of the baptized must be baptized, who according to Paul are already holy.

**25. 41.** "But the Apostle says: Your children would be unclean, but now they are holy[^183]; and therefore," they say, "the children of the faithful ought by no means already to be baptized." I wonder that those say this who deny that sin is drawn originally from Adam. For if they take this saying of the Apostle in such a way as to believe that sanctified children are born of the faithful, why do they themselves not doubt that these too ought to be baptized? Why, finally, are they unwilling to confess that some sin is drawn originally from a sinful parent, if some holiness is drawn from a holy one? And it is indeed not against our assertion, even if holy children are propagated from the faithful, that we say that, if they are not baptized, they go on to condemnation—those to whom they themselves too bar the kingdom of heaven, although they say that they have no sin, either their own or original. Or if it seems to them unworthy that the holy be condemned, how will it be worthy that the holy be separated from the kingdom of God?

Let them rather attend to how some sin is not drawn from sinful parents, if some holiness is drawn from holy ones and uncleanness from the unclean. For he said both who said: *Otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy.* Let them also explain how it is just that the holy, born of the faithful, and the unclean, born of unbelievers, are nevertheless equally, if they have not been baptized, not permitted to enter the kingdom of God. What, then, does this holiness profit them?

For if they confessed that the unclean born of unbelievers are condemned, but that the holy children of the faithful cannot enter into the kingdom of God unless they have been baptized, yet are not condemned, because they are holy, there would be some sort of distinction; but as it is, they say that those born of the holy are holy and those born of the unclean are unclean equally, and that, because they have no sin, they are not condemned, and that, because they do not have baptism, they are separated from the kingdom of God. Who would believe that such minds do not see this absurdity?

## The sanctification of children from their own baptism and that of their parents.

**25. 42.** But how little contrary to our opinion—nay, to the Apostle's own, who said, *From one all unto condemnation*, and, *from one all unto justification of life*[^184]—is what he says, when he was dealing with another matter, *Otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*[^185], attend a little.

**26.** Sanctification is not of one kind; for I think that catechumens too are in a certain way of their own sanctified, through the sign of Christ and the prayer of the laying on of hands, and that what they receive, although it is not the body of Christ, is nevertheless holy, and holier than the foods by which we are nourished, since it is a sacrament[^186]. But the same Apostle said that even the foods themselves, which we use for the necessity of sustaining this life, are sanctified[^187] through the word of God and prayer, by which we surely pray when we are about to refresh our little bodies. As, then, this sanctification of foods does not bring it about that what has entered the mouth does not go into the belly and is not cast out into the privy through the corruption[^188] by which all earthly things are dissolved—whence the Lord too exhorts us to another food, which is not corrupted[^189]—so the sanctification of a catechumen, if he has not been baptized, does not avail him for entering the kingdom of heaven or for the forgiveness of sins. And by this, that sanctification too, of whatever kind it is, which the Apostle said is in the children of the faithful, does not pertain at all to this question concerning baptism and the origin or forgiveness of sin. For he says that unbelieving spouses too are sanctified in believing spouses, speaking thus in that very place: *For the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving woman is sanctified in the brother; otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*[^190]. No one, I suppose, understands so unbelievingly—whatever he understands in these words—as on this account to judge that a husband who is not a Christian, because his wife was a Christian, both no longer needs to be baptized, and has already come to the forgiveness of sins, and is going to enter into the kingdom of heaven, because he was said to be sanctified in his wife.

## Whoever has not been born cannot be reborn.

**27. 43.** But whoever is still disturbed as to why those who are now born of the baptized are baptized, let him briefly receive this. Just as the generation of the flesh of sin through the one Adam draws unto condemnation all who are generated in that way, so the regeneration of the spirit of grace through the one Jesus Christ leads unto justification of eternal life all who, predestined, are regenerated in that way[^191]. But the sacrament of baptism is surely the sacrament of regeneration. Wherefore, just as a man who has not lived cannot die, and one who has not died cannot rise again[^192], so one who has not been born cannot be reborn. From which it is concluded that no one could have been reborn in his parent, not having been born. But it is fitting that, if he has been born, he be reborn, because *unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God*[^193]. It is fitting, therefore, that even the little one be imbued with the sacrament of regeneration, lest without it he go ill out of this life; which is done only for the forgiveness of sins. Which Christ also showed in that very place, when, asked how these things could come about, he recalled what Moses did in the lifting up of the serpent[^194]. Since, then, through the sacrament of baptism infants are conformed to the death of Christ, it must be confessed that they are freed from the serpent's bite, if we do not wish to stray from the rule of Christian faith. Which bite, nevertheless, they received not in their own life but in him on whom it was first inflicted.

## In an unbaptized son the sins he drew from his parent are harmful.

**27. 44.** Nor let this deceive anyone, that a parent's own sins, after his conversion, do not harm him; "for how much more," they say, "can they not harm his son!" But those who hold this do not attend that, just as a parent's own sins do not harm him through the fact that he was reborn in spirit[^195], so the things drawn from a parent will harm one who is born of him unless he is reborn in that way, because even renewed parents beget carnally not from the first fruits of newness but from the remnants of oldness, and the children, propagated wholly old in the flesh of sin from the remaining oldness of their parents, escape the condemnation owed to the old man through the sacrament of spiritual regeneration and renewal[^196].

For this we ought especially to attend and remember, on account of the questions that have been raised on this matter or can still be raised: that only the full and perfect forgiveness of all sins comes about by baptism[^197], but that the quality of the man himself is not all at once wholly changed; rather, the spiritual first fruits, in those advancing well, with newness growing from day to day, change in themselves what is carnally old, until the whole be so renewed[^198] that even the animal infirmity of the body comes to spiritual firmness and incorruption.

## Parents beget children carnally and therefore transmit sin into them.

**28. 45.** But this law of sin—which the Apostle also calls sin, when he says, *Let not sin, then, reign in your mortal body, so as to obey its desires*[^199]—does not so remain in the members of those who have been reborn of water and the Spirit[^200] as though forgiveness of it had not been made, where there comes about an altogether full and perfect forgiveness of sins, all the enmities being slain[^201] by which we were separated from God[^202]; but it remains in the oldness of the flesh as overcome and slain, if it does not in a certain way revive through unlawful consentings and is recalled into its own reign and dominion.

But from this oldness of the flesh, in which is that law of sin, or sin now forgiven, the life of the spirit—in whose newness the baptized are reborn through the grace of God—is so distinguished that it was too little for the Apostle to say[^203] that such men are not in sin, unless he also said that they are not in the flesh itself, before they depart from this mortal life. *For those who are in the flesh*, he says, *cannot please God; but you are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit, if indeed the Spirit of God dwells in you*[^204].

Nevertheless, just as those use the flesh itself well, corruptible though it is, who turn its members to good works—and they are not in this flesh, because they are not wise according to it nor live according to it—just as, finally, they use even death, which is the penalty of the first sin, well who expend it bravely and patiently for their brethren, for the faith, for any true and holy righteousness, so the faithful married too use well even that law of sin which, now forgiven, remains in the oldness of the flesh: who, from the fact that they are in the newness of Christ, by no means suffer lust to have dominion over them, but, from the fact that they still draw the oldness of Adam, mortally beget children to be immortally regenerated, with that propagation of sin by which those who have been reborn are not held bound and by which those who are born are loosed by being reborn[^205].

As long, then, as the law remains by way of concupiscence in the members, while it itself remains, its guilt is loosed; but it is loosed for one who has received the sacrament of regeneration and has now begun to be renewed. But what is born of that remaining oldness of concupiscence needs to be reborn, that it may be healed, because faithful parents, both born carnally and reborn spiritually, begot their children carnally; but the children, before they were born, how could they be reborn?

## How concupiscence may remain in the baptized, its guilt being slain.

**28. 46.** Nor wonder that I said that, while the law of sin remains by way of concupiscence, its guilt is loosed through the grace of the sacrament. For just as wicked deeds and words and thoughts, as far as concerns the very movements of soul and body, have already passed away and are not, yet, though they have passed away and no longer exist, their guilt remains unless it is loosed by the forgiveness of sins, so, on the contrary, in this law of concupiscence, not now past but still remaining, its guilt is loosed and will be no more, when the full forgiveness of sins comes about in baptism. Finally, if departure from this life follows at once, there will be nothing at all to hold the man bound, all the things that held him being loosed. As, then, it is no wonder that the guilt of past deeds, thoughts, and words remains before the forgiveness of sins, so, on the contrary, it ought to be no wonder that the guilt of remaining concupiscence passes away after the forgiveness of sins.

## All the predestined are saved through Christ the one mediator and through the one faith.

**29. 47.** Since these things are so, from the time when *through one man sin entered into this world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men*[^206] until the end of this carnal generation and corruptible age, whose sons beget and are begotten[^207], there being no man of whom, set in this life, it could be truly said that he has no sin at all, except the one Mediator[^208], who reconciles us to our Creator through the forgiveness of sins, that same our Lord did not in any times of the human race, before the last judgment still to come, deny this his remedy to those whom by a most certain foreknowledge and most just beneficence he predestined to reign with him unto eternal life. For before the birth of his flesh and the infirmity of his passion and the power of his resurrection, he formed those who then were, by faith in those future things, unto the inheritance of eternal salvation—those things by faith in whose present reality he formed those who were present while they were being done and saw the things foretold being fulfilled; and by faith in those same things, now past, he does not cease to form those who came afterward, both ourselves and those who will be hereafter. There is, then, one faith which saves all[^209] who are saved by being reborn from carnal generation into spiritual[^210], terminated in him who came to be judged and to die for us, the judge of the living and the dead. But the sacraments of this one faith, according to the opportunity of signification, have been varied through various times.

## Christ is the Savior of little ones too.

**29. 48.** The same, therefore, is the Savior of little ones and of the older, of whom the angels said, *Today a Savior is born to you*[^211], and of whom it was said to the Virgin Mary, *You will call his name Jesus; for he will save his people from their sins*[^212], where it was openly shown that he is named with this name, by which he was called Jesus, on account of the salvation which he grants us; for Jesus in Latin is "Savior." Who, then, is there who would dare to say that the Lord Christ is Jesus only to the older and not also to little ones? He who came in the likeness of sinful flesh[^213], that he might make void the body of sin[^214], in which—most weak, with infantile members suited or fit for no use—the rational soul is weighed down by a pitiable ignorance.

Which ignorance, plainly, I would in no way believe to have been in that infant in whom the Word was made flesh, that he might dwell among us[^215]; nor would I have suspected in the little Christ that infirmity of soul which we see in little ones. For through this, when they are disturbed by irrational movements, they are restrained by no reasoning, by no command, but sometimes by pain or by the fear of pain, so that you may see them altogether to be sons of that disobedience which is moved in the members, warring against the law of the mind[^216], and does not rest when reason wills; but it itself too is often checked, as it were by a beating, by bodily pain, or by being frightened, or by some such movement of the soul, yet not with the will commanding. But because there was in him the likeness of sinful flesh, he willed to undergo the changes of the ages, beginning from infancy itself, so that that flesh seems to have been able to come to death even by growing old, had he not been slain as a young man. Which death, nevertheless, in the flesh of sin is rendered as owed to disobedience, but in the likeness of sinful flesh was taken up by the will of obedience. For, about to go to it and to suffer it, he says this: *Behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he will find nothing; but that all may know that I do the will of my Father, rise, let us go hence*[^217]. Having said these things, he went on to a death not owed, made obedient unto death[^218].

## The redemption of Christ profited us more than the sin of Adam harmed us.

**30. 49.** Wherefore those who say, *If by the sin of the first man it came about that we die, by the coming of Christ it ought to come about that, believing in him, we do not die*, and add, as it were a reason, saying, *For the transgression of the transgressor did not harm us more than the incarnation or redemption of the Savior profited us*—why do they not rather attend to this, hear this, believe this without dispute, which the Apostle spoke without ambiguity: *Since by a man came death, and by a man the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^219]? For he was speaking of nothing other than the resurrection of the body. He said, then, that the death of the body of all came about through one man, and he promised that the resurrection of the body of all unto eternal life would come about through the one Christ. How, then, *did that one harm us more by sinning than this one profited by redeeming*, when through that one's sin we die temporally, but through this one's redemption we rise again not to a temporal life but to a perpetual one? Our body, then, is dead on account of sin, but the body of Christ alone is dead without sin, so that, by the shedding of blood without guilt, the bonds of all guilts might be blotted out[^220], by which the debtors who believe in him were before held by the devil. Therefore: *This is my blood, which will be shed for many unto the forgiveness of sins*[^221].

## Why through baptism death itself is not abolished together with sins.

**31. 50.** But he could have granted this too to believers, that they should not experience the death even of this body; but if he had done this, a certain felicity would be added to the flesh, but the fortitude of faith would be diminished. For men so fear this death that they would call Christians happy for no other reason than that they could not die at all. And by this no one, on account of that life which after this death is to be blessed, would hasten to the grace of Christ through the virtue even of despising that death, but men would believe in Christ more daintily, for the removing of the trouble of death.

He bestowed, then, more grace; he granted, without doubt, more to his faithful. For what great thing was it, seeing that those who believed did not die, to believe oneself not going to die? How much greater is it, how much braver, how much more praiseworthy, so to believe that, about to die, one hopes to live without end! Finally, this he will grant to some at the end, that they will not feel this death by a sudden transformation, but, together with those rising, will be caught up in the clouds to meet Christ in the air, and so will always live with the Lord[^222].

And rightly for them, because there will be no posterity, who for this reason believe not by hoping for what they do not see but by loving what they see. Such a faith is nerveless and weak and not to be called faith at all, since faith has been thus defined: *Faith is the substance of those who hope, the conviction of things that are not seen*[^223].

Hence too in the same letter to the Hebrews, where this too is written, when he had consequently enumerated certain men who pleased God by faith, he says: *According to faith all these died, not having received the promises, but seeing them far off and saluting them and confessing that they are guests and pilgrims upon the earth*[^224]. And a little after he thus concluded the same praise of faith: *And all these*, he says, *having obtained testimony through faith, did not receive the promises of God; for God provided something better for us, that they should not be perfected without us*[^225]. This praise of faith would not be, nor would there be faith at all, as I have already said, if men in believing pursued visible rewards—that is, if the reward of immortality were rendered to the faithful in this age.

## Death itself pertains to the contest of faith.

**31. 51.** Hence the Lord himself too willed to die, that, as it is written of him, *through death he might make void him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and might free those who through fear of death were all their life subject to slavery*[^226]. By this testimony that too is sufficiently shown: that this death of the body came about with the devil as prince and author—that is, from the sin which he persuaded; for not otherwise would he be most truly said to have the power of death.

Hence he who was dying without any sin, either original or his own, said what I recalled a little before: *Behold, the prince of the world comes*—that is, the devil, who has the power of death—*and in me he will find nothing*[^227], that is, of sin, on account of which he has made men die. And as though it were said to him, "Why, then, do you die?"—*But that all may know*, he says, *that I do the will of my Father, rise, let us go hence*; that is, "that I may die, having no cause of death from sin under the author of sin, but, from obedience and righteousness, made obedient unto death"[^228]. By that testimony, then, this too was shown: that the fact that the faithful conquer the fear of death pertains to the contest of faith itself—which surely would have been lacking, if immortality had at once attended those who believed.

## Why Christ after his resurrection withdrew his visible presence from the world.

**32. 52.** And so, although the Lord did many visible miracles, whence faith itself might, as it were from certain milky beginnings, germinate and from that tenderness grow together into its own strength—for it is the stronger the more it now does not seek these things—nevertheless he willed that what we hope is promised should be awaited invisibly, that the just man might live by faith[^229]; to such a degree that he himself, who rose on the third day, did not will to be among men, but, having shown in his flesh the example of resurrection to those whom he deigned to have as witnesses of this matter[^230], ascended into heaven, withdrawing himself even from their eyes and granting to no one's flesh anything such as he had shown in his own flesh, so that they too might live by faith and might now, in the meantime, await invisibly, through patience, the reward of that righteousness in which one lives by faith—a reward that will afterward be visible.

To this understanding I believe that too is to be referred which he says of the Holy Spirit: *He cannot come unless I go away*[^231]. For this was to say: "You will not be able to live justly by faith—which you will have from my gift, that is, from the Holy Spirit—unless I take from your eyes this which you gaze upon, so that your heart may advance spiritually by believing the invisible."

This righteousness from faith he commends, again speaking of the Holy Spirit, thus: *He*, he says, *will convict the world of sin, of righteousness, of judgment: of sin indeed, because they did not believe in me; of righteousness, because I go to the Father and you will no longer see me*[^232]. What is this righteousness, by which they would not see him, except that the just man should live by faith[^233], and that, looking not to the things that are seen but to the things that are not seen, we should by the spirit await from faith the hope of righteousness?

## The forgiveness of sins does not take away the penalties of sins.

**33. 53.** But those who say, *If this death of the body had come about by sin, surely we would not die after the forgiveness of sins which the Redeemer grants us*, do not understand how things whose guilt God looses—lest they harm after this life—he nevertheless allows to remain for the contest of faith, so that through them those advancing may be instructed and exercised in the struggle of righteousness. For another, not understanding this, could also say: "If on account of sin God said to man, *In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, and the earth will bring forth thorns and thistles for you*[^234], why does this toil too remain after the forgiveness of sins, and the earth bring forth these hard and rough things even for the faithful?

Likewise, if on account of sin it was said to the woman, *In groaning you will bring forth*[^235], why even after the forgiveness of sins do faithful women suffer those same pains in childbearing? And yet it is established that on account of the sin they had committed those first men heard these things from God and deserved them; nor does anyone resist these words of the divine book, which I have set down concerning the toil of man and the childbearing of woman, except one who, altogether a stranger to the catholic faith, opposes those same writings.

## Why the penalties of sins remain.

**34. 54.** But because such men too are not lacking, just as we answer them, when this question is proposed, by saying that those things are before forgiveness the punishments of sins, but after forgiveness the contests and exercises of the just, so also to those whom the death of the body likewise disturbs we ought to answer, so as both to confess that it came about by sin, and not to disdain that, after the forgiveness of sins, it has been left to us for the contest, that its great fear may be overcome by those advancing. For if the virtue of faith, which works through love[^236], were a small thing, to overcome the fear of death would not be so great a glory of the martyrs, nor would the Lord say: *No one has greater love than this, that he lay down his soul for his friends*[^237]. Which John in his letter says thus: *As he laid down his soul for us, so we too ought to lay down our soul for the brethren*[^238]. By no means, then, would a special patience be praised in undergoing or despising death for righteousness, if death were not a great and very hard trouble. He who overcomes its fear by faith gains a great glory for that faith itself and a just reward. Hence it is no wonder both that the death of the body would not have befallen man unless sin had gone before—whose penalty too this would be—and that after the forgiveness of sins it befalls the faithful, so that in overcoming its fear the fortitude of righteousness may be exercised.

## Sin and penalty in Adam.

**34. 55.** For the flesh which was first made was not the flesh of sin, in which man refused, amid the delights of paradise, to keep righteousness. Hence God determined that, after his sin, the propagated flesh of sin should strive, by toils and troubles, to receive righteousness. For this reason too Adam, dismissed from paradise, dwelt over against Eden[^239]—that is, over against the seat of delights—to signify that the flesh of sin, which in delights did not keep obedience before it was the flesh of sin, was to be instructed in toils, which are contrary to delights. As, then, those first men, by living justly afterward—whence they are deservedly believed to have been freed by the blood of the Lord from the uttermost punishment—nevertheless did not in that life deserve to be recalled to paradise, so too the flesh of sin, even if a man, his sins forgiven, has lived justly in it, does not at once deserve not to undergo that death which it drew from the propagation of sin.

## Sin and penalty in David.

**34. 56.** Something of this kind is intimated to us concerning the patriarch David in the *book of Kings*, to whom, when the prophet had been sent and threatened him, on account of the sin he had committed, with evils to come from the wrath of God, he deserved pardon by the confession of his sin, the prophet answering[^240] that that disgrace and crime was forgiven him; and yet the things God had threatened followed, so that he was humbled by his son. Why is it not here too said, "If God had threatened on account of that sin, why, the sin being forgiven, did he fulfill what he had threatened?" Unless because it will most rightly be answered, if it is said, that that forgiveness of sin was made lest the man be hindered from receiving eternal life, but that the effect of that threat followed, so that the man's piety might be exercised and proved in that humility? So too God both inflicted the death of the body on man on account of sin, and, after the forgiveness of sins, did not take it away, for the exercising of righteousness.

## We must turn aside neither into a proud presumption of righteousness nor into a secure love of sin.

**35. 57.** Let us, then, hold the undeviating confession of faith. There is one alone who was born without sin in the likeness of sinful flesh, lived without sin amid the sins of others, died without sin for our sins. *Let us not turn aside to the right or the left*[^241]; for to turn aside to the right is to deceive oneself by saying that one is without sin, but to the left is, by some perverse and crooked security, to give oneself over to sins as though with impunity. For *the ways that are on the right the Lord knows*[^242], who alone is without sin and can blot out our sins; *but perverse are those on the left*[^243], friendships with sins. Such a figure of the new people too those young men of twenty years prefigured who entered into the land of promise[^244], who were said to have turned aside neither to the right nor to the left[^245]. For the age of twenty years is not to be compared to the innocence of little ones; but, unless I am mistaken, this number shadows forth and resounds something mystical. For the Old Testament excels in the five books of Moses, but the New shines forth with the authority of the four Gospels. These numbers, multiplied by each other, come to twenty; for four times five, or five times four, is twenty. Such a people, as I have foretold, instructed in the kingdom of heaven through the two Testaments, the Old and the New, not turning aside to the right by a proud presumption of righteousness nor to the left by a secure love of sin, will enter into the land of that promise, where we may now neither wish further sins to be forgiven us nor fear sins to be punished in us, freed by that Redeemer who, not sold under sin, redeemed Israel from all its iniquities—whether committed in anyone's own life or drawn originally.

## The Pelagians confess that redemption is necessary for little ones.

**36. 58.** For they yielded not a little to the authority and truth of the divine Scriptures who, although they refused to express openly in their writings that forgiveness of sins is necessary for little ones, nevertheless confessed that redemption is needful for them. For by another word—itself too drawn from Christian teaching—they said nothing else at all. Nor is it to be doubted by those who faithfully read, faithfully hear, faithfully hold the divine writings, that from that flesh which first, by the will of sin, became the flesh of sin, the flesh of sin has thereafter been propagated, the proscription of iniquity and death passing through all by succession—except the one likeness of sinful flesh, which nevertheless would not be unless there were the flesh of sin.

## Questions about the soul. The Scriptures are clear in the things necessary for salvation.

**36. 59.** But concerning the soul—whether it too, propagated in the same way, is bound by a guilt which is forgiven it (for we cannot say that only the flesh of a little one, and not also the soul, needs the help of the Savior and Redeemer, or that the soul is a stranger to that thanksgiving which is in the Psalms, where we read and say: *Bless the Lord, O my soul, and forget not all his recompenses, who is gracious to all your iniquities, who heals all your weaknesses, who redeems your life from corruption*[^246])—or whether, even though not propagated, by the very fact that it is mingled with the flesh of sin to be weighed down, it now needs the forgiveness of that sin and its own redemption, God by his supreme foreknowledge judging which little ones do not deserve to be absolved from this guilt, even those who, not yet born[^247], have done nothing anywhere in their own life, whether good or evil; and how God, even if he does not create souls by transmission, is nevertheless not the author of that same guilt, on account of which the redemption of the sacrament is necessary for the soul of a little one—this is a great question and demands another discussion, tempered nevertheless, as I judge, with such moderation that a cautious inquiry be praised rather than a hasty assertion be reproved. For where a most obscure matter is discussed, without the certain and clear documents of the divine authorities helping, human presumption ought to restrain itself, doing nothing by turning aside to either side. For even if I am ignorant how any of these things can be demonstrated and explained, yet I believe this: that on this point too there would be the clearest authority of the divine oracles, if a man could not be ignorant of it without the loss of the promised salvation.

You have, worked out as far as my powers allowed—would that it were as convenient as it is lengthy!—a work whose length I would perhaps defend, did I not fear to do so by defending it at greater length.
