# Book III

To his dearest son Marcellinus, Augustine, bishop, servant of Christ and of the servants of Christ, greeting in the Lord.

## The occasion of this letter.

**1. 1.** Concerning the questions which you had proposed to me, that I should write you something against those who say that Adam, even if he had not sinned, would have died, and that nothing passed from his sin to his posterity by propagation—especially on account of the baptism of little ones, which by a most pious and motherly custom the universal Church frequents—and against those who say that there are, have been, and will be in this life sons of men having no sin at all, I had already finished two lengthy books. By these I seemed to myself indeed not to have met all the disturbances of all minds in this cause—which I do not know whether it can be done by me or by anyone; nay, I do not doubt that it cannot be done—but yet to have accomplished something by which the defenders of the faith handed down by our forefathers concerning these matters might stand not everywhere unarmed against the novelties of those who think otherwise. But after very few days I read certain writings of Pelagius—a holy man, as I hear, and a Christian of no small advancement—which contained very brief expositions on the letters of the apostle Paul; and there I found, when he had come to that place where the Apostle says *that through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death*[^1], and so passed into all men, a certain argument of those who deny that little ones bear original sin, which, I confess, I did not refute in those so long volumes of mine, because it had not at all come into my mind that anyone could think or say such things. Wherefore, since I did not wish to add anything to that work, which I had already closed with a definite end, I thought it should be inserted into this letter—both the argument itself, in the same words in which I read it, and what seems to me against it.

## Infants through baptism become faithful.

**2. 2.** The argument, then, was set down thus: *But those*, he says, *who are against the transmission of sin try to assail it thus: If, they say, Adam's sin harmed even those who do not sin, then Christ's righteousness too profits even those who do not believe, since he says that in like manner—nay, even more—are saved through one than perished through one before.* To this argument, then, as I said, in those two books which I wrote to you I answered nothing, nor did I at all set it before myself to be refuted. Now, then, first attend to how, when they say, *If Adam's sin harms even those who do not sin, then Christ's righteousness too profits even those who do not believe*, they surely judge it most absurd and most false that Christ's righteousness should profit even those who do not believe. Whence they think it is concluded that neither could the sin of the first man have harmed the little ones who do not sin, just as Christ's righteousness cannot profit any who do not believe.

Let them say, therefore, what Christ's righteousness profits baptized little ones; let them say altogether what they will. For surely, if they remember that they are Christians, they do not doubt that it profits something. Whatever, then, it profits, it cannot profit those who do not believe, as they themselves too assert.

Hence they are compelled to reckon baptized little ones in the number of believers and to consent to the authority of the holy Church everywhere, which does not deem them unworthy of the name of the faithful—those to whom Christ's righteousness, even according to these men, could profit only if they believe.

As, then, the spirit of righteousness of those through whom they are reborn transfers into them by its response the faith which they could not yet have by their own will, so the flesh of sin of those through whom they are born transfers into them the harm which they have not yet contracted by their own life.

And as the spirit of life regenerates them faithful in Christ, so the body of death had generated them sinners in Adam; for the one is a carnal generation, the other spiritual; the one makes sons of the flesh, the other sons of the spirit; the one sons of death, the other sons of resurrection; the one sons of the age, the other sons of God; the one sons of wrath, the other sons of mercy; and by this the one makes them bound by original sin, the other makes them freed from the bond of all sin.

## Infants not baptized would be unjustly punished, unless they had original sin.

**2. 3.** Finally, as to what we are unable to attain by the most penetrating understanding, let us be compelled to consent by divine authority. It is good that they themselves admonish us that Christ's righteousness cannot profit any but believers, and confess that it profits little ones something. Hence, as we have said, it is necessary that they set the baptized in the number of believers without any evasion. Consequently, then, if they are not baptized, they will be among those who do not believe, and by this they will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon them, since he who *does not believe in the Son will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon him*[^2]; and they are judged, since he who *does not believe is already judged*[^3]; and they will be condemned, since *he who believes and is baptized will be saved, but he who does not believe will be condemned*[^4]. Now, then, let these men see by what justice they try or attempt to assert that men who are without sin pertain not to eternal life but to the wrath of God, and are divinely judged and condemned, if, just as there is no proper sin in them, so there is also no original sin.

## We must pray to overcome the difficulties of problems.

**2. 4.** To the rest of the things which Pelagius intimates that those say who argue against original sin I have, I think, answered sufficiently and clearly enough in those two books of my lengthy work. And if to some it will seem either too little or too obscure, let them grant pardon and settle the matter with those who perhaps reprove it not because it is too little but because it is too much; and let those who do not yet understand the things that I think were said clearly, according to the nature of the questions, not slander me for negligence or for a lack of my ability, but rather beseech God for the receiving of understanding.

## Under another's name Pelagius reports arguments against original sin.

**3. 5.** Nevertheless we ought not negligently to notice that this man—a good and praiseworthy man, as those who know him speak of him—brought forward this argument against the propagation of sin not from his own person, but intimated what those say who do not approve it; and not only this which I just now set forth and answered, but also the rest, to which I recalled that I had already answered in those books. For when he had said, *If, they say, Adam's sin harmed even those who do not sin, then Christ's righteousness too profits even those who do not believe*—which, in the things I answered, you see how it not only does not assail what we say, but even admonishes us what to say—he went on and added: *Then they say: If baptism cleanses that ancient trespass, those who are born of two baptized persons ought to be free of this sin; for they could not transmit to their posterity what they themselves did not have at all. This too is added*, he says, *that, if the soul is not from transmission but only the flesh, then it alone has the transmission of sin and it alone deserves the penalty, saying that it is unjust that a soul born today, not from the mass of Adam, should bear so ancient a sin of another. They say also*, he says, *that it is by no reason conceded that God, who forgives proper sins, should impute another's.*

## Pelagius's own opinion is uncertain.

**3. 6.** Do you see, I beseech you, how Pelagius set all this in his writings not from his own person but from that of others—so far knowing that this is some I-know-not-what novelty, which has now begun to sound against the ancient opinion planted in the Church, that he himself was either ashamed or afraid to confess it?

And perhaps he himself does not hold this—that a man is born without sin, for whom he confesses baptism to be necessary, in which the forgiveness of sins comes about; that a man is condemned without sin, whom, if unbaptized, it is necessary to reckon among those who do not believe, since surely the Gospel Scripture cannot deceive, in which it is most openly read: *He who does not believe will be condemned*[^5]; finally, that without sin the image of God is not admitted to the kingdom of God, since *unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God*[^6]; and thus is either cast headlong into eternal death without sin, or—what is more absurd—has eternal life outside the kingdom of God, although the Lord, foretelling what he will say to his own at the end, *Come, blessed of my Father, receive the kingdom which was prepared for you from the beginning of the world*[^7], made manifest also what that very kingdom was of which he spoke, concluding thus: *So those will go into eternal burning, but the just into eternal life*[^8].

These things, then, and others which follow from this error, very perverse and repugnant to Christian truth, I believe that so eminently Christian a man does not hold at all. But it can be that he is still perhaps so moved even by the arguments of those who hold against the transmission of sin that he awaits to hear or learn what is said against them; and therefore he was both unwilling to be silent about what those who hold against the transmission of sin say, that the question might be intimated for discussion, and removed it from his own person, lest he too should be judged to hold this.

## We must cling to the things that are most open in the Scriptures, that from them the obscure may be understood.

**4. 7.** But even if I am unable to refute the arguments of these men, I see nevertheless that we must cling to the things that are most open in the Scriptures, that from them the obscure may be revealed, or, if the mind is not yet fit to be able either to discern them when demonstrated or to track them down when hidden, they may be believed without any hesitation. But what is more open than so many and so great testimonies of the divine oracles, by which it appears most clearly both that no man can come to eternal life and salvation apart from the fellowship of Christ, and that no one can be unjustly condemned by the divine judgment—that is, separated from that life and salvation? Whence it follows that, since nothing else is done when little ones are baptized but that they are incorporated into the Church, that is, joined to the body and members of Christ[^9], it is manifest that they pertain to condemnation unless this has been conferred on them. But they could not be condemned if they surely had no sin. Because this age could contract none in its own life, it remains to understand—or, if we cannot yet do this, at least to believe—that little ones draw original sin.

## Clear testimonies of the Scriptures for understanding Romans 5:12.

**4. 8.** And by this, if the apostolic words have anything ambiguous in which he says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men*[^10], and can be led and transferred to another meaning, is that too ambiguous: *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God*[^11]? Is that too: *You will call his name Jesus; for he will save his people from their sins*[^12]? Is even that, that *the physician is not needed by the healthy but by the sick*[^13]—that is, that Jesus is not necessary for those who have no sin, but for those who are to be saved from sin? Is even that, that unless men eat his flesh[^14]—that is, are made partakers of his body—they will not have life?

By these and other testimonies of this kind, which I now pass over—most clear with the divine light, most certain with the divine authority—does not the truth proclaim, without any ambiguity, not only that unbaptized little ones cannot enter into the kingdom of God, but also that they cannot have eternal life apart from the body of Christ, into which, that they may be incorporated, they are imbued with the sacrament of baptism?

Does not the truth testify, without any doubt, that they are brought to Jesus—that is, to Christ the Savior and physician—by the pious hands of those carrying them for no other reason than that, through the medicine of his sacraments, they may be healed of the plague of sin? Why, then, do we delay to understand even the words of the Apostle, about which perhaps we were doubting, in such a way that they agree with those testimonies about which we cannot doubt?

## Romans 5:12 is not ambiguous.

**4. 9.** Although in that whole place, where the Apostle speaks of the condemnation of many through the sin of one and the justification of many through the righteousness of one[^15], nothing seems to me ambiguous, except that he says *Adam is the form of the one to come*[^16]. For this in truth not only fits this meaning, by which it is understood that his future posterity were generated from the same form with sin, but these words can also be drawn into various other understandings. For we too have at some time said one thing from it, and will perhaps say another, which nevertheless is not opposed to this understanding; and Pelagius himself expounded it in more than one way. But the rest of the things that are said there, if they are diligently noticed and treated—as in the first of those two books I attempted, as best I could—even if, by the necessity of the matters themselves, they bring forth an obscure discourse, will nevertheless be unable to have any other sense than that through which it came about that from antiquity the universal Church held that faithful little ones obtain, through the baptism of Christ, the forgiveness of original sin.

## The authority of St. Cyprian.

**5. 10.** Hence not undeservedly the blessed Cyprian sufficiently shows how the Church keeps this as believed and understood from the beginning. When he asserted that little ones, most recently from the maternal womb, were already fit to receive the baptism of Christ—since he had been consulted whether this ought to be done before the eighth day—he tried, as much as he could, to show them perfect, lest anyone should suppose, as it were on account of the number of days, that they were still to be made perfect, because formerly infants were circumcised on the eighth day. But although he had furnished them so great a patronage of defense, he nevertheless confessed that they were not free from original sin, because, if he denied this, he would take away the very cause of that baptism for whose receiving he was defending them. You can read the letter of the said martyr *On Baptizing Little Ones*[^17], if you wish; for it cannot be lacking at Carthage. But into this our work too, as far as seemed sufficient for the present question, I thought a few things from it should be transferred, which attend to prudently: *But as far as pertains to the case of infants*, he says, *whom you said ought not to be baptized within the second or third day on which they are born, and that the law of the ancient circumcision should be considered, so that you thought that one who has been born should not be baptized and sanctified within the eighth day, a far different thing seemed good in our council. For in this which you thought ought to be done no one consented; but we all rather judged that the mercy and grace of God is to be denied to no man born. For since the Lord in his Gospel says, 'The Son of man came not to lose the souls of men but to save them'*[^18], *as far as is in us, if it can be done, no soul is to be lost.* Do you notice what he says, how he holds it to be deadly and death-bearing, not only to the flesh but also to the soul of an infant, to go out of this life without that saving sacrament?

Hence, even if he said nothing else, it was for us to understand that the soul cannot perish without sin. But see a little after, while defending the innocence of little ones, what he nevertheless most openly confesses of them: *But if anything could hinder men from the obtaining of grace*, he says, *graver sins could rather hinder the adult and the advanced and those greater in age. Further, if even to the gravest offenders and to those who have sinned much against God before, when they have afterward believed, the forgiveness of sins is given, and no one is forbidden from baptism and grace, how much more ought an infant not to be forbidden, who, newly born, has sinned nothing, except that, born carnally according to Adam, he has at his first birth contracted the contagion of the ancient death! Who approaches the more easily to receiving the forgiveness of sins by this very thing, that what is forgiven him are not his own sins, but another's.*

## St. Cyprian speaks from the ancient rule of faith.

**5. 11.** You see with how great a confidence, from the ancient and undoubted rule of faith, so great a man says these things. He brought forth these most certain documents in order that what was uncertain—about which the one to whom he writes back had consulted him, and about which he recalls that a decree of the council was established, namely that even before the eighth day from the day on which an infant was born, no one should doubt to baptize him if he were brought—might be proved by these supports.

For it was not then being decreed or confirmed in the council as something new or as something assailed by anyone's contradiction, that infants were held bound by original sin; but while that consultation was being turned over and discussed there—on account of the law of carnal circumcision, whether they ought to be baptized even before the eighth day—therefore no one consented to him who denied this, because it was now held not a matter for consulting or disputing, but firm and certain, that a soul would perish from eternal salvation if it ended this life without the obtaining of that sacrament, although little ones most recently from the womb were bound by the guilt of original sin alone; wherefore for them, even though much easier, because of another's, the forgiveness of sins was nevertheless necessary. By these certain things that uncertain question about the eighth day was resolved, and it was judged in the council that it is permitted to succor a man born on any day, lest he perish forever—a reason being given even for that carnal circumcision itself, that it was a shadow of the future; not that we should understand that baptism too ought to be given on the eighth day from that on which a man is born, but that we are spiritually circumcised in the resurrection of Christ, who indeed rose from the dead on the third day after the passion, yet, in the days of the weeks by which the times roll on, on the eighth day—that is, on the first day after the Sabbath.

## Concerning original sin there is the consent of all.

**6. 12.** And now, by the boldness of I know not what new disputation, certain men try to make us keep silent as uncertain what our forefathers brought forward as most certain for resolving certain things that seemed to some uncertain. For when this first began to be disputed I do not know; but this I know, that even St. Jerome—who today too is occupied in ecclesiastical letters with the fame and labor of so excellent a learning—employs this too, for resolving certain questions in his books, as a most certain document without any dispute. For in what he wrote on the prophet Jonah, when he had come to that place where even little ones are recalled as chastened by fasting, he says: *The greater age begins, and it comes down even to the lesser. For no one is without sin, not even if his life has been but of one day and the years of his life are numbered. For if the stars are not clean in the sight of God, how much more a worm and rottenness, and those who are held bound by the sin of the offending Adam*[^19]! If we could easily question this most learned man, how many treaters of the divine Scriptures in both tongues and writers of Christian disputations would he recall, who, from the time the Church of Christ was established, held nothing else, received nothing else from their forefathers, handed down nothing else to their posterity! For my part, although I have read far fewer, I do not remember to have heard anything else from Christians who accept both Testaments—not only those set in the catholic Church, but even in any heresy or schism whatever—I do not remember to have read anything else among those whom I could read writing something on these matters who followed the canonical Scriptures, or believed they followed them, or would have wished to believe so.

Whence this business suddenly emerged for us I do not know. For a little while ago my ears were brushed in passing by certain men conversing cursorily, when we were there at Carthage, *that little ones are baptized not in order to receive the forgiveness of sins, but to be sanctified in Christ*[^20]. Moved by this novelty, both because it was not opportune that I should say anything against it, and because they were not such men about whose authority I was anxious, I easily held it among things settled and abolished. And behold, now it is defended with a flaming zeal; behold, it is even committed to memory by writing; behold, the matter is brought to this crisis, that we are even consulted by the brethren on it; behold, we are compelled to argue and write against it!

## St. Jerome's testimony for original sin against Jovinian.

**7. 13.** A few years ago there appeared at Rome a certain Jovinian, who is said to have persuaded even consecrated women, now of advanced age, to marry—not by enticing them, that he might wish to take any of them to wife, but by arguing that virgins dedicated to holiness have nothing more of merit with God than the faithful married.

Yet this contrivance never came into his mind, that he should try to assert that the sons of men are born without original sin. And surely, if he had built this up, women would much more readily wish to marry, being about to bring forth most clean offspring. When the brethren had sent his writings—for he dared even to write—to Jerome to be refuted, he not only found nothing of the kind in them, but even, for refuting certain vain things of his, brought forth this, as most certain, concerning the original sin of man—about which surely he believed not even that man doubted—among his many documents[^21].

These are the words of one doing this: *He who says*, he says, *that he abides in Christ ought himself to walk as he walked*[^22]. *Let the adversary choose of two what he will; we give him the option. Does he abide in Christ or not abide? If he abides, then let him walk as Christ. But if it is rash to promise the likeness of the Lord's virtues, he does not abide in Christ, because he does not go as Christ. He did no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth*[^23]; *who, when he was reviled, did not revile in return, and like a lamb before its shearer so he did not open his mouth; to whom the prince of this world came and found nothing in him*[^24]; *who, when he had done no sin, God made sin for us*[^25]. *But we, according to the letter of James, 'all sin in many things'*[^26] *and 'no one is clean from sins, not even if his life has been but of one day'*[^27]. *'For who will boast that he has a chaste heart? Or who will be confident that he is clean from sins?'*[^28]. *And we are held guilty in the likeness of the transgression of Adam*[^29]. *Hence David too: 'Behold,' he says, 'I was conceived in iniquities, and in trespasses my mother conceived me'*[^30].

## In Adam all men were that one man.

**7. 14.** I have recalled these things not because we rely on the opinions of any disputants as on canonical authority, but that it may appear that from the beginning until the present time, in which this novelty has arisen, this concerning original sin has been guarded in the faith of the Church with so great a constancy that by those who treated the dominical oracles it was brought forward as most certain for refuting other false things, rather than that it was attempted by anyone to be refuted as though it were false.

But in the holy canonical books the clearest and fullest authority of this opinion is in force. The Apostle cries out: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^31]. Hence neither can it be clearly said that the sin of Adam harmed even those who do not sin, since the Scripture says, *in whom all sinned.* Nor are these called another's sins as though they pertained not at all to little ones—since indeed all then sinned in Adam, when in his nature, by that implanted power by which he could beget them, all were still that one man—but they are called another's, because they themselves were not yet living their own lives, but the life of one man contained whatever was in the future progeny.

## God forgives sins, but to those regenerated by the spirit, not generated by the flesh.

**8. 15.** *It is by no reason conceded*, they say, *that God, who forgives proper sins, should impute another's.* He forgives, but to those regenerated by the spirit, not generated by the flesh; but he imputes not now another's, but their own. For they were another's when those who would bear them propagated were not yet; but now, by carnal generation, they are already the sins of those to whom, not yet by spiritual regeneration, they have been forgiven.

## How there can be original sin in those whom the baptized have begotten.

**8. 16.** But *if baptism*, they say, *cleanses that ancient trespass, those who are born of two baptized persons ought to be free of this sin. For they could not transmit to their posterity what they themselves did not have at all.* Behold whence error usually grows strong, when men are fit to ask these questions which they are not fit to understand.

For to what hearer, or in what words, may I explain how mortal, corrupted beginnings do not harm those who have been begun with other, immortal beginnings, and yet harm those whom those same persons—to whom they no longer harm—have generated from those same corrupted beginnings? How is a man to understand this, whose somewhat slow mind is hindered also by the prejudice of his own opinion and by the heaviest bond of obstinacy? Nevertheless, if I had undertaken this cause against those who altogether forbade little ones to be baptized, or contended that they were baptized superfluously—saying that, born of the faithful, they necessarily obtain the merit of their parents—then I ought perhaps to be stirred more laboriously and toilsomely to convict this opinion; then, if before dull and contentious men a difficulty of refuting falsehoods and persuading truths resisted me, on account of the obscurity of the nature of things, I would perhaps flee to those examples that are in common use and ready at hand, and in turn ask, so that—because it moved them how a sin which is cleansed through baptism remains in those whom the baptized have begotten—they themselves might explain how the foreskin which is taken away through circumcision remains in those whom the circumcised have begotten, how also the chaff which by human labor is separated with so great diligence remains in the fruit which is born of the cleansed wheat.

## As a non-Christian is born of Christians, so an uncleansed one is born of the cleansed.

**9. 17.** By these and such examples I would perhaps somehow try to persuade men who believed that the sacraments of cleansing were applied superfluously to the children of the cleansed, by what right counsel the little ones of the baptized are baptized, and how it can come about that a man who has both seeds—of death in the flesh and of immortality in the spirit—is not harmed, having been regenerated through the spirit, by what harms his son generated through the flesh, and that there is in this man, cleansed by forgiveness, what is also in that one, to be cleansed by a like forgiveness—as by circumcision, as by threshing and winnowing.

But as it is, since indeed we deal with those who confess that the children of the baptized are to be baptized, how much better do we deal with them thus, by saying: "You who assert that of men cleansed from the stain of sin children ought to have been born without sin, why do you not attend that in the same way it can be said to you that of Christian parents Christian children ought to have been born? Why, then, do you judge that they ought to be made Christians? Was not the body Christian in their parents, to whom it was said: *Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ*[^32]?

Or was the body indeed born Christian of Christian parents, but did not receive a Christian soul? But this is much more wonderful; for, whichever you hold concerning the soul—since surely with the Apostle you do not believe that it did anything good or evil before it was born[^33]—either it was drawn by transmission, and, like the body Christian of Christians, the soul too ought to have been Christian; or, created by Christ, whether in a Christian body or on account of a Christian body, it ought to be either created or sent Christian.

Unless perhaps you will say that Christian men could beget a Christian body, but that Christ himself could not procreate a Christian soul. Yield, therefore, to the truth, and see that, just as it could come about—which you too confess—that of Christians a non-Christian, of members of Christ a non-member of Christ, and (that we may meet also all who, though falsely, are nevertheless held by the name of some religion) of the consecrated a non-consecrated is born, so it also comes about that of the cleansed an uncleansed is born. What will you answer as to why of Christians a non-Christian is born, except that it is not generation but regeneration that makes Christians?

Render us, then, this reason: that likewise no one is cleansed from sins by being born, but all by being reborn. And by this, of men cleansed because reborn, the man who is born is reborn, that he too may be cleansed.

For parents could transmit to their posterity what they themselves did not have at all—not only as grain transmits chaff and the circumcised the foreskin, but also, as you yourselves say, the faithful transmit unbelief into their posterity; which is no longer of them, regenerated through the spirit, but the vice of mortal seed, by which they were generated in the flesh. For surely those little ones whom you judge are to be made faithful through the sacrament of the faithful, you do not deny were born unbelieving of faithful parents."

## The obscure question of the origin of the soul in relation to original sin.

**10. 18.** But indeed: *If the soul is not from transmission, but only the flesh, then it alone has the transmission of sin, and it alone deserves the penalty*—for this they hold—*saying that it is unjust that a soul born today, not from the mass of Adam, should bear so ancient a sin of another.* Attend, I beseech you, how the circumspect Pelagius—for from his book I transcribed these words which I just now set down—felt how difficult a question concerning the soul he was engaged in. For he does not say "because the soul is not from transmission," but *if the soul is not from transmission*, doing most rightly, concerning a matter so obscure—of which we can find in the holy Scriptures no certain and open testimonies, or can only with the greatest difficulty—to speak hesitantly rather than confidently.

Wherefore I too answer this proposition not by a hasty assertion: "If the soul is not from transmission, then what justice is this, that, newly created and altogether free from every trespass, utterly free from every contagion of sin, it should be compelled to bear in little ones the passions of the flesh and various tortures and—what is more horrible—even the assaults of demons? For the flesh suffers none of these things in such a way that the soul, which lives and feels, does not rather there pay the penalties. For if this is shown to be just, then it can likewise be shown by what justice it undergoes, even in the flesh of sin, the original sin to be cleansed by the sacrament of baptism and the compassion of grace. But if that cannot be shown, neither, I think, can this. Either, then, let us bear both as hidden and remember that we are men, or let us by arguing attempt, with sober caution, some other work concerning the soul, if it shall seem necessary."

## Outside the Church there is no salvation, but no one is within the Church without the sacraments.

**11. 19.** Now, however, let us so take that which the Apostle says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^34], that we may not be judged to resist, too foolishly and unhappily, so many and so great most open testimonies of the divine Scriptures, by which we are taught that, apart from the fellowship of Christ—which comes about in him and with him, when we are imbued with his sacraments and incorporated into his members—no one can obtain eternal life and salvation. For it was said to the Romans, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men*[^35], in no other sense than that in which it was said to the Corinthians, *By a man came death, and by a man the resurrection of the dead; for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^36]. For no one doubts that this was said there of the death of the body, since the question, with the Apostle's great intent, was turning upon the resurrection of the body. And therefore he seems there to have been silent about sin, because there was no question about righteousness; but here, to the Romans, he set down both and commended both at great length: sin in Adam, righteousness in Christ; and death in Adam, life in Christ. All these words of the apostolic discourse, as far as I could and as far as seemed sufficient, I searched out and opened, as I have already said, in the first of those two books.

## The death of the body too came about by the desert of sin.

**11. 20.** Although even there to the Corinthians he so concluded, at the end, that very place concerning the resurrection, long treated, that he did not allow us to doubt that the death of the body too came about by the desert of sin. For when he had said: *This corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. But when this corruptible has put on incorruption and this mortal immortality, then will come about*, he says, *the word that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?*—then he added: *But the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law*[^37].

Since, then, as the Apostle's most open words declare, death will be swallowed up in victory by the same means by which this corruptible and mortal will put on incorruption and immortality[^38]—that is, by which God will give life even to our mortal bodies, on account of his Spirit dwelling in us—it is manifest that the sting of this death of the body too, which is contrary to the resurrection of the body, was sin: the sting, that is, by which death was caused, not which death caused; for we die by sin, we do not sin by death.

And so the "sting of death" was said in the way "the tree of life"[^39] was—not what man's life would cause, but by which man's life would come about—and in the way "the tree of knowledge," through which man's knowledge would come about, not which man caused by his own knowledge. So, then, also the sting of death: by which death was caused, not which death caused. For so we say also "the cup of death," by which someone has died or could die, not which the dying or dead man made. The sting of death, then, is sin; by the prick of sin the human race was put to death.

Why do we still ask, of what death—whether of the soul or of the body? Whether of the first, by which we now all die, or of the second, by which the ungodly will then die[^40]? There is no cause for stirring up the question, no place for evasion; the Apostle's words, with which he was doing this, answer when questioned. *When this mortal*, he says, *has put on immortality, then will come about the word that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting? But the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law*[^41]. He was dealing with the resurrection of the body, that death will be swallowed up in victory when this mortal has put on immortality. Then death itself will be insulted, which will be swallowed up in victory by the resurrection of the body. Then it will be said to it: *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?* To the death of the body, then, this will be said. For this is what victorious immortality will swallow up, when this mortal is clothed with immortality. To the death of the body, I say, it will then be said: *Where, O death, is your victory?*—by which here you had so conquered all that even the Son of God contended with you and overcame you not by avoiding but by taking you up? You conquered in those dying, you were conquered in those rising. Your victory, by which you had swallowed up the bodies of the dying, was temporal; our victory, by which in the bodies of the rising you are swallowed up, will stand eternal.

*Where is your sting?*—that is, sin, by which we were pricked and poisoned, that you should come to be even in our bodies and possess them through so long an age? *But the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law*[^42]. We sinned in one, all of us, that we might die in one, all of us; we received the law, not that by amendment we might end sin, but that by transgression we might increase it. *For the law entered in, that sin might abound*; and, *Scripture has shut up all things under sin. But thanks be to God, who has given us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^43], so that, where sin abounded, grace abounded yet more[^44], and *that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^45], and we might conquer death by an immortal resurrection, and its sting, sin, by a free justification.

## The sense of 1 Corinthians 7:14.

**12. 21.** Let no one, then, be deceived about this matter or deceive others. This manifest sense of holy Scripture removes and takes away all ambiguities. As death is drawn from the origin in the body of this death, so sin too was drawn from the origin in this flesh of sin[^46]; for the healing of which—both drawn by propagation and increased by the will—and for the raising again of the very flesh, the physician came in the likeness of sinful flesh, who is not needed by the healthy but by the sick, and came not to call the just but sinners[^47].

Accordingly, what the Apostle says, when he admonished the faithful not to separate themselves from unbelieving spouses—*For the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving woman is sanctified in the brother; otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*[^48]—is either to be taken as we too elsewhere[^49] and Pelagius, when he treated the same letter to the Corinthians, expounded it,

namely, that examples had already gone before—both of husbands whom their wives, and of women whom their husbands, had gained for Christ, and of little ones for whose making into Christians the Christian will even of one parent had prevailed—or, if (which the Apostle's words rather seem to sound and in a certain way to compel) some sanctification is to be understood there, by which an unbelieving husband or wife was sanctified in a believing spouse, and by which the children of the faithful were born holy, whether because, whoever, man or woman, had learned this in the law, abstained from intercourse during the woman's menstrual flow

—for this Ezekiel places among those precepts that are not to be taken figuratively[^50]—or on account of any other sprinkling of holiness, not openly set down there, from the very bond of marriages and children: this nevertheless must be held without doubt, that, whatever that sanctification is, it does not avail to make Christians and to forgive sins, unless they are made faithful by Christian and ecclesiastical institution and the sacraments. For neither are unbelieving spouses, however much they cling to holy and just spouses, cleansed from the iniquity which drives those separated from the kingdom of God to come into condemnation; nor are little ones procreated of any holy and just persons absolved from the guilt of original sin, unless they have been baptized in Christ—for whom we ought to speak the more earnestly the less they themselves can speak for themselves.

## We are commanded to bring help to infants.

**13. 22.** For this is what that disputation does, against whose novelty we must strive by the ancient truth: it makes it seem that infants are baptized altogether superfluously. But this is not said openly, lest a custom of the Church so wholesomely established be unable to bear its violators. But if we are commanded to bring help to orphans, how much more ought we to labor for these, who will remain more destitute and more wretched than orphans, even under their parents, if the grace of Christ is denied them—which they cannot demand for themselves!

## On the perfection of man on earth.

**13. 23.** But as for what they say, that some men, already using the reason of their own will, have lived or live in this age without any sin, it is to be wished that it may come about, to be striven for that it may come about, to be prayed for that it may come about; yet it is not to be confessed as though it had come about. For to those wishing and striving and beseeching with worthy supplication, whatever of sins remains is daily loosed through this, that we truly say in prayer: *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^51]. Whoever says that this prayer was not necessary in this life for anyone—even a holy man, knowing and doing the will of God—except the one Holy One of holy ones, errs greatly and cannot at all please the very one whom he praises; but if he thinks himself such a one, he deceives himself and the truth is not in him, for no other reason than that he thinks what is false[^52].

That physician, then, who is not needed by the healthy but by the sick[^53], knows how by healing to perfect us unto eternal salvation—he who both does not take away in this age, from those to whom he forgives sins, even that death itself, although it was inflicted by the desert of sin, so that, by overcoming the fear of it too, they may undertake the contest for the sincerity of faith; and in certain things does not help even his just ones—since they can still be lifted up—to perfect righteousness, so that, while not every living person is justified in his sight[^54], we may always owe the giving of thanks to his indulgence, and thus be healed, by holy humility, of that first cause of all vices, that is, of the swelling of pride. While my plan was bringing forth this letter as a short one, a lengthy book was born—would that it were as perfect as it is at last at length finished!
