# Book Three

**3. 1. 1.** Thus far follow the things which they slanderously charge against us; they do not yet begin to weave together the things which they themselves think. But lest the length of the volumes give offense, we have divided these very things which they charge into two books; the earlier of these being finished, which is the second book of this whole work, we begin from here the other and join it, as the third, to the first and the second.

**3. 2. 2.** "We say"—they assert—"that the Law of the Old Testament was given not for this, that it might justify the obedient, but that it might become the cause of a graver sin." They utterly fail to understand what we say about the Law, because we say what the Apostle says, whom they do not understand. For who would say that those who are obedient to the Law are not justified, when, unless they were justified, they could not be obedient? But we say that it comes about through the Law that what God wills to be done is heard; but through grace that the Law is obeyed; *for not the hearers of the Law*, says the Apostle, *are just before God, but the doers of the Law will be justified*[^1]. The Law, then, makes hearers of righteousness, grace makes doers. *For what was impossible for the Law*, says the same Apostle, *in that it was weakened through the flesh, God, sending his own Son in the likeness of the flesh of sin and on account of sin, condemned sin in the flesh, that the righteousness of the Law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the spirit*[^2]. Behold what we say; let them pray that they may at some time understand, let them not wrangle that they may never understand. For it is impossible to fulfill the Law through the flesh, that is, through carnal presumption, by which the proud, *being ignorant of the righteousness of God*—that is, the righteousness which is from God for a man to be just—*and wishing to establish their own*, as though through their own choice, not helped divinely, the Law could be fulfilled, *are not subject to the righteousness of God*[^3]. Therefore the righteousness of the Law is fulfilled in those who walk not according to the flesh, that is, according to a man ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish his own, but who walk according to the spirit. But who walks according to the spirit except whoever is led by the Spirit of God? *For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are the sons of God*[^4]. Therefore *the letter kills, but the spirit gives life*[^5]. Nor is the letter evil because it kills, but it convicts the evil by their transgression. For *the Law is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good*. *Did then that which is good*, he says, *become death to me? God forbid! But sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good, that the sinner, or sin, might become through the commandment exceedingly sinful*[^6]. Behold what is meant by *the letter kills*. For *the sting of death is sin, but the power of sin is the Law*[^7]; for it increases by forbidding the desires of sin, and thence it kills, unless grace gives life by coming to help.

**3. 2. 3.** Behold what we say, behold the very thing they charge against us, that we so "say"—say they—"the Law was given that it might be the cause of a graver sin"; not hearing the Apostle saying: *For the Law works wrath; for where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^8]; and: *The Law was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made*[^9]; and: *If a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would indeed be entirely from the Law. But the Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe*[^10]. Hence it is that the Old Testament, from Mount Sinai, where the Law was given, begets unto slavery, which is Hagar. *But we*, he says, *are not the children of the slave woman, but of the free*[^11]. They, then, are not the children of the free woman who received the Law of the letter, by which they could be shown to be not only sinners but, besides, transgressors, but those who received the spirit of grace, by which the Law itself, holy and just and good, can be fulfilled. Behold what we say; let them attend and not contend, let them be enlightened and not slander.

**3. 3. 4.** *They also assert*, they say, *that baptism does not truly make men new, that is, does not give full remission of sins, but they contend that men become in part sons of God, but in part remain sons of the world, that is, of the devil*. They lie, they lie in wait, they shuffle; we do not say this. For we say that all men who are sons of the devil are also sons of the world; but we do not say that all sons of the world are also sons of the devil. For far be it that we should say that the holy fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and others of this kind, were sons of the devil, when they begot through marriage, and those faithful who up to now and henceforth still beget. Nor yet can we contradict the Lord saying: *The sons of this world marry and are given in marriage*[^12]. Some, then, are sons of this world and yet are not sons of the devil; for although the devil is the author and prince of all sins, yet not all sins make sons of the devil. For even the sons of God sin, since, if they say that they have no sin, they deceive themselves and the truth is not in them; but they sin by that condition by which they are still sons of this world; but by the grace by which they are sons of God, they surely do not sin, because everyone *who is born of God does not sin*[^13]. But it is unbelief that makes sons of the devil, which is properly called sin, as though it alone were sin, if it is not expressed what kind of sin it is—just as, when "the Apostle" is said, if it is not expressed which apostle, none is understood but Paul, because he is better known by his many letters and labored more than all those others. Hence what the Lord says about the Holy Spirit: *He will convict the world of sin*[^14], he wished to be understood as unbelief. For in expounding this he says: *Of sin indeed, because they did not believe in me*[^15]; and where he says: *If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin*[^16]. For they did not before lack sin, but he wished to be understood that very distrust by which they did not believe even him present and speaking, belonging to him of whom the Apostle says: *According to the prince of the power of the air, who now works in the sons of distrust*[^17]. Therefore those in whom there is no faith are sons of the devil, because they do not have in the inner man wherefore there should be forgiven them whatever is committed by human weakness or ignorance or by any evil will at all; but those are sons of God who surely, if they say that they have no sin, deceive themselves, and the truth is not in them—surely, as follows, "when they confess their sins—which the sons of the devil do not do, or do not do according to the faith which is proper to the sons of God—he is faithful and just to forgive them their sins and to cleanse them from all iniquity"[^18]. But that what we say may be more fully understood, let Jesus himself be heard, who was surely speaking to the sons of God, saying: *If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!*[^19] For if they were not sons of God, he would not say to them *your Father who is in heaven*. And yet he says that they are evil and know how to give good things to their children. Are they then evil whence they are sons of God? Far be it; but they are evil whence they are still sons of the world, yet already made sons of God by the pledge of the Holy Spirit.

**3. 3. 5.** Baptism, therefore, indeed washes away all sins—absolutely all, of deeds, words, thoughts, whether original or added, whether committed in ignorance or knowingly; but it does not take away the weakness against which the regenerate man resists when he wrestles the good fight, but to which he consents when, as a man, he is caught beforehand in some fault—rejoicing on account of the former in the giving of thanks, but groaning on account of the latter in the pleading of prayers; there saying, *What shall I render to the Lord for all the things that he has rendered to me?*[^20]; here saying, *Forgive us our debts*[^21]; on account of the former saying, *I will love you, Lord, my strength*[^22]; on account of the latter saying, *Have mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak*[^23]; on account of the former saying, *My eyes are ever toward the Lord, for he will pluck my feet out of the snare*[^24]; on account of the latter saying, *My eye is troubled because of anger*[^25]—and innumerable things with which the divine writings are full, which are said by the sons of God out of faith, in alternating voices, either exulting over the goods of God or grieving over our own evils, as long as they are still sons even of this world, on account of the weakness of this life—whom yet God distinguishes from the sons of the devil not only by the washing of regeneration but also by the uprightness of that faith itself which *works through love*[^26], *because the just man lives by faith*[^27]. But this weakness, with which we struggle until the death of the body, with failing and advancing alternating, and it greatly matters which overcomes in us, will be consumed by another regeneration, of which the Lord says: *In the regeneration, when the Son of man shall sit on the seat of his majesty, you also will sit upon twelve seats*[^28], and the rest. For in this place, with no one in doubt, he calls the final resurrection "regeneration," which Paul the Apostle too calls both adoption and redemption, saying: *But even we ourselves, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^29]. Were we not, through the holy washing, regenerated, adopted, redeemed? And yet there remains a regeneration, adoption, redemption which we ought now patiently to await as coming at the end, so that then we may in no part be any longer sons of this world. Whoever, therefore, detracts from baptism what we now receive through it corrupts faith; but whoever attributes to it now what indeed we will receive through it, yet afterward, cuts off hope. For if anyone asks me whether we have been saved through baptism, I cannot deny it, the Apostle saying: *He saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit*[^30]. But if he asks whether through the same washing he has already made us saved in every way whatever, I will answer: It is not so. For likewise the same Apostle says: *For by hope we have been saved. But hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he also hope for? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it with patience*[^31]. The salvation of man, then, was effected in baptism, because what he contracted of sin from his parents was remitted, or whatever even he himself sinned properly before baptism; but his salvation will afterward be so great that he can in no way sin.

**3. 4. 6.** These things being so, by them too those things which they next charge against us are refuted. For what Catholic says what they boast that we say, "that the Holy Spirit was not a helper of virtue in the Old Testament," except when we understand the Old Testament as the Apostle said, *begetting unto slavery from Mount Sinai*[^32]? But because in it the New was prefigured, those men of God who understood this then are shown, according to the distribution of the times, to be dispensers and bearers indeed of the Old Testament, but heirs of the New. Or shall we deny that he pertains to the New Testament who says: *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew an upright spirit within me*[^33]? Or he who says: *He set my feet upon a rock and directed my steps, and put into my mouth a new song, a hymn to our God*[^34]? Or him, before the Old Testament which is from Mount Sinai, the father of the faithful, of whom the Apostle says: *Brethren, I speak after the manner of men: yet a man's ratified testament no one annuls or adds to. The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his seed. He does not say: And to seeds, as in many, but as in one: And to your seed, which is Christ. But this I say*, he says, *the testament ratified by God the Law, which was made after four hundred and thirty years, does not annul, so as to make void the promise. For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise; but to Abraham God gave it through the promise*[^35].

**3. 4. 7.** Here surely, if we ask whether this testament, which he says, ratified by God, is not annulled by the Law which was made after four hundred and thirty years, is to be understood as New or Old, who would hesitate to answer "New"—but hidden in the prophetic recesses, until there should come the time when it would be revealed in Christ? For if we say "Old," what will that be, *begetting unto slavery from Mount Sinai*? For there the Law was made after four hundred and thirty years, by which Law he affirms that this testament of the promise of Abraham cannot be annulled; and what was done with Abraham he wishes rather to pertain to us, whom he wishes to be sons of the free woman, not of the slave, heirs by promise, not by Law, when he says: *For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise; but to Abraham God gave it through the promise*—so that the Law, which was made after four hundred and thirty years, entered in for this, that sin might abound; when, through the sin of transgression, the pride of a man presuming on his own righteousness is convicted, and *where sin abounded*, grace might *superabound*[^36] through the faith of a man now humble, failing in the Law and fleeing to the mercy of God. Therefore, when he had said, *For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise; but to Abraham God gave it through the promise*—as though it were said to him: To what end, then, was the Law afterward made?—he added and said: *What then is the Law?* To which question he soon replied: *It was set forth for the sake of transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made*[^37]. This same thing he says thus: *For if those who are of the Law are heirs, faith is made empty and the promise is made void. For the Law works wrath; for where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^38]. What he says in that testimony—*For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise; but to Abraham God gave it through the promise*—this he says in that one: *For if those who are through the Law are heirs, faith is made empty and the promise is made void*; showing sufficiently that what God gave to Abraham through the promise pertains to our faith, which surely is of the New Testament. And what he says in that testimony—*What then is the Law?*—and answered, *It was set forth for the sake of transgression*, this he likewise added in that one: *For the Law works wrath; for where there is no law, neither is there transgression*.

**3. 4. 8.** Whether, therefore, Abraham, or the just men before him, or those after him up to Moses himself, through whom was given the Testament begetting unto slavery from Mount Sinai, or the other prophets after him and the holy men of God up to John the Baptist, they are sons of the promise and of grace, according to Isaac the son of the free woman, heirs of God not from the Law but from the promise, and coheirs of Christ. For far be it that we should deny that just Noah, and the just men of an earlier time, and whoever from him to Abraham could be just, whether conspicuous or hidden, pertain to the heavenly Jerusalem, *which is our mother*[^39], although they are found to be earlier in time than Sarah, who bore the prophecy and figure of that free mother herself. How much more evidently, then, after Abraham, to whom that very promise was so declared that he was called the father of many nations, whoever pleased God are to be held sons of the promise! For from Abraham onward it is not a truer begetting of the just that is found, but a more manifest prophecy.

**3. 4. 9.** But to the Old Testament, which is *begetting unto slavery from Mount Sinai, which is Hagar*[^40], those belong who, when they have received the Law, holy and just and good, suppose that the letter can suffice them unto life; and therefore they do not seek the divine mercy whereby they may become doers of the Law, but, *being ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish their own by righteousness, are not subject to the righteousness of God*[^41]. Of this kind was that multitude which murmured against God in the wilderness and made an idol, and that which already, in the very land of promise, fornicated after strange gods. But this is, even in the Old Testament itself, a very reprobate multitude. Those too, whoever were there, following only the earthly things which God there promises and ignorant of what they themselves signify in regard to the New Testament, by love of obtaining them and fear of losing them kept the precepts of God—nay, did not keep them but seemed to themselves to keep them; for faith did not work in them through love, but earthly desire and carnal fear. But he who does the precepts thus does them without doubt unwillingly, and thereby does them not in his soul; for he would prefer not to do them at all, if, according to the things he desires and fears, he were permitted with impunity. And thereby in his very will he is guilty within, where he himself who commands, God, looks. Such were the sons of the earthly Jerusalem, of which the Apostle says: *For she is in slavery with her children*, pertaining to the Old Testament: *begetting unto slavery from Mount Sinai, which is Hagar*. Of that same kind were those who crucified the Lord and remained in the same unbelief. Thence too are still their sons, in the huge multitude of the Jews, although the New Testament has now, as was prophesied, been laid open and confirmed through the blood of Christ, and the Gospel spread abroad from the river where he was baptized and professed his teaching, even to the ends of the earth. These Jews, according to the prophecies which they read, are scattered everywhere through all lands, so that from their books too the testimony to Christian truth may not be lacking.

**3. 4. 10.** God therefore established the Old Testament too, because it pleased God, until the fullness of time, to veil the heavenly promises with earthly promises set up as a reward, and to give to a people gaping after earthly goods and therefore having a hard heart a Law that was nevertheless spiritual, yet on tables of stone. For, apart from the sacraments of the old Books, which were commanded for the sole purpose of signifying by reason—although in these too, since they are to be understood spiritually, the Law is rightly called spiritual—the rest, surely, which, pertaining to piety and good morals, are to be referred by no interpretation to any signification but to be done utterly as they are said, no one would doubt that that Law of God is necessary, not only for that people then but even now for us, for the right ordering of life. For if Christ has taken away from us that most heavy yoke of many observances—that we should not be circumcised carnally, not immolate victims of cattle, not rest from even necessary works on the sabbath, with the cycle of seven days returning, and other things of this kind, but should hold them understood spiritually, and, the signifying shadows removed, should keep watch in the light of the very things which are signified—shall we therefore say that what is written does not pertain to us, that whoever finds anything lost belonging to another should restore it to him who lost it, and many other similar things by which one learns to live piously and rightly, and especially the Decalogue itself, which is contained in those two tables of stone, except the carnal observance of the sabbath, which signifies spiritual sanctification and rest? For who would say that Christians ought not to observe that one God be served by the obedience of religion, that an idol be not worshiped, that the name of the Lord be not taken in vain, that parents be honored, that adulteries, murders, thefts, false testimonies be not perpetrated, that a wife, that anything at all of another's be not coveted? Who is so impious as to say that he does not keep these precepts of the Law for this reason, because he himself is a Christian and established not under the Law but under grace?

**3. 4. 11.** But there is plainly a great difference, in that those set under the Law, whom the letter kills, do these things either by desire of obtaining earthly happiness or by fear of losing it, and therefore do not truly do them, since carnal desire, by which sin is rather changed or increased by another desire, is not healed. These pertain to the Old Testament, which begets unto slavery; because carnal fear and desire make them slaves, not evangelical faith and hope and love free. But those set under grace, whom the Spirit gives life, do these things out of that faith which works through love, in hope of goods not carnal but spiritual, not earthly but heavenly, not temporal but eternal, especially believing in the Mediator, through whom they do not doubt that both the spirit of grace is ministered to them, that they may do these things well, and that pardon can be granted when they sin. These pertain to the New Testament, sons of the promise, regenerated by God the Father and the free mother. Of this kind were all the ancient just men and Moses himself, the minister of the Old Testament, heir of the New, because by the one and same faith by which we live they lived, believing the incarnation, passion, and resurrection of Christ would come, which we believe has come—up to John the Baptist himself, as a certain boundary of the past dispensation, who, signifying that the Mediator himself would come, not by any shadow of the future or allegorical signification or any prophetic foretelling, but pointing him out with his finger, said: *Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sin of the world*[^42]; as though saying: "He whom many just men longed to see, in whom, as about to come, they believed from the very beginning of the human race, about whom the promises were spoken to Abraham, about whom Moses wrote, of whom the Law and the Prophets are witnesses: Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sin of the world." From this John onward there began to be done concerning Christ, as past or present, the things which by all those just men of the earlier time were believed, hoped, desired as future. The same faith, therefore, is both in those who before were Christians not yet in name but in the reality itself, and in these who not only are but are also called so, and in both the same grace through the Holy Spirit. Whence the Apostle says: *But having the same spirit of faith, according to what is written: I believed, therefore I have spoken, we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^43].

**3. 4. 12.** Thus, the custom of speaking now prevailing, the Old Testament is called in one way—the Law and all the Prophets who prophesied up to John (which is more distinctly called the old instrument than the Old Testament)—and in another way as the apostolic authority calls it, whether expressing this name or signifying it. For he expresses it where he says: *Even to this present day, as long as Moses is read, the same veil remains in the reading of the Old Testament, which is not unveiled, because in Christ it is done away*[^44]. For thus surely he referred the Old Testament to the ministry of Moses. Likewise he says: *That we may serve in newness of spirit and not in oldness of the letter*[^45], signifying that same Testament by the name of *letter*. Likewise in another place: *Who also made us fit*, he says, *ministers of the New Testament, not of the letter but of the spirit; for the letter kills, but the spirit gives life*[^46]. And here, by mention of the New, he surely wished that Old to be understood. But much more evidently—although he did not say either Old or New—he distinguished those two Testaments by the two sons of Abraham, one by the slave woman, the other by the free, which we have already recalled above. For what is more express than that he should say: *Tell me, you who wish to be under the Law, have you not heard the Law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave woman and one by the free; but he indeed who was of the slave woman was born according to the flesh, but he who was of the free, by promise. These things are in an allegory; for these are the two Testaments, one indeed from Mount Sinai, begetting unto slavery, which is Hagar—for Sinai is a mountain in Arabia, which is joined to that which is now Jerusalem; for she is in slavery with her children—but the Jerusalem which is above is free, who is our mother*[^47]. What is clearer, what more certain, what more removed from all obscurity and ambiguity? And a little later: *But we, brethren*, he says, *according to Isaac are sons of the promise*[^48]. Likewise a little later: *But we, brethren, are not children of the slave woman but of the free; by which freedom Christ has set us free*[^49]. Let us choose, then, whether we say that the ancient just men were sons of the slave woman or of the free. But far be it that they were of the slave woman; therefore, if of the free, they pertain to the New Testament in the Holy Spirit, whom, as giving life, the Apostle sets against the killing letter. For in what way do those not pertain to the grace of the New Testament, from whose sayings and Books we, by refuting, convict these most senseless and most ungrateful enemies of that same grace?

**3. 4. 13.** But someone will say: "How is that called old which was made through Moses after four hundred and thirty years, and that called new which was made so many years before, to Abraham?" Let him who is moved by this not contentiously but studiously understand, first, that if it is called old from the earlier time and new from the later, their revelations are considered in these names, not their institutions. For through Moses was revealed the Old Testament, through whom the Law was given, holy and just and good, by which there came about not the abolition but the knowledge of sin; by which the proud were convicted, wishing to establish their own righteousness as though not needing divine help, and, made guilty by the letter, fled to the spirit of grace, to be justified not by their own righteousness but by God's, that is, by the one which was theirs from God. For, as the same Apostle says: *Through the Law comes the knowledge of sin. But now the righteousness of God has been manifested without the Law, attested by the Law and the Prophets*[^50]. For the Law, by this very fact that in it no one is justified, bears witness to the righteousness of God. *For that in the Law no one is justified before God is manifest, because the just man lives by faith*[^51]. So, then, when the Law does not justify the ungodly man convicted of transgression, it sends him to the justifying God, and thus bears witness to the righteousness of God; but the Prophets bear witness to the righteousness of God by foretelling Christ, *who was made for us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification and redemption, that, as it is written: He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^52]. But this law was hidden from the beginning, when nature itself convicted unjust men as they did to others what they would not have wished done to themselves. But the revelation of the New Testament was made in Christ, when he *was manifested in the flesh*[^53]; in whom appeared the righteousness of God, that is, the one which is for men from God. For thus he says: *But now the righteousness of God has been manifested without the Law*. Behold for what cause that is called the Old Testament, because it was revealed in an earlier time, and this the New, because in a later. Then, because the Old Testament pertains to the old man, from whom a man must necessarily begin, but the New to the new man, into whom a man ought to pass out of his oldness, therefore in the former are earthly promises, in the latter heavenly promises; because this too pertained to the mercy of God, lest anyone should suppose that even earthly happiness, of whatever kind, could be conferred on anyone except by the Lord, the creator of the universe. But if God is worshiped for the sake of that, it is a servile worship pertaining to the sons of the slave woman; but if for the sake of God himself, that in eternal life *God may be all in all*[^54], it is a free service pertaining to the sons of the free woman, who is our eternal mother in heaven. She, who first appeared as it were barren, when she did not have conspicuous sons, now we see what was prophesied of her: *Rejoice, you barren one who do not bear; break forth and cry out, you who do not travail, for many are the children of the desolate, more than of her who has a husband*[^55]—that is, "more than of that Jerusalem which is in a way wedded by the bond of the Law and is in slavery with her children." In the time of the Old Testament, therefore, we say that the Holy Spirit was, in those who even then were sons of the promise according to Isaac, not only a helper—which these men suppose suffices for their dogma—but even a bestower of virtue, which these men deny, attributing it rather to free choice, against those fathers who knew how to cry out to the Lord with truthful piety: *I will love you, Lord, my strength!*[^56]

**3. 5. 14.** They also say: "That all the Apostles or Prophets are defined by us as not fully holy, but that we say they were less evil in comparison with worse men, and that this is the righteousness to which God bears witness, so that, just as the Prophet says Sodom was justified in comparison with the Jews, so too we say that the saints exercised some virtue in comparison with criminals." Far be it that we should say these things; but either they are not able to understand, or they are unwilling to notice, or out of zeal for slandering they dissemble that they know what we say. Let them hear, then, either themselves, or rather those whom they are striving to deceive as unlearned and uninstructed simple folk. Our faith, that is, the Catholic faith, distinguishes the just from the unjust not by the law of works but by that of faith itself, because *the just man lives by faith*[^57]. By which distinction it comes about that a man leading a life without murder, without theft, without false testimony, without coveting of any other's thing, rendering due honor to his parents, chaste even to continence from all intercourse whatever, even conjugal, most generous in almsgiving, most patient of injuries, who not only does not take away what belongs to others but does not even demand back his own that has been taken, or even, with all his goods sold and distributed to the poor, possesses nothing of his own and proper—yet with these of his, as it were praiseworthy, morals, if he does not hold a right and Catholic faith in God, departs from this life to be condemned. But another, having indeed good works from a right faith, which works through love, yet not so well-mannered as that one, supports his incontinence by the honorableness of marriage, both renders and demands the carnal debt of wedlock, and that not for the cause of propagation alone but also of pleasure, although he lies only with his wife, which the Apostle concedes to the married by way of pardon; receives injuries not so patiently, but, angry, is carried away by desire of avenging, although, that he may be able to say *As we also forgive our debtors*[^58], when asked, he forgives; possesses a family estate, making from it indeed alms, yet not so generous as that one; does not take away what belongs to others, but demands back his own, though by an ecclesiastical, not however a forensic, judgment—surely this man, who in morals seems inferior to that one, on account of the right faith which is his in God, by which he lives, and according to which in all his faults he accuses himself, in all his good works praises God, attributing to himself disgrace, to him glory, and receiving from him both the forgiveness of sins and the love of right deeds, departs to be set free from this life and received into fellowship with those who will reign with Christ. Why, except on account of faith? Which, although without works it saves no one (for it is the faith not reprobate which works through love), yet through it even sins are loosed, because *the just man lives by faith*; but without it even those works which seem good are turned into sins; for *whatever is not of faith is sin*[^59]. And it comes about, on account of this greatest difference, that, while with no one in doubt persevering virginal integrity is more excellent than conjugal chastity, yet even a Catholic woman twice married is preferred to a heretical woman who has professed virginity; nor is she so preferred that this one is better in the kingdom of God, but that the other is not there at all. For even that man whom we described as of, as it were, better morals, if a right faith is present to him, surpasses that other; but both will be there; but if faith is lacking to him, he is so surpassed by the other that he himself is not there.

**3. 5. 15.** Since, therefore, all the just, both the more ancient ones and the Apostles, lived by a right faith, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord, and had morals so holy together with faith that, although they could not be of so perfect a virtue in this life as is to be after this life, yet whatever of sin crept in from human weakness was at once blotted out by the piety of that faith itself—whence can it come about that, in comparison with the unjust, whom God will condemn, they ought to be said to have been just, when by their pious faith they are so far removed to the contrary from those ungodly that the Apostle cries: *What part has the believer with the unbeliever?*[^60]? But evidently the new Pelagian heretics seem to themselves to be religious lovers and praisers of the saints, if they do not dare to say that they were of imperfect virtue, although the Vessel of election confesses this, who, considering where he still was and that the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul: *Not that I have already received*, he says, *or am already perfect, brethren; I do not reckon that I myself have laid hold of it*[^61]. And yet a little later, he who had denied that he was perfect says: *Let us, then, as many as are perfect, be of this mind*[^62]; that he might show that, according to the measure of this life, there is a certain perfection, and that to that perfection this too is reckoned, if each has come to know that he is not yet perfect. For what was more excellent in the old people than the holy priests? And yet God commanded them first to offer sacrifice for their own sins. And what is holier in the new people than the Apostles? And yet the Lord commanded them to say in prayer: *Forgive us our debts*[^63]. Of all the pious, therefore, groaning under this burden of corruptible flesh and in the weakness of this life, there is one hope: that *we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just; and he is the propitiation for our sins*[^64].

**3. 6. 16.** This advocate those men do not have who are removed far to the contrary from the just—even if this alone were the difference. Far be it that we should say, as they slander, that this just advocate "lied out of the necessity of the flesh," but we say that he, *in the likeness of the flesh of sin, condemned sin on account of sin*[^65]. Which, perhaps, these men, not understanding and blinded by desire of slandering, ignorant in how diverse ways the name of sin is wont to be put in the holy Scriptures, boast that we affirm a sin of Christ. We say, then, that Christ both had no sin, neither in soul nor in flesh, and, by taking on flesh, *in the likeness of the flesh of sin condemned sin on account of sin*. This, said somewhat obscurely by the Apostle, is resolved in two ways: either because the likenesses of things are wont to be called by the names of those things to which they are like, so that the Apostle is understood to have wished to call the very likeness of the flesh of sin "sin"; or because in the Law the sacrifice for sins was called sins, all of which were figures of the flesh of Christ, which is the true and only sacrifice for sins—not only for those which are all washed away in baptism, but also for those which afterward creep in from the weakness of this life. On account of which the whole Church daily cries to God in prayer: *Forgive us our debts*[^66], and they are forgiven us through the singular sacrifice for sins, which the Apostle, speaking according to the Law, did not hesitate to call *sin*. Whence is also that much more evident saying of his, uncertain by no crossroad of anyone's ambiguity: *We beseech you on Christ's behalf, be reconciled to God. Him who knew no sin he made sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in him*[^67]. For as to what I recalled above, *he condemned sin on account of sin*—because it was not said "on account of his own sin"—someone could so understand it as to say that he condemned sin on account of the sin of the Jews, because it came about, on account of the sin of those who crucified him, that he poured out his blood for the remission of sins; but this, where it is said that God made Christ himself, who knew no sin, to be sin, seems to me to be understood no more fittingly than that Christ was made a sacrifice for sins and on this account was called *sin*.

**3. 7. 17.** But who could endure them charging against us "that we say such advances will be in the future after the resurrection that men will there begin to fulfill the commandments of God which here they were unwilling to fulfill," since we say that there will be there no sin at all, nor any conflict with any desire of sin—as though they themselves dared to deny this? Who too will deny that wisdom and the knowledge of God will then be perfected in us, and in the Lord so great an exultation that it is full and true security, unless he be so averse to the truth that on this account he cannot attain to it? But these things will not be in the precepts, but in the reward of the precepts which here are to be observed. The contempt of which precepts does not lead there to the reward; but here zeal for keeping the precepts the grace of God bestows. And if anything even in these precepts is less kept, he pardons it; on account of which in prayer we say both *Your will be done*, and *Forgive us our debts*[^68]. Here, then, is the precept, that we sin not; there the reward, not to be able to sin; here is the precept, that we obey not the desires of sin; there the reward, that we have not the desires of sin; here is the precept: *Understand, then, you who are foolish among the people, and be wise at last, you fools*[^69]; there the reward is full wisdom and perfect knowledge—*For now we see through a mirror in an enigma*, says the Apostle, *but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known*[^70]; here is the precept: *Exult in God our helper*[^71], and *Exult, you just, in the Lord*[^72]; there the reward is to exult with a perfect and ineffable joy; lastly, in the precept it is set down: *Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness*; but in the reward: *for they shall be satisfied*[^73]. With what, I ask, will they be satisfied except with what they hunger and thirst for? Who, then, is so abhorrent not only from the divine but from human sense as to say that there can be in a man so great a righteousness, while it is hungered and thirsted for by him, as there will be when he will be satisfied with it? But when we hunger and thirst for righteousness, if the faith of Christ is awake in us, what is to be believed but that we hunger and thirst for Christ? *Who was made for us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification and redemption, that, as it is written: He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^74]. And because now, not seeing him, we believe in him, therefore we thirst and hunger for righteousness. For as long as *we are in the body, we are absent from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight*[^75]. When we shall see him, coming surely to sight, we shall exult with unspeakable joy, and then we shall be satisfied with righteousness, because now we say to him in pious desire: *I shall be satisfied when your glory shall be manifested*[^76].

**3. 7. 18.** But how—I do not say impudent, but insane—is the pride of one not yet equal to the angels of God and yet thinking that he can already have a righteousness equal to the angels of God, and not perceiving so great and holy a man, who surely hungered and thirsted for that very perfection of righteousness, when by the greatness of revelations he was unwilling to be lifted up; and yet, that he might not be lifted up, he was not committed to his own choice and will, but received a sting of the flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet him. On account of which he asked the Lord three times that it might depart from him, and the Lord said to him: *My grace is sufficient for you; for power is made perfect in weakness*[^77]. What power, except that to which it pertains not to be lifted up? And who doubts that this pertains to righteousness? With the perfection of this righteousness, then, are endowed the angels of God, who always see the face of the Father, and thereby of the whole Trinity, because through the Son they see in the Holy Spirit. But nothing is more sublime than that revelation; and yet none of the angels rejoicing in that contemplation has need of an angel of Satan by whom he may be buffeted, lest so great a greatness of revelation lift him up. This perfection of virtue the apostle Paul surely did not yet have, not yet equal to the angels of God, but there was in him the weakness of lifting himself up, which had to be repressed even through an angel of Satan, lest he be lifted up by the greatness of revelations. Although, therefore, that first elation cast down Satan himself, yet that supreme physician, who knows how to use well even evils, applied from an angel of Satan, against the vice of elation, a wholesome though troublesome medicine, as an antidote is wont to be made even from serpents against the venoms of serpents. What, then, is *My grace is sufficient for you*, except "lest, failing, you sink under the buffet of the angel of Satan"? And what is *Power is made perfect in weakness*, except that in this place of weakness the perfection of virtue can be so far only that, with weakness itself present, elation is repressed?—which weakness surely will be healed by future immortality. For how can it be called full health, where even against the buffet of the angel of Satan medicine is still necessary?

**3. 7. 19.** From this it has come about that virtue, which is now in a just man, is called perfect only so far that to its perfection there pertains even both the knowledge in truth and the confession in humility of its own imperfection. For then this small righteousness is, according to this weakness, perfect for its own measure, when it understands also what is lacking to it. And therefore the Apostle calls himself both imperfect and perfect; imperfect, namely, in thinking how much is lacking to him for righteousness, the fullness of which he still hungers and thirsts for; but perfect in that he is not ashamed to confess his own imperfection and advances well that he may arrive—just as we can say that a wayfarer is perfect whose advance is well promoted, although his aim is not perfected unless the arrival has been made. Therefore, when he had said *According to the righteousness which is in the Law, having been blameless*, he soon added: *The things that were gains to me, these I have counted loss for the sake of Christ. But indeed I count all things to be loss on account of the eminent knowledge of Christ Jesus our Lord, for the sake of whom I counted all things not only detriments but even reckoned them to be dung, that I may gain Christ and be found in him, not having my own righteousness which is from the Law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is from God in faith*[^78]. Behold, the Apostle says, surely not falsely, that he was blameless according to the righteousness which is from the Law; and yet these things which were gains to him he casts away for the sake of Christ and counts as losses, detriments, and dung—not only these, but all the rest which he recalled above—on account of not just any knowledge but the *eminent*, as he himself says, *knowledge of Christ Jesus our Lord*, which without doubt he still had in faith, not yet in sight. For then will the knowledge of Christ be eminent, when he will be so revealed that what is believed is seen. Whence in another place he says thus: *For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ shall appear, your life, then you too shall appear with him in glory*[^79]. Hence the Lord himself: *He who loves me*, he says, *will be loved by my Father, and I will love him and will manifest myself to him*[^80]. Hence John the Evangelist: *Beloved, we are the sons of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be. We know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is*[^81]. Then will the knowledge of Christ be eminent; for now it is indeed hidden in faith, but does not yet appear eminent in sight.

**3. 7. 20.** Blessed Paul, therefore, casts away those past things of his righteousness as detriments and dung, that he may gain Christ and be found in him, not having his own righteousness which is from the Law. Why "his own," if it is from the Law? For that Law surely is not other than God's. Who said this except Marcion and Mani and other similar plagues? Since, then, that Law is God's, he says that the righteousness which is from the Law is his own; which righteousness of his own he was unwilling to have, but cast it away as dung. Why so, except because this is what we also demonstrated above: that they are under the Law who, being ignorant of the righteousness of God and wishing to establish their own, are not subject to the righteousness of God? For they suppose that by the strength of their own choice they fulfill the Law that commands, and, entangled in this pride, are not turned to the grace that helps. So the letter kills them, either openly, by their not doing what it commands, also making themselves guilty; or by their supposing that they do, what they do not do, with the spiritual love which is from God. Thus they remain either openly unjust or falsely just, in open iniquity evidently dashed down, in false righteousness foolishly lifted up. And thereby in a wondrous way indeed, yet a true one, the righteousness of the Law is not fulfilled by the righteousness which is in the Law, or from the Law, but by the one which is in the spirit of grace. For the righteousness of the Law is fulfilled in those, as it is written, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the spirit. But the Apostle says that he was blameless according to the righteousness which is in the Law, in the flesh, not in the spirit, and he says that the righteousness which is from the Law was his own, not God's. It is to be understood, therefore, that the righteousness of the Law is not fulfilled according to the righteousness which is in the Law, or from the Law, that is, according to the righteousness of man, but according to the righteousness which is in the spirit of grace; therefore according to the righteousness of God, that is, the one which is for a man from God. Which can be said more plainly and briefly thus: that the righteousness of the Law is not fulfilled when the Law commands and a man does it as though by his own strength, but when the spirit helps and a man's will, not free but freed by the grace of God, does it. The righteousness of the Law, then, is to command what pleases God, to forbid what displeases; but the righteousness which is in the Law is to serve the letter and to seek outside it no help of God for living rightly. For when he had said *not having my own righteousness which is from the Law, but that which is through the faith of Christ*, he added: *which is from God*. That, then, is the righteousness of God, which the proud, being ignorant, wish to establish as their own. For it is not called the righteousness of God because God is just by it, but because it is for a man from God.

**3. 7. 21.** But according to this righteousness of God, that is, the one which is for us from God, faith now works through love. And it works this: how a man may arrive at him in whom, not now seeing him, he believes. When he shall see him, then what was in faith through a mirror in an enigma will be in sight face to face; then love itself too will be perfected. For it is said too foolishly that God is loved as much before he is seen as he will be loved when he will be seen. Furthermore, if in this life, with no pious person in doubt, the more we love God, so much the more surely are we more just, who doubts that pious and true righteousness, when the love of God shall be perfect, will then be perfected? Then, therefore, the Law, so that absolutely nothing at all is lacking, will be fulfilled, of which *Law*, according to the Apostle, *love is the fullness*[^82]. And thereby, when he had said *not having my own righteousness which is from the Law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is from God in faith*, he then added: *to the knowing of him and the power of his resurrection and the fellowship of his sufferings*[^83]. All these things were not yet full and perfect in the Apostle, but, as one set on the way, he was running to their fullness and perfection. For how had he already perfectly come to know Christ, who says in another place *Now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known*[^84]? And how had he already perfectly come to know the power of his resurrection, when it remained for him to come to know it more fully by experiencing it at the time of the resurrection of the flesh? And how had he already perfectly come to know the fellowship of his sufferings, not yet having experienced for him the suffering of death? Finally he adds and says: *If in any way I may attain to the resurrection of the dead*[^85], and then he says: *Not that I have already received or am already perfect*[^86]. What, then, does he confess he has not yet received, and in what is he not yet perfect, except in that righteousness which is from God, which, desiring it, he was unwilling to have his own, which is from the Law? For from here he was speaking, and this was the cause that he said these things, resisting the enemies of the grace of God, for the bestowing of which Christ was crucified; of whose kind these men too are.

**3. 7. 22.** For from the place where he undertook to say these things, he thus began: *Beware of the dogs, beware of the evil workers, beware of the mutilation. For we are the circumcision, who serve God in the spirit*—or, as some manuscripts have, *who serve God in spirit*, or *the Spirit of God*—*and glory in Christ Jesus, and put no confidence in the flesh*[^87]. Hence it is manifest that he is dealing against the Jews, who, observing the Law carnally and wishing to establish their own righteousness, were killed by the letter, were not given life by the spirit, and gloried in themselves, while the Apostles and all the sons of the promise gloried in Christ. Then he added: *Although I might have confidence even in the flesh. If any other thinks he has confidence in the flesh, I more*[^88]. And, enumerating all the things which have glory according to the flesh, he ended at this, where he says: *According to the righteousness which is in the Law, having been blameless*[^89]. And when he had said that these things had been to him altogether losses and detriments and dung, that he might gain Christ, he added what is in question: *And be found in him, not having my own righteousness which is from the Law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is from God*[^90]. The perfection, then, of this righteousness, which will be only in that eminent knowledge of Christ, on account of which he said all things were loss to him, he confessed he had not yet received, and therefore was not yet perfect. *But I follow on*, he says, *if I may lay hold of that for which I was laid hold of by Christ Jesus*[^91]. Such is *I may lay hold of that for which I was laid hold of* as is *I shall know even as I am known*. *Brethren*, he says, *I do not reckon that I myself have laid hold of it. But one thing—forgetting the things that are behind, and stretching forth to the things that are before, according to my aim I follow on to the prize of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus*[^92]. The order of the words is *but one thing I follow on*. About which one thing it is well understood that the Lord too admonished Martha, where he says: *Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things. But one thing is necessary*[^93]. This, wishing to lay hold of it, as one set on the way, he said that he followed on to the prize of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus. But who would hesitate that, when he has laid hold of what he asserts that he follows on to, he will then have a righteousness equal to the righteousness of the holy angels, none of whom, surely, an angel of Satan buffets, lest he be lifted up by the greatness of revelations? Then, admonishing those who might suppose themselves already perfect by the fullness of that righteousness: *Let us, then, as many as are perfect*, he says, *be of this mind*—as though he said: "If, according to the capacity of mortal man, we are perfect for the measure of this life, let us understand that this too pertains to that very perfection, that we are of this mind, that we are not yet perfect with respect to that angelic righteousness which will be ours in the manifestation of Christ"—*And if you are of a different mind in anything*, he says, *this too God will reveal to you*[^94]. How, except to those walking and advancing on the way of the right faith, until this pilgrimage is finished and one comes to sight? Whence he consequently added: *Nevertheless, in that to which we have come, let us walk*[^95]. Then he concludes, that those may be guarded against from whom this passage of his discourse took its beginning: *Be imitators of me, brethren*, he says, *and observe those who so walk as you have us for a model. For many walk, of whom I often told you, and now tell you even weeping, as the enemies of the cross of Christ, whose end is destruction*[^96], and the rest. These are the very ones of whom, beginning, he had said: *Beware of the dogs, beware of the evil workers*, and what follows. All, then, are enemies of the cross of Christ who, wishing to establish their own righteousness which is from the Law, that is, with the letter only commanding, not the spirit fulfilling, are not subject to the righteousness of God. *For if those who are heirs through the Law, faith is made empty*[^97]. *If righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died in vain*[^98], *therefore the stumbling-block of the cross is done away*[^99]. And thereby those are the enemies of his cross who say that righteousness is through the Law, to which it pertains to command, not to help. But the grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ, in the Holy Spirit, helps our weakness.

**3. 7. 23.** Wherefore he who lives according to the righteousness which is in the Law, without faith in the grace of Christ, as the Apostle recalls that he lived blamelessly, is to be thought to have no true righteousness—not because the Law is not true and holy, but because to wish to obey the commanding letter, without the life-giving Spirit of God, as it were by the strength of free choice, is not true righteousness. But the righteousness according to which the just man lives by faith, since it is for a man from God through the spirit of grace, is true righteousness. Which, although it is not undeservedly called perfect in some just men according to the capacity of this life, yet is small compared with that great righteousness which the equality of the angels receives. He who did not yet have this both called himself perfect on account of the one which was already in him and imperfect on account of the one which was still lacking. But plainly that lesser righteousness makes merit, that greater becomes the reward. Whence he who does not follow on to the former does not attain to the latter. Therefore to deny the fullness of righteousness to come after the future resurrection of man, and to suppose that there will be only so much righteousness in the body of that life as can be in the body of this death, is of singular madness. But it is most true that men do not there begin to fulfill the commandments of God which here they were unwilling to fulfill. For there will be the fullness of a most perfect righteousness—not, however, of men pursuing commandments and striving by advancing to that fullness, but in the twinkling of an eye, just as the resurrection of the dead itself is to be, since that greatness of perfect righteousness will be given as a reward to those who here did the commandments, not also itself commanded to be done. But I would so say "did the commandments" that we remember that the very commandments pertain to the prayer in which daily the holy sons of the promise say truly both *Your will be done*, and *Forgive us our debts*[^100].

**3. 8. 24.** When, therefore, the Pelagians are pressed by these and similar testimonies and voices of the truth, that they may not deny original sin; that they may not say that the grace of God, by which we are justified, is given not freely but according to our merits; that they may not say that in mortal man, however holy and well-doing, there is so great a righteousness that the remission of sins is not necessary for him even after the washing of regeneration, until he ends this life—therefore, when they are pressed that they may not say these three things, and through these alienate from the grace of the Savior the men who believe them, and persuade men, lifted up into pride, to go headlong into the judgment of the devil, they send in the fogs of other questions, in which their impiety may lurk among simpler men, whether the more sluggish or the less learned in the holy writings. These are the fogs "about the praise of the creature, the praise of marriage, the praise of the Law, the praise of free choice, the praise of the saints," as though any of us found fault with these, and did not rather proclaim them with due praises in honor of the Creator and Savior. But neither does the creature wish to be so praised that it does not wish to be healed; and the more marriage is to be praised, the less is to be imputed to it the shameful concupiscence of the flesh, which is not from the Father but is from the world. Which surely marriage found in men, did not make, because both that concupiscence is in very many without marriage, and marriage itself, had no one sinned, could have been without it. And the Law, holy and just and good, is not itself grace, and nothing is rightly done from it without grace, because it was not given as one that could give life, but was set down for the sake of transgression, that it might shut up the convicted under sin, and that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe. And free choice, taken captive, avails only for sin, but for righteousness it does not avail unless it is divinely freed and helped. And thereby all the saints too, whether from that ancient Abel up to John the Baptist, or from the Apostles themselves up to this time, and henceforth up to the end of the world, are to be praised in the Lord, not in themselves, because both the voice of those earlier ones is *In the Lord shall my soul be praised*[^101], and the voice of these later ones is *By the grace of God I am what I am*[^102]. And to all pertains *That he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^103], and the confession is common to all: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us*[^104].

**3. 9. 25.** But since in these five matters which I have set forth, in which they seek lurking-places and out of which they weave slanders, they are exposed and overcome by the divine documents, they have devised to deter, by the detestable name of the Manichees, such unskilled persons as they could, lest these lend ears to the truth against their most perverse dogmas—because, namely, the Manichees condemn by blaspheming the first three of these five, saying that neither the human creature nor marriage nor the Law was instituted by the most high and true God. But they do not accept what the truth says, that sin took its beginning from free choice and that from it is every evil, whether of angel or of man, because, straying too far from God, they preferred to believe the nature of evil always evil and coeternal with God. The holy Patriarchs and Prophets too they pursue with as great execrations as they can. Behold whence the new heretics think, by putting forward the name of the Manichees, to escape the bond of the truth; but they do not escape. For it pursues them and at once overthrows both the Manichees and the Pelagians. For man, while he is born, because he is something good, insofar as he is a man, refutes the Manichee and praises the Creator; but insofar as he contracts original sin, he refutes Pelagius and has a necessary Savior. For the fact too that this nature is said to be healed strikes back at both, because it would not need medicine if it were healthy—which is against Pelagius—nor could it be healed at all if it were an eternal and unchangeable evil—which is against the Manichee. Likewise, that we do not say that the concupiscence of the flesh is to be imputed to marriage, which we praise as instituted by the Lord, is both against the Pelagians, who place it among things to be praised, and against the Manichees, who attribute it to an alien evil nature, when it is an evil accidental to our nature, not to be separated by a sundering of God but to be healed by the mercy of God. Likewise, that we say the Law, holy and just and good, was set down for the sake of transgression, not to justify the ungodly but to convict the proud, is both against the Manichees, because, according to the Apostle, it is praised, and against the Pelagians, because, according to the Apostle, no one is justified by the Law, and therefore the spirit of grace freely helps to give life to those whom the letter kills, that is, whom the good Law, commanding, makes guilty by transgression. Likewise, that we say free choice, free in evil, is to be set free by the grace of God for doing good, is against the Pelagians; but that we say there arose from it that which before was not, an evil, is against the Manichees. Likewise, that we honor the holy Patriarchs and Prophets with due praises in God is against the Manichees. But that we say that even to them, although just and pleasing to God, the propitiation of the Lord was necessary, is against the Pelagians. The Catholic faith, then, finds both, like the other heretics, to be adversaries, and convicts both by the authority of the divine testimonies and the light of the truth.

**3. 10. 26.** The Pelagians, to be sure, add to the fogs of their lurking-places a question about the origin of the soul, not necessary, to this end: that by disturbing manifest matters with the obscurity of other matters they may contrive a place for hiding. For they say: "We hold the transmission of souls together with the transmission of sin." Where and when they heard this in the discourses, or read it in the writings, of those who defend the Catholic faith against them, I do not know; because, even though I find something written by Catholics about this matter, the defense of the truth had not yet been undertaken against these men, nor was it being done that an answer might be made to them. But this I say: that original sin is so manifest according to the holy Scriptures, and that it is remitted by the washing of regeneration in little ones is confirmed by so great an antiquity and authority of the Catholic faith, so well known by the clear celebration of the Church, that whatever is discussed, by anyone's inquiry or affirmation, about the origin of the soul, if it be against this, cannot be true. Wherefore whoever, whether about the soul or about any obscure matter, builds up that whereby he may destroy this which is most true, most well-founded, most well-known, whether he be a son or an enemy of the Church, is either to be corrected or to be guarded against. But let this be the end of this volume, that what follows may have another beginning.
