# Book One

**1. 1. 1.** I had indeed come to know you through the widespread proclamation of your renown, and from very frequent and most truthful reports I had learned how full you were of the grace of God, most blessed and venerable Pope Boniface. But after my brother Alypius also saw you in your bodily presence, and, received by you with the greatest kindness and sincerity, exchanged mutual conversations as love prompted, and living with you, though for a short time, was joined to you by a great affection, he poured both himself and me into your mind and brought you back to me in his own; and so my acquaintance with your Holiness grew the greater the surer our friendship became. For you do not disdain, you who are not haughty in mind although you preside from a loftier height, to be the friend of the lowly and to repay the love bestowed on you. For what else is friendship, which takes its name from nothing other than love, and which is faithful nowhere except in Christ, in whom alone it can even be everlasting and happy? Having therefore received it through that brother through whom I came to know you more intimately, I have ventured with greater confidence to write something to your Beatitude about those matters which at this time stir up the episcopal care—if there is any in us—to a fresh vigilance on behalf of the Lord's flock.

**1. 1. 2.** I know indeed that the new heretics, the enemies of the grace of God that is given to small and great through our Lord Jesus Christ, although they are now more clearly shown to be men to guard against by a more open censure, do not for all that cease to try the hearts of the less cautious or the less learned by their writings. To these, surely, an answer had to be given, lest they confirm themselves or their followers in that wicked error, even if we did not fear that they might deceive some Catholic by their plausible discourse. But since they do not cease to rage at the folds of the Lord's flock, and to search out an entry from every side in order to plunder the sheep redeemed at so great a price, and since the pastoral watchtower is common to all of us who hold the office of bishop (although you yourself rise above it on a higher summit), I do what I can for my own small share of the ministry, as far as the Lord deigns to grant me, with the help of your prayers, so that I may set out against their pestilent and treacherous writings other writings that heal and fortify; that by them the madness with which they rave may either itself be cured or be turned away from injuring others.

**1. 1. 3.** Now these matters, which I am answering in reply to two of their letters—one, namely, that Julian is said to have sent to Rome (in order, I suppose, to find or to make there such followers as he could), and the other, which eighteen so-called bishops who share his error dared to write to Thessalonica, not to anyone whatever but, by their cunning, to the bishop of that very place, that they might tempt him and, if it could be done, draw him over to their party—these matters, then, which I am answering in this discussion in reply to those two letters of theirs, as I said, I have decided to direct above all to your Holiness, not so much to be learned as to be examined and, where perhaps something has displeased you, to be corrected. For my brother indicated to me that you yourself deigned to give him those letters, which could not have come into your hands except by the most watchful diligence of our brothers, your sons. And I give thanks for your most sincere goodwill toward us, in that you did not wish those letters of the enemies of the grace of God to be hidden from me, in which you found my name maliciously and openly singled out. But I hope from the Lord our God that it is not without a reward, which is in heaven, that they tear me with slanderous tooth, against whom I set myself for the sake of the little ones, lest they be left to perish under the deceitful flatterer Pelagius, instead of being offered to be set free by the truthful Savior Christ.

**1. 2. 4.** Now therefore let us answer Julian's letter. *Those Manichees*, he says, *with whom we do not now hold communion—that is, all those with whom we disagree—say that by the sin of the first man, that is, of Adam, free choice perished, and that no one now has the power of living well, but all are forced by the necessity of their flesh into sin*. He calls Catholics Manichees after the manner of that Jovinian, who a few years ago, as a new heretic, was undoing the virginity of holy Mary and was making the marriages of the faithful equal to sacred virginity. And he brought this charge against Catholics for no other reason than that he wished them to seem to be accusers or condemners of marriage.

**1. 2. 5.** But in defending free choice they go headlong, so that one trusts in it rather than in the Lord's help for doing justice, and so that each glories in himself, not in the Lord. But which of us says that by the sin of the first man free choice perished from the human race? Freedom indeed perished through sin—but that freedom which was in paradise, of having full righteousness together with immortality. On account of this, human nature stands in need of divine grace, as the Lord says: *If the Son makes you free, then you will be free indeed*[^1]—free, that is, for living well and justly. For free choice has so far not perished in the sinner that it is precisely through it that all sin most of all who sin with delight and with love of sin, and to whom that is pleasing which gives them pleasure. Hence the Apostle too says: *When you were the slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness*[^2]. See, they are shown to have been unable to serve even sin except by another freedom. They are therefore not free from righteousness except by the choice of the will; but they are not made free from sin except by the grace of the Savior. On account of this, that admirable Teacher distinguished the very words: *For when you were the slaves of sin*, he says, *you were free in regard to righteousness. What fruit then did you have at that time in the things of which you are now ashamed? For the end of those things is death. But now, having been set free from sin and become slaves to God, you have your fruit unto sanctification, and the end eternal life*[^3]. He said the slaves of sin were "free" in regard to righteousness, not "set free"; but in regard to sin he did not say "free," lest they should attribute this to themselves, but most watchfully he chose to say "set free," referring this to that saying of the Lord: *If the Son makes you free, then you will be free indeed*. Since, therefore, the sons of men do not live well unless they are made the sons of God, what is it that this man wishes to attribute to free choice the power of living well, when this power is not given except by the grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ, the Gospel saying: *But to as many as received him he gave the power to become sons of God*[^4]?

**1. 3. 6.** But lest perhaps they say that men were helped to this, that they might have *the power to become sons of God*—but that, in order to merit to receive this, they first received him by free choice, helped by no grace (for this is the aim by which they strive to undo grace, namely, to maintain that it is given according to our merits)—lest, then, they should perhaps so divide this evangelical sentence as to place merit in what was said, *But to as many as received him*, and then make the grace not freely given but rendered to this merit in what follows, *he gave the power to become sons of God*: surely, if it should be asked of them what *received him* means, they will say nothing other than "they believed in him." That they may know, then, that this too pertains to grace, let them read what the Apostle says: *In nothing be terrified by the adversaries; this is for them a cause of perdition, but for you of salvation, and this from God; for to you it has been granted on Christ's behalf not only to believe in him but also to suffer for him*[^5]—for he said that both were granted—and likewise what he says: *Peace to the brethren and love with faith from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ*[^6]. Let them read also that which the Lord himself says: *No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him*[^7]. And here let no one suppose that *to come to me* means anything other than "to believe in me." A little later, when he was speaking of his body and blood and very many had been scandalized at his discourse, he said: *The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life; but there are some of you who do not believe*[^8]. Then the Evangelist added: *For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were that believed and who it was that would betray him; and he said: Therefore I told you that no one can come to me unless it has been given to him by my Father*[^9]. He repeated, namely, the sentence in which he had said: *No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him*. And he made plain that he had said this on account of those who believe and those who do not believe, by explaining what he had said, *unless the Father who sent me draws him*, when he repeated the very same thing in other words in saying, *unless it has been given to him by my Father*. For he is drawn to Christ to whom it is given to believe in Christ. The power, therefore, to become sons of God is given to those who believe in him, when this very thing is given, namely, that they believe in him. And unless this power is given by God, it can be none from free choice; for that will not be free in good which the liberator has not set free, but it has free choice in evil, into which a deceiver, whether hidden or manifest, has implanted the delight of malice, or which it has persuaded itself.

**1. 3. 7.** It is therefore not, as some say that we say and as this man besides dares to write, that *all are forced into sin*, as if unwilling, *by the necessity of their flesh*; but rather, if they are already of an age to use the choice of their own mind, they are both held in sin by their own will and hurled headlong from sin into sin by their own will. For not even he who persuades and deceives acts in them otherwise than that they commit sin by their will—whether through ignorance of the truth, or through delight in iniquity, or through both evils, of blindness and of weakness. But this will, which is free in evils because it delights in evils, is for that reason not free in goods, because it has not been set free. Nor can a man will anything good unless he is helped by him who cannot will evil, that is, by the grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ. For *whatever is not of faith is sin*[^10]. And on account of this a good will, which draws itself away from sin, is faithful, because *the just man lives by faith*[^11]. And it pertains to faith to believe in Christ, and no one can believe in him, that is, come to him, unless it has been given to him. No one, therefore, can have a just will unless, with no preceding merits, he has received the true grace, that is, the gratuitous grace, from above.

**1. 4. 8.** This these men, lifted up and proud, are unwilling to admit—men who are not the defenders of free choice by purifying it but its overthrowers by exalting it, and who are indignant at us for saying these things for no other reason than that they disdain to glory in the Lord. Yet Pelagius feared the episcopal judgment in Palestine and, when it was charged against him that he said "the grace of God is given according to our merits," he denied that he said it and, by anathematizing them, condemned those who would say this. And yet in the books that he afterward wrote he is found defending nothing else, thinking that he had practiced a fraud upon the men who judged by lying, or by covering over his own meaning, in I know not what way, with ambiguous words.

**1. 5. 9.** But now let us see what follows. *They also say*, he says, *that those marriages which are now carried on were not instituted by God; which is read in the book of Augustine, against whom I have just now replied in four little books. And the sayings of this Augustine our enemies have taken up in hatred of the truth*. To these most slanderous words of his I see that a brief reply must be made, since he repeats them later, where he himself wishes to suggest, as it were against our writings, what they say. There I must contend with him, with the Lord's help, as far as the matter shall seem to demand. Now therefore I answer that marriages were instituted by God, both then, when it was said: *Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall be in one flesh*[^12]; and now, in regard to what is written: *By the Lord is a woman joined to a man*[^13]. For nothing else happens even now than this, that a man cleaves to his wife and the two are in one flesh. For about these very marriages which take place now, the Lord was consulted by the Jews whether it was lawful to dismiss a wife for any cause whatever, and after recalling this testimony of the Law he added: *What therefore God has joined together, let not man put asunder*[^14]. This testimony of the Law the apostle Paul also employed when he was admonishing husbands that wives should be loved by them. Far be it, then, that in my book this man read anything against these divine testimonies; rather, whether by not understanding or, more likely, by slandering, he strives to wrest into another sense what he reads. As for my book, against which he records that he has replied in four little books, I composed it after the condemnation of Pelagius and Celestius. This I thought ought to be said, since this man says "my sayings were taken up by his enemies in hatred of the truth," lest anyone suppose that the new heretics, the enemies of the grace of Christ, were condemned on account of this book of mine. Now in that book there is a defense rather than a reproof of marriage.

**1. 5. 10.** *They also say*, he says, *that the motion of the genitals and the intercourse of spouses were devised by the devil, and that for this reason those who are born, though innocent, are guilty and are made by the devil, not by God, because they are born of this diabolical intercourse. But this, without any ambiguity, is Manichean*. On the contrary, just as we say that marriages were instituted by God for the sake of the orderly begetting of children, so we say that the sowing of children to be begotten could not have existed, even in paradise, if children were to be begotten, without the motion of the genitals and without the intercourse of spouses. But whether such a motion and intercourse of theirs would have existed, had no one sinned, as now exists with shameful lust—this is the question about which we shall later, if God wills, discuss more carefully.

**1. 6. 11.** Yet what these men want, what they aim at, to what end they are striving to bring the matter—the words of this man, added on, make plain, where he says that we say: *that for this reason those who are born innocent are guilty and are made by the devil, not by God, because they are born of this diabolical intercourse*. Since, therefore, we neither call the intercourse of spouses diabolical—especially of the faithful, which takes place for the sake of begetting children who are afterward to be reborn—nor say that any men are made by the devil, but by God, insofar as they are men, and yet that even of faithful spouses guilty children are born, as a wild olive from an olive, on account of original sin, and on this account are under the devil unless they are reborn in Christ, since the devil is the author of fault, not of nature: against this, those men, saying that little ones contract no original sin and therefore are not under the devil, what do they labor to bring about except that that grace of God may be made void in the little ones, by which, as the Apostle says, *he has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^15]—since indeed they deny that little ones are in the power of darkness, even before the help of the Lord their liberator, thus praising in them the work of the Creator in such a way as to undo the mercy of the Redeemer? And because we confess this mercy both in the older and in the little ones, "this," he says, "without any ambiguity is Manichean"—though it is most ancient and Catholic, the very thing by which the new heretical dogma of these men is overthrown.

**1. 7. 12.** *They say*, he says, *that the saints in the Old Testament were not free from sins, that is, that they were not freed from crimes by amendment, but were caught in guilt by death*. On the contrary, we say that, whether before the Law or in the time of the Old Testament, they were freed from sins—not by their own power, since *cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^16] (and under this curse, without doubt, are those whom the divine Psalm also marks: *those who trust in their own strength*[^17]), nor by the Old Testament, which begets unto slavery, although by the grace of a sure dispensation it was divinely given, nor by the Law itself, holy and just and good, where it is written: *You shall not covet*[^18], since it was not given as one that could give life, but was set down for the sake of transgression until the seed should come to whom the promise was made; but rather they were freed by the blood of the Redeemer himself, who is the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus. But these enemies of the grace of God, which is given to small and great through our Lord Jesus Christ, say that the ancient men of God were of perfect righteousness for this reason: that they may not be believed to have had need of the incarnation, passion, and resurrection of Christ, by faith in which they were saved.

**1. 8. 13.** *They say too*, he says, *that the apostle Paul, or even all the apostles, were always polluted by immoderate lust*. Who, even of the profane, would dare to say this? But doubtless this man slanders in this way because they contend that what the Apostle said—*I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, what is good; for to will is present to me, but to accomplish the good I do not find*[^19], and other such things—was not said by him of himself, but that he put on the character of some other person, I know not whom, who suffered those things. On account of which the very passage in his letter must be carefully considered and searched out, lest in some obscurity of it the error of these men lie hidden. Although, therefore, the Apostle discusses this more broadly, defending grace in a great and long struggle against those who gloried in the Law, yet we touch upon the few things pertaining to the matter. Hence he says: *Because by the Law no flesh shall be justified before him; for through the Law comes the knowledge of sin. But now the righteousness of God has been manifested without the Law, attested by the Law and the Prophets; the righteousness of God, that is, through faith in Jesus Christ, unto all who believe. For there is no distinction; for all have sinned and need the glory of God, being justified freely by his grace, through the redemption which is in Christ Jesus*[^20]. And again: *Where then is your boasting? It is excluded. By what law? Of works? No, but by the law of faith. For we hold that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the Law*[^21]. And again: *For the promise to Abraham or to his seed, that he should be heir of the world, was not through the Law, but through the righteousness of faith. For if those who are of the Law are heirs, faith is made empty and the promise made void. For the Law works wrath; for where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^22]. And in another place: *But the Law entered in that sin might abound; but where sin abounded, grace abounded all the more*[^23]. Likewise in another place: *For sin shall not have dominion over you; for you are not under the Law but under grace*[^24]. And again in another place: *Or do you not know, brethren—for I am speaking to those who know the Law—that the Law has dominion over a man for as long a time as he lives? For a woman is bound by the Law to her husband while her husband lives; but if her husband dies, she is loosed from the law of her husband*[^25]. And a little later: *And so, my brethren, you also have become dead to the Law through the body of Christ, that you may belong to another, who rose from the dead, that we may bear fruit to God. For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins, which are through the Law, were at work in our members, that they might bear fruit unto death; but now we have been freed from the law of death, in which we were held fast, so that we serve in newness of spirit and not in the oldness of the letter*[^26]. By these and similar attestations that Teacher of the Gentiles shows plainly enough that the Law was not able to take away sin but rather had increased it, and that grace takes it away, since the Law knows how to command, to which weakness succumbs, and grace to help, by which love is poured in. For lest anyone, on account of these testimonies, find fault with the Law and contend that it is evil, the Apostle saw what might occur to those who understand badly, and he set the same question for himself: *What then shall we say?* he says. *Is the Law sin? God forbid. But I did not know sin except through the Law*[^27]. This he had already said above: *Through the Law comes the knowledge of sin*; not, therefore, the removal but the knowledge.

**1. 8. 14.** But from here he now begins, on account of which we have undertaken to consider these things, to introduce his own person and to speak, as it were, of himself. Here the Pelagians are unwilling that the Apostle himself be understood, but hold that he transfigured another into himself, that is, a man still set under the Law, not yet set free through grace. Here indeed they must already concede that *in the Law no one is justified*[^28], as the same Apostle says elsewhere, but that the Law avails unto the knowledge of sin and unto the transgression of the Law itself, so that, sin being known and increased, grace may be sought through faith. Now they are not afraid that these things be understood of the Apostle, which he could also say of his own past life; but they fear the things that follow. For here he says: *I did not know covetousness unless the Law had said: You shall not covet. But sin, taking the occasion, worked in me through the commandment all manner of covetousness. For without the Law sin was dead. But I was alive once without the Law; but when the commandment came, sin revived, and I died. And the commandment, which was unto life, was found to be unto death for me. For sin, taking the occasion through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me. And so the Law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did then that which is good become death to me? God forbid! But sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good, that the sinner, or sin, might become through the commandment exceedingly sinful*[^29]. All these things, as I said, the Apostle can be seen to have recalled about his own past life, so that what he says—*But I was alive once without the Law*—he meant to be understood of his first age, from infancy, before the years of reason; and what he added—*but when the commandment came, sin revived, and I died*—he might show himself to be now capable of the precept, but not effective in it, and therefore a transgressor of the Law.

**1. 9. 15.** Nor let it disturb us that he wrote to the Philippians: *According to the righteousness which is in the Law, having been blameless*[^30]. For he could have been inwardly, in his depraved affections, a transgressor of the Law, and yet have fulfilled the conspicuous works of the Law, either through fear of men or even of God himself—but through dread of punishment, not through love and delight in righteousness. For it is one thing to do good by a will to do good, and another to be so inclined by the will to do evil that one would even do it if this could be permitted with impunity. For thus he surely sins inwardly in the very will who does not sin by will but by fear. Knowing himself to have been such in these inward things, before the grace of God which is given through our Lord Jesus Christ, the Apostle confesses this most openly elsewhere. For writing to the Ephesians, he says: *And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit who now works in the sons of unbelief, among whom we also all once conducted ourselves in the desires of our flesh, doing the will of the flesh and of the affections, and we were by nature children of wrath, like the rest; but God, who is rich in mercy, on account of the great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, made us alive together with Christ, by whose grace we have been saved*[^31]. Again, to Titus: *For we ourselves also were once foolish and unbelieving, going astray, serving various desires and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, hating one another*[^32]. Such was Saul, when he says that he had been blameless according to the righteousness which is in the Law. For that it was not after this abominable life, in order to be blameless, that he had advanced in the Law and changed his ways, he shows plainly in what follows, since indeed he says that he was changed from these evils only through the grace of the Savior; for adding this very thing here too, as to the Ephesians, he says: *But when the goodness and kindness of God our Savior appeared, not by works of righteousness which we did, but according to his mercy he saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out richly upon us through Jesus Christ our Savior, that, being justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life*[^33].

**1. 9. 16.** Now what he says in this place of the letter to the Romans—*sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good*—agrees with the things above where he said: *but I did not know sin except through the Law; for I did not know covetousness unless the Law had said: You shall not covet*; and above: *Through the Law comes the knowledge of sin*. For this is what he said here too: *that it might appear sin*; so that what he had said—*for without the Law sin was dead*—we should understand only as, "as though it did not exist, it lies hidden, does not appear, is utterly unknown," as though it were buried in I know not what darkness of ignorance. And what he says—*But I was alive once without the Law*—what does he say but "I seemed to myself to be alive"? And what he added—*but when the commandment came, sin revived*—what else is it than "it stood out and appeared"? Yet he does not say "it lived," but *revived*. For it had lived once in paradise, when against the precept that had been given the transgression was sufficiently apparent; but when it is contracted from those being born, it lies hidden as though it were dead, until the evil that resists righteousness is felt by its prohibition, when one thing is commanded and approved, another delights and dominates. Then sin in a certain way revives in the awareness of the man who is born, sin which had once already lived in the awareness of the man who was made.

**1. 10. 17.** But what follows is not so readily understood, as to how it can be understood of Paul. *For we know*, he says, *that the Law is spiritual; but I am carnal*[^34]. He does not say "I was," but *I am*. Was the Apostle, then, carnal when he wrote these things? Or does he say this according to the body? For he was still in the body of this death, not yet made into that of which he says elsewhere: *The body is sown an animal body, it rises a spiritual body*[^35]. For then a man will be wholly spiritual—that is, in both parts of which he consists—when the body too will be spiritual. For it is not absurd that in that life there should be even spiritual flesh, if in this life, in those who still relish carnal things, the spirit itself could be carnal. Thus, therefore, he said *but I am carnal* for this reason, that the Apostle did not yet have a spiritual body, just as he could say "but I am mortal," which surely would be understood as said only according to the body, which had not yet been clothed with immortality. Likewise, when he added *sold under sin*, lest anyone suppose him not yet redeemed by the blood of Christ, this too can be understood according to what he says: *And we ourselves, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^36]. For if he says that he was sold under sin according to this, that his body is not yet redeemed from corruption, or that it was sold once in the first transgression of the precept, so that he has a corruptible body which weighs down the soul, what prevents us from understanding that here the Apostle says of himself what he says in such a way that even in himself it can be understood—even if in his own person he wishes not himself alone but all to be understood who have come to know themselves struggling, with spiritual love against the affection of the flesh without consenting?

**1. 10. 18.** Or do we perhaps fear what follows: *For what I work, I do not know; for it is not what I will that I do, but what I hate, that I do*[^37], lest perhaps from these words someone suspect that the holy Apostle consented to the concupiscence of the flesh unto evil works? But what he adds must be considered: *But if I do what I do not will, I agree with the Law that it is good*[^38]. For he says rather that he agrees with the Law than with the concupiscence of the flesh—for this he calls by the name of sin; he said, therefore, that he did and worked, not by the disposition of consenting and fulfilling, but by the very motion of coveting. "Hence, therefore," he says, *I agree with the Law that it is good*; I agree, because I do not will what it does not will. Then he says: *But now it is no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^39]. What is *But now* except "Now already under grace, which has set free the delight of the will from consent to desire"? For *I do not work it* is not better understood except as that he does not consent to present his members as weapons of iniquity to sin. For if he both covets and consents and acts, how does he not himself work it, even if he grieves that he works it and groans heavily at being overcome?

**1. 10. 19.** Now does not what follows show most plainly from what source he speaks? *For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, what is good*[^40]. For if he had not explained by adding *that is, in my flesh*, what he said—*in me*—might perhaps be taken otherwise. And on this account he turns this very thing over, repeating and impressing it: *For to will is present to me, but to accomplish the good, no*. For this is to accomplish the good: that a man not even covet; but the good is imperfect when he covets, even if he does not consent to the concupiscence unto evil. *For it is not the good that I will that I do*, he says, *but the evil that I do not will, that I do. But if I do that which I do not will, it is now no longer I that work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^41]. This he repeated, impressing it and, as it were, rousing the most sluggish from sleep: *I find, then, a law for me when I will to do good, since evil is present to me*[^42]. That, then, is good for one who wills to do it, but evil is present from concupiscence, to which he does not consent who says: *It is now no longer I that work it*.

**1. 10. 20.** But what follows declares both more openly: *For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man, but I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin which is in my members*[^43]. But what he said—*taking me captive*—can disturb us, if there is no consent. Hence, on account of those three things—two, namely, about which we have already discussed, namely that he said *but I am carnal*, and *sold under sin*, and this third, *taking me captive in the law of sin which is in my members*—the Apostle can be seen to be describing the man who still lives under the Law, not yet under grace. But as we have explained those two as said on account of the flesh still corruptible, so this too can be understood, so that he said *taking me captive* meaning "in flesh," not in mind, "in motion," not in consent, and therefore *taking me captive*, because even in the flesh itself there is not a nature foreign to us, but our own. As, therefore, he himself explained what he said—*For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, what is good*—so too from his explanation we ought to take this place, as though he had said: *taking me captive*, that is, "my flesh," *in the law of sin which is in my members*.

**1. 10. 21.** Then he adds that for the sake of which all these things were said: *Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^44]; and from this he concludes: *Therefore I myself with the mind serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*[^45]—with the flesh, namely, the law of sin by coveting; but with the mind the law of God by not consenting to the same concupiscence. *There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus*[^46]. For no one is condemned except him who consents to the concupiscence of the flesh unto evil. *For the law of the spirit of life in Christ Jesus has freed you from the law of sin and death*[^47]—that is, lest the concupiscence of the flesh claim your consent for itself. And the things that follow show this same sense more and more; but a measure must be observed.

**1. 10. 22.** Now it had at one time seemed even to me that "the man under the Law" was described by this discourse of the Apostle. But these words afterward exerted force upon me, namely that he says: *But now it is no longer I that work it*. For to this pertains also what he says afterward: *There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus*; and because I do not see how a man under the Law could say *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man*, since that very delight in the good, by which he also does not consent to evil—not from fear of punishment but from love of righteousness (for this is to delight together)—is to be ascribed to nothing but grace.

**1. 11. 23.** For as to that where he says: *Who will free me from the body of this death?*—who would deny that the Apostle, when he said these things, was still in the body of this death? From which, surely, the ungodly are not freed, since the same bodies are restored to them for eternal torments. To be freed, then, from the body of this death is, with every languor of the concupiscence of the flesh healed, to receive the body not unto punishment but unto glory. To this place that too is sufficiently in harmony: *And we ourselves also, having the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body*[^48]. For surely it is with this groaning that we groan in which we say: *Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death?* That too where he says: *For what I work, I do not know*—what is it other than "I do not will," "I do not approve," "I do not consent," "I do not do"? Otherwise it is contrary to what he said above: *Through the Law comes the knowledge of sin*, and: *I did not know sin except through the Law*, and: *Sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good*. For how did he come to know through the Law the sin that he does not know? How does the sin appear that is not known? Thus, therefore, *I do not know* means "I do not do," because by no consent do I myself commit it—just as the Lord will say to the ungodly: *I do not know you*[^49], from whom without doubt nothing can lie hidden; and just as it was said: *him who had not known sin*[^50], which is "had not done it," for surely he was not unaware of that which he reproved.

**1. 11. 24.** These and similar things in this context of the apostolic Scripture having been carefully considered, the Apostle is rightly understood to have signified not indeed himself alone in his own person, but others also established under grace, yet not established with him in that perfect peace in which death will be swallowed up in victory. Of which he says afterward: *But if Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead on account of sin, but the spirit is life on account of righteousness. If, then, the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^51]. When, therefore, our mortal bodies are made alive, not only will there be no consent unto sinning, but not even will there remain the concupiscence of the flesh itself to which one would not consent. That this should not resist the spirit in mortal flesh, that man alone could achieve who did not come to men through it. And therefore, since the apostles were men and were carrying in the mortality of this life a body that is corrupted and weighs down the soul, far be it that we should say, as this man slanderously charges, that they were *always polluted by immoderate lust*; rather we say that they were free from consent to depraved lusts, yet that, with regard to the concupiscence of the flesh, which they restrained by moderating it, they groaned with such great humility and piety that they wished rather not to have it than to subdue it.

**1. 12. 25.** Furthermore, as for what this man added, that we say "Christ too was not free from sins, but lied out of the necessity of the flesh and was stained with other faults"—let him see from whom he has heard this or in whose writings he has read it; which perhaps he did not understand and, with deceiving malice, twisted into slanderous senses.

**1. 13. 26.** *They also say*, he says, *that baptism does not give every pardon of sins nor take away crimes, but shaves them, so that the roots of all sins are retained in the evil flesh*. Who would affirm this against the Pelagians except an unbeliever? We say, then, that baptism gives the pardon of all sins and takes away crimes, does not shave them, nor does it act so *that the roots of all sins are retained in the evil flesh, like the hairs shaved on the head, from which the sins to be cut off grow again*. For I have learned that they apply this comparison too to their slander, as though we held and said this.

**1. 13. 27.** But about this concupiscence of the flesh I believe that they are deceived or that they deceive, with which it is necessary that even the baptized—and this even if he advances most diligently and is led by the Spirit of God—should struggle with a pious mind. But this concupiscence, even if it is called sin, is so called not because it is sin but because it was made by sin, just as a writing is called someone's hand because the hand made it. But sins are those things which are unlawfully done, spoken, or thought according to the concupiscence of the flesh or to ignorance; and these, once committed, hold one guilty also, if they are not forgiven. And this very concupiscence of the flesh is so remitted in baptism that, although it is contracted by those being born, it does no harm to those being reborn. Yet from those reborn, if they beget children carnally, it is again contracted and will again do harm to those being born, unless it is remitted by the same form to those being reborn and remains in them, destined to do no harm to the future life, since its guilt, contracted by generation, has been remitted by regeneration; and therefore it is now no longer sin, but is so called, either because it was made by sin or because it is moved by the delight of sinning, even if it is not consented to, the delight of righteousness prevailing. And it is not on account of it, whose guilt has now been consumed by the washing of regeneration, that the baptized say in prayer, *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^52], but on account of the sins that are committed, whether in consents to it, when something that is pleasing is overcome by it, or when through ignorance evil is pleasing as though it were good. And these are committed either by acting or by speaking or—what is easiest and quickest—by thinking. From all of which, what one even of the faithful will glory that he has a chaste heart, or who will glory that he is clean from sin? Indeed, that which follows in the prayer is said on account of it: *Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil*[^53]. For *each one*, as it is written, *is tempted by being drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence; then concupiscence, when it has conceived, gives birth to sin*[^54].

**1. 14. 28.** All these offspring of concupiscence, and the ancient guilt of concupiscence itself, have been remitted by the washing of baptism; and whatever this concupiscence now gives birth to, if those offspring are not such as are called not only sins but even crimes, is cleansed by that compact of daily prayer where we say, *Forgive as we forgive*, and by the sincerity of almsgiving. For no one is so foolish as to say that the Lord's precept does not pertain to the baptized: *Forgive, and it will be forgiven you; give, and it will be given to you*[^55]. But no one could rightly be ordained a minister in the Church if the Apostle had said "if anyone is without sin" where he said *if anyone is without crime*[^56], or if he had said "having no sin" where he said *having no crime*[^57]. For many baptized faithful are without crime; but without sin in this life I would say no one is, however much the Pelagians, because we say these things against them, may be puffed up and burst with madness—not because anything of sin remains that is not remitted in baptism, but because, while we remain in the weakness of this life, those things do not cease to be done daily which are daily remitted to those who pray faithfully and act mercifully. This is the soundness of the Catholic faith, which the Holy Spirit everywhere sows, not the vanity of heretical depravity and the presumption of a spirit.

**1. 15. 29.** Now therefore let us see how, after he thought to charge us slanderously with things we believe and to invent things we do not believe, he himself professes his own faith, or that of the Pelagians. *Against these things*, he says, *we dispute daily, and therefore we are unwilling to give consent to transgressors, because we say that free choice exists naturally in all and could not have perished by the sin of Adam, which is confirmed by the authority of all the Scriptures*. If you said these things as you ought, not against the grace of God, you would not be giving consent to transgressors but correcting your own meaning. But on this point we have discussed above as much as we could and as much as seemed sufficient.

**1. 15. 30.** *We say*, he says, *that these marriages which are now carried on in the world were instituted by God, and that spouses are not guilty, but fornicators and adulterers are to be condemned*. This is true and Catholic; but what you wish to bring about from this—namely, that those being born contract nothing of sin from the intercourse of male and female, sin which would be expiated by the washing of regeneration—this is false and heretical.

**1. 15. 31.** *The motion of the genitals*, he says, *that is, manhood itself, without which there cannot be intercourse, we say was instituted by God*. To this we answer that God instituted the motion of the genitals and—to use his word—manhood, without which there cannot be intercourse, in such a way that it had nothing shameful. For it was not right that his creature should blush at the work of its Creator; but the disobedience of the members was rendered as a just punishment to the first men, who were disobedient, at which they blushed when they covered with fig leaves the parts that became shameful, which before were not shameful.

**1. 16. 32.** For they did not sew tunics for themselves to cover the whole body after sin, but aprons, which some of our less careful translators rendered "coverings." Which indeed is true; but "covering" is a general name by which every garment and wrapping can be understood. And therefore the ambiguity ought to have been avoided, so that just as the Greek put down a word for things by which nothing is covered but the shameful parts of the body, so the Latin should either put down the Greek word itself (since usage already employs it as Latin) or, as some did, "aprons," or, as others better named them, "loincloths." For this name comes from that custom by which young men of old, in the Roman manner, covered their private parts when they exercised naked on the field; whence even today those who cover those same members by girding them are called "girded for the field." And yet, if those things by which sin was committed had to be covered after sin, they ought not even to have been clothed with tunics, but to have covered the hand and the mouth, because they sinned by taking and eating. What, then, does it mean that, the forbidden food having been taken, when the transgression of the precept had been committed, attention was turned to those members? What newness, unknown there, is felt and compels itself to be noticed?—which is signified by the opening of their eyes. For their eyes were not closed, either when he gave names to the cattle and the birds, or when she saw that the tree was beautiful and good; rather, they were opened, that is, made intent upon looking. So it is written of Hagar, Sarah's maidservant, that she opened her eyes and saw a well, which surely she did not have closed. So that they should suddenly be so ashamed of their nakedness, which they surely beheld daily and were not confounded, that they could no longer bear those members naked but at once took care to cover them—did they not, he in an open motion and she in a hidden one, feel those members disobedient against the choice of their will, members over which they surely ought to have had dominion at their voluntary nod as over the rest? Which they rightly suffered, because they themselves had not been obedient to their Lord. They blushed, then, that they had not rendered service to their Creator in such a way that in those members from which children were to be procreated they deserved to lose dominion.

**1. 16. 33.** This kind of shame, this necessity of blushing, is certainly born with every man and is, in a way, commanded by the very laws of nature, so that in this matter even chaste marriages feel shame, and no one advances so badly and shamefully that, because he has come to know that God is the founder of nature and the author of marriage, therefore, when he is about to be joined to his wife, he should not, if anyone should see him, blush at these motions and seek out a secret place where he can avoid the sight not only of strangers but even of all his own. And so let human nature be permitted to recognize as an evil befalling it by its own fault, lest it be forced either—what is most shameless—not to blush at these motions of its own, or—what is most ungrateful—to blush at the works of its Creator. Yet this evil chaste marriages use well for the sake of the good of begetting children; but to consent to lust for the sake of carnal pleasure alone is sin, although it is conceded to the married by way of pardon.

**1. 17. 34.** But settle this, Pelagians, granting the honor and fruitfulness of marriage: if no one had sinned, what kind of life would you wish to imagine for those men in paradise, and choose one of these four things. For without doubt either, as often as they wished, so often they would have had intercourse; or they would have curbed lust when intercourse was not necessary; or then lust would have arisen at the nod of the will, when chaste prudence had foreseen that intercourse was necessary; or, with no lust at all existing there, just as the other several members serve their works, so the genitals too would have served their proper work at the bidding of those willing, without any difficulty. Of these four, choose which you will. But I suppose that you will reject the first two, in which lust is either served or resisted. For so excellent an honor does not want that first, and so great a happiness does not want this second. For far be it that the glory of that great blessedness should either, by always following lust, carry on a most shameful slavery, or, by resisting it, not have the fullest peace; far be it, I say, that the concupiscence of the flesh, rising not opportunely for begetting but by a disordered commotion, should either please that mind to satisfy it by consenting, or should be necessary for that repose to restrain it by dissenting.

**1. 17. 35.** But whichever of the two remaining ones you choose, there is no need to labor against you with any contention. For even if you are unwilling to choose the fourth, where there is the highest tranquillity of all the obedient members without any lust—since now the onset of your disputations has made you its enemies—at least that will please you which we put in the third place, namely, that that carnal concupiscence, whose motion arrives at the lowest pleasure, the one that delights you greatly, would never in paradise have arisen at the nod of the will except when it was necessary for begetting. If it pleases you to place this in paradise, and through such a concupiscence of the flesh, which would neither anticipate nor delay nor exceed the command of the will, it seems to you that in that happiness children could have been begotten, we do not resist. For to the purpose of what we are doing it is enough for us that such a concupiscence as you grant could have existed in that place of happiness does not now exist in men. For what kind it now is, surely the perception of all mortals confesses, even if with shame, since it harasses even the chaste against their will, and those who chastise it by temperance, with a disordered and importunate restlessness, and often withdraws itself from those who will it and forces itself upon those who do not, so that by its disobedience it testifies that it is nothing else than the punishment of that ancient disobedience. Whence rightly about it both the first men, when they covered their shameful parts, and now whoever considers himself in any way to be a man—everyone, shameful and shameless—is confounded, far be it on account of the work of God, but on account of the punishment of the first and ancient sin. But you, not for a religious reason but for a spirited contention, and not for human shame but for your own fury, lest the concupiscence of the flesh itself be believed to be corrupted and original sin to be contracted from it, attempt by disputing to recall into paradise such a concupiscence as now exists, and to contend that there could have existed there that lust which either an unbecoming consent would always follow or a pitiable dissent would sometimes restrain. But we do not much care what it pleases you to think about it. Nevertheless, whatever of men is born through it, unless it is reborn, is without doubt condemned, and must necessarily be under the devil, if it is not set free from there by Christ.

**1. 18. 36.** *We maintain*, he says, *that men are the work of God, and that no one is forced by his power either to evil or to good against his will, but that by his own will he either does good or evil; and that in a good work he is always helped by the grace of God, but to evil he is incited by the suggestions of the devil*. To these things we answer that men are the work of God insofar as they are men, but that they are under the devil insofar as they are sinners, unless they are rescued from there through him who became the mediator between God and men for no other reason than that, sprung from men, he could not be a sinner; and that no one is forced by the power of God either to evil or to good against his will, but that with God deserting him for his merits he goes into evil, and with God helping him without merits he is turned to good. For a man is not good if he does not will it; but he is helped by the grace of God even to this, that he may will, since it is not written in vain: *For it is God who works in you both to will and to work, for his good will*[^58], and: *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^59].

**1. 19. 37.** But you think that a man is helped by the grace of God in a good work in such a way that in rousing his will to the good work itself you believe grace works nothing. Which your own words sufficiently declare. For why did you not say that a man is roused by the grace of God to a good work, as you said *incited to evil by the suggestions of the devil*, but said *in a good work he is always helped by the grace of God*?—as though by his own will, having undertaken a good work with no grace of God, he were then in the work itself helped divinely, for the merits, namely, of the good will, so that a grace owed is rendered, not an unowed grace given, and thus grace is now no longer grace, but is that which Pelagius condemned with a feigned heart in the judgment in Palestine, that *the grace of God is given according to our merits*. Tell me, I beg, what good did Paul, while still Saul, will, and not rather great evils, when, breathing slaughter, he was going to lay waste the Christians with horrible blindness and fury of mind? By what merits of a good will did God, by a marvelous and sudden calling, turn him from these evils to good? What shall I say, then, by what merits, when he himself cries out: *Not by works of righteousness which we did, but according to his mercy he saved us*[^60]? What of that which I have already recalled the Lord to have said: *No one can come to me*—which is understood as "believe in me"—*unless it has been given to him by my Father*[^61]? Is this given to one already willing to believe, for the merits of a good will, or rather is the will itself, that he may believe, roused from above, as in Saul's case, even if he is so turned away from faith that he even persecutes believers? For why has the Lord commanded us to pray for those who persecute us? Do we pray for this, that the grace of God be repaid to them for their good will, and not rather that the will itself be changed from evil into good? Just as we believe that it was not in vain that the saints, whom he was persecuting, then prayed for Saul, that his will be turned to the faith which he was laying waste. And his conversion, indeed, wrought from above, appeared even by a manifest miracle; but how many enemies of Christ are daily drawn suddenly to Christ by the hidden grace of God! Which word, if I had not taken from the Gospel, how much this man would have said about me on account of it, since even now he struggles not against me but against him who cries: *No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him*[^62]! For he does not say "leads him," so that we might understand the will in some way to precede there. Who is drawn, if he already willed? And yet no one comes unless he wills. He is drawn, therefore, in wondrous ways, that he may will, by him who knows how to work within, in the very hearts of men—not that men should believe unwilling, which cannot happen, but that they should become willing from unwilling.

**1. 20. 38.** That this is true we do not suspect by human conjecture, but recognize by the most evident authority of the divine Scriptures. It is read in the books of *Chronicles*: *And indeed in Judah the hand of God was at work, to give them one heart, that they might do the commandment of the king and of the princes in the word of the Lord*[^63]. Likewise, through the prophet Ezekiel the Lord says: *I will give them another heart, and I will give them a new spirit, and I will tear their stony heart out of their flesh, and I will give them a heart of flesh, that they may walk in my precepts and keep my judgments and do them*[^64]. But what is it that Queen Esther prays and says: *Give a fitting word into my mouth, and make my words clear in the sight of the lion, and turn his heart to hatred of him who fights against us*[^65]? Why does she say these things to God in prayer, if God does not work the will in the hearts of men? But perhaps this woman prayed foolishly. Let us see, then, whether the affection of the one praying was sent forth in vain and the effect of the one hearing did not follow. Behold, she goes in to the king—not to say much—and because she did not go in by her own order, compelled by great necessity, he looked upon her, as it is written, like a bull in the onset of his indignation; and the queen was afraid, and her color was changed by faintness, and she leaned upon the head of her maid who went before her. And God turned and transferred his indignation into gentleness. Now what need is there to recall what follows, where the divine Scripture testifies that God fulfilled what she had asked, working in the heart of the king nothing other than the will by which he commanded, and what the queen had asked of him was done? Which God, that it might be done, had already heard, he who turned and transferred the heart of the king, before he had heard the speech of the woman asking, by a most hidden and most effective power, from indignation to gentleness, that is, from a will to harm to a will to favor, according to that saying of the Apostle: *God works in you both to will*[^66]. Did the men of God who wrote these things—nay, the very Spirit of God, by whose authorship through them these things were written down—assail the free choice of man? God forbid; rather, he commended both the most just judgment and the most merciful help of the Almighty in all things. For it suffices a man to know that there is no iniquity with God. Now how he dispenses these things, making some vessels of wrath according to merit, others vessels of mercy according to grace, *who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?*[^67] If, therefore, we belong to the honor of grace, let us not be ungrateful by attributing to ourselves what we have received. For what do we have that we have not received?

**1. 21. 39.** *We say*, he says, *that the saints of the Old Testament passed over to eternal life with their righteousness here made perfect—that is, that by zeal for virtue they withdrew from all sins, because even those whom we read to have sinned in some way we have learned afterward to have amended themselves*. However great the virtue he may proclaim the ancient just men to have had, nothing saved them except faith in the Mediator, who shed his blood for the remission of sins. For it is their own voice: *I believed, therefore I have spoken*[^68]. Hence the apostle Paul also says: *But having the same spirit of faith, according to what is written: I believed, therefore I have spoken, we also believe, therefore we also speak*[^69]. What is *the same spirit*, except the one which those just men also had, who said these things? The apostle Peter also says: *Why do you wish to lay a yoke upon the Gentiles which neither we nor our fathers were able to bear? But by the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ we believe that we are saved, just as they too*[^70]. This you are unwilling to admit, enemies of this grace—that the ancients are believed to have been saved by the same grace of Jesus Christ; but you distribute the times according to Pelagius, in whose books this is read, and you say that before the Law men were saved by nature, then through the Law, and finally through Christ, as though for the men of the two earlier times—before the Law, namely, and under the Law—the blood of Christ had not been necessary, making void what was said: *For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^71].

**1. 22. 40.** *We confess*, they say, *that the grace of Christ is necessary for all, both for the older and for the little ones, and we anathematize those who say that a child born of two baptized parents ought not to be baptized*. We know how you say these things not according to the apostle Paul but according to the heretic Pelagius—namely, that baptism is necessary for the little ones not on account of the remission of sins but only on account of the kingdom of heaven. For you give them a place of salvation and eternal life outside the kingdom of God, even if they have not been baptized, and you do not attend to what is written: *He who believes and is baptized will be saved; but he who does not believe will be condemned*[^72]. On account of which, in the Church of the Savior, little ones believe through others, just as from others they have contracted the sins which are remitted to them in baptism. Nor do you consider that they cannot have life who have been without a share of the body and blood of Christ, since he himself says: *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^73]. Or if by the evangelical words you are compelled to confess that the little ones cannot have life and salvation, departing from the body, unless they have been baptized, ask why the unbaptized are compelled to undergo the punishment of the second death, judged by him who condemns no one undeserving, and you will find—what you do not wish—original sin.

**1. 23. 41.** *Those too who say*, he says, *that baptism does not blot out all sins, we condemn, because we know that full purgation is conferred through the mysteries themselves*. This we too say; but that the little ones too are loosed through these same mysteries from the bonds of their first nativity and of a guilty succession—this you do not say. On account of which, from the Church of Christ, which holds this from of old, it is fitting that you too, like other heretics, be set apart.

**1. 24. 42.** Now indeed, as for the way he so concludes his letter as to say: *Let no one therefore seduce you; nor let the ungodly deny that they hold these views, but, if they speak the truth, let either a hearing be granted, or at least let those very bishops who now dissent condemn the views I have stated above, which they hold together with the Manichees, just as we condemn those views which they cast at us, and let full concord be made. But if they are unwilling, know that they are Manichees, and keep yourselves from fellowship with them*—this is to be despised rather than refuted. For which of us hesitates to say anathema to the Manichees, who say that neither men nor marriages were instituted by the good God, nor was the Law given which was administered through Moses to the Hebrew people? But to the Pelagians too we say anathema, not undeservedly, who are such enemies of the grace of God, which comes through our Lord Jesus Christ, that they say it is given not freely but according to our merits, and thus grace is now no longer grace; and who place so much in free choice—into the depths of which man has been plunged—that they say that by using it well a man merits grace, although no one can use it well except through grace, which is rendered not according to debt but is given by God, freely showing mercy; and who, moreover, so contend that the little ones are already saved as to dare to deny that they are to be saved by the Savior. And, holding and sowing these execrable dogmas, they still besides demand a hearing, when, being condemned, they ought to do penance.
