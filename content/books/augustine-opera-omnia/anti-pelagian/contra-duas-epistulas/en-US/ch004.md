# Book Four

**4. 1. 1.** After these things which we have treated and to which we have replied, they repeat the same things—they whose letter we are refuting—but in a different way. For first they set them forth as charges against us, as things we wrongly think; but afterward, expounding what they themselves think, they rendered the same things conversely, adding certain two things which they had not said, that is, "that they say baptism is necessary for all ages," and "that through Adam death passed to us, not crimes." Which things too are to be treated in their place. Accordingly, since in the earlier book, which we have just finished, we said that they hold out the obstacles of five matters, in which their dogmas, hostile to the grace of God and to the Catholic faith, lie hidden—namely, the praise of the creature, the praise of marriage, the praise of the Law, the praise of free choice, the praise of the saints—I judge it more convenient to distinguish by kind all the things which they defend, the contraries of which they charge against us, and to demonstrate which of their things pertain to which of these five, so that, by the distinction itself, our reply may be clearer and briefer.

**4. 2. 2.** The praise of the creature, so far as pertains to the human race, about which the question now is, they pursue with these statements: "That God is the founder of those being born and that the sons of men are the work of God, and that all sin descends not from nature but from will." To this praise of the creature they apply "that they say baptism is necessary for all ages, namely, that that creature may be adopted into the sons of God, not that it contracts anything from its parents which must be expiated by the washing of regeneration." To this praise they join also "that they say the Lord Christ was sprinkled with no stain of sin, so far as pertains to his infancy," because they assert that his flesh is most pure from all contagion of sin not by his proper excellence and singular grace but by a communion of nature, which is in all infants. To this also pertains the fact that they insert a question "about the origin of the soul," thus striving to make equal to the soul of Christ all the souls of little ones, which they wish to be likewise sprinkled with no stain of sin. On this account they also say: *That from Adam nothing of evil passed through the rest except death, which is not always an evil*, they say, *since it is for the martyrs the cause of rewards, and what makes a death be called good or evil is not the dissolution of bodies, which will be raised up in every kind of men, but the diversity of merits, which comes about from human liberty*. These things they write in this letter about the praise of the creature. But marriage they praise according to the Scriptures, "because the Lord speaks in the Gospel: *He who made them from the beginning made them male and female. And he said: Increase and multiply and fill the earth*." Although this is written not in that place of the Gospel but rather in the Law. They add also: *What therefore God has joined together, let not man put asunder*[^1]. Which evangelical words we acknowledge. In the praise of the Law they say: "The old Law, according to the Apostle, just and holy and good, could confer perpetual life on those who keep its commandments and live justly through faith, as on the Prophets and Patriarchs and all the saints." In the praise of free choice they say: "Free choice has not perished, since the Lord speaks through the Prophet: *If you are willing and will hear me, you shall eat the good things of the land; if you are unwilling and will not hear, the sword shall devour you*[^2]. And thereby grace also helps the good purpose of each, yet does not instill zeal for virtue into one who resists, because there is no respect of persons with God." With the praise of the saints they cover themselves, saying: "That baptism perfectly renews men, since indeed the Apostle is a witness, who testifies that through the washing of water the Church is made holy and immaculate from among the Gentiles. That the Holy Spirit too helped good minds in ancient times, the Prophet saying to God: *Your good Spirit will lead me into the right way*[^3]. That all the Prophets and Apostles, or saints, of the New as well as of the Old Testament, to whom God bears witness, were just not in comparison with the wicked but by the rule of virtues; but that in the time to come there is a reward both of good works and of evil; and, moreover, that no one can fulfill there the commandments which here he despised, because the Apostle said: *We must be made manifest before the tribunal of Christ, that each may receive the proper things of the body, according to what he has done, whether good or evil*[^4]."  
In all these statements, whatever they say in praise of creation and marriage they try to direct toward denying original sin; whatever they say in praise of the law and free choice, toward claiming that grace helps only merit, and so grace is no longer grace[^5]; and whatever they say in praise of the saints, toward making it appear that the saints’ mortal life is without sin and that they have no need to pray to God for the forgiveness of their debts.

**4. 3. 3.** Whoever, with a Catholic mind, shrinks from these wicked and damnable dogmas in this threefold division, let him avoid the lurking-places and ambushes of that fivefold one, and let him be so cautious between the two that, in declining the Manichee, he does not incline toward Pelagius; and again, let him so separate himself from the Pelagians that he does not join himself to the Manichees; or, if he is already held by one of the two, let him not so rescue himself from either party as to rush into the other. For they seem to be contrary to one another, since the Manichees manifest themselves by disparaging those five things, the Pelagians hide themselves by praising them. Wherefore whoever, according to the rule of the Catholic faith, both so glorifies the Creator over the good creature of flesh and soul in men being born—which the Manichee does not wish—that yet, on account of the vice which passed over into them through the sin of the first man, he confesses a Savior necessary even for little ones—which Pelagius does not wish; so distinguishes the evil of shameful concupiscence from the goodness of marriage that, neither like the Manichees does he find fault with that whence we are born, nor like the Pelagians does he praise that whereby we are confounded; so defends that the Law given through Moses, holy and just and good, was given by the holy and just and good God—which, against the Apostle, the Manichee denies—that he says it both shows sin, yet does not take it away, and commands righteousness, yet does not give it—which again, against the Apostle, Pelagius denies; so asserts free choice that he says that both the evil of the angel and of man began not from I know not what always-evil nature, which is none, but from choice itself—which overthrows the Manichean heresy—nor yet that the captive will can therefore breathe again into wholesome freedom except by the grace of God—which overthrows the Pelagian heresy; so praises in God the holy men of God, not only from the Christ manifested in the flesh and thenceforward, but also of the earlier times—whom the Manichees dare to blaspheme—that yet about them he believes themselves confessing rather than the Pelagians lying. For it is the voice of the saints: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us*[^6].

**4. 4. 4.** These things being so, what does it profit the new heretics, the enemies of the cross of Christ and the assailants of divine grace, that they seem sound from the error of the Manichees and die of another pestilence of their own? What does it profit them that in the praise of the creature they say, "the good God is the founder of those being born, through whom all things were made, and the sons of men are his work"—whom the Manichees say are the work of the prince of darkness—when, between both or in both, the creature of God which is in infants perishes? For both are unwilling that it be freed by the flesh and blood of Christ: the former, because they destroy the very flesh and blood of Christ, as though he did not take these up at all in man or from man; the latter, because they assert that there is no evil in infants whence they should be freed through the sacrament of this flesh and blood. The human creature lies between them in the little ones, good by institution, vitiated by propagation, confessing by its goods a most excellent founder, seeking by its evils a most merciful redeemer; having the Manichees as disparagers of its goods, having the Pelagians as deniers of its evils, both as persecutors. And although through infancy it cannot speak, yet by its silent appearance and its hidden weakness it accuses the impious vanity of both, saying to the former: "Believe that I am created by him who creates good things"; and saying to the latter: "Allow me to be healed by him who created me." The Manichee says: "There is nothing of this infant except the good soul to be freed; the rest, pertaining not to the good God but to the prince of darkness, is to be rejected." The Pelagian says: "Nay, of this infant nothing is to be freed, because we show the whole to be safe." Both lie; but the accuser of the flesh alone is now milder, because the praiser is convicted of raging over the whole. But neither does the Manichee help the human soul by blaspheming God the author of the whole man, nor does the Pelagian allow the divine medicine to help human infancy by denying original sin. God, therefore, has mercy through the Catholic faith, which, refuting both ruins, helps the infant unto salvation, saying to the Manichees: "Hear the Apostle crying: *Do you not know that your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you?*[^7], and believe that the good God is the creator of bodies too, because the work of the prince of darkness cannot be the temple of the Holy Spirit"; saying to the Pelagians: "In iniquities he was conceived, and in sins his mother nourished him in the womb, whom you behold a little one. Why, by defending him as though free from all harm, do you not allow him to be freed through pardon? *No one is clean from filth, not even an infant whose life is of one day upon the earth*[^8]. Allow the wretched one to receive the remission of sins through him who alone, neither small nor great, could have sin."

**4. 4. 5.** What, then, does it profit them that they say, "All sin descends not from nature but from will," and resist by the truth of this statement the Manichees, who say that an evil nature is the cause of sin, when, unwilling to admit original sin—although it too, without doubt, descends from the will of the first man—they make little ones depart from the body guilty? What does it profit them "to confess that baptism is necessary for all ages," which the Manichees say is superfluous for every age, when these men say it is false in the case of little ones, so far as pertains to the remission of sins? What does it profit them that, against the Manichees, who contend that the flesh of Christ was none, or simulated, these men defend against them not only "that it was true, but that the soul itself was sprinkled with no stain of sin," when they so make the other infants equal to his infancy in no unequal purity that both he does not seem to keep, by comparison with these, his own proper holiness, and these obtain from him no salvation?

**4. 4. 6.** In that indeed which they say, "that through Adam death passed to us, not crimes," they do not have the Manichees as adversaries, because neither do these build up that original sin passed and passes, together with death, into all from the first man, who was first pure and upright in body and spirit and afterward depraved through free choice; but they say that the flesh was from the beginning created an evil body from an evil body and by an evil spirit and with an evil spirit, but that the good soul—a particle, namely, of God—on account of the merits of its own defilement through the foods and drinks in which it was previously bound up, comes into a man, and thus through intercourse is bound also by the chain of the flesh. And thereby the Manichees agree with the Pelagians that the crime of the first man did not pass into the human race, neither through the flesh, which they say was never good, nor through the soul, which they declare comes into the flesh of man with the merits of its own defilements by which it was polluted before the flesh. But how do the Pelagians say "that death alone passed to us through Adam"? For if we die for this reason, because he died—but he died because he sinned—they say that the punishment passes without fault, and that innocent little ones are punished by an unjust penalty, contracting death without the merits of death. This the Catholic faith knows about the one and only mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who deigned for us to undergo death, that is, the penalty of sin, without sin. For just as he alone was made the son of man, that we through him might be made the sons of God, so he alone took up for us, without evil merits, the penalty, that we through him might obtain, without good merits, grace; because, just as nothing of good was owed to us, so neither anything of evil to him. Commending, therefore, his love toward those to whom he was about to give undeserved life, he willed to suffer for them an undeserved death. This singular prerogative of the Mediator the Pelagians try to make void, so that it is now no longer singular in the Lord, if Adam so suffered owed death on account of his fault that the little ones, contracting no fault from him, suffer undeserved death. For although much good is conferred on the good through death, whence some have fittingly even discussed the good of death, yet what is here to be proclaimed except the mercy of God, that the penalty of sin is turned to good uses?

**4. 4. 7.** But these men say these things while they wish to twist men, from the words of the Apostle, into their own sense. For where the Apostle says: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all men*, there they wish it to be understood that not sin passed but death. What, then, is that which follows: *in whom all sinned*[^9]? For the Apostle says either that all sinned in that one man, of whom he had said *Through one man sin entered into the world*, or in that sin, or surely in death. For it ought not to disturb us that he did not say "in which" but *in whom all sinned*; for death, in the Greek tongue, is of the masculine gender. Let them choose whichever they will. For either "in that man all sinned" was said for this reason, that when he sinned all were in him; or in that sin all sinned, because what those being born were going to contract became generally the deed of all; or it remains that they say all sinned in that death. But how this can be understood I do not plainly see. For men die in sin, not in death that sins; for sin going before, death follows, not death going before, sin. For *the sting of death is sin*[^10]—that is, the sting by whose pricking death comes about, not the sting with which death pricks, just as poison, if it is drunk, is called the cup of death, because by that cup death was caused, not because the cup was made by death or was given by death. But if, for this reason, sin cannot be understood by those words of the Apostle, *in whom all sinned*, because in the Greek, from which the letter was translated, sin is put in the feminine gender, it remains that all be understood to have sinned in that first man, because in him all were when he sinned, whence sin is contracted by being born, which is not loosed except by being reborn. For thus too holy Hilary understood what is written, *in whom all sinned*; for he says: *In whom, that is, in Adam, all sinned*. Then he added: *It is manifest that all sinned in Adam as in a mass; for he himself, corrupted through sin, all whom he begot were born under sin*. Writing these things, Hilary without ambiguity reminded us how it was to be understood, *in whom all sinned*.

**4. 4. 8.** But on what account does the same Apostle say that we are reconciled to God through Christ, except on account of the fact that we had been made enemies? And what is this except sin? Whence the Prophet too says: *Your sins separate between you and God*[^11]. On account of this separation, therefore, the Mediator was sent, to take away the sin of the world, by which, as enemies, we were separated, and that, reconciled, we might from enemies be made sons. From here surely the Apostle was speaking; from here it came about that he interposed what he says, that *through one man sin entered*. For these are his words above: *But God commends his love toward us*, he says, *in that, while we were still sinners, Christ died for us; much more, then, being now justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him. For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, shall we be saved in his life; and not only so, but glorying also in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation*[^12]. Then he adds: *On account of this, just as through one man sin entered into this world and through sin death, and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^13]. Why do the Pelagians shuffle? If reconciliation through Christ is necessary for all, sin passed through all, by which we were enemies, so that we had need to be reconciled. This reconciliation is in the washing of regeneration and in the flesh and blood of Christ, without which not even little ones can have life in themselves. For just as there was one unto death on account of sin, so there is one unto life on account of righteousness, because, *as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive*[^14]; and: *As through the offense of one, unto all men, unto condemnation; so also through the justification of one, unto all men, unto justification of life*[^15]. Who, against these apostolic words, has become deaf with so great a hardness of wicked impiety that, having heard these things, he contends that death passed into us through Adam without sin, except the assailants of the grace of God, the enemies of the cross of Christ, *whose end is destruction*[^16], if they persist in this obstinacy? But let it suffice to have said these things on account of that serpentine craftiness of theirs, by which they wish to corrupt simple minds and turn them away from the chastity of the Catholic faith under the guise, as it were, of the praise of the creature.

**4. 5. 9.** But further, concerning the praise of marriage, what does it profit them that, against the Manichees, who assign marriage not to the true and good God but to the prince of darkness, these men oppose them with words of true piety, saying: "Because the Lord speaks in the Gospel: *He who made them from the beginning made them male and female, and he said: Increase and multiply and fill the earth. What therefore God has joined together, let not man put asunder*"[^17]? What does it profit them to seduce, through the true, to the false? For they say this so that infants may be believed to be born free from all harm, and thus there be no need for them to be reconciled to God through Christ, since they have no original sin, on account of which reconciliation is necessary for all through the one who came into the world without sin, just as the enmities of all were caused through the one through whom sin entered into the world. Which is believed in a Catholic manner for the saving of the nature of men, with the praise of marriage preserved, because the praise of marriage is the just coupling of the sexes, not the iniquitous defense of vices. And thereby, when these men, by praising marriage, wish to draw men over from the Manichees to themselves, they desire to change their disease for them, not to heal it.

**4. 5. 10.** Again, in the praise of the Law, what does it profit them that they say the truth against the Manichees, when from this they wish to lead to that which they falsely think against Catholics? For they say: *We confess also that the old Law, according to the Apostle, is just and holy and good, which could confer perpetual life on those who keep its commandments and live justly through faith, as on the Prophets and Patriarchs and all the saints*. By which words, most cunningly placed, they praise the Law against grace. For that Law, although holy and just and good, could confer perpetual life not on all those men of God, but faith, which is in Christ, could. For this faith works through love, not according to the letter which kills, but according to the Spirit which gives life; to which grace of God the Law leads, terrifying by transgression as a pedagogue, so that there may be conferred on a man what it itself could not confer. For to those words of theirs, by which they say "the Law could confer perpetual life on the Prophets and Patriarchs and all the saints who keep its commandments," the Apostle answers: *If righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died in vain*[^18]. *If the inheritance is from the Law, it is now no longer from the promise*[^19]. *If those who are through the Law are heirs, faith is made empty and the promise is made void*[^20]. *But that in the Law no one is justified before God is manifest, because the just man lives by faith; but the Law is not of faith, but he who does these things shall live in them*[^21]. This testimony from the Law recalled by the Apostle is understood either on account of temporal life, for fear of losing which men did the works of the Law, not out of faith, because transgressors of the Law were commanded by that same Law to be killed by the people; or, if it is to be understood more deeply that it was written on account of eternal life, *He who does these things shall live in them*, then the command of the Law was so expressed that the weakness of man, failing in itself for doing the things which the Law commands, might rather seek the help of the grace of God out of faith, by whose mercy even faith itself is given. For faith is so had, *as God has apportioned to each the measure of faith*[^22]. For if men have not of themselves but receive the spirit of power and of love and of self-control—whence the same Teacher of the Gentiles says: *For we have not received the spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of self-control*[^23]—surely there is received also the spirit of faith, of which he says: *But having the same spirit of faith*[^24]. Truly, then, the Law says: *He who does these things shall live in them*. But that one may do them and live in them, not the Law which commands this but faith which obtains this is necessary. Yet this faith, that it may merit to receive these things, is itself given freely.

**4. 5. 11.** But nowhere do these enemies of grace contrive more hidden ambushes for more violently assailing that same grace than where they praise the Law, which without doubt is to be praised. For by diverse modes of speaking and by a variety of words, in all their disputations they wish grace to be understood as the Law—namely, that from the Lord God we have the help of knowledge, by which we may know the things which are to be done, not the inspiration of love, that we may do, with holy love, the things known, which is properly grace. For the knowledge of the Law without love puffs up, does not build up, according to the same Apostle saying most openly: *Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up*[^25]. Which sentence is like that by which it was said: *The letter kills, but the spirit gives life*[^26]. For such as is *knowledge puffs up* is *the letter kills*; such as is *love builds up* is *the spirit gives life*, because *the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^27]. The knowledge of the Law, therefore, makes a proud transgressor, but through the gift of love it delights one to be a doer of the Law. We do not, then, make void the Law through faith, *but we establish the Law*[^28], which, by terrifying, leads to faith. For the Law works wrath for this reason, that, to one terrified and turned to fulfilling the righteousness of the Law, the mercy of God may bestow grace through our Lord Jesus Christ, who is the wisdom of God, of which it is written: *She carries the Law and mercy on her tongue*[^29]—the Law by which she terrifies, the mercy by which she helps; the Law through a servant, the mercy through herself; the Law as in the staff which Elisha sent to raise the widow's son and he did not rise—*For if a law had been given which could give life, righteousness would indeed be entirely from the Law*[^30]—but the mercy as in Elisha himself, who, bearing the figure of Christ, was joined to the dead one to be made alive, by the signification of a great sacrament, as of the New Testament.

**4. 6. 12.** Likewise, that against the Manichees they praise free choice, employing the prophetic testimony: *If you are willing and will hear me, you shall eat the good things of the land; but if you are unwilling and will not hear me, the sword shall consume you*[^31]—what does this profit them? Since indeed they not so much defend free choice against the Manichees as exalt it against Catholics. For they so wish what was said—*If you are willing and will hear me*—to be understood, as though in that preceding will there were the merit of the consequent grace, and thus grace be now no longer grace, which is not gratuitous when it is rendered as owed. But if they so understood what was said, *If you are willing*, that they confessed that even that good will is prepared by him of whom it is written *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^32], then, as Catholics, they would use this testimony, and not only would they conquer the old heresy of the Manichees but they would not found the new one of the Pelagians.

**4. 6. 13.** What does it profit them that, in praise of free choice itself, they say, "Grace helps the good purpose of each"? This would be received without scruple as said in a Catholic way, if they did not place merit in the good purpose, to which merit the reward would now be rendered according to debt, not according to grace, but understood and confessed that even the good purpose itself, which a consequent grace helps, could not have been in a man if grace did not precede. For how is there a good purpose of a man without the Lord first showing mercy, since the good will itself is what is prepared by the Lord? But that when they had said, "Grace also helps the good purpose of each," they soon added, "yet does not instill zeal for virtue into one who resists"—this could be well understood, if it were not said by these men, whose meaning is known. For to one resisting, the very hearing of the divine calling is procured by the grace of God itself, and then in him, now no longer resisting, zeal for virtue is kindled. But truly, in all the things which anyone does according to God, *his mercy goes before him*[^33], which these men do not wish, because they wish to be not Catholics but Pelagians. For it greatly delights proud impiety that even that which it is compelled to confess given by the Lord should seem to be not bestowed on it but rendered to it—namely, that the sons of perdition, not of promise, may be believed to have made themselves good, and that God has now rendered the owed reward, for this work of theirs, to those made good by themselves.

**4. 6. 14.** For their very swelling has so blocked up the ears of their heart that they do not hear: *For what do you have that you have not received?*[^34]; do not hear: *Without me you can do nothing*[^35]; do not hear: *Love is from God*[^36]; do not hear: *God has apportioned the measure of faith*[^37]; do not hear: *The Spirit breathes where he wills*[^38]; and: *Those who are led by the Spirit of God, these are the sons of God*[^39]; do not hear: *No one can come to me unless it has been given to him by my Father*[^40]; do not hear what Ezra writes: *Blessed is the Lord of our fathers, who put these things into the heart of the king, to glorify his house which is in Jerusalem*[^41]; do not hear what the Lord says through Jeremiah: *And I will put my fear into their heart, that they may not depart from me, and I will visit them, that I may make them good*[^42]; and especially that through the prophet Ezekiel, where God shows plainly that he is provoked by no good merits of men to make them good, that is, obedient to his commandments, but rather that he repays these goods to them for evils, doing this for his own sake, not for theirs. For he says: *Thus says the Lord: I do this not for your sake, O house of Israel, but for the sake of my holy name, which you profaned among the nations, into which you entered. And I will sanctify my great name, which has been profaned among the nations, and which you profaned in their midst, and the nations will know that I am the Lord, says the Lord Adonai, when I will have been sanctified in you before their eyes. And I will take you from among the nations and gather you out of all lands, and bring you into your own land. And I will sprinkle clean water upon you, and you will be cleansed from all your uncleannesses, and I will cleanse you, and I will give you a new heart and a new spirit I will put within you; and the heart of stone will be taken away from your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit within you and will cause you to walk in my judgments and to keep my decrees and to do them*[^43]. And after a little, through the same Prophet: *Not for your sake*, he says, *do I do it, says the Lord God, be it known to you. Be confounded and ashamed of your ways, O house of Israel. Thus says the Lord God: In the day in which I will cleanse you from all your iniquities, and will resettle the cities, and the deserts will be built up, and the desolated land will be tilled, whereas it was desolated before the eyes of every passer-by, and they will say: That desolated land has become like a garden of delight, and the deserted and desolated and dug-up cities sit fortified; and the nations, whoever have been left around you, will know that I the Lord have built up the destroyed places, have planted the desolated ones; I the Lord have spoken and have done it. Thus says the Lord: Yet for this too will I be sought by the house of Israel, that I may do for them: I will multiply them like sheep—men, like holy sheep, like the sheep of Jerusalem in her feast days; so will those desolated cities be full of sheep—men, and they will know that I am the Lord*[^44].

**4. 6. 15.** What has remained to the dead skin, whence it can be inflated and disdain, when it glories, to glory in the Lord? What has remained to it, when, whatever it shall have said that it did, so that, with the merit of man preceding and arising from man, God should consequently do that of which man is worthy, it will be answered, it will be cried out against, it will be contradicted: *I do it, but for the sake of my holy name, not for your sake do I do it, says the Lord God*[^45]? Nothing so overthrows the Pelagians saying "the grace of God is given according to our merits"—which indeed Pelagius himself, even if not by correcting himself, yet by fearing the Eastern judges, condemned—nothing so overthrows this presumption of those saying, "We do that we may merit those with whom the Lord may do." There answers you not Pelagius but the Lord himself: *I do it, and not for your sake, but for the sake of my holy name*. For what can you do of good from a heart that is not good? But that you may have a good heart: *I will give you*, he says, *a new heart, and a new spirit I will give you*. Can you say: We first walked in his judgments and kept his judgment and acted so as to be worthy to whom he would give his grace? For what good would you, evil men, do, and how would you do these good things unless you were good? But that men may be good, who makes it except he who said *And I will visit them, that I may make them good*; and who said *And I will put my Spirit within you and will cause you to walk in my judgments and to keep my decrees and to do them*? Do you not yet awaken? Do you not yet hear: *I will cause you to walk, I will cause you to keep*, and finally *I will cause you to do*? Why do you still inflate yourselves? We indeed walk—it is true—we keep, we do; but he causes that we walk, that we keep, that we do. This is the grace of God making us good; this is his mercy going before us. What do the deserts and desolated and dug-up places merit?—which yet will be built up and tilled and fortified. Are these things for the merits of their desertion, desolation, digging up? Far be it! For those merits are evil, these gifts good. Goods are therefore rendered to the evil; gratuitous, then, not owed, and therefore grace. *I*, says the Lord, *I the Lord*. Does not this voice repress you, O human pride, who say, "I do it, that I may merit to be built up and planted by the Lord"? Do you not hear: *Not for your sake do I do it? I the Lord have built up the destroyed places, have planted the desolated ones; I the Lord have spoken and have done it*—yet *not for your sake, but for the sake of my holy name*? Who multiplies men like sheep, like holy sheep, like the sheep of Jerusalem? Who causes those desolated cities to be full of sheep—men, except he who follows and says *And they will know that I am the Lord*? But with what sheep—men does he fill the cities, as he has promised: whether those he finds or those he makes? Let us ask the Psalm. Behold, it answers; let us hear: *Come, let us adore and fall down before him, and let us weep before the Lord who made us, for he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture and the sheep of his hand*[^46]. He himself, then, makes the sheep with which he fills the desolated cities. What wonder? Since to that one sheep, that is, the Church, whose members are all the sheep—men, it is said: For I am the Lord who makes you. Why do you hold out to me free choice, which will not be free for doing righteousness unless you have been a sheep? He, therefore, who makes men sheep, he himself frees human wills unto the obedience of piety.

**4. 6. 16.** But why he makes these men sheep and does not make those, he with whom there is no respect of persons—this is the very question which the blessed Apostle, to those who propose it more curiously than capably, says: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing molded say to him who molded it: Why have you made me thus?*[^47] This is the very question which pertains to that depth which the same Apostle, wishing to look into it, in a way was terrified and cried out: *O depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and his ways past finding out! For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid to him? For of him and through him and in him are all things; to him be glory forever and ever*[^48]. Let them not, therefore, dare to scrutinize this unsearchable question, who, defending merit before grace and therefore now against grace, wish to give first to God, that it may be repaid to them—to give first, surely, something out of free choice, that there may be grace to be repaid as a reward—and let them wisely understand, or faithfully believe, that even what they suppose they have given first they have received from him of whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things. But why this one receives, that one does not receive, when both do not merit to receive, and whichever of them receives, receives undeservedly—let them measure their own strength and not scrutinize things stronger than themselves. Let it suffice them to know that there is no iniquity with God. For when the Apostle had found no merits by which Jacob the twin might precede his brother before God: *What then shall we say?* he says; *is there iniquity with God? God forbid! For he says to Moses: I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I will be merciful. So then it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^49]. Let his gratuitous mercy, then, be pleasing to us, even if this deep question remains unresolved. Which, however, is resolved only so far as the same Apostle resolves it, saying: *But if God, willing to show his wrath and to make known his power, bore in much patience the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for perdition, and that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared unto glory*[^50]. For wrath is not rendered except as owed, lest there be iniquity with God; but mercy, even when it is offered unowed, is not iniquity with God. And from this the vessels of mercy understand how gratuitous a mercy is offered to them, in that to the vessels of wrath, with whom the cause of perdition and the mass is common to them, owed and just wrath is repaid. And let these things now suffice against those who, through the freedom of choice, wish to destroy the generosity of grace.

**4. 7. 17.** But as to the fact that, by the praise of the saints, they are unwilling that we, with the disposition of that Publican, hunger and thirst for righteousness, but rather, with the vanity of that Pharisee, belch as though satisfied and full, what does it profit them that, against the Manichees, who destroy baptism, these men say "men are perfectly renewed by baptism," and to this employ the testimony of the Apostle, who testifies that through the washing of water the Church is made holy and immaculate from among the Gentiles, when with a proud and perverse sense they put forth their disputations against the prayers of the Church herself? For they say this so that the Church may be believed, after holy baptism, in which there comes about the remission of all sins, to have no further sin, when against them she, from the rising of the sun to its setting, in all her members cries to God: *Forgive us our debts*[^51]. What of the fact that even about themselves, if they are questioned in this cause, they find nothing to answer? For if they say that they have no sin, John answers them that they deceive themselves and the truth is not in them. But if they confess their sins, since they wish to be members of the body of Christ, how will that body, that is, the Church, be in this present time perfectly, as these men think, without spot or wrinkle, whose members confess, not falsely, that they have sins? Wherefore both in baptism all sins are remitted, and through that same washing of water in the word the Church is presented to Christ without spot or wrinkle, because, unless she had been baptized, she would say fruitlessly *Forgive us our debts*, until she is brought to glory, where more perfectly no spot or wrinkle will be in her.

**4. 7. 18.** It is to be confessed that "the Holy Spirit" too, "even in ancient times," not only "helped good minds," which these men too wish, but also made them good, which they do not wish. There is no doubt too that "all the Prophets and Apostles, or saints, both of the Gospel and ancient, to whom God bears witness, were just not in comparison with the wicked but by the rule of virtues." Which is against the Manichees, who blaspheme the Patriarchs and Prophets; but which is against the Pelagians too, since all of them, questioned about themselves while they lived in this body, would answer with one most harmonious voice: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us*[^52]. "But in the time to come" it is not to be denied "that there is a reward both of good works and of evil, and that no one is commanded to fulfill there the commandments which here he despised"; but the fullness of perfect righteousness, where sin cannot be, which here is hungered and thirsted for by the saints, is here to be hoped for in the precept, there to be received in the reward, with almsgiving and prayers obtaining that what here was less fulfilled of the commandments may be made unpunished through the forgiveness of sins.

**4. 7. 19.** These things being so, let the Pelagians cease, by the most treacherous praises of these five matters—that is, "the praise of the creature, the praise of marriage, the praise of the Law, the praise of free choice, the praise of the saints"—to pretend that they wish to rescue men, as it were, from the snares of the Manichees, in order that they may entangle them in their own nets; that is, in order that they may deny original sin and begrudge little ones the help of Christ the physician, and that they may say that the grace of God is given according to our merits, and thus grace be now no longer grace, and that they may say that the saints in this life had no sin, and thus the prayer be made void which he handed to the saints who had no sin, and through whom all sin is remitted to the saints when they pray. To these three evils they seduce incautious and unlearned men by the fraudulent praise of those five goods. About all of which I judge that I have sufficiently answered their most cruel and most impious and most proud vanity.

**4. 8. 20.** But since they say: "Our enemies have taken up our sayings in hatred of the truth, and throughout the whole West a dogma no less foolish than impious has been taken up, and they complain that a subscription was extorted from simple bishops, sitting in their own places without the gathering of a synod, for confirming this"—when rather the Church of Christ, both of the West and of the East, has shrunk in horror from their profane novelties of words—I judge it to pertain to my care not only to bring forward the holy canonical Scriptures as witnesses against them, which we have already done sufficiently, but also to bring forth some documents from the writings of the saints, who treated them before us with most widespread renown and immense glory; not that the authority of any disputant is made equal by us to the canonical Books, as though there were not at all something that might be thought better or more truly by one Catholic than by another likewise Catholic, but that those who suppose these men to say something may be admonished how, about these matters, before the new empty talk of these men, the Catholic bishops, following the divine words, thought, and that they may know that by us the right and anciently founded Catholic faith is defended against the recent presumption and ruin of the Pelagian heretics.

**4. 8. 21.** The most blessed Cyprian, most glorious also by the crown of martyrdom, most well known not only to the African and Western but also to the Eastern Churches by the proclamation of fame and the writings that spread him far and wide—even the very heresiarch of these men, Pelagius, recalls him with assuredly due honor, where, writing a book of *Testimonies*, he asserts that he is imitating him, saying that "he does to Romanus what that man did to Quirinus." Let us see, then, what Cyprian thought about original sin, *which through one man entered into the world*. In the letter *On the Work and Almsgiving* he speaks thus: *When the Lord, in his coming, had healed those wounds which Adam had borne and had cured the ancient poisons of the serpent, he gave a law to the healed man and commanded him not to sin any more thereafter, lest something graver befall the one sinning. We had been hemmed in and shut up in the narrowness of innocence by the prescription. Nor would the weakness and feebleness of human frailty have anything to do, had not the divine piety again coming to help, by showing the works of justice and mercy, opened a certain way of guarding salvation, so that whatever filth we afterward contract we may wash away by almsgiving*. By this testimony this witness refutes two falsehoods of these men: one, by which they say that the human race contracts nothing of vices from Adam which is to be cured and healed through Christ; the other, by which they say that the saints have no sin after baptism. Again, in the same letter: *Let each one*, he says, *set before his eyes the devil with his servants, that is, with the people of perdition and death, leaping into the midst, to provoke the people of Christ, with Christ himself present and judging, to the examination of a comparison, saying: For these whom you see with me I neither received blows nor endured scourgings nor bore a cross nor shed blood nor redeemed my household by the price of suffering and gore; but neither do I promise them the heavenly kingdom nor call them back again to paradise, with immortality restored*. Let the Pelagians answer when we were in the immortality of paradise and how we were expelled from there, that we may be called back to it by the grace of Christ. And when they cannot find what to answer here for their perversity, let them attend how Cyprian understood what the Apostle says, *in whom all sinned*, and let the new Pelagian heretics not dare to pour out any slander on any Catholic, drawn from the old Manichean heretics, lest they be convicted of doing so wicked an injury even to the ancient martyr Cyprian.

**4. 8. 22.** For he says this too in the letter which is entitled *On Mortality*, thus: *The kingdom of God, most beloved brethren, has begun to be at hand; the reward of life and the joy of eternal salvation and perpetual gladness and the possession of paradise lately lost are now coming, with the world passing away*. This again in the same: *Let us embrace*, he says, *the day which assigns each to his dwelling, which, when we have been snatched from here and loosed from worldly snares, restores us to paradise and the kingdom*. Likewise, in the letter *On Patience*: *Let the sentence of God be considered*, he says, *which Adam, forgetful of the precept and a transgressor of the law given, received straightway at the origin of the world and of the human race. Then we will know how patient we ought to be in this world, we who are born in such a way that here we labor under afflictions and conflicts. "Because you have heard the voice of your wife and have eaten from that tree, from which alone I had commanded you not to eat, the earth will be cursed in all your works. In sadness and groaning you will eat from it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it will cast forth for you, and you will eat the fodder of the field. In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you return to the earth from which you were taken, since you are earth and into earth you will go." Bound and constrained by the chain of this sentence we all are, until, death being paid out, we depart from this world*. And likewise, in the same: *For since, in that*, he says, *first transgression of the precept, the firmness of the body departed together with immortality, and with death came weakness, nor can firmness be received again unless immortality too has been received, it is necessary always to wrestle and contend in this frailty and weakness of the body. Which wrestling and contending cannot be sustained except by the strength of patience*.

**4. 8. 23.** But in the letter which he wrote to Bishop Fidus, together with his sixty-six fellow bishops, by whom he had been consulted on account of the law of circumcision, whether it was permitted to baptize an infant before the eighth day, this cause is treated in such a way as though, by divine provision, the Catholic Church were already confuting the Pelagian heretics, destined to come into being so long afterward. For he who had consulted did not on that account doubt whether those being born contract original sin, which they wash away by being reborn—for far be it that the Christian faith should ever have doubted this!—but he doubted whether the washing of regeneration, by which he did not doubt that original sin was to be loosed, ought to be administered before the eighth day. Answering this consultation, the most blessed Cyprian says: *But as to the cause of the infants, whom you said ought not to be baptized within the second or third day after they are born, and that the law of ancient circumcision was to be considered, so that you thought one who is born ought not to be baptized and sanctified within the eighth day—it seemed far otherwise to our whole council. For in this which you thought ought to be done, no one consented, but we all rather judged that the grace of the merciful God is to be denied to no man born. For since the Lord in his Gospel says: "The Son of man did not come to destroy souls but to save them," so far as is in us, if it can be done, no soul is to be destroyed*. And a little later: *Nor ought any of us*, he says, *to shudder at this which the Lord deigned to do. For although an infant is still new from birth, yet it is not so that anyone ought to shudder to kiss him in the giving of grace and the making of peace, since in the kiss of an infant each one of us ought, by his own religion, to think of the very recent hands of God, which in a man only just formed and recently born we in a way kiss, when we embrace that which God has made*. Likewise, a little later: *But if anything could hinder men from obtaining grace, the graver sins could rather hinder the adult and the advanced and the older. Furthermore, if even to the gravest offenders and to those who sinned much before against God, when they have afterward believed, the remission of sins is given, and no one is barred from baptism and grace, how much more ought not an infant to be barred, who, recently born, has sinned in nothing except that, born carnally according to Adam, he has contracted by his first nativity the contagion of the ancient death—who comes the more easily, for this very reason, to receive the remission of sins, in that what are remitted to him are not his own but another's sins!*

**4. 8. 24.** What will they say to these things, not only the deserters but even the persecutors of the grace of God? What will they say to these things? In what way is the possession of paradise restored to us? How are we restored to paradise, if we were never there? Or how were we there, except because we were in Adam? And how do we pertain to the sentence which was spoken against the transgressor, if we do not contract the harm from the transgressor? Finally, he judges that they are to be baptized even before the eighth day, lest the souls of little ones perish through the contagion of the ancient death contracted by the first nativity. How do they perish, if those who are born even of faithful parents are not held by the devil until they are reborn in Christ and, rescued from the power of darkness, are transferred into his kingdom? And who says that the souls of those being born, unless they are reborn, will perish? Surely he who so praises the creator and the creature, the maker and the work, that, by interposing the veneration of the Creator himself, he checks and corrects the horror of human sense by which men disdain to kiss little ones recent from the womb, saying that in the kiss of that age the recent hands of God are to be thought of. Did he, then, by confessing original sin, either condemn nature or accuse marriage? Did he, because to one born guilty from Adam he applied the purgation of regeneration, therefore deny that God is the founder of those being born? Did he, because, fearing that the souls of any age perish, he judged, with the council of his colleagues, that they were to be freed even before the eighth day by the sacrament of baptism, therefore accuse marriage—since, in the infant, whether from marriage or from adultery, yet because a man is born, he showed the recent hands of God worthy even of the kiss of peace? If, then, the holy bishop and most glorious martyr Cyprian could judge that original sin in infants is to be healed by the medicine of Christ, with the praise of the creature preserved, with the praise of marriage preserved, why does the new pestilence, since it does not dare to call him a Manichee, think that another's crime is to be charged against the Catholics who defend these things, in order to cover its own? Behold, a most renowned treater of the divine words, before even the slightest odor of the Manichean pestilence had touched our lands, without any disparagement of the divine work and of marriage, confesses original sin, not saying that Christ was sprinkled with any stain of sin, nor yet comparing to him the flesh of sin in the other infants, to whom through the likeness of the flesh of sin he furnishes the help of cleansing, nor is he terrified by the obscure question of the origin of souls from confessing that those who are freed by the grace of Christ return to paradise. Does he say that the condition of death passed into men from Adam without the contagion of sin? For it is not on account of avoiding the death of the body, but on account of the sin which through one entered into the world, that he says little ones, however recent from the womb, are helped through baptism.

**4. 9. 25.** But now how Cyprian proclaims the grace of God against these men, where he discusses *On the Lord's Prayer*, plainly appears. For he says: *We say: "Hallowed be your name," not that we wish God to be hallowed by our prayers, but that we ask of God that his name be hallowed in us. But by whom is God hallowed, who himself hallows? But because he himself said: "Be holy, for I too am holy," we ask and request this, that we who have been hallowed in baptism may persevere in what we have begun to be*. And in another place in the same letter: *We add also*, he says, *and say: "Your will be done in heaven and on earth," not that God may do what he wills, but that we may be able to do what God wills. For who resists God, that he may not do what he wills? But because we are resisted by the devil, that our mind and act may not in all things obey God, we pray and ask that the will of God be done in us. And that it may be done in us, there is need of the will of God, that is, of his aid and protection, because no one is strong by his own strength, but is safe by the indulgence and mercy of God*. Likewise in another place: *But we ask*, he says, *that the will of God be done in heaven and on earth, both of which pertain to the consummation of our well-being and salvation. For since we possess a body from the earth and a spirit from heaven, we ourselves are earth and heaven, and in both, that is, in body and spirit, we pray that the will of God be done. For there is a struggle between flesh and spirit, and a daily contention of them disagreeing against each other, so that we do not do the very things that we will, while the spirit seeks heavenly and divine things, the flesh covets earthly and worldly things. And therefore we ask that, by the aid and help of God, concord be made between these two, so that, while the will of God is carried out both in spirit and in flesh, the soul which has been reborn through him may be preserved. Which the apostle Paul openly and manifestly declares with his own voice: "The flesh," he says, "lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the very things that you will"*. And a little later: *It can also be so understood*, he says, *most beloved brethren, that, since the Lord commands and teaches us to love even our enemies and to pray also for those who persecute us, we ask also for those who are still earth and have not yet begun to be heavenly, that even concerning them the will of God may be done, which Christ accomplished by preserving and restoring man*. And likewise in another place: *But we ask that this bread*, he says, *be given to us daily, lest we who are in Christ and daily receive the Eucharist as the food of salvation, with some graver offense intervening, while we are kept off and, not communicating, are barred from the heavenly bread, be separated from the body of Christ*. And somewhat later in the same: *But when we ask*, he says, *that we may not come into temptation, we are admonished of our weakness and feebleness, while we ask thus, that no one may insolently lift himself up, that no one may proudly and arrogantly take anything to himself, that no one may count as his own the glory either of confession or of suffering, since the Lord himself, teaching humility, said: "Watch and pray, that you may not come into temptation; the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak"; so that, while a humble and submissive confession goes before and everything is given to God, whatever is suppliantly asked with fear and honor of God may be granted by his own piety*. Likewise, *To Quirinus*, in which work Pelagius wishes to seem his imitator, he says in the third book: *That in nothing is one to glory, since nothing is ours*. To which proposition, adding the divine testimonies, he set down among the rest that apostolic one by which the mouths of these men especially are to be shut: *For what do you have that you have not received? But if you have received it, why do you glory as if you had not received it?* Likewise in the letter *On Patience*: *For that virtue is*, he says, *common to us with God; thence patience begins, thence its brightness and dignity takes its head, the origin and greatness of patience proceeds with God as author*.

**4. 9. 26.** Does this holy and so memorable instructor of the Churches in the word of truth deny that there is free choice in men, because he attributes wholly to God that we live rightly? Does he find fault with the Law of God, because he signifies that a man is not justified by it, since indeed he declares that what it commands is to be obtained from the Lord God by prayers? Does he assert fate under the name of grace, although he says: "In nothing is one to glory, since nothing is ours"? Does he, like these men, believe the Holy Spirit to be a helper of virtue in such a way that the very virtue which is thus helped arises from us, when, asserting that nothing is ours, he recalls that the Apostle on this account said: *For what do you have that you have not received?* And does he say that the most excellent virtue, that is, patience, begins from us and is then helped by the Spirit of God—or rather that it takes its head, its origin, from him? Finally, he confesses that neither a good purpose nor zeal for virtue nor good minds begin to be in men without the grace of God, when he says: "In nothing is one to glory, since nothing is ours." What is so set in free choice as that which the Law says: not to adore an idol, not to commit adultery, not to perpetrate murder? But these and such are crimes which, if anyone has committed them, he is removed from the communion of the body of Christ. And yet, if the most blessed Cyprian thought that for not committing these our will suffices, he would not so understand what we say in the Lord's prayer, *Give us this day our daily bread*, as to assert that we ask this lest "with some graver offense intervening, while we are kept off and, not communicating, are barred from the heavenly bread, we be separated from the body of Christ." Let the new heretics surely answer what good merits precede in men who are enemies of the Christian name. For not only do they not have a good merit, but they have even a most evil one. And yet even so Cyprian understands what we say in the prayer, *Your will be done in heaven and on earth*, that we pray also for those very men who on this account are understood as earth. We pray, then, not only for the unwilling, but even for those who resist and assail. What, then, do we ask, except that from unwilling they become willing, from resisting consenting, from assailing loving? From whom except from him of whom it is written *The will is prepared by the Lord*? Let those learn to be Catholics who disdain, if they do anything of evil and if they do anything of good, to glory not in themselves but in the Lord.

**4. 10. 27.** Let us now see that third matter, at which no less, in these men, does every member of Christ and his whole body shudder, because they contend that there are or were in this life just men having no sin at all. By which presumption they most openly contradict the Lord's prayer, in which all the members of Christ cry, with a truthful heart and daily voices: *Forgive us our debts*[^53]. Let us see, then, what about this too Cyprian, most glorious in the Lord, thought; what, for the instructing of the Churches—not of the Manichees, surely, but of Catholics—he not only said but even committed to writing and memory. In the letter *On the Work and Almsgiving*: *Let us therefore acknowledge, dearest brethren*, he says, *the wholesome gift of the divine indulgence, and let us cure our wounds with spiritual remedies for cleansing and purging our sins, we who cannot be without some wound of conscience. Nor let anyone so flatter himself about a pure and immaculate breast that, relying on his innocence, he thinks medicine ought not to be applied to his wounds, since it is written: Who will glory that he has a chaste heart, or who will glory that he is clean from sins? And again, John sets down and says in his letter: "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us." But if no one can be without sin, and whoever has said himself blameless is either proud or foolish, how necessary, how kind is the divine clemency, which, since it knows that to the healed certain wounds afterward are not lacking, gave wholesome remedies for curing and healing the wounds anew!* Again in the same: *And since daily*, he says, *that cannot be lacking which is sinned in the sight of God, the daily sacrifices were not lacking, by which sins could be wiped away*. Likewise in the letter *On Mortality*: *We have a struggle with avarice*, he says, *with unchastity, with anger, with ambition; we have a constant and troublesome wrestling with carnal vices, with worldly enticements; the mind of man, besieged and on every side hedged about by the assault of the devil, scarcely meets them one by one, scarcely resists. If avarice is laid low, lust rises up; if lust is repressed, ambition succeeds; if ambition is despised, anger exasperates, pride inflates, drunkenness invites, envy breaks concord, jealousy cuts off friendship; you are forced to curse, which the divine law forbids, you are compelled to swear, which is not lawful. The mind suffers so many persecutions daily, the breast is pressed with so many perils: and does it delight to stand long here among the swords of the devil, when rather it should be desired and wished to hasten to Christ by a swifter death coming to help?* Likewise in the same: *The blessed apostle Paul*, he says, *sets down and says in his letter: "To me to live is Christ and to die is gain," counting it the greatest gain to be no longer held by worldly snares, to become no longer liable to any sins and vices of the flesh*. Likewise, *On the Lord's Prayer*, expounding what we ask when we say *Hallowed be your name*, he says among the rest: *For we have need of daily sanctification, so that we who daily fall into fault may cleanse our faults anew by constant sanctification*. Again in the same, when he expounded what we say, *Forgive us our debts*: *But how necessarily*, he says, *how providently and wholesomely are we admonished that we are sinners, since we are compelled to ask for our sins, so that, while indulgence is sought from God, the mind may recall its conscience! Lest anyone please himself as though innocent, and by exalting himself perish the more, he is instructed and taught that he sins daily, while he is commanded daily to pray for his sins. So, finally, John too in his letter admonishes, saying: "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us; but if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins"*. Deservedly too, to Quirinus, about this matter, he set forth his most absolute opinion, to which he would add the divine testimonies: *That no one is without filth and without sin*. Where he set down also those testimonies by which original sin is confirmed, which these men try to twist into I know not what new and depraved senses—whether that which holy Job says: that no one is without filth nor he whose life is of one day upon the earth; or that which is read in the Psalm: *In wickedness I was conceived, and in sins my mother nourished me in the womb*[^54]. To which testimonies, on account also of those who are now holy at a greater age, because not even they are without filth and sin, he added also that of the most blessed John, which he often recalls in many other places too: *If we say that we have no sin*[^55], and the rest of the same sentence, which by all Catholics are not kept silent against these men who deceive themselves, and in whom the truth is not.

**4. 10. 28.** Let the Pelagians say, if they dare, that this man of God was perverted by the error of the Manichees, because he so praises the saints that yet he confesses that no one in this life arrives at so great a perfection of righteousness as to have no sin at all, confirming his opinion by the clear truth of the canonical testimonies and the divine authority. For does he deny "that all sins are remitted in baptism," because he confesses that there remains a frailty and weakness whence, he says, we sin after baptism, and that up to the end of this life we have an unceasing conflict with carnal vices? Or had he therefore forgotten what the Apostle had said about the immaculate Church, because he commanded "that no one ought so to flatter himself about a pure and immaculate breast that, relying on his innocence, he thinks medicine ought not to be applied to his wounds"? I suppose that the new heretics concede that this Catholic man knows "the Holy Spirit helped good minds even in ancient times"; nay, even—what they themselves do not wish—that they could not have good minds except through the Holy Spirit. I suppose that Cyprian knew that "all the Prophets and Apostles, or any saints whatever who pleased the Lord at any time, were just not in comparison with the wicked," as these men slander us as saying, "but by the rule of virtues," as they boast that they say—Cyprian, who yet says: *No one can be without sin, and whoever has said himself blameless is either proud or foolish*. Nor does he understand it written for any other reason: *Who will glory that he has a chaste heart, or who will glory that he is clean from sins?* I suppose that Cyprian did not need to be taught by these men what he knew most excellently, "that in the time to come there is a reward of good works, but of evil works a punishment; but that no one can fulfill there the commandments which here he despised"; and yet he understands and asserts that even the apostle Paul himself, surely not a despiser of the divine commandments, said for no other reason *To me to live is Christ and to die is gain*, except because he counted it the greatest gain to be, now after this life, no longer held by worldly snares, to become no longer liable to any sins and vices of the flesh. The most blessed Cyprian, then, perceived and saw in the truth of the divine Scriptures that even the life of the Apostles themselves, although good, holy, and just, endured some bonds of worldly snares, was liable to some sins and vices of the flesh, and that therefore they desired death, that they might lack these evils and that they might arrive at that perfect righteousness which would not suffer these things, and which was now to be not done in a commandment but received in a reward. For when there shall come that which we pray for, saying *Your kingdom come*, there will not be in that kingdom of God any lack of righteousness, since the Apostle says: *For the kingdom of God is not food and drink, but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit*[^56]. Surely these three are commanded among the other divine precepts. Here righteousness is commanded us when it is said *Do righteousness*; peace is commanded when it is said *Have peace among yourselves*[^57]; joy is commanded when it is said *Rejoice in the Lord always*[^58]. Let the Pelagians, then, deny that these will be in the kingdom of God, where we shall live without end, or, if it seems good, let them rave to such a degree as to contend that righteousness, peace, joy will be there such as they are here for the just. But if they both will be and will not be such, surely the doing of them here in the precept is to be cared for, the perfection of them there in the reward is to be hoped for, where, held by no worldly snares and liable to no sins and vices of the flesh—on account of which the Apostle, as Cyprian took this testimony, said that to die was gain for him—we may perfectly love God, of whom there will be the contemplation face to face, and may perfectly love our neighbor too, when, with the thoughts of the heart made manifest, no suspicion of any evil about anyone can disturb anyone concerning anyone.

**4. 11. 29.** But now, to the most glorious martyr Cyprian, for refuting these men more fully, let us add the most blessed Ambrose, since Pelagius too so praised him that he said not even by enemies was anything found in his books to be reproved. Since, therefore, the Pelagians say that there is no original sin with which infants are born, and charge the Catholics, who resist them on behalf of the most ancient and most firm faith of the Church, with the crime of the Manichean heresy, let Ambrose answer them about this matter, a Catholic man of God and praised in the truth of the faith by Pelagius himself. Who, when he was expounding the prophet Isaiah, said: *Therefore Christ is immaculate, because not even he was stained by the usual condition of being born*. And in another place in the same work, speaking of the apostle Peter: *He himself offered*, he says, *what before he thought to be a sin, asking that not only his feet but also his head be washed, because he immediately understood that by the washing of the feet the filth of a guilty succession—those who fell in the first man—is abolished*. Likewise in the same work: *It was therefore so arranged*, he says, *that from man and woman, that is, through that intermingling of bodies, no one is seen to be free from offense; but he who is free from offense is free also from this kind of conception*. Likewise, writing against the Novatians: *All men*, he says, *are born under sin, whose very origin is in vice, as you have read, David saying: "Behold, in iniquities I was conceived, and in offenses my mother bore me"*. Likewise in the *Apology of the prophet David*: *Before we are born*, he says, *we are stained by contagion, and before the enjoyment of light we receive the injury of our very origin; in iniquity we are conceived*. Likewise, speaking of the Lord: *For it was fitting*, he says, *that he who was not going to have the sin of a bodily lapse should feel no natural contagion of generation. Rightly, then, did David tearfully bewail in himself the very defilements of nature and that the stain begins in a man before life*. Likewise, *On the Ark of Noah*: *Through the one Lord Jesus*, he says, *salvation to come for the nations is declared, who alone could be just, when all generation went astray, except that, born of a virgin, he was held by no privilege of a guilty generation. "Behold," he says, "in iniquities I was conceived, and in offenses my mother bore me," says he who was thought just above the rest. Whom, then, shall I now call just except him who is free from these bonds, whom the bonds of the common nature do not hold?* Behold a holy man, by the testimony of Pelagius too most approved in the Catholic faith, refuting the Pelagians who deny original sin by so great a manifestation, and yet, with the Manichees, neither denying that God is the founder of those being born nor accusing marriage, which God instituted and blessed.

**4. 11. 30.** The Pelagians say that merit begins from man through free choice, to which God consequently repays the help of grace. Here too let the venerable Ambrose refute them, saying in the *Exposition of the prophet Isaiah*: *Because human care without divine aid is feeble for healing, it seeks God as helper*. Likewise in the book which is entitled *On Fleeing the World*: *Frequent is our discourse*, he says, *about fleeing this world; and would that, as the discourse is easy, so were the disposition cautious and careful! But what is worse, frequently the enticement of earthly desires and the diffusion of vanities creeps in and occupies the mind, so that what you strive to avoid, this you think of and turn over in your mind. Which it is difficult for a man to beware of, but impossible to put off. Finally, that it is a matter of wishing rather than of accomplishing the Prophet testifies by saying: "Incline my heart to your testimonies, and not to avarice." For our heart and our thoughts are not in our power, which, suddenly diffused, confound the mind and the spirit and drag it elsewhere than you had proposed, recall it to worldly things, insert mundane things, bring in voluptuous things, weave in enticing things, and at the very time at which we prepare to lift up the mind, with empty thoughts inserted we are for the most part cast down to earthly things. But who is so blessed as to ascend always in his heart? But how can this be done without divine aid? In no way at all. Finally, above, the same Scripture says: Blessed is the man whose help is from you, O Lord, ascents in his heart*. What can be said more openly and more sufficiently? But lest perhaps the Pelagians answer that by this very fact, that divine aid is asked for, the merit of man precedes—saying that this itself is a merit, because by praying he becomes worthy to whom divine grace comes to help—let them attend to what the same holy man says in the *Exposition of Isaiah*: *And to pray to God*, he says, *is a spiritual grace. For no one says "the Lord Jesus" except in the Holy Spirit*. Whence too, expounding the *Gospel according to Luke*: *You see surely*, he says, *that everywhere the power of the Lord cooperates with human endeavors, so that no one can build without the Lord, no one guard without the Lord, no one begin anything without the Lord*. Does so great a man as Ambrose, because he says these things and commends the grace of God, as befits a son of promise, with grateful piety, therefore destroy free choice? Or does he wish that grace to be understood which the Pelagians, by diverse modes of speech, are unwilling should seem anything but the Law—namely, that God is believed to help us not for doing what we have come to know but for coming to know what we should do? If they suppose this man of God to relish this, let them hear what he said about the Law itself. In the book *On Fleeing the World*: *The Law*, he says, *could stop the mouth of all, it could not convert the mind*. Likewise in another place in the same book: *The Law*, he says, *condemns the deed, it does not take away malice*. Let them see a faithful and Catholic man agreeing with the Apostle saying: *But we know that whatever the Law says, it speaks to those who are in the Law, that every mouth may be stopped and all the world may be made guilty before God, because no flesh will be justified by the Law before him*[^59]. For from this apostolic sense Ambrose took and wrote those things.

**4. 11. 31.** But now, since the Pelagians say that there are or were just men in this life who lived without any sin, to such a degree that the future life, which is to be hoped for as a reward, cannot be more advanced and more perfect, let Ambrose answer them about this too and refute them. For, expounding the prophet Isaiah, on account of what is there written—*I have begotten sons and exalted them, but they have despised me*—he undertook to discuss the generations which are from God, and in that very discussion he recalled the testimony of John, where he says: *He who is born of God does not sin*. And, treating that same most difficult question: *Since in this world*, he says, *there is no one who is immune from sin, since John himself says: "If we say that we have not sinned, we make him a liar"; but if those born of God do not sin, and we understand this of those who are in this world, it is necessary that we reckon innumerable those who through the regeneration of the washing have obtained the grace of God. But yet, since the Prophet says: "All things wait for you, that you may give them food in due time: when you give to them, they will gather for themselves; when you open your hand, all things will be filled with goodness; but when you turn away your face, they will be troubled. You will take away their spirit, and they will fail and return to their dust. You will send forth your spirit, and they will be created, and you will renew the face of the earth"—these can seem to be said not of any time whatever, but of the future, in which there will be a new earth and a new heaven. They will be troubled, then, that they may take a beginning, and "when you open your hand, all things will be filled with goodness," which is not easily of this world. For about this world what does the Scripture say? "There is none that does goodness, there is not even one." If, therefore, there are diverse generations, and this entrance into this life is a receiver of offenses, to such a degree that even he who begot is despised, but the other generation does not receive sins, let us see whether there be, after the course of this life, that regeneration of ours, of which it was said: "In the regeneration, when the Son of man shall sit on the throne of his glory." For just as there is called a regeneration of the washing, by which, the foul flood of sins wiped away, we are renewed, so there seems to be called a regeneration by which, purified from all stain of bodily concretion, with the sense of the soul clean, we are regenerated into eternal life; in that there is a certain purer quality of that regeneration than of this washing, so that not only into its acts, but not even into our very thoughts, does any suspicion of sins fall*. Likewise in another place in the same work: *We see*, he says, *that it is impossible that anyone be perfectly immaculate while set in the body, since even Paul says that he is imperfect. For he has it thus: "Not that I have already received or am already perfect"; and yet a little after he says: "As many of us therefore as are perfect"—unless perhaps because there is one perfection in this world, another after that perfect thing of which he says to the Corinthians: "When that which is perfect shall have come"; and elsewhere: "Until we all meet into the unity of faith and the acknowledgment of the Son of God, into a perfect man, into the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ." So, then, as the Apostle says that there are many perfect men set in this world, who, if you look to true perfection, could not be perfect, because he himself said: "We see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face; now I know in part, but then I shall know even as I am known"; so there are immaculate men in this world, and there will be immaculate men in the kingdom of God, although surely, if you examine more minutely, no one can be immaculate, because no one is without sin*. Likewise in the same: *You see*, he says, *that, while we live this life, we ought to cleanse ourselves and to seek God, and to begin from the cleansing of our soul, and as it were to lay the foundations of virtue, so that after this we may merit to attain the perfection of purification*. And likewise in the same: *But who, weighed down*, he says, *and groaning, would not say: "Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death?" Thus, with the same teacher, we have resolved all the varieties of interpretation. For if everyone is wretched who recognizes himself entangled in the troubles of the body, surely every body is wretched; for neither would I call him happy who, confounded by certain shadows of his mind, does not know his own condition. That too came into the understanding not absurdly. For if a man who knows himself is wretched, surely all are wretched, because each either recognizes his own weakness through wisdom, or, through folly, does not know it*. Likewise in the book *On the Good of Death*: *Let death therefore work in us*, he says, *that life too may work—a good life after death, that is, a good life after victory, a good life with the contest finished, so that now the law of the flesh may not know how to fight against the law of the mind, so that now there may be for us no contention with the body of death*. Again in the same: *Therefore*, he says, *since the just have this reward, that they see the face of God and that light which enlightens every man, let us hence put on such zeal that our soul may draw near to God, that prayer may draw near, that our desire may cleave to him, that we may not be separated from him. And here indeed, set in this place, by meditating, by reading, by seeking, let us be joined to God, let us come to know him, as we can. "For here we know in part," because here all things are imperfect, there perfect; here we are little ones, there robust. "We see," he says, "now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face." Then, with the face unveiled, it will be permitted to behold the glory of God, whom now souls, wrapped in the compacted inwards of this body and overshadowed by certain stains and foulnesses of this flesh, cannot purely see. "For who," he says, "will see my face and live?" And rightly; for if our eyes cannot bear the rays of the sun, and, if anyone gazes too long over against the sun, he is wont, it is said, to be blinded—if a creature cannot gaze upon a creature without harm and offense to itself, how can one, covered with the spoils of this body, without peril to himself behold the flashing face of the eternal Creator? For who is justified in the sight of God, when not even an infant of one day can be clean from sin, and no one can glory about the integrity and chastity of his own heart?*

**4. 12. 32.** It will be too long if I should wish to recall all the things which holy Ambrose said and wrote against this heresy of the Pelagians, destined to arise so long afterward—not, indeed, answering them, but proclaiming the Catholic faith and building men up in it. But neither could I, nor ought I, to recall all those things which Cyprian, most glorious in the Lord, set down in his letters, by which it is demonstrated how this faith which we hold is true and truly Christian and Catholic—as anciently handed down through the holy Scriptures, so retained and preserved by our fathers and up to this time, in which these men have attempted to tear it away, and henceforth, with God favorable, to be retained and preserved. For that it was handed down thus to Cyprian and by Cyprian, these and similar testimonies brought forth from his writings attest; and that it was preserved thus up to our times, those things show which, about these matters, before these men began to bubble forth, Ambrose wrote, and that the Catholic ears which are everywhere shuddered at their profane novelties; and further, that it is to be preserved thus henceforth, the partly condemnation, partly correction of these men has wholesomely enough declared. For whatever they dare to mutter against the sound faith of Cyprian and Ambrose, I do not think they will burst forth into so great a fury as to dare to call those recalled and memorable men of God Manichees.

**4. 12. 33.** What is it, then, that with a raving blindness of mind they now boast, "that throughout the whole West a dogma no less foolish than impious has been taken up," since, with the Lord showing mercy and mercifully governing his Church, the Catholic faith was so watchful that a dogma no less foolish than impious—just as that of the Manichees, so also that of these men—was not taken up? Behold, holy and learned men, Catholic by the attestation of the fame of the whole Church, praise with due and fitting proclamations both the creature of God, and the marriage instituted by him, and the Law given by him through holy Moses, and the free choice implanted in the nature of man, and the holy Patriarchs and Prophets—all of which five things the Manichees condemn, partly by denying, partly even by detesting. Whence it appears that these Catholic teachers are far removed from the views of the Manichees. And yet they assert original sin, they assert that the grace of God precedes all merit over free choice, that it may furnish a truly gratuitous divine help, they assert that the saints so lived justly in this flesh that there was necessary to them, in order that their daily sins be forgiven, the help of prayer, and that perfect righteousness, which can have no sin, will be in the other life the reward for those who here lived justly.

**4. 12. 34.** What is it, then, that they say: *A subscription was extorted from simple bishops, sitting in their own places without the gathering of a synod?* Was a writing extorted from the most blessed and most excellent men in the Catholic faith, Cyprian and Ambrose, before these men, against these men—who overthrow their impious dogmas with so great a manifestation that we can scarcely find things more manifest to say against them? Or was there indeed need of a gathering of a synod, that an open ruin might be condemned?—as though no heresy were ever condemned except by the gathering of a synod, when rather most rarely are found those for whose condemnation such a necessity has arisen, and far and incomparably more are those which, where they arose, there deserved to be disapproved and condemned, and from there could become known throughout the other lands as to be avoided. But the pride of these men, which so exalts itself against God that it wishes to glory not in him but rather in free choice, is understood to grasp at this glory too, that for their sake a synod of the East and West be gathered. For since, with the Lord resisting them, they cannot pervert the Catholic world, they try at least to disturb it—when rather, with pastoral vigilance and diligence, after a fitting and sufficient judgment has been made about them, wherever these wolves appear, they are to be crushed, either that they may be healed and changed, or that they may be avoided away from the salvation and integrity of others, with the help of the Shepherd of shepherds, who seeks the lost sheep even in the little ones, who freely makes the sheep holy and just, who, although they are sanctified and justified, yet in this frailty and weakness, for the daily sins without which one does not live here, even when one lives well, both providently instructs them to ask for daily remission, and mercifully hears them asking.
