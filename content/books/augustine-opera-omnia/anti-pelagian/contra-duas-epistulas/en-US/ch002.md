# Book Two

**2. 1. 1.** Now let us consider another letter, not of Julian alone but common to him with several Pelagian bishops, which they sent to Thessalonica, and let us answer it, with the Lord's help, as best we can. But that this work of ours may not become longer than the necessity of the case itself demands, what need is there to refute also those things which do not contain the treacherous poisons of their dogma, but only seem to ask, in aid of themselves or in defense of the Catholic faith against the profanity (as they call it) of the Manichees, for the agreement of the Eastern bishops—striving for nothing else than that, with a horrible heresy thrown forward, of which they pretend to be the adversaries, they, the enemies of grace, may lie hidden in the praise of nature? For who ever stirred up a question against them on this point? Or to which of the Catholics are they displeasing because they condemn those whom the Apostle foretold would depart from the faith, having their conscience seared, forbidding to marry, abstaining from foods which they think unclean, and not thinking that all things were created by God? Who ever compelled them to deny that every creature of God is good and that there is no substance which the most high God did not make—except God himself, who was made by no one? These things are not reproved or condemned in them, things which are agreed to be Catholic. For the impiety of the Manichees, exceedingly foolish and harmful, not only the Catholic faith detests, but also all heretics who are not Manichees. Hence these Pelagians too do this well, to say anathema to the Manichees and to gainsay their errors. But they do two evils for which they themselves too are to be anathematized: one, that they charge Catholics with the name of the Manichees; the other, that they themselves too introduce the heresy of a new error. For not because they do not labor with the disease of the Manichees are they therefore of a sound faith. There is not one kind of pestilence, as in bodies, so also in minds. As, therefore, a physician of the body would not at once have pronounced free from the danger of death one whom he had denied to be dropsical, if he perceived him to be sick with another deadly disease, so the truth does not congratulate these men because they are not Manichees, if they are mad with another kind of perversity. Wherefore it is one thing that we anathematize together with them, another that we anathematize in them. For we detest together with them what rightly displeases even themselves, yet in such a way that we detest in them that for which they themselves rightly displease us.

**2. 2. 2.** The Manichees say that the good God is not the creator of all natures; the Pelagians say that God is not the cleanser, savior, and liberator of all ages in men. The Catholic faith refutes both: against the Manichees defending God's creature, lest any nature be denied to have been instituted by him, and against the Pelagians, that in all ages lost human nature may seek to be restored. The Manichees disparage the concupiscence of the flesh not as an accidental vice but as a nature evil from eternity; the Pelagians, moreover, praise it as no vice but a natural good. The Catholic faith refutes both, saying to the Manichees: "It is not a nature but a vice," and saying to the Pelagians: "It is not from the Father but from the world"—so that both may allow it, as a bad state of health, to be healed, the former ceasing to believe it incurable, the latter ceasing to proclaim it praiseworthy. The Manichees deny that for the good man the beginning of evil was from free choice; the Pelagians say that even the evil man has sufficient free choice for doing the good commandment. The Catholic faith refutes both, saying to the former: *God made man upright*[^1]; and to the latter: *If the Son makes you free, you will be free indeed*[^2]. The Manichees say that the soul, a particle of God, has sin from the intermingling of an evil nature; the Pelagians say that the just soul—not indeed a particle but a creature of God—does not have sin even in this corruptible life. The Catholic faith refutes both, saying to the Manichees: *Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree evil and its fruit evil*[^3]—which would not be said to a man who cannot make a nature, except because sin is not a nature but a vice; and saying to the Pelagians: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us*[^4]. With these diseases, contrary to one another, the Manichees and the Pelagians struggle, of unlike will but like vanity; separated by a diverse opinion, but near by a perverse mind.

**2. 2. 3.** Now indeed they together assail the grace of Christ, together make void his baptism, together dishonor his flesh—but even these things by diverse modes and causes. For the Manichees declare that divine aid is given to the merits of a good nature, the Pelagians to the merits of a good will. The former say: "God owes this to the labors of his members"; the latter say: "God owes this to the virtues of his servants." To both, therefore, the reward is reckoned not according to grace but according to debt. The Manichees say that the washing of regeneration, that is, the water itself, is superfluous, and contend with profane heart that it profits nothing; the Pelagians, moreover, assert that what is said in holy baptism for the expiation of sins is of no help to infants who have no sin. And by this, in the baptizing of little ones, so far as pertains to the remission of sins, the Manichees destroy the visible element; the Pelagians, moreover, even the invisible sacrament. The Manichees dishonor the flesh of Christ by blaspheming the Virgin's childbearing; the Pelagians, moreover, by making the flesh of those to be redeemed equal to the flesh of the Redeemer. For Christ was born for this reason, not indeed in the flesh of sin, but in the likeness of the flesh of sin, because the flesh of other men is born flesh of sin. The Manichees, therefore, utterly detesting all flesh, take away from the flesh of Christ its clear truth; but the Pelagians, asserting that no flesh of sin is born, take away from the flesh of Christ its proper dignity.

**2. 2. 4.** Let the Pelagians, then, cease to charge Catholics with what they are not, but let them rather hasten to amend what they themselves are; and let them not wish themselves to be lovable because they oppose the odious error of the Manichees, but let them rightly acknowledge themselves to be hateful because they do not oppose their own error. For two errors can be contrary to each other, yet both are to be detested, because both are contrary to the truth. For if the Pelagians are to be loved because they hate the Manichees, then the Manichees too are to be loved because they hate the Pelagians. But far be it that the Catholic mother, on account of hatred of the one party, should choose to love the other, since, with the Lord admonishing and helping, she ought to avoid both and desires to heal both.

**2. 3. 5.** Moreover, they even accuse the Roman clergy, writing that "they, struck by the terror of the imperial order, were not ashamed to commit the crime of prevarication, so that, against their former opinion, by which in the proceedings they had supported the Catholic dogma, they afterward pronounced that the nature of men is evil." On the contrary, the Pelagians had hoped, by a false hope, that the new and execrable dogma of Pelagius or of Celestius could be persuaded upon the Catholic minds of certain Romans, when those talents, though perverted by a wicked error, yet not contemptible, since they seemed to need to be diligently corrected rather than easily condemned, were treated somewhat more gently than the stricter discipline of the Church required. For with so many and such great ecclesiastical writings running back and forth between the Apostolic See and the African bishops, and with proceedings drawn up about this case before that See, with Celestius present and answering, what letter at last of Pope Zosimus of venerable memory, what interlocutory statement, is found, where he ordered it to be believed that a man is born without any taint of original sin? Nowhere at all did he say this, nowhere at all did he write it. But when Celestius had set this down in his little book—among those things, at any rate, about which he confessed that he still doubted and wished to be instructed—in a man of most keen talent, who surely, if he had been corrected, would have profited very many, it was the will to amend, not the falsity of the dogma, that was approved. For this reason his little book was called Catholic, because this too belongs to a Catholic mind: if perhaps it relishes anything otherwise than the truth requires, not to define it most certainly but, once it is uncovered and demonstrated, to reject it. For it was not to heretics but to Catholics that the Apostle was speaking where he says: *Let us, then, as many as are perfect, be of this mind; and if you are of a different mind in anything, this too God will reveal to you*[^5]. This was thought to have been done in him, when he answered that he agreed with the letters of Pope Innocent of blessed memory, by which all doubt about this matter was removed. And that this might be done more fully and more manifestly in him, the letters to come from Africa were awaited, in which province his cunning had become somewhat more evidently known. After these letters came to Rome, containing this—that it was not enough for the more sluggish and more anxious men that he confessed in general that he agreed with the letters of Bishop Innocent, but that he ought openly to anathematize the depraved things he had set down in his little book, lest, if he did not do this, many of little understanding should believe those poisons of faith in his little book to have been approved by the Apostolic See, on account of what had been said by it, that the book was Catholic, rather than amended, on account of his having answered that he agreed with the letters of Pope Innocent—then, therefore, when his presence was demanded, so that by certain and clear answers either the man's craftiness or his correction might come to light and remain doubtful to no one, he withdrew himself and refused the examination. Nor was that to be deferred any longer, as was done, which would profit others, even if it could not profit the obstinacy and madness of the excessively perverse. But if—far be it!—it had then been so judged about Celestius or Pelagius in the Roman Church that those dogmas of theirs, which in them and with them Pope Innocent had condemned, were pronounced to be approved and to be held, then rather the mark of prevarication would have to be branded upon the Roman clergy. But now, since first the letters of the most blessed Pope Innocent, answering the letters of the African bishops, condemned alike this error which these men try to persuade, and his successor too, holy Pope Zosimus, never said, never wrote, that what these men think about the little ones is to be held, and moreover bound Celestius—who was endeavoring to clear himself—by frequent interlocutory statements to consent to the aforesaid letters of the Apostolic See: surely whatever in the meantime was done more gently with Celestius—provided that the firmness of the most ancient and most robust faith was preserved—was the most clement persuasion of correction, not the most destructive approval of depravity. And that afterward Celestius and Pelagius were condemned by the same priest with renewed authority was a reason for a severity now necessarily brought forth after being briefly intermitted, not a prevarication of truth previously known or a new acknowledgment of it.

**2. 4. 6.** But what need is there for us to delay longer in speaking about this matter, when there exist, on the one side and the other, the proceedings and the writings sent, where all those things can be either known or recognized, in what manner they were done? For from the interrogations of your holy predecessor and the responses of Celestius, by which he professed that he agreed with the letters of blessed Pope Innocent, who does not see how Celestius was bound and constrained by a most wholesome bond, that he might no longer dare to defend that original sin is not remitted in the baptism of little ones? For these are the words of the venerable Bishop Innocent about this matter to the Council of Carthage: *For free choice, which that man of old endured—while he used his goods too rashly—falling and plunged into the depths of transgression, found no way how he might thereafter rise, and, deceived forever by his own freedom, would have lain hidden under the weight of this ruin, had not the coming of Christ afterward, by his grace, lifted him up, he who, through the purification of a new regeneration, cleansed every past fault by the washing of his baptism*. What is clearer or more manifest than this judgment of the Apostolic See? With this Celestius professed that he agreed, when, after it had been said to him by your holy predecessor, *Do you condemn all those things which have been bandied about in your name?*, he himself answered: *I condemn them according to the judgment of your predecessor Innocent, of blessed memory*. Now among the other things which had been bandied about in his name, the deacon Paulinus had charged Celestius with saying "that the sin of Adam harmed himself alone and not the human race; and that infants newly born are in that state in which Adam was before sin." Accordingly, if he condemned the charges of Paulinus with a truthful heart and mouth, according to the judgment of blessed Pope Innocent, what would thereafter remain to him from which to contend that there is in little ones no fault from the past transgression of the first man, a fault which is cleansed by holy baptism through the purification of a new regeneration? But that he had answered this falsely he showed by his very last exit, when he withdrew himself from the examination, lest he be compelled, according to the African rescripts, to recall and anathematize the very words about this question which he had set down in his little book.

**2. 4. 7.** What of the fact that the same Pope wrote back about this very cause also to the bishops of Numidia—because he had received writings from both councils, namely from that of Carthage and from that of Mileve—does he not speak most openly about little ones? For these are his words: *But that which your Fraternity asserts they preach, that little ones can be granted the rewards of eternal life even without the grace of baptism, is utterly foolish. For unless they eat the flesh of the Son of man and drink his blood, they will not have life in themselves. But those who defend that they have this without regeneration seem to me to wish to make void baptism itself, when they preach that these little ones have what we believe is to be conferred on them only by baptism*. What does the ungrateful man say to these things—he whom the Apostolic See had already spared, as though he had been corrected by his own profession, with the most kindly gentleness? What does he say to these things? Whether, after the end of this life, little ones, even if while they live they are not baptized in Christ, will be in eternal life or not? If he says "they will be," how then did he answer that he condemned, according to the judgment of Innocent of blessed memory, the things bandied about in his name? Behold, Pope Innocent of blessed memory says that without the baptism of Christ and without participation in the body and blood of Christ little ones do not have life. If he says "they will not be," how then, not receiving eternal life, are they not consequently condemned to eternal death, if they contract no original sin?

**2. 4. 8.** What do those men say to these things, who dare even to write their slanderous impieties, dare even to send them to the Eastern bishops? Celestius is held bound to have given his consent to the letters of the venerable Innocent; the very letters of the aforesaid bishop are read, writing that unbaptized little ones cannot have life. But who will deny that it is consequent that they have death who do not have life? Whence, then, in infants this pitiable punishment, if there is no original fault? How, therefore, are the Roman clergy charged with prevarication, under Bishop Zosimus, by these deserters of the faith and assailants of grace, as though they held something different in the later condemnation of Celestius and Pelagius than what they held under Innocent in the earlier one? For surely, since by the letters of the venerable Innocent the antiquity of the Catholic faith shone clear—that little ones, unless they are baptized in Christ, will remain in eternal death—surely whoever had deviated from that judgment would rather be a prevaricator against the Roman Church. And since, God being favorable, this was not done, but the very thing was preserved by the constantly repeated condemnation of Celestius and Pelagius, let these men understand that they themselves are what they charge others with, and let them at some time be healed from prevarication against the faith. For the Catholic faith does not say that the nature of men is evil, insofar as man was first instituted by the Creator; nor is what God now creates in that nature, when he makes men from men, his evil, but what it contracts from that fault of the first man.

**2. 5. 9.** Now let us look at those things which, charging us in their letter, they briefly set down, to which this is our answer: We do not say that by the sin of Adam free choice perished from the nature of men, but that it avails for sinning in men subject to the devil; but that it does not avail for living well and piously, unless the will of man itself has been set free by the grace of God and helped to every good of action, of speech, of thought. We say that no one but the Lord God is the founder of those being born, and that marriages were instituted not by the devil but by him, yet that all are born under sin on account of the fault of their descent, and therefore are under the devil until they are reborn in Christ. Nor do we assert fate under the name of grace, because we say that the grace of God is preceded by no merits of men. But if it pleases some to call the will of the almighty God by the name of fate, we indeed avoid profane novelties of words, but we do not love to contend about words.

**2. 5. 10.** But whence it seemed good to them to charge us with this, that we assert fate under the name of grace, I inspected, when I considered it somewhat more attentively, their words that follow. For they thought this ought to be charged against us thus: *Under the name of grace*, they say, *they so assert fate as to say that, unless God breathes into a man, unwilling and resisting, the desire even of an imperfect good, he can neither turn away from evil nor lay hold of good*. Then somewhat later, where they themselves recall what they defend, I attended to what was said by them about this matter: *We confess*, they say, *that baptism is necessary for all ages, and that grace also helps the good purpose of each, yet does not instill zeal for virtue into one who resists, because there is no respect of persons with God*. From these words of theirs I understood that they either think, or wish it to be thought, that we assert fate under the name of grace, because we say that the grace of God is given not according to our merits but according to his most merciful will, who said: *I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I will be merciful*[^6]. Where it was consequently added: *So then it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^7]. Here too someone similarly foolish could think or say that the Apostle is an assertor of fate. But here these men reveal themselves sufficiently. For when they slander us, saying that we assert fate under the name of grace, because we say that the grace of God is given not according to our merits, they confess without doubt that they themselves say it is given according to our merits; thus their blindness could not hide or dissemble that they relish and think this very thing which, charged against him, Pelagius in the episcopal judgment in Palestine condemned out of crafty fear. For it was charged against him, indeed from the words of his disciple Celestius, that he too said "the grace of God is given according to our merits." This he, detesting it, or as though detesting it—with his mouth at least—did not delay to anathematize; but, as his later books indicate and the assertion of these his followers lays bare, he kept it with a feigned heart, until afterward he brought forth even into writing, by boldness, what he had then covered out of fear by the cunning of one denying. And still the Pelagian bishops do not dread—are not even at all ashamed—to send their letters to the Catholic Eastern bishops, in which they charge us with being assertors of fate, because we do not say that the grace of God is given according to our merits—which Pelagius, fearing the Eastern bishops, both did not dare to say and was compelled to condemn.

**2. 6. 11.** Is it so, then, you sons of pride, enemies of the grace of God, O new Pelagian heretics, that whoever says that all the good merits of a man are preceded by the grace of God, and that grace is not given for merits—lest it be no grace, if it is not given freely but is rendered as owed to those who deserve it—seems to you to be asserting fate? Do not you yourselves too, with whatever intention, say that baptism is necessary for all ages? Did you not, in this very letter of yours, set down that opinion about baptism side by side with the one about grace? Why did not baptism, which is given to infants, by its very nearness admonish you what you ought to think about grace? For these are your words: *We confess that baptism is necessary for all ages, and that grace also helps the good purpose of each, yet does not instill zeal for virtue into one who resists, because there is no respect of persons with God*. In all these words of yours, what you said about grace I pass over for now. About baptism render an account: why do you say it is necessary for all ages; tell why it is necessary for little ones—surely because it confers some good upon them, and that some good is neither small nor middling, but great. For even though you deny that they contract original sin, which is remitted in baptism, yet you do not deny—nay, you even preach—that by that washing of regeneration they are adopted out of the sons of men into the sons of God. Tell us, then: by what preceding merits did those little ones, baptized in Christ, who have departed from the body, receive this so sublime a gift? If you say that they merited this by the piety of their parents, it will be answered you: Why is this good sometimes denied to the children of the pious and granted to the children of the impious? For sometimes offspring sprung from religious parents, at a tender age and most recently from the womb, is anticipated by death before it is washed by the washing of regeneration, and an infant born of the enemies of Christ is baptized in Christ by the mercy of Christians; a baptized mother bewails her own unbaptized child, and a chaste woman gathers up a stranger's offspring, exposed by an unchaste woman, to be baptized. Here surely the merits of the parents are of no account; they are of no account, by your own confession, even of the little ones themselves. For we know that you do not believe this about the human soul, that, giving it an earthly body, it lived somewhere and did something either of good or of evil, whence it would merit this difference in the flesh. What cause, then, procured baptism for this little one and denied it to that one? Or do these have fate, because they have no merit? Or is there in these a respect of persons with the Lord? For you said both, fate first, respect of persons afterward, so that, since both are to be refuted, there may remain what you wish to introduce against grace—merit. Answer, then, about the merits of little ones: why do some, baptized, and others, unbaptized, depart from their bodies, and neither prevail by the merits of their parents nor lack so excellent a good as to be made the sons of God out of the sons of men, by no merits of their parents, by none of their own. Surely you fall silent, and you rather find yourselves in the very thing you charge against us. For if, where there is no merit, you say there is consequently fate, and on this account you wish a merit of man to be understood in the grace of God, lest you be compelled to confess fate: behold, you rather assert fate in the baptism of little ones, of whom you confess there is no merit. But if in baptizing little ones you grant that no merit at all precedes, and yet that there is no fate, why, when we say that the grace of God is given freely for this reason, lest it be no grace, and is not rendered as owed to preceding merits, do you boast that we are assertors of fate—not understanding that in justifying the ungodly, just as for that reason there are no merits, because it is the grace of God, so for that reason there is no fate, because it is the grace of God; so for that reason there is no respect of persons, because it is the grace of God?

**2. 6. 12.** For those who affirm fate contend that not only acts and events but even our very wills depend on the position of the stars at the time when each is conceived or born—what they call the constellations; but the grace of God transcends not only all the stars and all the heavens but even all the angels. Then the assertors of fate attribute both the goods and the evils of men to fate; but God, in the evils of men, pursues their merits with the owed retribution, while the goods he bestows by an unowed grace with a merciful will, doing both not through a temporal partnership of the stars but through the eternal and deep counsel of his severity and his goodness. Neither, therefore, do we see to pertain to fate. Here, if you answer that this very benevolence of God, by which he does not follow merits but bestows unowed goods by a gratuitous goodness, ought rather to be called fate—although the Apostle calls this grace, saying: *By grace you have been saved through faith; and this not of yourselves, but it is the gift of God, not of works, lest perhaps anyone be lifted up*[^8]—do you not attend, do you not perceive, that fate is not asserted by us under the name of divine grace, but that divine grace is rather called by you under the name of fate?

**2. 7. 13.** Likewise, respect of persons is rightly said to exist there where he who judges, leaving aside the merit of the cause about which he judges, casts his vote for one against another, because he finds something in the person which is worthy of honor or of pity. But if anyone has two debtors and wishes to forgive the debt of one, to exact it from the other, he gives to whom he wills but defrauds no one; nor is it to be called respect of persons, since there is no iniquity. Otherwise, to those who understand little, respect of persons could seem to exist where the owner of the vineyard gave to the workers who had worked there one hour as much as to those who had borne the burden of the day and the heat, making them equal in pay whose toil had differed by so great a distance. But what did he answer to those murmuring against the householder over this seeming respect of persons? *Friend*, he says, *I do you no wrong. Did you not agree with me for a denarius? Take what is yours and go. But I wish to give to this last man as also to you. Or is it not lawful for me to do what I will? Or is your eye evil because I am good?*[^9] Surely here is all justice: "This I will." "To you," he says, "I have rendered, to this man I have given; nor, in order to give to this man, have I taken anything from you or either diminished or denied what I owed. Is it not lawful for me to be silent about what I will? Or is your eye evil because I am good?" As, therefore, here there is no respect of persons, because one is freely honored in such a way that another is not defrauded of what is owed, so too when, according to the purpose of God, one is called, another is not called, the call is given as a gratuitous good, of which good the call itself is the beginning; to the one not called no evil is rendered, because all are guilty from the fact that through one man sin entered into the world. And in that comparison of the workers, indeed, where they received one denarius who had labored one hour and those who had labored twelve times as much—who surely, according to human but vain reckonings, ought to have received twelve denarii for the amount of their labor—both were made equal in good, not some freed and others condemned; for even those who labored more, both that they were so called as to come, and that they were so fed as not to fail, they had from the householder himself. But where it is said: *Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and he hardens whom he wills*[^10], he who makes *one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor*[^11]: the good indeed is given undeserved and freely, because it is from the same mass to which it is given, but the evil is rendered deservedly and as owed, because in the mass of perdition evil is rendered to evil not evilly, and it is evil to him to whom it is rendered, because it is his punishment, but to him by whom it is rendered it is good, because it is his right doing. Nor is there any respect of persons in two debtors equally guilty, if to one is forgiven, from the other is exacted, what is equally owed by both.

**2. 7. 14.** But that what we say may become clear by the demonstration of some example, let us suppose some twins brought forth by some harlot and, that they might be gathered up by others, exposed; of these one expired without baptism, the other was baptized. What shall we say was here fate or fortune, which are altogether nothing? What respect of persons—since there was none with God—even if there could have been any in these, who surely had nothing whereby the one should be preferred to the other, and no proper merits of their own, either good, by which the one should merit to be baptized, or evil, by which the other should merit to die without baptism? Or were there any of the parents, where the father was a fornicator, the mother a harlot? But of whatever kind those were, they were surely not, for these dying in so diverse a condition, any diverse merits, but common to both. If, therefore, neither fate, because no stars decree these things, nor fortune, because chance happenings do not bring these things about, nor a diversity of persons or of merits brought this about, what remains, as far as pertains to the baptized one, except the grace of God, which is given freely to the vessels made unto honor, and, as far as pertains to the unbaptized one, the wrath of God, which is rendered to the vessels made unto dishonor for the merits of the mass itself? But in the one who was baptized we compel you to confess the grace of God, and we prove that no merit of his preceded; but about the one who died without baptism—why the sacrament was lacking to him, which you too confess to be necessary for all ages, and what in this way was punished in him—you yourselves see to it, who do not wish there to be original sin.

**2. 7. 15.** For us, in these two twins who without doubt have one cause, the Apostle resolves the difficulty of the question—why the one died in one way, the other in another—as though by not resolving it. For when he too had set forth something of the kind about two twins, on account of which it was said, not from works, since they had not yet done anything good or evil, but from the one who calls: *The elder shall serve the younger*[^12]; and: *Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated*[^13]; and had carried the dread of this depth to the point of saying: *Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and he hardens whom he wills*[^14], he felt at once what might disturb, and set against himself the words of one contradicting, which he would restrain by apostolic authority. For he says: *You will say to me then: Why does he still find fault? For who resists his will?*[^15] And he answered the one saying these things: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing molded say to him who molded it: Why have you made me thus? Or does not the potter have power over the clay, from the same lump to make one vessel indeed unto honor, another unto dishonor?*[^16] Then, having pursued so great and hidden a secret, as far as he judged it ought to be opened to men, he opened it, saying: *But if God, willing to show his wrath and to make known his power, bore in much patience the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for perdition, and that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared unto glory*[^17]. This is not only the help of the grace of God, but also its proof—the help, namely, in the vessels of mercy; but in the vessels of wrath, the proof; for in them he shows his wrath and demonstrates his power, because his goodness is so powerful that it uses well even evils, and in them he makes known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, since what the justice of him who punishes exacts from the vessels of wrath, this the grace of him who frees remits to the vessels of mercy. Nor would the benefit, which is freely bestowed on some, appear, unless God, condemning others, equally guilty, from the same mass, with a just punishment, showed what was owed to both. *For who distinguishes you?*[^18] the same Apostle says to a man glorying, as it were, about himself and his own proper good. *For who distinguishes you?*—surely from the vessels of wrath, from the mass of perdition, which through one man sent all into condemnation. *For who distinguishes you?* And, as though he had answered, "My faith distinguishes me, my purpose, my merit": *For what do you have*, he says, *that you have not received? But if you have also received it, why do you glory as if you had not received it?*—that is, as if it were of your own whence you are distinguished. Therefore he distinguishes who imparts whence you are distinguished, removing the owed punishment, bestowing unowed grace. He distinguishes who, when there was darkness over the deep, said: *Let there be light, and there was light; and he divided*—that is, distinguished—*between the light and the darkness*[^19]. For when there was only darkness, he did not find what to distinguish, but by making light he distinguished, so that to the justified ungodly it might be said: *For you were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord*[^20]; and thus *he who glories* glories not in himself but *in the Lord*[^21]. He distinguishes who, about those not yet born nor who had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not from works but from himself who calls, said: *The elder shall serve the younger*; and commending this same thing afterward through the prophet: *Jacob*, he says, *I have loved, but Esau I have hated*. For he said "election," where God did not find what he might choose, made by another, but himself makes what he finds, as is written of the remnant of Israel: *A remnant has been made by the election of grace. But if by grace, then no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^22]. On account of which you surely are foolish, since, the Truth saying, *not of works, but of the one who calls it was said*, you say: "From future works, which God foreknew he would do, Jacob was loved," and thus you contradict the Apostle saying *not of works*, as though he could not say "not of present but of future works." But he says *not of works*, that he might commend grace; *but if by grace, then no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*. For grace that is not owed but gratuitous precedes, that through it good works may be done; lest, if good works precede, grace be rendered as though to works, and thus grace be no longer grace.

**2. 7. 16.** But that every lurking-place of your fog might be taken away from you, I therefore set forth such twins, who would neither be helped by the merits of their parents and would both, in the very beginning of infancy, die, one baptized, the other without baptism, lest you should say, as you say of Jacob and Esau against the Apostle, that God foreknew their future works. For how did he foreknow as future those things which, since they were about to die in infancy—and his foreknowledge cannot be deceived—he rather foreknew would not be? Or what does it profit those who are snatched from this life, lest malice change their understanding or deceit beguile their soul, if even sin which was not done, spoken, thought, is so punished as though it had been committed? But if it is most absurd, most insipid, most insane that any men should be condemned for those sins whose guilt they could neither, as you say, contract from their parents, nor not only commit but not even so much as think—then that twin brother of the baptized one, the unbaptized one, returns to you, and silently asks of you whence he was distinguished from his brother's happiness, why punished with that unhappiness, so that, while the one was adopted into the sons of God, he himself did not receive the sacrament necessary for all ages, as you confess; if, just as there is no fortune or fate, or respect of persons with God, so there is no gift of grace without merits, no original sin. To this infant, surely, you submit your tongue and your voice; to this one who does not speak you have nothing to say.

**2. 8. 17.** Now let us see, as best we can, this very thing which they wish to precede in a man, so that he may be held worthy of the help of grace and so that grace may be repaid to him for its merit, not as unowed but as owed, and thus grace may now no longer be grace—let us see, however, what that is. *Under the name of grace*, they say, *they so assert fate as to say that, unless he breathes into a man, unwilling and resisting, the desire even of an imperfect good, he can neither turn away from evil nor lay hold of good*. We have already shown how empty are the things they say about fate and grace. Now this is what we ought to notice: whether God breathes into a man, unwilling and resisting, the desire of good, so that he is now no longer resisting, no longer unwilling, but consenting to the good and willing the good. For these men wish the desire of good to begin in a man from himself, so that the grace also of accomplishing may follow the merit of this beginning—if indeed they wish even this; for Pelagius says that what is good is fulfilled "more easily" if grace helps. By which addition, that is, by adding "more easily," he surely signifies that he relishes this: that, even if the help of grace be lacking, the good can be fulfilled, though more difficultly, through free choice. But what these men think in this matter, let us not prejudge from that author of this heresy—let us permit them to be free with their free choice even from Pelagius himself—and let us rather attend to these words of theirs which they set down in this letter to which we are replying.

**2. 8. 18.** For they thought this ought to be charged against us, that *we say God breathes into a man, unwilling and resisting*, not the desire of any great good whatever, but *even of an imperfect good*. Perhaps, then, they themselves in that way at least preserve a place for grace, so that they think a man can, without it, have the desire of good, but of an imperfect good, while of a perfect good he cannot "more easily" through it, but cannot at all except through it. But even so they say that the grace of God is given according to our merits, which, in the East, Pelagius, fearing to be condemned in the ecclesiastical proceedings, condemned. For if without the grace of God the desire of good begins through us, that beginning itself will be a merit to which, as though out of debt, the help of grace comes, and thus the grace of God will not be given freely but will be given according to our merit. But the Lord, to answer the future Pelagius, did not say "Without me you can do something with difficulty," but said: *Without me you can do nothing*[^23]. And to answer these future men too, in that very evangelical sentence, he did not say *Without me you can* "accomplish" *nothing*, but *do*. For if he had said "accomplish," these men could say that the help of God is necessary not for beginning the good, which is from us, but for accomplishing it. But let them hear the Apostle too. For when the Lord says *Without me you can do nothing*, by this one word he comprehends both beginning and end; but the Apostle, as though an expositor of the Lord's sentence, distinguished both more openly, saying: *Because he who began a good work in you will perfect it unto the day of Christ Jesus*[^24]. But in the holy Scriptures, in the same Apostle, we find more about this of which we are speaking. For we are now speaking of the desire of good, which, if they wish it to begin from us, to be perfected by the Lord, let them see what they answer the Apostle saying: *Not that we are sufficient to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God*[^25]. He says *to think anything*—something good, surely; but to think is less than to desire. For we think everything that we desire, yet we do not desire everything that we think, since sometimes we think even what we do not desire. Since, therefore, to think is less than to desire—for a man can think a good which he does not yet desire, and by advancing afterward desire what before, not desiring, he thought—how is it that for that which is less, that is, for thinking something good, we are not sufficient as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God, and for that which is more, that is, for desiring something good, we are sufficient without divine help from free choice? For here too the Apostle does not say: *Not that we are sufficient to think* what is perfect *as of ourselves*, but he says *to think anything*, to which the contrary is nothing. Whence is that of the Lord: *Without me you can do nothing*.

**2. 9. 19.** But doubtless what is written—*It is for a man to prepare the heart, and from the Lord comes the answer of the tongue*[^26]—they are deceived by not understanding well, so that they suppose that *to prepare the heart*, that is, to begin the good, pertains to a man without the help of the grace of God. Far be it that the sons of promise should so understand, as though, when they have heard the Lord saying *Without me you can do nothing*, they should as it were refute him, saying: "Behold, without you we can prepare the heart," or when they have heard from Paul the Apostle *Not that we are sufficient to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God*, they should as it were refute him too, saying: "Behold, we are sufficient of ourselves to prepare the heart and thereby to think something good for it." For who can, without a good thought, prepare the heart for good? Far be it that any should so understand except the proud defenders of their own choice and the deserters of the Catholic faith. For it is written, *It is for a man to prepare the heart, and from the Lord comes the answer of the tongue*, because a man prepares the heart, yet not without the help of God, who so touches the heart that a man prepares the heart. But in the answer of the tongue, that is, in this, that the divine tongue answers the prepared heart, a man has no work, but the whole is from the Lord God.

**2. 9. 20.** For as it was said, *It is for a man to prepare the heart, and from the Lord comes the answer of the tongue*, so too it was said: *Open your mouth and I will fill it*[^27]. For although, unless he helps without whom we can do nothing, we cannot open the mouth, yet we open it by his help and by our work, but the Lord fills it without our work. For what is it to prepare the heart and to open the mouth except to prepare the will? And yet in the same writings it is read: *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^28], and: *You will open my lips, and my mouth will declare your praise*[^29]. Behold, God admonishes that we prepare the will, in that we read *It is for a man to prepare the heart*, and yet, that a man may do this, God helps, because *the will is prepared by the Lord*. And *Open your mouth* he so says, commanding, that no one can do it unless he himself does it by helping, to whom it is said: *You will open my lips*. Or are these men so foolish as to contend that the mouth is one thing, the lips another, and to say, with a marvelous vanity, that the man opens the mouth, God the lips of the man? Although God restrains them even from this absurdity, where he says to his servant Moses: *I will open your mouth and will instruct you what you ought to speak*[^30]. In that sentence, then, where it is said *Open your mouth, and I will fill it*, one of them seems to pertain to the man, the other to God; but in this, where it is said *I will open your mouth and will instruct you*, both pertain to God. Why this, except because in the one of these he cooperates with the man doing it, the other he alone does?

**2. 9. 21.** Wherefore God does many good things in a man which the man does not do, but a man does none which God does not bring about that the man should do. Accordingly, the desire of good would not be in a man from the Lord if it were not good; but if it is good, it is in us from none but him who is supremely and unchangeably good. For what is the desire of good except love, of which the apostle John speaks without ambiguity, saying: *Love is from God*[^31]? Nor is its beginning from us and its perfection from God; but, if love is from God, the whole of it is in us from God. For let God avert this madness, that in his gifts we should make ourselves first, himself later, since *his mercy will go before me*[^32], and he himself it is who is faithfully and truly sung of: *for you went before him with blessings of sweetness*[^33]. And what is more aptly understood here than that very desire of good of which we are speaking? For the good begins to be desired when it begins to grow sweet; but when the good is done out of fear of punishment, not love of righteousness, the good is not yet done well, nor is that done in the heart which seems to be done in the deed, when a man would prefer not to do it if he could with impunity. Therefore the *blessing of sweetness* is the grace of God, by which it comes about in us that what he commands us delights us and we desire, that is, love, it. In which, if God does not go before us, it is not only not perfected but not even begun from us. For if without him we can do nothing, surely we can neither begin nor perfect, since, that we may begin, it was said: *His mercy will go before me*; and that we may perfect, it was said: *His mercy will follow me*[^34].

**2. 10. 22.** What is it, then, that in the things that follow, where they recall what they themselves think, they say they confess "that grace also helps the good purpose of each, yet does not instill zeal for virtue into one who resists"? For they say this as though a man has, of himself, without the help of God, a good purpose and zeal for virtue, by which preceding merit he is worthy to be helped by a grace that follows. For they suppose perhaps that the Apostle said: *We know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose*[^35], meaning that he wished the purpose of man to be understood, which purpose, as though a good merit, the mercy of the God who calls follows; not knowing that it was said *who are called according to his purpose* so that the purpose of God, not of man, might be understood, by which he chose, before the foundation of the world, those *whom he foreknew and predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son*[^36]. For not all who are called are called according to his purpose, since *many are called, few chosen*[^37]. They, then, are called according to his purpose who were chosen before the foundation of the world. About this purpose of God that too was said which I have already recalled about the twins Esau and Jacob: *That the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of the one who calls, it was said to him that the elder shall serve the younger*[^38]. This purpose of God is recalled also in that place where, writing to Timothy, he says: *Labor together for the Gospel according to the power of God who saves us and calls us with his holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given to us in Christ Jesus before times eternal, but is now manifested through the coming of our Savior Jesus Christ*[^39]. This, then, is the purpose of God, whence it is said: *All things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose*. But the good purpose of man, indeed, a following grace helps, but it itself would not be unless grace preceded. The zeal too of man, which is called good, although, when it has begun to be, it is helped by grace, yet does not begin without grace, but is breathed in by him of whom the Apostle says: *But thanks be to God, who gave the same zeal for you in the heart of Titus*[^40]. If God gives that anyone have zeal for others, who else is going to give that he have it for himself?

**2. 10. 23.** Since these things are so, I see nothing commanded to man by the Lord in the holy Scriptures, for the sake of proving free choice, which is not found either to be given by his goodness or to be asked for in order to demonstrate the help of grace; nor does a man begin at all to be changed from evil to good through the beginning of faith, unless the unowed and gratuitous mercy of God works this in him. About which mercy, recalling his own thought, a certain man, as we read in the Psalms, says: *Will God forget to be merciful, or will he in his anger restrain his mercies? And I said: Now I have begun; this is the change of the right hand of the Most High*[^41]. When, therefore, he had said *Now I have begun*, he did not say *this is the change* "of my choice," but *of the right hand of the Most High*. Let the grace of God, then, be so considered that from the beginning of his good change to the end of his consummation *he who glories may glory in the Lord*[^42], because, just as no one can perfect the good without the Lord, so no one can begin it without the Lord. But let this be the end of this volume, that the attention of the reader may be refreshed and restored for what follows.
