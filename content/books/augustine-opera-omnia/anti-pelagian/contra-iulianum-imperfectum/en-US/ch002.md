# Book One

**I. 1.** *Julian.* Although I have been hindered by great anguish, which the present state of the Churches inspires in me as I look upon it, partly by indignation, partly by pity, I had not yet abandoned the pledge of my promise, namely that, having become a debtor by promising, I should take care also to discharge the debt by paying it. For in the books that I dictated against the writings of Augustine, addressed to our brother Bishop Turbantius, a man conspicuous for the great splendor of his virtues, I promised that, if no setback should arise to obstruct my efforts, I would at once confront all the arguments of those who, following the opinion of the Manichees, defend a transmission of sin, that is, a natural evil; from which task I have hitherto been held back by various and undisguisable necessity.

*Augustine.* Would that you might imitate Turbantius, after those writings of yours and after these praises of yours, in which you call him a man conspicuous for the great splendor of his virtues, by being set free from your error! But an answer was given to those books of yours, and it was shown to you what catholic lights, most brilliant in their treatment of the holy Scriptures, you are trying to obscure with this reproach, that is, with the name of Manichees.

**I. 2.** *Julian.* But as soon as I was allowed to draw breath, my plan was, as far as the very nature of the matter would permit, to fulfill my promises briefly, had you not wished me, most blessed father Florus, to enter once again upon a more laborious field; and since you have such power through the reverence due to your holiness that I would judge it irreligious to obey your commands sluggishly, you easily prevailed upon me to extend the conciseness I had chosen into longer paths. You will therefore favor with your authority the work you have undertaken, whose name I have for this very reason inserted, so that my pen might proceed more securely and more cheerfully under the patronage of so great a command. There was, then, conceived in my mind a not unseasonable plan of brevity; because in those four books the truth of the catholic faith—for which and with which we earn the hatred of a falling world—armed both with unconquerable arguments and with the testimonies of the sacred Law, had ground to pieces nearly everything that the Manichees had invented and that Augustine had uttered against us, and scarcely anything was left, if we had fair judges to deal with.

*Augustine.* Against those four books of yours six books were written by me. For after the mention of the catholic teachers (whom you make out to be Manichees, charging me with this crime, that they learned and taught in the catholic Church), which I set forth in the first two volumes, I rendered the following four, one against each of yours; refuting the darkness of your heresy with the light of catholic truth, which by deserting you have gone blind and rave, and, like a new heretic, you seek fair judges in a matter about which there has never been any controversy in the Church of Christ; as though any could seem fair judges to you except those whom you have deceived with your error. But what judge will you be able to find better than Ambrose? Of whom your master Pelagius says that not even an enemy has dared to find fault with his faith and his most pure understanding in the Scriptures? Would this man, then, with a most pure understanding in the Scriptures, retain the teaching of the most impure Manichee, saying: *We are all born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*? Now you yourself judge how impure is the understanding with which you find fault with this catholic teaching, and do not delay to correct yourself with Ambrose as judge.

**I. 3.** *Julian.* Yet I had passed over some testimonies of the Scriptures by which they think something can be argued against us, which I had promised I would go on to explain, so that I might teach that whatever ambiguous words of the Law are wont to be taken up by our enemies neither prejudge what is clearly true and are to be understood in accordance with what is established by the most absolute authorities of holy Scripture and by unconquerable reasoning. For indeed by this very thing he shows how unskilled and profane an interpreter of the divine Law he is, whoever thinks that what justice cannot vindicate is defended by its sanction.

*Augustine.* Rather, what you say cannot be defended by any reasoning of justice. For the misery of the human race, from which we see no human being exempt from birth to death, does not pertain to the just judgment of the Almighty, if there is no original sin.

**I. 4.** *Julian.* For if the Law of God is the source and teacher of justice, then by its aids too the equity of God can be assisted, it cannot be assailed. The nature of things therefore allows no support for iniquity to be obtained from the strength of that Scripture, which had this one cause of its promulgation, that by its testimonies, remedies, threats, and punishments iniquity might be destroyed.

*Augustine.* By its testimonies it is declared that *man is made like to vanity, his days pass away like a shadow*[^1]. And that he is born with this vanity is shown not only by the truthful Scripture in which it is lamented, but also by the laborious and toilsome care by which he is educated. In its remedies it is read that, even when a little one has been born, a sacrifice is to be offered for sin[^2]. In its threats it is read that the soul of a little one would perish if it were not circumcised on the eighth day[^3]. In its punishments it is read that even little ones were ordered to be slain, whose parents had provoked God to anger, so that they might be destroyed in the slaughter of war[^4].

**I. 5.** *Julian.* Nothing, then, can be done through the Law of God against God the author of the Law. By this one summary there is indeed excluded whatever those in error are accustomed to object; but in order to teach how rich in truth is the one in whom we believe, we are accustomed to bring the light of exposition also to those passages of the Scriptures that have veiled the understanding of their meaning by an intricacy of expression; so that, once unlocked, they may possess the dignity of their origin and not be separated from the sacred lineage as though illegitimate or degenerate.

*Augustine.* On the contrary, the lights of the holy Scriptures, shining with sure truth, you are trying to obscure with the intricacy of your crooked arguments. For what is clearer than what I just said: *man is made like to vanity, his days pass away like a shadow*[^5]? This certainly would not happen if he remained in the likeness of God in which he was created. What is clearer than what was said: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive*[^6]? What is clearer than what was said: *Who is clean from filth? Not even an infant whose life upon the earth is of one day*[^7]; and very many other passages, which you contrive to wrap up in your darkness and to twist with empty loquacity into your perverse meaning?

**I. 6.** *Julian.* This, then, after my custom, was the only thing I was still planning to carry out, namely to free from the interpretations of the Traducianists those members of the divine Law that lay subject to insult; that is, to show that they were divine by showing that they were just.

*Augustine.* With an insulting mouth you call Cyprian, Ambrose, Gregory, and their other associates who confess original sin Traducianists. But it is no wonder that new heretics impose a new name on the Catholics from whom they go out; others too have done this, when they went out in like manner.

**I. 7.** *Julian.* But because you urgently demanded it, or rather enjoined it by a father's authority, that I should confront the book of the Punic preacher, which Alypius, a home-bred slave of his sins, recently brought to Count Valerius, from this my response has been made longer.

*Augustine.* This Punic disputant is a great punishment to you; and long before you were born, Cyprian the Punic was prepared as a great punishment for your heresy.

**I. 8.** *Julian.* For he has once again given monuments of his talent and his faith, which are understood with the greatest difficulty, expounded with the greatest hardship, scarcely heard without horror; but are refuted most easily, transfixed most sharply, and, for the sake of respect for decency, consigned to oblivion and banished.

*Augustine.* Those who read do not judge as you wish.

**I. 9.** *Julian.* His first book, then, which alone was published before this one, charges that we are new heretics, because we oppose an opinion which, like a whitewashed sepulcher—which, according to the saying of the Gospel, is clothed on the outside with color but within is full of filth and iniquity[^8]—belches forth, under the praise of Baptism, the Manichees' filth and a natural sin, so as to contaminate the hitherto pure Sacraments of the catholic Church.

*Augustine.* The ancient catholic faith, which you have just now begun to attack, shows that you are new heretics, since it was proclaimed by the most renowned teachers who lived before us. But not everything need be answered—reproaches rather than accusations of yours—which you hurl, with a most shameless brow and a most insolent tongue, not against me only, but also against Ambrose, Hilary, Gregory, Cyprian, and the other most celebrated teachers of the Church.

**I. 10.** *Julian.* He also praises a powerful man, because, against our petitions—we who were clamoring for nothing other than that judges be granted for so great a business, so that what was clearly done by underhanded means might be corrected rather than punished by an examination—he stood in the way with the weight of his rank and did not allow time or place for a hearing to be obtained. Whether the man to whom he writes acted so wickedly as that praise testifies, let him see to it himself. We, however, in proportion as we have thought better of him, the mention of his name, inserted honorably into my little work, has made clear. But that book may perhaps contain false things about its patron. Furthermore, it faithfully shows what its writer desires; namely, that contrary to reason, contrary to faith, contrary to all holiness of morals and of doctrines, the fight be waged with savage force and blind violence.

*Augustine.* Far be it from the Christian powers of the earthly commonwealth to doubt about the ancient catholic faith and on this account to grant place and time of examination to its assailants; rather than, being certain and grounded in it, to impose the discipline of correction on such enemies of it as you are. For what was done on account of the Donatists, their most violent mobs compelled to be done, not knowing what had been transacted before, which ought to have been shown to them; may God turn away from you that you should have such mobs; yet, God being favorable, you do not have them.

**I. 11.** *Julian.* After these proceedings, among the first parts of his volume, he advanced to the distinction between marriage and desire, as he had promised by the inscription of the title; and thereafter throughout the whole work he gave proof of his skill and his strength. For, tormented by the utmost necessity between the denial of those who confess and the confession of those who deny, he made public what miseries his foul conscience was suffering.

*Augustine.* Revile as much as you can; for what reviler cannot do this?

**I. 12.** *Julian.* To his earlier work, then, I replied in four little books with such ability as the truth supplied; having stated beforehand, of course, that I would pass over what both appeared to have no weight for his teaching and could convict me of verbosity, had I pursued everything weak and empty. And yet, if it had been permitted to keep this rule, as was fitting—that is, that things should not earn even an attack by their inept presentation—nearly all his inventions would have had to be spurned with public silence. But since, with affairs hastening to the worse (which is a sign of the world bearing down toward its end), folly and baseness have gained mastery even in the Church of God, *we discharge an embassy for Christ*[^9], and according to our manly share we bring such help as we can to the defense of the catholic religion; nor does it irk us to commit to writing the remedies that we prepare against the poisons of errors. Augustine: Folly and baseness brought you forth; but if it had gained mastery in the Church, it would certainly have kept you there.

**I. 13.** *Julian.* I had certainly testified, as I said, that in the first work I would neither dispute against all the kinds of transmission to be defended, nor repeat everything that book contained; but that I would grapple with those points in which he had placed the sum and force of his teaching. And that I faithfully pledged this, anyone who is a diligent reader of both works—however biased he may be—will recognize. But I, secure in the honesty of my conscience, both exhort and goad our enemy, that if he thinks any argument brought forward by him has been passed over by me, which he himself judges to be of any moment, he should bring it into the open and convict me of fear and deceit.

*Augustine.* I do not believe that you thought the things you passed over to be of no moment; though even if I grant that you thought this, yet a catholic and intelligent reader, if he has both that one book of mine and your four, and applies diligence, will find that it is not so.

**I. 14.** *Julian.* Certain testimonies of the Scriptures I expounded at greater length, certain ones more briefly; because I pledged that I would do this fully in the work that was to follow. And so nothing there of all Augustine's arguments and propositions remained without being exploded, nothing was fulfilled by me otherwise than promised; many things among his inventions I convicted as false, many as stupid, many as sacrilegious.

*Augustine.* This indeed you say, but it is you who say it; whoever, however, reads and understands, if he is not a Pelagian, does not say this.

**I. 15.** *Julian.* By this profession we have no reputation for arrogance to fear; because we confess that the truth was defended not by my talent, but that the weakness of our talent was helped by the strength of the truth.

*Augustine.* You would have said "crushed," if you had wished to speak the truth.

**I. 16.** *Julian.* Since, then, these things, just as I have said, stand accomplished, I cannot sufficiently marvel at the man's shamelessness, who in this recent work of his accuses my books of falsity, which nevertheless he testifies have not yet come into his hands. It is hard indeed that the habit of sinning produces love of the offense; but nothing is harder than that it extinguishes shame; which, although it was established by the practice of wickedness, yet the present dangers have taught more than any of us could have surmised. For when would I have believed that the Numidian's brow had hardened to such a degree that in one work and one line he would confess both that I had spoken false things and that he had not read what I said?

*Augustine.* If you did not do this, he did it who excerpted from your books the things he thought ought to be sent to Count Valerius. And because I, not believing that he had done so, falsely attributed to the author what I owed to the excerptor. For I had not yet read your books, but I had read what he had excerpted from them. If you considered yourself a human being, when you discerned that this could happen, you would by no means seek so spiteful a calumny against a man on this occasion.

**I. 17.** *Julian.* For writing to the one whom he marvels at as being a student of his books, although he is occupied with the toils of military service, he indicates that little sheets were brought to him by Alypius, which were thus superscribed: *Chapters from a book of Augustine which he wrote, against which I have excerpted a few things from his books*. Here I see that the one who directed those writings to your Excellency wished to excerpt them from I know not what books, for the sake, as I think, of a quicker response, lest he delay your insistence. But when I considered who these books might be, I judged them to be those of which Julian makes mention in the letter that he sent to Rome, a copy of which reached me at the same time. For there he says: *They say too that these present marriages were not instituted by God, which is read in the book of Augustine, against which I have just now replied in four little books*. And after these words he again adds in his own words: *I believe these things were excerpted from these little books; so that it might perhaps have been better for our purpose to labor at refuting and confuting his whole work, which he set forth in four volumes; had not I too been unwilling to delay my response, just as you did not delay the transmission of the writings to which a reply must be made*. He shows here, then, most plainly, that he suspects those excerpts to have been gathered hastily from my work, but is ignorant of the whole books, to which nevertheless he dares to say he has been able to reply.

*Augustine.* Why should I not have dared, when I certainly ought not to have doubted that you had said empty things in them? For you could not speak against true things except empty ones. Nor did my judgment deceive me; for I found those books of yours, when I read them, to be such as I had presumed them to be before I read them.

**I. 18.** *Julian.* He also makes mention of a letter which he says was directed by me to Rome; but from the words he set down, we have been unable to recognize what writing he was speaking of. For to Zosimus, once bishop of that city, I sent two letters on these questions; but at that time, when I had not yet begun the books.

*Augustine.* This letter is not to Zosimus; but for seducing those who at Rome could be seduced by such persuasion. But if you do not acknowledge it, behold, let it not be yours. Would that those books too were not yours, but another's; lest through them you become far estranged from the truth.

**I. 19.** *Julian.* Further, let him make use of the evidence of the letter, by which he either received or fabricated that I had set forth in four volumes a response against the new Manichees (since the old one disdains to be seen). Why did he not take care to learn the things we had objected? Why did he not, since he was about to engage, strive to recognize them; but, stirred by most shameful frivolity, advanced into a very great contest with his eyes covered, after the manner of the Andabatae? This deed he defends by such an allegation that he says he wished to imitate, by a headlong response, the haste his patron had in transmitting the documents; as though he could not most honorably have intimated that some time ought to be granted him in which to arrive at reading the published work; that it is a disgrace among the learned to fail in the gravity of writing and, through impatience of deliberation, to attack what one does not know. To this is added that, intending to contrive against us an odium of cunning—as though it had curtailed the sequence of his words—he lent credit to these excerpts, which seem more plausibly to have been composed by his own falsity and malice than by the inexperienced simplicity of any of us. But by whatever mind, by whatever author this came about, it nevertheless supports us in two ways; because it became clear at once how great is the frivolity, and how great the weakness, in the enemy of truth, who showed himself both unable, though he ought not to speak, to keep silent, and, with a few half-full and torn-apart rather than gathered statements, drawn from my first book alone, to have so given way, broken, as to stir up against us the breasts of the common folk with quite womanish cries; which will appear in the course of our disputation.

*Augustine.* Why are you angry with me, because your books could reach me only rather late; or because, seeking them, I could not quickly find them? Yet I could, and altogether ought, to inspect with plainly open, not covered, eyes the things contained in the little sheet sent to me, whoever's and of whatever sort they were, lest they be thought unconquered, and to refute them without delay; because even if I could never have found your books, it was fitting that I should, as far as I was able, argue against the things which he believed to be of some moment, who judged that they ought to be sent to so great a man, lest anyone reading them be deceived by them. You would not, then, object to me what you objected, unless you were rather speaking, I will not say with extinguished, but at least with closed eyes. But in no way would you say that the breasts of the common folk are stirred by us against you, unless you knew that the catholic faith, which you are trying to overturn, is not hidden from the Christian multitude in both sexes.

**I. 20.** *Julian.* Yet here too I give warning, as was done by us in the earlier work, that I will not set down all his words exactly; but those chief points which, once destroyed, the opinion of a natural evil is crushed.

*Augustine.* The things you pass over will perhaps be gathered up, either by us or by others, so that it may appear why you passed them over.

**I. 21.** *Julian.* Although this stands fully accomplished in the first work; yet because he has now proposed for himself certain things from one book of mine only to be refuted, and, as I said before, charges me with having for the most part truncated the chief points of his sayings which I inserted; I will first show that what he reprehends was not done by me, and was practiced most shamelessly by him in this very work. Then I will prove that, by those very concise and brief statements which he interposes from my writings, by which he had been assailed, he has so withstood them with no solid responses that both those things remain unshaken, and here he confesses, more plainly than our work had labored to set forth, things deserving to be detested.

*Augustine.* I have already answered these things above.

**I. 22.** *Julian.* Astonished, then, let us hear what he has written against me. *The words*—he says—*from my book sent to you by me and most well known to you, these he set down, which he tried to refute: "they most spitefully cry out that we are condemners of marriage and of the divine work by which God creates human beings from males and females; because we say that those who are born from such union draw original sin, and that those, from whatever parents they are born, are still under the devil, we do not deny, unless they are reborn in Christ"*. *In these words of mine he passed over the testimony of the Apostle, which I had inserted, by whose great weight he felt himself pressed. For when I had said that human beings draw original sin, I soon added: of which the Apostle says: "through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned"*[^10]; *which testimony, as we have said, being passed over, he wove together those other things which were recalled above. For he knows how the faithful hearts of catholics are accustomed to take these apostolic words*, *which he passed over; which words, so right and shining with such great light, the new heretics contrive to obscure and to deprave with dark and tortuous interpretations. Then he subjoined other words of mine, where I said: "they do not consider that the good of marriage cannot be accused on account of the original evil that is drawn from it, just as the evil of adulteries and fornications cannot be excused on account of the natural good that is born from it. For, as sin, whether drawn by little ones from this side or from that, is the work of the devil, so a human being, whether born from this side or from that, is* *the work of God." Here too he passed over the things in which he feared catholic ears. For, that one might come to these words, it had been said above by us: "because, therefore, we say what is contained in the most ancient and most firm rule of the catholic faith, these assertors of a novel and perverse teaching, who say there is no sin in little ones that is washed away by the laver of regeneration, calumniate, faithlessly or unskillfully, as though we condemn marriage, and as though we say that the work of God, that is, the human being who is born from them, is the work of the devil"*. These things of ours, then, being passed over, there follow those words of ours which he set down, as is written above. How long will you, who speak these things, persist in mocking the simplicity of religious hearts and inexperienced ears? To what end will unbridled shamelessness vaunt itself? Did nothing, when you wrote these things, move you—neither the censure of learned men, nor reverence for the future judgment, nor the very monuments of letters? Are you now open to your own deceit, and do you not see that, once detected, it is held fast? What you wrote in the first work, what in the second, whom of us do you think ignorant of it? For it pleased me utterly, and it was fitting, to assail you with those manners in which the eloquent consul roared against the public parricide.

*Augustine.* You do well to indicate to us, lest perhaps we should not recognize it, that you took these things from the invectives of Cicero and turned them about; but we do not fear Julian, when we see that he has become Tullian; rather, we grieve that he is mad, when we see that he has lost his Christian sense. For what is more mad than to forbid the physician Christ to little ones, by saying that there is not in them what he came to heal? Cicero, inveighing against the parricide of his fatherland, was defending that city which its king Romulus founded by gathering sinners from everywhere; but you, so many little ones who die without holy Baptism both cry out that they have no sin, and do not permit them to approach the city of the King, to whose image they were made.

**I. 23.** *Julian.* You falsely pretend that I passed over the testimony of the Apostle, which can neither help you, and was not passed over by me; but it was inserted in the order in which it had been placed by you; and as it was faithfully recalled in the first, so it was explained—although cursorily and briefly—in the fourth book of my work. The mention too of the catholic Church, which you had made for this purpose, that those deceived by you might desert the catholic faith and, wretched, rejoice in the catholic name, I did not pass over. And although there was no force of argument in words of this kind, yet the chief point of your sayings was set forth by me no otherwise than it had been arranged by you. Read my published books and, looking at the fidelity of the response which you charge with fraud, pronounce me meanwhile to speak true things; but you, if your custom permits, blush. But now that the inexcusable falsity has been shown—which, though always base, becomes baser when it invades the place of the censor and reproaches its own deformity to another's comeliness—answer: what do either the name of the Church or the words of the Apostle profit the Manichaean meanings, that you should allege them to have been passed over with so great a spite?

*Augustine.* I have already answered above this calumny of yours, by which you charge me with the falsity of having set down your words less than completely. But what that excerptor did, you would not so gladly attribute to me, if you were not willing to be a deceiver of those who read these things.

**I. 24.** *Julian.* This has always been the greatest difference between the Manichees and the Catholics, and a certain very wide boundary by which the teachings of the pious and the impious are mutually separated from each other—rather, a great mass dividing our opinions, as it were, like the heavens from the earth, by its depth—namely, that we ascribe every sin to an evil will, but they to an evil nature; and they, although they follow diverse errors, yet, as though flowing out from the head of this spring, arrive consequently at sacrileges and disgraceful deeds; just as the Catholics, on the opposite side, beginning from a good start, increased by good advances, are carried to the summit of religion, which reason and piety fortify. You, therefore, attempting to assert a natural evil with profane wish but ineffectual intention, have usurped the testimony of the Apostle, whom by this same writing-out I show to have meant nothing such as you try to persuade, since you both, in self-contradictory ways, confess him to be catholic and judge his sayings to support the Manichee.

*Augustine.* The catholic teachers whom you assert to support the Manichee, who understood in the apostolic words that little ones draw original sin, and did not, in your insane fashion, praise nature as though sound, but rather applied to it, to be healed, the Christian medicine—if you thought with a Christian heart, you would blush, you would tremble, you would fall silent.

**I. 25.** *Julian.* Did not the same, Adimantus and Faustus (whom in the books of your *Confessions* you call your teacher), do this by the tradition of their heresiarch, snatching and corrupting the more obscure statements, whether from the Gospel or from the Epistles of the Apostles, in order to defend a profane teaching by the authority of those names? Yet why do I speak of the Manichees? Absolutely all heresies fortify their inventions, by which they have strayed from piety and faith, with the expressions and statements of the Scriptures.

*Augustine.* They turned obscure statements to their teaching; you try to obscure clear ones by your very teaching. For what is clearer than what the Apostle says: that sin entered this world through one man, and through sin death, and so it passed into all human beings[^11]? And if the same Apostle were compelled to prove this, he would give as witness the very misery of the human race, which begins from the wailing of little ones and reaches all the way to the groans of the decrepit. For in no way, under the Almighty and just one, would so great a misery be inflicted on human nature, unless in two human beings it had been driven, by the deserving of sin, from all the happiness of paradise into this unhappiness.

**I. 26.** *Julian.* Will, then, either the authors of the sacred Book be proved to be authors of errors, or will the dignity of the Scriptures expiate the crimes of those who perish?

*Augustine.* Say this to yourselves.

**I. 27.** *Julian.* Let the lust for undisciplined expositions therefore be extinguished; let the words be believed to do nothing against the manifest justice of God; which words, if they belong to that Person whom it is necessary to venerate, let them be defended by explanations agreeing with the divine equity; but if they were uttered by an author not to be feared, let them be expelled even unrefined. Now, therefore, we are disputing about the judgment of God, of whom it is written: *God is faithful, in whom there is no iniquity; just and holy is the Lord God*[^12]. And again: *The Lord is just, and he loved justice, his face beheld equity*[^13]. And again: *All your judgments are equity*[^14]. Numberless are the testimonies by which the divine equity is proclaimed in the sacred Volumes; about which, nevertheless, no one, whether of the Gentiles or of the heretics, except the Manichees and the Traducianists, has doubted.

*Augustine.* From this equity *there is a heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their going out from their mother's womb*[^15]; which whoever denies original sin asserts to be altogether unjust.

**I. 28.** *Julian.* For it has been so instilled in all generally, by nature's teaching, that God is just, that it is manifest that he is not God who has been established not to be just. A human being, therefore, can also be just; but God cannot be anything but just.

*Augustine.* Say it to yourself.

**I. 29.** *Julian.* Since he is the one true God in whom we believe, and whom we venerate in the Trinity, he is beyond doubt the most just toward all in his manner of judgment.

*Augustine.* Say it to yourself: and show that he is just, who is born with so manifest a misery, or unto so manifest a misery, by whom no original sin is drawn.

**I. 30.** *Julian.* By his laws, then, what is established to be unjust can in no way be proved and vindicated, so that, if this could happen, his whole divinity would become worthless. By the same one, therefore, will it be proved that a teaching of injustice is established from the holy Scriptures, by whom it could be approved that the Trinity, in which we believe, can be deprived of the glory of its divinity.

*Augustine.* You speak the truth: but say it to yourselves, who endeavor to take away from Christ the glory by which he heals little ones.

**I. 31.** *Julian.* And since neither any reason nor any piety sustains this, either teach that a natural sin can either exist or be just to be imputed to anyone; or else depart from the contamination of the holy Scriptures, by whose statements you judge to be sanctioned what you are compelled to confess to be unjust.

*Augustine.* You are in error: it is rather you who are compelled to confess as unjust the heavy yoke upon little ones; if, just as they have no sin of their own, so they draw no original sin.

**I. 32.** *Julian.* But if you will do neither of the two things we have spoken of, and assert that you believe in this God, by whose institutions you judge injustice to be fortified, know that you, the new one, are far worse than the ancient Manichee, in that you have such a God as the Manichee imagined the enemy of his God to be.

*Augustine.* You rage against little ones worse than the Manichees. For they at least wish the soul, which they think a part of God, to be healed through Christ in the little one; but you do not permit the one whom you say has no evil either in soul or in flesh to be healed through Christ in any part. And you, illustrious preachers, so preach Jesus that you deny him to be the Jesus of little ones. For from where he received this name, read in the Gospel[^16], and do not deny the Savior to little ones who are not saved.

**I. 33.** *Julian.* What windings, then, what pillows of lies and follies—which the prophet Ezekiel imputes to fornicating Jerusalem[^17]—will you here apply to me, on which womanish souls may recline, holding the names of the mysteries, while in their uncovered profanity they have offended against divinity itself? With all the conjurer's tricks removed, and the throngs of the little common folk so often summoned by you, teach that what you try to affirm through the holy Scriptures is just.

*Augustine.* The throngs of the little common folk, whom you mock, know the catholic faith, which confesses that infants are saved by the Savior; and therefore they detest the error of the Pelagians, who deny this.

**I. 34.** *Julian.* Lest, then, my speech be extended into infinite volumes, here, here let the genus, species, difference, mode, and quality of these things we are dealing with be discerned; rather, more anxiously, whether they exist, whence they exist, where they exist, what too they deserve, and from whom. For in this way there will be no long wandering through the windings of disputations, and what ought to be held as certain will appear.

*Augustine.* You wrote eight books against one book of mine for this reason: that, through your dialectical summaries, you were unwilling to dispute at length.

**I. 35.** *Julian.* Here, then, the account of Creator and creature is at issue, that is, of God and the human being; the one judges, the other is judged; and so let it be seen what the nature of justice and of fault is. Justice is—both as it is wont to be defined by the learned, and as we can understand it—the virtue (if it may be permitted, against the Stoics, to prefer one to another) greatest of all virtues, diligently discharging its office to restore to each his own without fraud, without favor.

*Augustine.* Say, then, by what justice the heavy yoke of so great and manifest a misery has been repaid to little ones; say by what justice that little one is adopted in Baptism, and that one dies without this adoption; why this honor, or estrangement from this honor, is not common to both, when their case, whether good or evil, is common to both. You do not say, because, a Pelagian more than a Christian, you understand neither the grace of God nor the justice of God.

**I. 36.** *Julian.* But if Zeno would not allow it to be called the greatest—he who affirms so great a bond and unity of the virtues that, where one has been present, he says all the virtues are present, and where one has not been present, all are absent; and that that is true virtue which is perfected by this fourfold yoking—then too he will furnish us with very great help, when he has taught that neither prudence nor fortitude nor temperance can be maintained without justice; in accordance with which truth *Ecclesiastes* too declares: *he who sins in one thing will lose many good things*[^18].

*Augustine.* Hear the same *Ecclesiastes* saying: *Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity; what abundance has a man in all his labor with which he labors under the sun?*[^19] And tell me, why is *man* too *made like to vanity*[^20], who had been made like to truth. Or do you here except little ones, whom we see, by growing and, if they are well educated, by advancing, diminish the great vanity with which they were born; nor are they wholly free of it, until all their days have passed away like a shadow in vanity[^21]?

**I. 37.** *Julian.* This august virtue, then, the assessor of each one's deserts, shines forth in the works of the image of God, that is, of the human soul, according to the measure and powers of the creature itself; but in God himself, the founder out of nothing of all things that are, it shines back through a measureless and bright orb forever. Its origin is divinity, its age is eternity, and an eternity that knows not, on either side, either to cease or to have begun. As, then, its genus (by which name I wish nothing else to be understood than its origin) is God; so its species appears in the promulgation of laws and in the effects of judgments.

*Augustine.* If the origin of justice is God, as you confess, why do you not confess that justice is given to a human being by him, and would rather have justice be the choice of the human will than the gift of God; so that you are among those of whom it is said: *Being ignorant of the justice of God, and wishing to establish their own, they did not submit to the justice of God*[^22]? Blush at last, I beseech you, and ask justice from him who is, as you have been compelled to confess, the origin of justice.

**I. 38.** *Julian.* Its difference, indeed, we can not absurdly understand to be the varying dispensation according to the opportuneness of the times. For example, in the Old Testament sacrifices of cattle had been commanded. To fulfill that then belonged to reverence for the command; but now the disregard of them, once they have been abolished, serves justice's precept just as the offering then served it. Its mode is its very steadiness, in that it neither enjoins on anyone more than his powers can bear, nor blunts mercy. Its quality, moreover, is understood to be that by which it is sweetly savored by pious minds. There is, then, beyond doubt, a justice without which there is no deity; which, if it did not exist, God would not exist; but God exists, and so without ambiguity justice exists. And it is nothing other than the virtue containing all things and restoring to each his own, without fraud, without favor. And it consists chiefly in the depth of divinity.

*Augustine.* You defined justice to be the virtue containing all things and restoring to each his own, without fraud, without favor. Accordingly, we see that it restored without fraud the denarius to those who had labored the whole day in the work of the vineyard; for this had been agreed, this had been settled, and they could not deny that they had been hired for this wage[^23]. But tell me, I beg you, how, without favor, it gave just as much to those who were in that work for one hour? Or had it perhaps lost its justice? Restrain yourself, then, rather. For the divine justice defrauds no one, but grace bestows many things on the undeserving. But why to this one thus, and to that one thus, look at what you go on to add. For you most truly say that it consists chiefly in the depth of divinity. In this depth is the fact that *it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^24]. In this depth is the fact that that little one is adopted into honor through the laver of regeneration, and that one is left in dishonor, not to be admitted to the Kingdom; though neither of them has any deserving, in either direction, through the choice of the will.

**I. 39.** *Julian.* Its testimony, indeed, just as it deserves from its author, so it deserves also either from the upright or from the wicked, in that it has both rightly advanced the former and rightly condemned the latter. But when, of itself, it permits liberal mercy to those who deserve neither anything good nor anything evil, it feels no injury; because this very thing, that God should be merciful to his own work when he is not driven to severity, is a great part of justice.

*Augustine.* At least heed the name of mercy, and look at whence it is named. What need, then, is there of mercy where there is no misery? But if you say there is no misery in little ones, you deny that mercy is to be shown them; if you say there is any, you show an evil deserving. For under a just God no one can be wretched unless he deserves it. Behold, two little ones lie here: one of them dies baptized, the other unbaptized; to which of them do you say God was merciful? If to the one, show the evil deserving of the other, you who deny that there is original sin; if to both, show any good deserving of the baptized one, you who deny grace, where there is no respect of persons; and say, if you can, why he was unwilling to adopt both, who certainly created both to his own image. Or is he so just that he is not almighty, if he willed and could not? Where certainly neither of them was unwilling, lest you refer the hindering of the divine power to the deserving of the human will; here certainly God can say to neither of them: I willed, and you were unwilling. Or if the infant is unwilling for the reason that he weeps when he is baptized, then let both be left, for both are unwilling; and yet one is taken up and the other left, because great is the grace of God, and true is the justice of God. But why this one rather than that one: *The judgments of God are unsearchable*[^25].

**I. 40.** *Julian.* For those whom he made because he willed, he does not condemn unless he is spurned; if, when he is not spurned, he should make them better by consecration; he both suffers no detriment to justice and is adorned by the munificence of mercy.

*Augustine.* You who condemn no one unless spurned, say whether he spurns his own image unless he is spurned. But if you dare not, say whence he spurns those little ones whom he does not adopt, by whom you will not find him spurned, unless you find them in Adam. There you will at once find both that, in order that they be spurned, it is owed to all through justice, and yet that not all are spurned because of ineffable and unsearchable grace.

**I. 41.** *Julian.* With these divisions of justice, then, which we have set forth, expounded, let us examine what the definition of sin is. Indeed, the writings both of those who philosophize and of those who were catholics supply me abundantly with what we are seeking; but I fear that you may refuse, and that, if I summon the senate of the philosophers, you will at once inflame against us the workshop laborers and all the common crowd.

*Augustine.* You are insulting toward the weak things of the world, which God chose in order to confound the strong[^26]. Indeed, they themselves confound those *who trust in their own strength*[^27]. Here what shall I say: It is you? Plainly, while I am silent, you appear, since you are not silent.

**I. 42.** *Julian.* Crying out with the women, and with all the dogs and tribunes, for whom your colleague Alypius recently brought eighty or more horses fattened from all Africa.

*Augustine.* Either you calumniate, or you do not know what you are saying; and therefore you say these things either as a liar or as a reckless man. But what is more worthless than you, if you yourself fabricated these things? What more stupid, if you believed those who fabricated them? And now, that you even dared to write it, not fearing that your books might reach those places which received my colleague Alypius as he passed or came by land and sea, where your falsehoods cannot be read most plainly without derision of you, or rather detestation; to what—I will not say shamelessness, but madness—is this to be compared?

**I. 43.** *Julian.* That you in no way acquiesce in the opinions of the learned, so that you add that, according to what agrees with your understanding, the Apostle said that *God made foolish the wisdom of the world*[^28]; but that our disputants can be despised by you without fear, because you are pressed by no authority of such men.

*Augustine.* It is you who despise those who, when they teach original sin, you so resist that you moreover charge them as Manichees, naming me, meaning them.

**I. 44.** *Julian.* What, then? Shall I acquiesce in you entirely, and in this place make a loss of all those whose support I could use, and be content with the definition that escaped from the mouth of your honesty, as a sign of a good nature, after the secret of the Manichees? In that book, then, whose title is either *The Two Souls* or *Against the Two Souls*, you speak thus: *Wait, allow us first to define sin. Sin is the will to commit or to retain what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. Although, if one is not free*, *it cannot even be called a will; but I preferred to define it more coarsely than too scrupulously*[^29].

*Augustine.* Here sin was defined that is sin only, not that which is also the punishment of sin. For this had to be dealt with, when the origin of evil was being sought, such as was committed by the first man before any evil of man. But you either cannot understand, or will not.

**I. 45.** *Julian.* O gold shining in dung! What truer, what fuller could have been said by anyone even orthodox? *Sin is*, you say, *the will to commit or to retain what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain*. *Ecclesiasticus* shows this: *God*, he says, *made man, and left him in the hand of his own counsel; he set before him life and death, water and fire; whatever shall please him will be given to him*[^30]. And through Isaiah God says: *If you are willing*, he says, *and listen to me, you shall eat the good things of the land. If you are unwilling, and do not listen, the sword shall devour you*[^31]. And the Apostle: *Come to your senses justly*, *and do not sin*[^32]; and again: *Do not err; God is not mocked; for whatever a man shall sow, that also shall he reap*[^33].

*Augustine.* These testimonies were spoken on account of that will by which each person does what he wills; so that, if it is not had, it may be asked from him *who works in us even to will*[^34]; but if it is had, let the works of justice be done, and to him who worked it let thanks be given.

**I. 46.** *Julian.* The will, then, is a motion of the mind, having in its own power whether it should run, more to the left, toward depraved things, or strive, more to the right, toward lofty things.

*Augustine.* What, then, is the meaning of: *Do not turn aside to the right, nor to the left*[^35]?

**I. 47.** *Julian.* But the motion of the mind of one who, by reason of age, can now use the judgment of reason; to whom, when punishment and glory are shown, or, on the contrary, advantage or pleasure, a help and, as it were, an occasion is offered, not a necessity imposed, of one side or the other. This will, then, which alternates, received the origin of its possibility in free choice; but the existence of the act itself it takes upon itself from itself, nor is it at all a will before it wills, nor can it will before it has also been able not to will; nor does it have both in the realm of sin, that is, to will and not to will, before it attains the use of reason. From which things gathered together, it appears that you defined most truly: *Sin is the will to retain or to commit what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain*. This sin, then, which has become clear to be nothing but the will, is established to have received its genus, that is, its very origin, from one's own appetite. Its species is found in each of those who are called individuals. Its difference, indeed, is both in the variety of faults and in the conditions of the times. Its mode is the very immoderation; for if the mode is to serve him whom you ought, whoever neglects this offends by transgression of the true mode. Here, however, it can be subtly said that there is a mode of sin, because no one offends more than he can; for if one sins beyond his strength, it is sinned with an ineffectual will; this very thing could come about even by the will alone. But quality is ascribed to the fault, by which it is shown what bitterness it carries with it, whether by disgrace or by pain. There is, then, sin, because if there were not, you would not pursue errors; but it is nothing other than the will departing from that path on which it ought to keep, and from which it is free not to swerve. And it comes about from the appetite for forbidden things; and it is nowhere except in that human being who both had an evil will and could have not had it.

*Augustine.* It is Adam himself whom that definition of ours, which pleased you, had in view, when I said: *Sin is the will to retain or to obtain what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain*. For Adam, when he sinned, had absolutely nothing of evil in himself by which, unwilling, he was driven to work evil, and on account of which he might say: *I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do*[^36]. And through this, in sinning he did what justice forbade, and from which he had been free to abstain. For to him who says: *The evil that I will not, this I do*, it is not free to abstain from it. And through this, if you distinguish these three things, and know that sin is one thing, the punishment of sin another, and both another—that is, sin in such a way that it is itself also the punishment of sin—you understand which of these three pertains to that definition, where there is the will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. For sin was defined in this way, not the punishment of sin, not both. But these three genera also have their species, about which it is now too long to dispute. If, however, examples of these three genera are sought, the example of the first genus occurs in Adam without any knot of difficulty. For there are many evils that human beings do, from which they are free to abstain; but to none is it so free as it was to him, who stood before his God, by whom he was created upright, depraved by absolutely no fault. But the example of the second genus, where there is only the punishment of sin, is in that evil which one in no part does, but only suffers; as when he who has sinned is, for his crime, killed, or is tortured by any other bodily punishment whatsoever. But the third genus, where it is both sin and the punishment of sin, can be understood in him who says: *The evil that I will not, this I do*. To this pertain also all the evils that, when they are done through ignorance, are not thought evils, or are even thought goods. For the blindness of the heart, if it were not sin, would be unjustly censured; but it is justly censured, where it is said: *Blind Pharisee!*[^37]; and in very many other places of the divine words. And again this same blindness, if it were not the punishment of sin, it would not be said: *For their malice blinded them*[^38]; and if it did not come from the judgment of God, we would not read: *Let their eyes be darkened that they may not see, and bend down their back always*[^39]. Now who is blind in heart willingly, since no one wishes to be blind even in body? Accordingly, original sin pertains neither to that which we put in the first place, where there is the will to do evil, from which one is free to abstain; otherwise it would not be in little ones, who do not yet use the choice of the will; nor to that which we mentioned in the second place. For we are now dealing with sin, not with punishment, which is not sin, although it follows by the deserving of sin; which indeed little ones too suffer, because there is in them a body dead because of sin[^40]. Yet the death of the body itself is not sin, nor any bodily torments whatsoever; but original sin pertains to this third genus, where it is so sin that it is itself also the punishment of sin; which is indeed in those being born, but begins to appear in them as they grow, when wisdom is necessary to the foolish, and continence to those who desire evil; yet the origin even of this sin descends from the will of the one sinning. *For there was Adam, and in him we all were; Adam perished, and in him all perished*.

**I. 48.** *Julian.* But it deserves both execration from the upright and lawful condemnation from that justice, in which the whole case here is at issue. With all the curtains drawn back, then, bring forth at last openly into the light by what you teach that sin is natural. Surely nothing has been gathered above falsely, either concerning the praise of the divine justice or concerning the definition of fault. Show, then, that these two things can stand in little ones: if there is no sin without the will, if there is no will where liberty has not been unfolded, if there is no liberty where there is no faculty of choice through reason; by what monster is sin found in infants, who do not have the use of reason? Therefore neither the faculty of choosing, and through this neither the will; and these things being irrefutably conceded, neither any sin at all. Pressed, then, by these masses, let us see whither you have burst out. *By no sin*, you say, *are little ones pressed of their own*, *but they are pressed by another's*. It has not yet become clear what evil you hold. For we suspect that you brought these things into the open for the purpose of incurring odium against some human being, whose iniquity, as a Punic orator, you might express. With whom, then, did an external crime burden unpolluted innocence? Who was that one, who would adjudge these guilty—so senseless, so savage, so forgetful of God and of equity, a barbarous foe? We praise your talent entirely; the erudition is apparent; you could not otherwise allege a person worthy of the hatred of the human race, of some judge or other, or rather a tyrant, than by swearing that he not only did not spare those who had sinned in nothing, but even those who could not even have sinned. For with a suspicious mind a good conscience is wont to labor for its own defense, lest perhaps it has offended, because it even could offend; but he is absolutely acquitted of crime who is defended by the very impossibility of the matter. Reveal, then, who is this condemner of the innocent. Answering: God; you have indeed struck the mind, but since so great a sacrilege scarcely deserves belief, let us be in doubt about what you have said. For we know that this name can be used homonymously: *For there are many gods and many lords; yet to us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things*[^41]. Which God, then, do you call into accusation? Here you, most religious priest and most learned rhetorician, exhale something more dismal and more dreadful than either the valley of Amsanctus or the pit of Avernus, rather more wicked than the very worship of idols had committed in these places. God, you say, the very one who *commends his love in us*, who loved us, and *did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us*[^42], he himself thus judges, he himself is the persecutor of those being born, he himself, for an evil will, hands over to eternal fires little ones whom he knows could have had neither a good nor an evil will. After this opinion, then, so monstrous, so sacrilegious, so deadly, if we had sound judges, I ought to report nothing but execration of you. For with just and probable gravity I would judge you unworthy of disputation, who had fled so far from religion, from erudition, from common sense, finally, that, what scarcely any barbarity would, you should think your Lord a criminal.

*Augustine.* It is no great thing that you see that little ones do not have their own will for choosing good or evil. I would wish you saw what he saw who, writing to the Hebrews, said that the son of Israel, Levi, was in the loins of his father Abraham when the latter was tithed, and that therefore he too was tithed in him[^43]. To this, if you had a Christian eye, you would discern by faith—if you were not strong in understanding—that in the loins of Adam were all who were going to arise from him through the concupiscence of the flesh; which, after the sin by which his nakedness was announced to him, he felt, beheld, blushed at, covered. Whence Ambrose, my teacher, praised excellently also by the mouth of your teacher, says: *What is more serious, then, Adam girded himself with this interpretation, in that place where he ought rather to have girded himself with the fruit of chastity. For in the loins, with which we are girded, certain seeds of generation are said to be. And therefore Adam was ill girded there with useless leaves, where he signified not the future fruit of the generation to come, but certain sins*. Deservedly too he says what I mentioned a little before: *There was Adam, and in him we all were; Adam perished, and in him all perished*. Not seeing this, you, blind, bark at me; but in me, whatever you say, you assuredly say also against him. Would, then, that my reward might be in common with him, just as I hear from you the reviling I have in common with him. What is it that you cry out, and say: *If we had sound judges, I ought to report nothing but execration of you*? Can I deal with you more generously, more beneficently, more liberally, than to set up between us as judge the very one about whom we already hold the judgment of your teacher Pelagius? Behold, here is present he *who, among the writers of the Latin tongue, shone forth as a certain beautiful flower, whose faith and most pure understanding in the Scriptures not even an enemy has dared to find fault with*. This Pelagius judged about Ambrose. What, then, did Ambrose judge about this which is at issue between us? I quoted above his statements about original sin, without any obscurity or ambiguity; but if that is too little, hear further: *We are all born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*. What do you answer to these things? Pelagius said those splendid things about Ambrose; these manifest things Ambrose declared for me against you; find fault with him about whom your master says that not even an enemy has dared to find fault with him; and you who seek sound judges, deny that this man is sound, so that you plainly profess yourself insane. But you, a most pious man, are indignant that little ones not reborn, if they die before the choice of their own will, are said, on account of another's sins, to be condemned by him who *commends his love in us*, who loved us, and *did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us*; as though the foolish and unlearned like you did not complain more seriously about him, who say: Why does he create those whom he foreknew would be impious and to be condemned? Why, finally, does he make those live, until they arrive at damnable impiety, whom, before they became such, he could take from this life; if he loves souls, *if he commends his love in us, if he did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*? To whom, if it be said: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^44] *His judgments are unsearchable*[^45]; they grow angry rather than gentle. But *the Lord knows who are his*[^46]. If, then, you wish to use sound judges, hear a sound judge praised singularly by your teacher. *There was Adam*, he says, *and in him we all were; Adam perished, and in him all perished*. But, you say, they certainly ought not to have perished by another's sins. They are another's, but they are a father's; and through this, by the right of sowing and germinating, they are ours too. Who frees from this perdition, except him who *came to seek what had perished*[^47]? In those, then, whom he frees, let us embrace mercy; but in those whom he does not free, let us acknowledge a judgment most hidden indeed, but without any doubt most just.

**I. 49.** *Julian.* That the god of light fought with the prince of darkness, the Manichee imagined and believed, and added that his substance is held captive in this world; but he strives to excuse so great an unhappiness with the color of piety, affirming that he fought, as it were, like a good citizen for his fatherland, and therefore exposed his members, lest he lose his kingdoms. You, who had learned these things, see how much you have profited by deserting them even for a time; you say that God did not endure the necessity of war, but admitted the iniquity of judgment; and that he is subject not to dark enemies, but to conspicuous crimes; that finally he did not impart his own substance, but violated eternal justice. Which of you is the worse, I leave to be judged by others. Yet this is clear, that you come back to one and the same wickedness of opinion. For the Manichee too subscribes injustice to his god, when he alleges that he will condemn, on the last day, the members he handed over; and you assert him to be unhappy through this, that he corrupts the glory by which he was renowned, and, by persecuting the innocence he created, lost the justice by which he was most holy. By so much, then, does the god whom your master had imagined excel this God whom you bring forward, as it is more excusable to have been overcome in battle than by fault.

*Augustine.* If the innocence of little ones pleases you, remove from them, if you can, the *heavy yoke* that is *upon the sons of Adam from the day of their going out from their mother's womb*[^48]. But I think that the Scripture which said this knew better than you what the innocence of the creature was, and what the justice of the Creator. Who, moreover, does not see that, if little ones have such innocence as you preach, there is no justice of God in their heavy yoke? Furthermore, since the divine justice is in their heavy yoke, there is not in them such innocence as you preach. Unless perhaps, for you laboring in this question, a God who is just indeed, but in some way weak, will be able to come to your aid, because he could not aid his own images, lest the innocent be pressed by the misery of a heavy yoke; so that you say he willed indeed, because he is just, but could not, because he is not almighty; and thus you escape from these straits in such a way as to lose the head of the faith, by which faith we first confess in the Creed that we believe in God the Father almighty. Your God, then, in so many and so great evils which little ones suffer, will lose either his justice, or his omnipotence, or his very care for human affairs; but whichever of these you say, see what you will be.

**I. 50.** *Julian.* Take yourself away, then, with such a God of yours, from the midst of the Churches; he is not the one in whom the Patriarchs, in whom the Prophets, in whom the Apostles believed, in whom the Church of the firstborn, which is enrolled in the heavens[^49], has hoped and hopes; he is not the one whom the rational creature believes to be its judge; whom the Holy Spirit announces will judge justly. No one of the prudent would ever have poured out his own blood for such a Lord; for he would not have deserved the affection of love, that he should impose the burden of a Passion to be undergone for him. Finally, this one whom you bring forward, if he existed anywhere, would be convicted of being a defendant, not a god; to be judged by my true God, not to judge in God's place. That you may, then, recognize the first foundations of the faith: our God, the God of the catholic Church, is unknown to us in substance, and likewise removed from sight; *whom no one of men has seen, nor can see*[^50]; as he is eternal without beginning, so he is holy and just without fault; most almighty, most equitable, most merciful, made known by the splendor of his virtues alone; the maker of all things that were not, the dispenser of those that are, the examiner of all who both are and will be and have been, on the last day; about to move the earth, the heaven, and all the elements together; the rouser of ashes, and the restorer of bodies; but for the sake of justice alone he will do all these things which we have said.

*Augustine.* If you worship the God of the Patriarchs, why do you not believe that the circumcision of the eighth day, which was commanded to Abraham, was a prefiguration of regeneration in Christ? For if you believed this, you would see that the soul of a little one could not justly be exterminated from his people, if he were not circumcised on the eighth day[^51], unless it had been bound by some sin? If you worship the God of the Prophets, why do you not believe that God so often said through them: *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the sons*[^52]? If you worship the God of the Apostles, why do you not believe that *the body is dead because of sin*[^53]? If you worship the God in whom the Church of the firstborn, which is enrolled in the heavens, has hoped and hopes, why do you not believe that little ones to be baptized are rescued from the power of darkness[^54], since for this reason the Church breathes upon them and exorcizes them, that the power of darkness may be sent out from them? But as for him whom the rational creature, which is in his saints and faithful, hopes will be its judge, read to us what third place, besides the kingdom for the good and punishment for the wicked, he has prepared and promised for your unregenerate innocents. But how do you say that no one of the prudent pours out blood for the Lord whom we worship, when the most glorious Cyprian both worshiped him and poured out his blood for him—Cyprian, who chokes you in this question, saying that a little one born carnally from Adam contracts, at his first birth, the contagion of the ancient death? Do you not see that you are rather the defendant, who blaspheme this God of the holy martyrs? You say that you worship a most almighty, most equitable, most merciful God; but he is most almighty who could beyond doubt take away from the sons of Adam the heavy yoke by which they are pressed from the day of their birth, or rather bring it about that they be weighed down by no such yoke at all; but he is most equitable, who would in no way impose it, or permit it to be imposed, unless he found in them sins with which they were born, the guilt of which the same most merciful one would loose for the reborn. If, then, the divine justice delighted you, you would surely see that it comes from that justice—not unjustly, of course—the human misery known to all, with which this life is carried through, beginning from the first weeping of those being born, all the way to the last breath of those dying; with happiness promised only to the saints and faithful, but in another life.

**I. 51.** *Julian.* For this my God, then, whom every creature and the holy Scripture announce to me to be such as I believe, I said I would do more rightly if I did not even think you worthy of contention by books. But since this office has been imposed on me above all by holy men, the confessors of our time, that I should examine what weight and reason your sayings have, it was opportune to show first that you do not believe in that God who has always been preached in the Church of the Catholics and will be preached until the end wherever she shall be.

*Augustine.* I have rather shown that you have not shown what you say you have shown; and I have taught, if you are not too blind, that I believe in that God who has always been preached by the Church of the Catholics.

**I. 52.** *Julian.* But now I will consequently examine by what testimonies you try to affirm this which the faith of the pious assails. But since I have set out to confront your second book, which Alypius brought, lest the sequence of my rejoinder be confused, a few things must still be answered, until the discourse reaches the testimony of the Apostle, by which you seem to yourself to be very much fortified. To those words of yours, then, which I set down above, you add these which follow: *In the things, then, which he passed over, he feared this, because it appeals to all the hearts of the catholic Church, and addresses, in a clear voice as it were, the very faith handed down and founded from of old, and most vehemently moves them against those who, as we said, "say there is no sin in little ones that is washed away by the laver of regeneration"*. *For all run to the Church with little ones for no other reason than that the original sin contracted in them by the generation of the first birth may be expiated by the regeneration of the second birth. Then he returns to our earlier words, which I do not know why he repeats:* *"But those who are born from such union we say draw original sin; and those, from whatever parents they are born, we do not deny are still under the devil, unless they are reborn in Christ"*. *These words of ours he had said a little before too. Then he subjoined what we said about Christ: "Who was unwilling to be born from the same union of both sexes." But here too he passed over what I set down: "That through his grace, rescued from the power of darkness, they may be transferred into the kingdom of him who was unwilling to be born from the same union of both sexes." See, I beseech you, what words of ours he avoided, as an enemy altogether of the grace of God, which came through Jesus Christ our Lord. For he knows that from that statement of the Apostle, in which he said of God the Father: "He rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love "*; *little ones are most wickedly and impiously separated; therefore, beyond doubt, he preferred to pass over these words rather than to set them down*. Am I an enemy of the grace of God, you most shameless of all men, who in my first book, from which you snatched these things torn from their context, in order to chatter something without reason, condemned with a pure and full profession your mouth dripping with the mysteries of the Manichees and your own?

*Augustine.* Will you make your case good by reviling? Say whose mouth, like mine, you boast that you have condemned. You will say: the Manichees'; but as a slanderer, not as a truth-teller. For I both detest the Manichees and their helpers, among whom you seek the chief place, and I refute both, with the Lord our God aiding and assisting, by catholic truth. But I will show you who are mine, whom you accuse in me, the more wickedly the more craftily. For in this case, where the question of original sin is at issue, on account of which you think me worthy of the most atrocious reproaches under the name of the Manichees, mine is Cyprian, who, though he said the little one had committed no sin, yet did not keep silent that he had contracted from Adam, at his first birth, the contagion of sin. Mine is Hilary, who, when he expounded what is read in the Psalm: *My soul shall live and shall praise you*[^55]: *He does not reckon*, he says, *that he lives in this life; since he had said: "Behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in offenses my mother bore me "*[^56], *he knows that he was born under the origin of sin, and under the law of sin*. Mine is Ambrose, praised most excellently by your teacher, who said: *We are all human beings born under sin, and our very origin is in fault; as you have read, David saying: "For behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in offenses my mother bore me ". Therefore the flesh of Paul was a body of death*. Mine is Gregory, who, when he was speaking about Baptism, says: *Venerate the birth through which you have been freed from the bonds of earthly birth*. Mine is Basil, who, when he was treating of fasting, says: *Because we did not fast, we fell from paradise; let us fast that we may return to it*. Mine is John of Constantinople, saying: *Adam committed that great sin, and condemned the whole human race in common*. All these, and their other associates who think the same, whom it is long to recall, are mine; if you acknowledge it, they are yours too; but they are my teachers, your disapprovers. How, then, did you condemn my mouth and that of mine, when you are rather condemned by the most concordant and most truthful mouth of those whom you behold to be mine? Do you dare, against these lights of the city of God, with a most darkened mind, a most shameless brow, a most insolent tongue, to cast the charge of being a Manichee? But if you do not dare, why do you dare it against me for no other reason than that I say what they say against whom you do not dare it?

**I. 53.** *Julian.* For there this order of words was set down by me, when I had said that God is the author of heaven and earth, and of all things that are in them, and through this also of human beings, for whose sake all things were made. *But it does not escape me*—I say—*that, when we say these things, this will be spread abroad about us, that we do not think the grace of Christ necessary for little ones. Which laudably and vehemently offends the Christian peoples; if, however, they did not consider us the authors of a saying in itself impious; for in that way they would neither incur a crime by believing false things about their brothers, and would prove themselves zealous about the love of the faith. This part, then, must be fortified by us* *against the assault of vanity, and the mouth of those who speak against us must be sewn up by a brief confession. We, then, confess the grace of Baptism to be useful to all ages so far that we strike with eternal anathema all those who think it not necessary even for little ones. But this grace we believe to be rich in spiritual gifts, which, fertile with many gifts and venerable for its virtues, according to the kinds of weaknesses and the diversity of human conditions, heals by one and the same power, the bestower both of remedies and of gifts. When it is applied, it is not to be changed according to the cases; for it dispenses its own gifts according to the capacity of those who approach. For just as all the arts, not according to the diversity of the materials which they take up to be worked, themselves also suffer either losses or increases, but, holding themselves always and in one way, are adorned with manifold effect; so too, according to the Apostle: "one faith, one Baptism "*[^57]. *And they are multiplied and extended in gifts, nor yet are they changed in the orders of the mysteries. But this grace is not opposed to justice, which washes away the stains of iniquity; nor does it make sins, but purifies them; which absolves the guilty, does not calumniate the innocent. For Christ, who is the redeemer of his own work, increases his benefits toward his own image by continual liberality; and those whom he had made good by founding them, he makes better by renewing and adopting them*. This grace, then, through which pardon for the guilty, spiritual illumination, the adoption of the sons of God, citizenship in the heavenly Jerusalem, sanctification, and transfer into the members of Christ, and possession of the Kingdom of heaven are given to mortals, whoever thinks must be denied to any, deserves the execration of all good men.

*Augustine.* In all these gifts of God's grace which you mentioned, that which you put first—that pardon for the guilty is given through it—you do not want to pertain to little ones, who, you deny, draw any guilt from Adam. Why, then, does God deny the rest to many little ones, who die without this grace at that age? Why, I say, is spiritual illumination not given to them, the adoption of the sons of God, citizenship in the heavenly Jerusalem, sanctification, and transfer into the members of Christ, and possession of the kingdom of heaven? Would God, in whom is the supreme power, deny these so many gifts, so necessary, to so many of his own images that, according to you, have no sin, when no contrary will of the little ones forbids this benefit from him? You certainly, in order to remove from yourselves this odium, by which you are said to deny the grace of Baptism to little ones, said that whoever thinks that grace must be denied to any deserves the execration of all good men. The equity of God almighty would not, then, deny it to the innumerable little ones who die without it under his omnipotence, if they deserved nothing evil in his hidden judgment. By which judgment, owed to all who come from the stock of Adam, whoever are freed according to grace, not according to debt, let them glory not in their own merits, but in the Lord. You, then, if you wish to be free of the reproach by which you have become detestable to the catholic Church, let Christ be Jesus to little ones. Which will not be at all, if he does not confer on them that for which he received this name, that is, if he does not save them from their sins[^58]. Say this, then, about this grace, so that you may be free of the offense to Christians of which you complain, what the learned catholic and teacher Gregory said: *Venerate the birth through which you have been freed from the bonds of earthly birth*. In no way, then, do you confess that little ones pertain to this grace, as long as you deny that they are freed by the heavenly birth from the bonds of earthly birth.

**I. 54.** *Julian.* Since I have fortified these things, as this place meanwhile allowed, let us return to the point from which we digressed; intending to speak more fully about this very matter wherever it shall be opportune. Behold, with how great a light of confession I have reproved both those who would deny Baptism to little ones, and you who, by its prejudgment, dare to stain the justice of God; protesting that I hold nothing other than that the instituted mysteries are to be treated, with the very words in which they were handed down, in absolutely every age, nor ought to be changed according to the variety of cases; but that a sinner is made from evil perfectly good; while the innocent one, who has no evil of his own will, is made from good better, that is, best; so that both indeed pass, consecrated, into the members of Christ; but the one caught in an evil life, the other in a good nature. For the former corrupted by depraved action the innocence which his origin had received; but the latter, without praise, without crime of the will, has this alone, which he received from God his founder, who, happier in his unfeigned newness, could not vitiate the good of his own simplicity, having no merit from his acts, but retaining this alone, which he possessed by the gracious favor of so great a workman.

*Augustine.* Why, then, is there a heavy yoke upon him from the day of his going out from his mother's womb[^59]? Why so great a corruptibility of the body, that his soul is weighed down by it[^60]? Why so great a dullness of mind, that his slowness is educated even by blows? How long, Julian, are you heavy of heart? How long do you love vanity, and seek a lie[^61], by which your heresy may be propped up? Surely, if no one had sinned, if human nature had remained in the goodness in which it was created, would a human being, even in paradise, be born to these miseries, to be silent about others?

**I. 55.** *Julian.* That age, then, just as, renewed, it proclaims the mercy of Christ, that is, advanced by the power of the renewing mystery; so, whether accused or weighed down, it convicts the iniquity of the judge, the infamy of justice.

*Augustine.* From what oldness is that age said to be renewed, since it is new in its origin? These are deceitful lips; if you wish to recognize the oldness from which little ones are renewed by Christian grace, hear faithfully what the man of God, Reticius, bishop of Autun, says, who once sat as judge with Melchiades, the Roman bishop, and condemned Donatus the heretic. For when he was speaking about Christian Baptism: *This, then*, he says, *that it is the principal indulgence in the Church, escapes no one, in which we lay aside the whole weight of the ancient crime, and blot out the old wicked deeds of our ignorance; where we also put off the old man with his inborn crimes*. Do you hear: not the deeds perpetrated afterward, but even the inborn crimes of the old man? Was this Reticius a Manichee? How, then, do you not deceitfully say that little ones are renewed in Christian regeneration, you who will not acknowledge in the old man the evils which the weight of the ancient crime engendered? Then, if that age, weighed down, convicts the iniquity of the judge, as you say, is it not so weighed down by the heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam? And yet God is not from this unjust; and through this it is deservedly weighed down. But there is no evil merit of this age, if there is no original sin.

**I. 56.** *Julian.* Infancy, then, is shown to be guilty not by the unity of the sacrament, but is approved, by the truth of judgment, to be nothing other than innocent.

*Augustine.* You think you have found why he is baptized; say why he is breathed upon. Surely the statement of Pelagius, your author, was thought great and unconquerable, where he says: *If Adam's sin harmed even those who do not sin, then the justice of Christ too profits even those who do not believe*. What, then, do you say about little ones when they are baptized? Do they believe, or do they not believe? If you say: They do not believe, how does the justice of Christ not profit them even though they do not believe, so that they possess the Kingdom of heaven? Or if it profits them, as you are compelled to confess; then Adam's sin so harmed them, who did not yet have a will to sin, as the justice of Christ profits them, who do not yet have a will to believe? But if you say: They believe through others, then they also sinned through another. And since it is true that they believe through others (for on this account they are called faithful throughout the whole Church), they surely pertain to that which the Lord said: *But he who will not believe shall be condemned*[^62]. They will, then, be condemned if they do not believe through others, since they cannot believe through themselves; but they could in no way be justly condemned, if they were not born under sin, and through this also under the prince of sin. For this reason, then, they are also breathed upon. Remove from them your deceptive vanity; *let the little ones come* to Jesus[^63], who *saves his people* (among whom they certainly are too) *from their sins*[^64].

**I. 57.** *Julian.* Although the sequence of reasoning bids me to linger over explaining the state of little ones—which sequence does not allow things joined by their own law to be divided. Moreover, the loss of those being born would be easier to bear, if majesty itself were not endangered along with them. Excuse God, then, and accuse the little one; let him be taught to do what is just, who without justice cannot be God; and let whatever person you please undergo the punishment. But now, apart from sacrilege, the things you think to be joined together vehemently contend with one another. For you say that, since little ones too are imbued with the same mysteries as idolaters and parricides, all can be convicted as wicked; and you add a thing much more absurd, that by the author of this Sacrament, with which we are dealing, another's sins are imputed to the innocent. This is what I said is at odds, because the nature of things does not admit that at one and the same time God should be both so merciful as to forgive each one confessing his own sins, and so cruel as to charge against the innocent another's. Of these, when you have granted one, you have taken away the other; if he grants pardon to the guilty, he does not calumniate the innocent; if he calumniates the innocent, he never spares the guilty.

*Augustine.* It is rather you who make God unjust, since it seems to you unjust to repay the sins of the fathers upon the sons, which he both often testifies in words that he does and shows by deeds. You, I say, make God unjust, under whose almighty care, when you see little ones pressed by the heavy yoke of misery, you contend that they have no sin, accusing at once both God and the Church; God indeed, if the undeserving are weighed down and afflicted; but the Church, if those free from the rule of diabolical power are breathed upon. But where did you dream that we equate the original sins of little ones to those of idolaters and parricides? Yet the remission of sins put into the mysteries, in sins both greater and lesser, and more and fewer, and single, is true; but in no sins, such as you say is the case with little ones, it is false. But original sins are another's, on account of no choice of our will in them, in such a way that nevertheless, on account of the contagion of origin, they are found to be ours too. What is it, then, that you cry out, and say that God cannot both forgive their own sins to adults and impute another's to little ones; and you will not attend to this, that he forgives both only to those reborn in Christ, but neither to those not reborn in Christ? For the very sacraments of Christian grace are hidden from the wise and prudent, and revealed to little ones[^65]. Among whom, oh, if you were, and did not trust as a great man in your own strength, you would surely understand that the injustice of the first man is so imputed to little ones who are begotten, to undergo punishment, as the justice of the second man is imputed to little ones who are reborn, to obtain the Kingdom of heaven; although by their own will and act neither are they found to have imitated the former in evil, nor the latter in good.

**I. 58.** *Julian.* Thus I passed over nothing in your words, compelled, as you say, by fear. For what could I dread in the monuments of so elegant a talent, unless perhaps this alone, that I endure horror at the assault of your obscenity?

*Augustine.* If you were buying these revilings, I would call you a spendthrift; they lie at hand for you free; why do you not enjoy them, with which you feed your slanderous mind?

**I. 59.** *Julian.* Hear, then, against what you have said, briefly: they are not hearts of the catholic Church, which your discourse addresses, if they are at odds with piety and reason. Which both they commit, when they neither think well about the equity of God, nor understand the wisdom and riches of the mysteries which they accuse. This is not the faith handed down and founded from of old, except in the councils of the malignant, inspired by the devil, brought forth by the Manichee, celebrated by Marcion, Faustus, Adimantus, and all their satellites, and now belched forth by you into Italy, which we grievously bemoan.

*Augustine.* With what mouth, with what brow do you call a council of the malignant the agreement of so many catholics who were teachers of the Churches before us? As though, indeed, if there sat, in a council of bishops which you say ought to be gathered—not wholesomely, but boastfully, on account of your questions—the bishops whom I mentioned above, to omit others, Cyprian, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Basil, John of Constantinople, you would easily find any of those who now remain whom you could equal to them, let alone prefer, in the ecclesiastical doctrine handed down from of old. Since, then, they themselves bring forth against you, concerning original sin, open and clear statements, both those which I set down a little before, and very many others; do you dare to call the agreement of these in catholic truth a council of the malignant? And do you consider what you contradict in them, and not rather where you may flee, if you are unwilling to agree with them? But since you said that I belched forth into Italy what you bemoan, I throw back upon your brow that same bishop of Italy, Ambrose, praised by your teacher. *We are all human beings*, he says, *born under sin, and our very origin is in fault; as you have read, David saying: "Behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in offenses my mother bore me "*. *Therefore the flesh of Paul was a body of death, as he himself says: "Who will free me from the body of this death? "*. *But the flesh of Christ condemned sin, which it did not feel by being born, which it crucified by dying, that there might be in our flesh justification through grace*, *where before there was foulness through fault*. This faith, then, I say is handed down and founded from of old; you refuse it, and do not look at whom you struggle against. Will you be able to say that it was inspired in this man by the devil? Is this man a Manichee? Is he Marcion, Faustus, Adimantus? Certainly not, but far unlike them, and far contrary. Let Pelagius at least say who this man is. He, he is the one, *whose faith and most pure understanding in the Scriptures not even an enemy has dared to find fault with*. What is it, Julian? Where do you see yourself? He whose faith not even an enemy has dared to find fault with had this faith, which you so find fault with that you attribute it to councils of the malignant. Behold, it is not theirs, but it is Ambrose's; because it is true, because it is sound, because, as I said, it is handed down and founded from of old, this faith is mine too. I did not, then, belch this forth into Italy (which you say you bemoan); but rather I received the laver of regeneration from this bishop of Italy who preached and taught it. Since this faith is catholic, and yet is not yours; where, then, are you? See, I beseech you, and return. It is to your advantage to see, not to envy; we desire that you return, not perish.

**I. 60.** *Julian.* There is no sin in a human being, if there is nothing of his own will or assent; this race of men, which has even a little understanding, agrees with me without doubt. But you concede that there was nothing of their own will in little ones; not I, but reason concludes: there is, then, no sin in them. In no way, then, are they brought to the Church for this, that they may be defamed, or rather that they may defame God: but they are brought that they may praise God, whom they declare the author both of natural goods and of spiritual gifts.

*Augustine.* They are not defamed, when they are breathed upon, but are rescued from the power of darkness; nor do they defame God, but by the Creator by whom they were born, by him they need a Savior; and therefore, by being reborn, they are transferred from Adam to Christ. But where you said: *There is no sin in a human being, if there is nothing of his own will or assent*, you would speak more fully the truth if you added: Or of contagion.

**I. 61.** *Julian.* But original sin, if it is contracted by the generation of the first birth, can indeed condemn the marriages instituted by God, but cannot be taken away from little ones; since what is born in remains, until the end of him to whom it has clung from the causes of his beginnings.

*Augustine.* It neither condemns marriages, because they themselves are not its cause; and it is taken away by that Almighty one, who could even be born a man without it.

**I. 62.** *Julian.* We stir up no calumny against you, then, as though you condemn marriage, and as though you say that the human being who is born from it is the work of the devil; nor do we object this faithlessly, nor gather it unskillfully; but we examine carefully and simply what is the effect of the consequent statements. For corporeal marriages are never without union. You say that whoever are born from that union pertain to the devil; beyond doubt you declare that marriages pertain to the right of the demon.

*Augustine.* Do we say that marriages could have existed in paradise without union of body, if no one had sinned? But there would not be there the evil which conjugal chastity now uses well. But that evil is from the wound which diabolical cunning inflicted. From this the offspring of mortals is held guilty; from this it is that whoever is born is born under the prince of sinners, until he is reborn in Christ, who had absolutely no sin; by whom alone the bond of death is loosed, because he alone was free among the dead[^66].

**I. 63.** *Julian.* You say that sin is drawn by the condition of nature, you who want this evil to have come about from the will of the first man. I defer here the response by which you must be convicted of lying without shame. But as for what concerns the present place, I gather, and believe the reasoning of wisdom, that you plainly define nature, without ambiguity, to be diabolical. For if in it or through it is that on account of which a human being is possessed by the devil; it is irrefutably the devil's, that through which he could claim for himself the image of God. Or rather, it is not even the image of God, which through its origin is in the kingdom of the devil.

*Augustine.* You believe, but with opining folly, not with reasoning wisdom. Allow little ones to be rescued from the power of darkness, that they may be transferred into the kingdom of Christ. For by saying that they do not have the contagion of the ancient offense, and so removing them from the mercy of the Savior, who *saves his people from their sins*[^67], whence he was called Jesus; you accomplish nothing except that the wrath of God remains upon them, of which Job spoke, saying: *Man born of woman, of short life, and full of wrath, and like the flower of the field he falls, but flees like a shadow, and does not stand; have you not made account of him too, and made him enter into judgment in your sight? For who will be clean from filth? Not even one, even if his life upon the earth be of one day*[^68]. But of course, merciful man that you are, you pity the image of God, that you may not say it is born carnally under sin. O how cruel is this vain mercy of yours, which denies to little ones the mercy of their Savior; who *came to seek what had perished*[^69]! Through that filth, then, without which the man of God says there is no one, *even if his life upon the earth be of one day*, the devil claims for himself the image of God, not through the substance which God created. For the nature is vitiated, it is not the vice. But you: *It is not even the image of God*, you say, *which through its origin is in the kingdom of the devil*. What if another should say to you: It is not the image of God, which, guilty of no sin, nevertheless does not enter into the kingdom of God? Will you not have what to answer, if you are unwilling to answer empty things? And certainly a human being is the image of God for this reason, that he was made to the likeness of God[^70]. Why, then, is he also *made like to vanity*, on account of which *his days pass away like a shadow*[^71]? For you will not separate little ones from this vanity, since their days pass away like a shadow. Finally, will you separate them even from the living? Hear, then, him who says in the Psalm: *Behold, you have made my days old, and my substance is as nothing before you; yet all is vanity, every living man*[^72]. Since, then, every living man is the image of God, say whence *every living man* is also *vanity*. But what will you say, you who will not acknowledge that one of these is from the condition of God, the other came about from the condition of sin? Permit, we beseech you, that the living man, who was made to the likeness of God, be rescued from the power of darkness, under which he *was made like to vanity*; let him be rescued now, meanwhile, from the obligation of guilt; but after this corruptible life, from all vanity too.

**I. 64.** *Julian.* If, then, you read my work, you will cease to wonder why I returned to your words, which I had set down above. For I had promised that I would prove from your writings that you, between the impiety you had drunk and the odium of it which you fear, had said both alike, both what is wont to be asserted by Catholics and what by Manichees. And so there is set down at your chapter such an order of words, which now, with the brow of a sycophant, you have lyingly said was interpolated. I know I promised great things, that is, that I would approve, from the discourses of my adversary, both that those who deny that human beings are the work of God are justly condemned, and that this same man, who confesses this, does nothing else than confirm that whatever proceeds from the fecundity of marriage is the property of the devil. For by this kind, by the very laws of his own defense, the opinion of the Manichees will be destroyed. But the very openings of his book made this public. For he says that human beings, who are born from marriages, that is, from males and females, are a divine work; by which statement he overturns all that he was going to do, and assents to us who say that those are impious who dare to deny these things. One part, then, is now settled: it remains that I show that he establishes what he had just now assailed. With these things said, I took up part of your chapter, in which you had said: *Those who are born from such union we say draw original sin, and those, from whatever parents they are born, we do not deny are still under the devil, unless they are reborn in Christ, and, rescued through his grace from the power of darkness, are transferred into the kingdom of him who was unwilling to be born from the same union of both sexes*. Why, then, do you think you can be excused from the error of the Manichees by this, that you dared to insert a statement with which you were struggling with all the powers of your talent; when this is not a defense of your error, but a testimony of singular stupidity, you who think that, in the manner of Calliphon, virtue and vices, justice and iniquity, can be brought into a covenant by your discourse? But as for what the Apostle says: *Who rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^73]; read the fourth book of my work, and then what the Teacher of the Gentiles meant will become known to you.

*Augustine.* Our sixth answered your fourth, and now I rather urge that those who wish to know how far you were there astray from the truth, and by how great a truth you are convicted, should read those books of yours and mine. But about this little sheet, in which certain things excerpted from your books were written out, you are free to impute to me what he did who sent it to the man by whom it was sent to me. For he put in it from your work what he wished, and passed over what he wished; about which I have already answered you above briefly and sufficiently. Why do you try to wrap yourself in your obscurities against the manifest things of the Apostle? He, proclaiming God, says: *Who rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*; and you say this was said by him with little ones excepted. If, then, little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness, they are not dead; if they are not dead, Christ did not die for them; but you confess that Christ died even for them; and the same Apostle says: *One died for all; therefore all are dead*[^74]. This conclusion of the Apostle is unconquerable; and through this, since he died also for little ones, surely little ones too are dead. Christ, moreover, died, *that he might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil*[^75]. Allow, then, little ones to be rescued from the power of darkness, that they may live. But what is it, that you object to me the manner, or the error, of Calliphon, in this, that you say I think virtue and vices, justice and iniquity, can be brought into a covenant by my discourse? Far be it from me that I should either hold this in my heart or persuade it in speech; but I congratulate you that you have understood this philosopher so well. For since he thought the good of a human being to be in the virtue of the mind and in the pleasure of the body; you say he wished to join virtues and vices; and through this, as was fitting, you have judged the appetite for bodily pleasure to be a vice; lust, then, which you praise, is a vice. The truth, then, crept somehow into your understanding, so that, deserting a little the cause you had taken up, you said what we say.

**I. 65.** *Julian.* I argued, then, and rightly argued, the dejected and feeble variation, by which it had been brought about that you both premised that you do not condemn marriage, and said that, on account of the union of man and woman—which it is clear comes from the condition and nature of marriage, or rather in which alone (so far as concerns our conflict) the truth of marriage is—human beings are transcribed into the rights of the devil.

*Augustine.* If the truth of marriage is in the union of man and woman alone; then the truth of adulteries is the same as that of marriages, because in both there is this union of both sexes. But if that is most absurd; the truth of marriage is not in the union of male and female alone, as you rave, although without it marriages cannot propagate children; but there are other things properly pertaining to marriage, by which marriages are distinguished from adulteries, such as the fidelity of the conjugal bed, and the care of procreating children in an orderly way, and—which is the greatest difference—the good use of an evil, that is, the good use of the concupiscence of the flesh, which evil adulterers use badly.

**I. 66.** *Julian.* Which union you have tried to persuade us is so execrable that you would have it understood that Christ wished to be born of a virgin mother, not on account of the splendor of the sign, but on account of the union of the sexes to be condemned. What, then, could ever have been said by anyone more wickedly or more shamelessly than this, that you separated two kings, as it were, contending for the possession of humanity, by these signs, and their two kingdoms, so as to say that whatever marriage had brought forth was the devil's, but God's only what the Virgin had borne? What is it else than to show the one who made the Virgin fruitful as most destitute by the poverty of his portion, and to deny that he is the founder of those who come forth from the marriages of human beings? Let the diligent reader, then, hold the handwriting of your word, and know that you, the faithful disciple of the Manichees and the chief of the Traducian nation, have condemned nothing other than the union of lawful marriage.

*Augustine.* You do not have senses exercised to separate good from evil. The nature and substance both of men and of Angels, whether good or evil, subsists with God as Creator; but the vices of natures and substances, which the Manichees say are natures and substances, but which the truth denies, the just and almighty God permits to exist by a judicial ordering; and these are evils, which cannot exist except from good and in good natures. But whatever things are subjected to him, by God's judging, are so in the power of the devil that they cannot be foreign to the power of God, under which the devil himself too has been placed. Since, then, all Angels and all human beings are under the power of God, vain is your loquacity, by which you say that God and the devil have divided between themselves which of them has whom under his own power. But against whom, with a raw breast, you vomit forth these revilings on which you feed, attend a little. Behold, here is Ambrose; about this which you inveigh against, see what he says: *How could he alone be just, when all generation went astray, unless, born of a Virgin, he were held least of all by the privilege of a generation subject to guilt?* Hear still, hear, and check your insolent tongue by striking your brow: *For no virile coition*, he says, *unsealed the secrets of the virginal womb; but the Holy Spirit poured an immaculate seed into the inviolable womb; for alone among those born of woman, the Lord Jesus is holy in all things, who, by the newness of his immaculate birth, did not feel the contagions of earthly corruption, and by his heavenly majesty drove them away*. You see indeed, do you not, who said what I say? You see, whatever you say against me, against whom you say it? For if I am from this a disciple of the Manichee, so too is he. But he who said these things before us is not this; this man, then, is not it, whoever says these things; but whoever contradicts this ancient catholic teaching is a manifest heretic.

**I. 67.** *Julian.* But let us now proceed to the rest. Writing about me, then, after those words of yours which I wove in above, you add these which follow: *After these things he set down that statement of ours, where we said: For this shameful concupiscence, which is shamelessly praised by the shameless, would be none, unless a human being had first sinned; but marriages would exist, even if no one had sinned; for the sowing of children would come about without this disease. Thus far he set down* *my words. For he feared what I added: It could come about in the body of that life, without which it now cannot come about in the body of this death. And here, that he might not finish my statement, but as it were lop it off, he dreaded that testimony of the Apostle, where he says: "Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord "*[^76]. *For there was no body of this death in paradise before sin, on account of which we said: In the body of that life, which was there, the sowing of children could come about without this disease, without which it cannot come about in the body of this death*. You indeed keep your custom—and consistently in this work especially—that, since you act against the truth, you say nothing true; but a multitude of corrections scarcely suffices for the numerous sins of your erudition. Therefore here I will now briefly note that you deceive; but me—you will understand, even after this work—to be unaccustomed to lying. To yourself, then, claim the whole possession of this vice, so that you can hear from the Gospel, and not unjustly indeed, that you are from the beginning a liar, like your father[^77]—whether the one to whose dominion you say you pertained at your birth, or the other, a secondary one, who imbued you with elegant mysteries, which nevertheless cannot be named among honest men. I brought forth, then, all this in my earlier work, which you pretend was passed over; with how great a truth and light of disputation it was refuted, if you read nearly the last parts of my first volume, even you yourself will be able to confess. Not, then, your statement lopped off, but your whole one, was destroyed by a strong response. But now hear briefly: this which the Apostle says: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me* *from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*; he referred not to the mortality of our body, which the flesh of animals received from the institution of nature, but to the habit of sinning; from which guilt, after the incarnation of Christ, through the New Testament, whoever migrates to the pursuits of virtue is freed. There, then, speaking under the person of the Jews, who err by the desire of allurements even after the prohibition of the sacred law, he shows that there was one sole help in that time, if they believed in Christ; who so enjoined cautions about the future as to forgive pardon for what was done; nor did he press the guilty under the temptation of punishment, but cherished those who ran with a most liberal bosom; not dismaying those cast down by terror, but restoring those corrected by kindness. Which kindness he himself had already felt, who said: *It is a human saying, that Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first; but therefore I obtained mercy, that in me Christ Jesus might show all patience, for the example of those who shall believe in him unto eternal life*[^78]. That you might understand this to pertain to an evil life, not to the nature of human beings; lest, through the cause of Christ's coming, you should think that even little ones were declared by him to be sinners, he says: *In me* he showed *all patience*. But the patience of God is that of which he speaks to the Romans: *Or are you ignorant that the goodness of God leads you to repentance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart you treasure up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath*[^79]. The patience of God is exercised, then, when a human conversion is awaited for no small time. But in little ones patience cannot appear. For if there were sins of nature, which the Savior would ascribe to them; he would falsely indeed be called patient, but most certainly savage. But God cannot be anything but pious and just, which my God Jesus Christ is; whose patience either Paul, long a persecutor, or others under whose person he speaks, experienced; because they were long awaited, though freed late. And through this, by the Apostle the life of human beings, not their nature, is condemned. Commending, then, this grace to the Jews, because the law punishes the wicked, and does not have that efficacy of mercy which Baptism has, in which the offenses of one's acts are purified by a brief confession, he shows that they ought to run to Christ, to implore the help of this indulgence; and to notice that the law of morals threatens by the wounds of vices, but grace heals efficaciously and quickly. He called sins, then, the body of death, not the flesh; for if he had declared this of the misery of the members, which you reckon befell on account of sin, he would more rightly have called it the death of the body than the body of death. But that you may know that, according to the custom of the Scriptures, sins are called members; read to the Colossians, where the same Apostle says: *Mortify your members, which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, and avarice, which is the service of idols; on account of which the wrath of God comes upon the sons of unbelief, among whom you too once walked, when you lived in them*[^80]. Behold how he calls members what he declares to be sins. But the body of sin, here the same to the Romans: *Our old man*, he says, *is crucified together with him, that the body of sin may be destroyed, that we may no longer serve sin*[^81]. In this manner, then, here too he exclaimed, under the person, as we said, of the Jews: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?* that is: Who will free me from the guilt of my sins, which I committed when I could have avoided them? Which the severity of the law does not forgive, but avenges? Who will be able to vindicate me from these members, that is, from the vices which I gathered by imitating the wicked, so as to build up a body full of sin? Who, I say? And he answered, as if admonished by the very voice of the things: *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*. The grace of God, which credits the justice of the faithful without works, according to what David says: *Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed the man to whom the Lord has not imputed sin*[^82]. He, then, who makes a human being blessed, is himself also blessed, the eternal justice, through which he does not forgive sin, except what he could by right have imputed. But he could not by right have imputed, if the one to whom it is imputed could not also have avoided it. But no one can avoid natural things. Therefore no one at all can have sin by the necessity of nature. Let it suffice to have said these things briefly.

*Augustine.* You have tried indeed to turn, by disputing, this which the Apostle says: *Who will free me from the body of this death?* into your meaning; but that you could not do this, he saw better who sent the little sheet to the illustrious man; and therefore, mentioning my words, he passed this over, lest your awaited and brought-forth response be laughed at. For who would not laugh that—I know not whether you could persuade yourselves of it, and yet you thought it should be persuaded to others—the Apostle introduced the person of a Jew, not yet placed under the grace of Christ, and saying: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me* *from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*. Is it really a Jew, and not yet a Christian, who says: The grace of God will free me through Jesus Christ our Lord? But this I pass over; but who could bear that a man should be thought to say, about his past sins: *Who will free me from the body of this death?* so that they may be remitted to him through the grace of the pardoning Christ; when it appears clearly whence he came to these words? Behold, his words are in our ears; let us see, then, whether he confesses himself wretched from what he does willing, or from what he does unwilling. The man cries out: *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*. He cries out: *It is now no longer I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me. For I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, the good; for to will is present to me, but to accomplish the good I do not find. For I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do*[^83]. He does not say: I did, but: *I do*; he does not say: I worked, but: *I work*; he does not say: I acted, but: *I do*; and this: Not what I will, but: *what I will not*. Finally, he is delighted together with the law of God according to the inner man; but he sees another law in his members, fighting against the law of his mind; by which law certainly he is compelled, not to do the good that he wills, but the evil that he wills not, this to do. On account of this he exclaims: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^84] And you, against the clearest truth, close your eyes, and expound his groan, not as it is plain to all, but as it pleases you, saying: *Who will free me from the body of this death?* that is: Who will free me from the guilt of my sins which I committed? He says: *The evil that I will not, this I do*; and you say: Which I committed. Do you so despair of the men who read these things that they would not rather hear him than you, and believe him rather than you? Allow a human being to ask for the grace of God, not only because he has sinned, that he may be absolved, but also that he may not sin, that he may be helped: which is dealt with in this place. For he who says: *The evil that I will not, this I do*, has no occasion to say: *Forgive us our debts*, but: *Lead us not into temptation*[^85]. *But each one is tempted*, as the apostle James says, *drawn and enticed by his own concupiscence*[^86]. This is the evil about which he says: *I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, the good*. This is the evil in the body of this death. This evil was not in paradise before sin; because this flesh was not yet the body of this death; to which it will be said in the end: *Where, O death, is your contention?*[^87] But it will be said then, when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality[^88]; but now it is a body of death; because the same Apostle said: *The body is dead because of sin*[^89]. Hear the catholic interpreters of the Apostle; receive their words, not mine, with which I receive your revilings; hear not Pelagius, but Ambrose. *The flesh of Paul too*, he says, *was a body of death, as he himself says: "Who will free me from the body of this death?"*. Hear not Caelestius, but Gregory: *Within ourselves*, he says, *we are assailed by our own vices and passions, and day and night we are pressed by the fiery goads of this body of lowliness and body of death, now secretly, now even openly, with the allurements of visible things provoking and irritating us everywhere, this mire of dregs, to which we have clung, exhaling the stench of its own filth through more capacious veins; but also with the law of sin, which is in our members, fighting against the law of the spirit*. Barking against these lights of the heavenly city, you say: *The body of death he called sins, not the flesh*; denying that the Apostle referred this to the mortality of our body: *Which the flesh*, you say, *of animals received from the institution of nature*. For you hold this opinion, which Pelagius in the judgment of Palestine condemned with a feigned heart, namely that Adam was made mortal, so that whether he sinned or did not sin, he would die. And so, resisting these men, and other associates of theirs in the sound faith, so many and so great teachers, you are compelled to fill paradise, even if no one had sinned, with the pain of those giving birth, the labor of those being born, the groans of those languishing, the funerals of those dying, the grief of those mourning. What wonder, then, if you are also outside this paradise, which is the Church, you who make that paradise, from which they went out who sent us by sinning into these miseries, such as—I do not say no Christian, but not even any human being—dares to suspect, unless he be insane?

**I. 68.** *Julian.* For in the first work these things were disputed at greater length. Although you do not even speak clearly what death you wish to be understood, whose body you say was not in paradise before sin; because in the books which you published to the name of Marcellinus, you professed that Adam was made mortal. But as for what you add, that the business of marriage is a disease, it can be tolerably heard, if you say this only about your own parents. For you can perhaps be conscious of some hidden disease of your mother, whom in the books of your *Confessions*, to use the very word, you noted to have been called a tippler. But in the wedlock of the saints, and of all honest men, there is absolutely no disease. Because the Apostle did not grant a disease as a remedy, when, through reverence for marriage, he was fortifying the men of the Church against the disease of fornication; which sense, how it has utterly erased both your brow and your teaching, is shown near the last part of my first volume; which has also been explained in the whole body of the response itself, as the opportuneness of the places afforded.

*Augustine.* Nowhere has your guile so appeared, and your conscience condemned through knowledge. For you know, you know altogether; for it is so open that whoever reads those books cannot fail to know it; you know, I say, that in the books I published to Marcellinus, I argued vehemently against your heresy, already beginning then to rise, that no one should believe that Adam, even if he had not sinned, would have died. But since I have hitherto said that he was mortal, because he could die—for he could sin—you wished, by an insidious deception, if those who have not read those books, and perhaps will not read them, should read these things of yours, to filch from them; as though I had said that Adam was made mortal in such a way that whether he sinned or did not sin, he would die. For this is what is dealt with between us and you, on this the whole question about this matter turns between us, that we say Adam, if he had not sinned, would not have suffered the death of the body; but you say that, whether he had sinned or not sinned, he would die in body. What is it, then, that you pretend not to know, what death I wish to be understood, whose body I say was not in paradise before sin; when you know what I argued in those books, and how openly and clearly I argued, that God would not have said in punishment to the sinner: *You are earth, and into earth you shall go*[^90] (which who does not understand to be said of the death of the body?), if Adam, even with no iniquity committed, were going to go into the earth, that is, to die in body? But as for the fact that you thought even my mother, who has in no way harmed you, nor disputed anything against you, ought to be torn at with reviling, you were overcome by the lust of slandering, not fearing what is written: *Nor shall slanderers possess the kingdom of God*[^91]. But what wonder is it that you show yourself an enemy even of her, when you are an enemy of the grace of God, by which I said she was freed from that girlish vice? But I hold your parents in honor as catholic Christians, and congratulate them that they died before they saw you a heretic. But we do not say that the business of marriage is a disease, which is to lie together for the cause of procreating children, not of sating lust; which lust you deny to be a disease, though you confess that the conjugal remedy was provided against it. For certainly, lest fornication be perpetrated, the lust which you praise is contradicted, resisted, fought against. So that, if it transgresses that limit which is established for procreating children; at least he who yields to it in a spouse sins venially; for the Apostle was speaking to spouses, where, when he had said: *Do not defraud one another, except by consent for a time, that you may be free for prayer; and again return to the same, lest Satan tempt you because of your incontinence*; he immediately added, and said: *But this I say by way of indulgence, not by way of command*[^92]. Conjugal chastity, then, uses this evil well by the sole intention of propagating offspring; one yields to this evil venially in a spouse, not for the cause of offspring, but only of carnal pleasure; this evil is resisted, lest the damnable appetite of pleasure be satisfied. This evil dwells in the body of this death; on account of whose motion, importunate even when the mind does not consent, it is said: *I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, the good*. This evil was not in the body of that life, where either, with the genital members too serving the will, there was no lust, or it never moved itself at all against the choice of the will. They were ashamed of this evil suddenly arising[^93], who before they sinned were naked, and were not ashamed[^94]. You scattered the praises of this evil shamelessly even in those four books of yours, to which I was compelled to reply in my six.

**I. 69.** *Julian.* But that the Apostle might come to this mention of human misery and divine grace, he had said above: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin*[^95]; after these words he exclaimed: *Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*. It is established, indeed, that after those premises which you set down, the Apostle subjoined: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*. But we are not asking at present whether the Apostle said this; rather we ask by what faith, in what sense, by what reasoning he said it. For he called the law in the members, rebellious to holy counsels through the practice of disgraceful deeds—yet in the midst of the beginnings of amendment—an evil habit, which is wont to be called by the learned even of the age a second nature. For a little before he had said, addressing those to whom he speaks with reproach: *It is a human thing that I say, on account of the weakness of your flesh; for as you presented your members to serve uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present your members to serve justice unto sanctification*[^96]. And that he might show that he called flesh abusively, not this body which has its causes in seeds, but vices; after two chapters perhaps he subjoins: *When we were in the flesh, the passions of sins which are shown through the law worked in our members, that they might bear fruit unto death*[^97]. He said thus: *When we were in the flesh*, as if at that time when he was disputing, he were not in the flesh; but whoever knows the Scriptures recognizes this kind of expression. And therefore, where the commonness of words introduces a question, let the rule of reason be applied, to whose level the things that were thought to have deviated may be brought. But Faustus, the bishop of the Manichees, your teacher, relies on this testimony of the Apostle above all against us, saying that by these words—of the law, namely, which dwells in the members fighting against counsel—nothing else than an evil nature was signified by him. Whence nothing ought less to have been done by you than that this passage be understood as it is expounded by them, lest, when you go in along the same lines which Faustus twisted, you seem not to have disputed, but to have repeated what is past.

*Augustine.* Let there answer you, not Faustus the Manichee, but the learned catholic and teacher Gregory, who did not, *in the midst of the beginnings*, as you say, *of amendment*, call *the law in the members rebellious to holy counsels through the practice of disgraceful deeds*, *an evil habit, which is wont to be called by the learned even a second nature*; but plainly and openly attributed the law of sin which is in our members fighting against the law of the mind to this mortal and earthly body of ours, saying: *That the law of sin which is in our members fights against the law of the spirit, while it strives to lead captive the royal image which is within us, so that there may yield to its spoils whatever it is that flowed into us by the benefit of the divine and primal creation. Whence scarcely anyone*, he says, *perhaps, governing himself by long and strict philosophy, and little by little recalling the nobility of his soul, calls back and turns to God the nature of the light which is in him, joined to this lowly and dark mire. Or if at least, God being favorable, he acts, he will call both back together; if, however, by long and assiduous meditation he accustoms himself always to look upward, and to lift up by tighter reins the matter that drags downward badly and weighs down, bound to himself*. These things the blessed Gregory said, not in the midst of the beginnings of his amendment, but already a bishop, wishing to expound, or rather to admonish of what is known, in how great and what kind of a contest with the interior vices, on account of the body that weighs down the soul, the saints are placed. Which contest certainly would not have been in that place of blessed peace, that is, paradise of holy delights, if no one had sinned. For the body of this death would not be there, by whose corruptibility the soul would be weighed down; but the body of that life, where the flesh would not lust against the spirit, so that it would be necessary for the spirit to lust against the flesh[^98]; but by the happy concord of both human nature would rejoice. If, then, you wished to assail, not to assist, the Manichees who introduce another nature and substance of evil; you would surely not deny, with your deceivers, these miseries of human life open to all and beginning from little ones; but you would say, with the catholic faithful and the most illustrious teachers, whence our nature, which was first instituted blessed, has fallen into them.

**I. 70.** *Julian.* That what we have done may, then, be gathered: neither did I bring fraud into your sayings, nor did you bring anything that you could either suffuse with even a paler color of piety, much less approve with the Scriptures as witnesses. Neither did the Apostle understand this which you think; nor was there at all in paradise any other condition of union than such as is now carried on in marriages, which God, having instituted them himself, taught thoroughly both by the very condition of the sexes and the quality of the members, and by the blessing he repeated. With these things settled, it is clear that all who are deceived by you are more worthy of wrath than of mercy, since, for the excusing of their crimes, which they commit by an evil will, they defame birth, with you as author, that they may not correct their action.

*Augustine.* The pious action in this life is to worship God, and by his grace to fight against the interior vices, and not to yield to them when they instigate or compel to unlawful things; and where one yields, to ask for pardon, and, that one may not yield, for the help of God, with the affection of religious piety. But in paradise, if no one had sinned, there would be no action of piety to assail vices; since the persistence of happiness would be to have no vices. But the praise of vices which is shamelessly repeated by you is not a sign of men truly fighting against vices. Was it really so, Julian, that when Ambrose said: *We are all human beings born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*; he either said it with me as author, or defamed birth, that he might not correct action? Was it really so, that Gregory, when he said: *Venerate the birth through which you have been freed from the bonds of earthly birth*; or when, speaking of Christ, or of the Holy Spirit, he said: *Through him the stains of the first birth are purified, through which we are conceived in iniquities, and in offenses our mothers bore us*; or when Hilary said about King David: *He knows that he was born under the origin of sin, and under the law of sin*; did these men defame birth, that they might not correct action? Will you dare to persuade your heart that the action of the Pelagians is to be preferred to the action of these men? Forgive me—we would never believe that you live a better life than they, not even if you did not so love the concupiscence of the flesh that you wish to place it, such as it now is, lusting against the spirit, even in paradise before sin. For if, as you say: *There was no other condition of union in paradise than such as is now carried on in marriages*; there was there, even before sin, carnal lust, without which now neither sex can be joined. If, then, you are unwilling that in that blessedness the genital members, not yet shameful, could, to perform their work by which offspring would be sown, serve the will of human beings without lust; I ask still what kind of lust you believe it then to be. Did it certainly follow the will when it was necessary; or even when it was not necessary for propagating children, did it nevertheless goad the mind, and drive it into intercourse either damnable, or venial with a spouse? For if it was such as it now is; it did this without doubt, whether it was resisted through temperance, or yielded to through intemperance. And thus a human being would be compelled either to serve lust by sinning, or to fight against it by an inner war; of which the one you perceive, if you have human sense, does not befit honesty, the other the peace of that happiness. It remains, then, that if lust were there, it was so subject to the will that it would neither draw the upright and quiet mind to offense, nor provoke it to battle; and would compel the spirit, obedient to God and enjoying God, neither to sin nor to fight. Since now it is not such, but itself gapingly, not obediently, desires lawful things; and in unlawful things either casts down the spirit, or lusts against the spirit; acknowledge the evil contracted by the vitiating of nature's integrity; which evil the chastity of spouses uses well in the office of propagating, and from which evil is derived the bond of a generation subject to guilt, to be loosed by regeneration.

**I. 71.** *Julian.* But let this be enough about this; now I will seize on what follows. *In the body of this death, then, such as certainly was not in paradise before sin, another law in our members fights against the law of the mind; because both when we are unwilling, and when we do not consent, nor present our members to it that it may fulfill what it desires; yet it dwells in them, and solicits the resisting and fighting mind; so that the conflict itself, even if it is not damnable, because it does not accomplish iniquity, is yet wretched, because it does not have peace*. That the pleasure of all the senses is natural, we teach by the testimony of the whole world. But that this pleasure and concupiscence was in paradise before sin, that very thing declares, because the way to the offense was through concupiscence, which, when it had incited the eyes with the beauty of the apple, also stirred up the hope of a pleasant taste. This concupiscence could not, then—which, when it does not keep its measure, sins; but when it is kept within the limit of permitted things, is a natural and innocent affection—it could not, I say, be the fruit of sin, which is taught to have been the occasion of sin, not indeed by its own fault, but by that of the will. Read also about this my second book: you will find that this which we say can be persuaded even to your mind. But as for what you set down, somewhat cleverly, that the law of sin is indeed in our members; but that it then has sin, when we consent to it; but that it then only stirs up battle, when we do not consent, and announces misery with peace disturbed; who that is prudent would not perceive that you fight against yourself? For if the law of sin, that is, sin, and the necessity of sin, is inserted in the members naturally; what does it profit not to give consent to it, when, on account of this very thing that it exists, it is necessary to undergo punishment? Or if there is indeed a law of sin, but when I do not consent to it, it does not sin; inestimable is the power of the human will, which (if the absurdity permit it to be said) compels the sin itself not to sin. But it returns to this, that what you say is *inconsistent*; for if it does not sin, neither is it the law of sin; if it is the law of sin, it sins; but if it sins, solely because it exists, how can it be resisted so that one not sin, which cannot be repelled so that one cease from the work of sin?

*Augustine.* I have already answered your second book in my fourth, and convicted you of having said empty things; but let those who read see whether one must answer a man who advances into such great insanity that, while he confesses sin to be an evil, he says the concupiscence of sins is good. And yet we are compelled to answer, unwilling to desert the slower talents of men, to whom these writings may have been able to reach. What is it, then, that you say, not knowing what you say? Was there, then, even in paradise, before the poisons of the evilly persuading serpent, before the will was corrupted by sacrilegious speech, already a lust for unlawful food? And—what is said more intolerably—did it provoke to evil, and was not evil? And did those human beings see the fruit of the forbidden tree, and did it desire, but, so that they should not eat, did the concupiscence of the spirit fight against the concupiscence of the flesh; and did they live in that place of so great blessedness not having in themselves the peace of mind and body? You are not so demented as to believe these things; you are not so shameless as to say these things. Understand, then, or do not, with empty loquacity, clamor against those who understand. The evil will went before, by which the cunning serpent was believed; and the evil concupiscence followed, by which the unlawful food was gaped at. Not, then, did some sort of desire there struggle against some sort of will; but rather, depraved, it served the depraved will. And through this, although both were now evil, yet the will led the desire, not the desire the will; it did not go before the will, nor resist the will. Finally, if before the consummation of the sin the will had been turned away from the unlawful work, the unlawful desire would have been quieted without any labor. Speaking of this, the blessed Ambrose says: *The flesh*, he says, *returned to the nature of its own vigor*, *recognizes its nurse; and, the daring of its obstinacy laid aside, is joined to the moderating choice of the soul; such as it was when it took up the secret places of paradise to be inhabited, before, infected by the poison of the pestiferous serpent, it knew sacrilegious hunger, and, by the zeal of greediness, passed over the memory of the divine precepts that clung to the senses of the soul. From this it is shown that sin flowed, as it were from begetting parents, body and soul; while the nature of the body is tempted, the unsound soul suffers along with it; which, if it had bridled the appetite of the body, the origin of sin would have been extinguished in its very rising*. Do you see how the catholic teacher, endowed with Christian wisdom, called the very concupiscence of unlawful food—which you assert to be innocent, if it is not permitted to fulfill what it desires—already a sacrilegious hunger? And yet, if the soul, with the will certainly corrected, had bridled this appetite of the body, the origin of sin, as he says, would have been extinguished in its very rising; but since, the appetite for unlawful food not being repressed, it came to the consummation of sin; the origin of sin was not extinguished, but even flowed into posterity; and so great a dissension of flesh and spirit followed, that, as the same teacher says in another place, it turned into nature through the transgression of the first man. But you, against these things, say that you *teach by the testimony of the whole world that the pleasure of all the senses is natural*; as if there could not be in the body, not of this death, but of that life, the pleasure of all the senses sufficient for nature in such a way that, with the supreme concord of the virtue of the mind and of the flesh, nothing unlawful would be desired. O how greatly you err, who conjecture the holy delights of paradise and that blessedness from this corruptibility and infirmity of nature which now exists! That immortality was one thing, where a human being could not die; this mortality is another, where a human being cannot but die; the supreme immortality will be another, where a human being will not be able to die. Why do you contend about the contending concupiscence, that is, about the law in the members fighting against the law of the mind? It is called the law of sin, because it persuades sins and, so to speak, commands them; and if it is served by the mind, one sins without excuse. It is called sin, because it was made by sin, and desires to sin. Its guilt has been loosed by regeneration, its conflict has been left for the struggle. It is an evil, that is clear. We resist it not, as you think, by the powers of our will, unless we are divinely helped. This evil must be warred down, not denied; conquered, not defended. Finally, if you consent to it, acknowledge the evil by sinning; if you resist it, acknowledge the evil by fighting.

**I. 72.** *Julian.* Or what does the modesty of a thing profit, whose very presence accuses itself? See, then, whither your subtleties are carried. First, that nature sins without the will; which it cannot. Second, that it is sin, and does not sin, that is, it is one thing, and is not. Then, when it disturbs peace, it is wretched, nor is it punished for so great a crime of dissipated quiet; but when it accomplishes iniquity, it is damnable. But the law of sin, as it deserves torment itself, so it excuses the will of the human being. For a law that is both compelling, and natural, and never receding, certainly cannot be overcome by the will; and no one is guilty for this, that he could not avoid it. But neither does the law itself sin, because it could do nothing else. But God ascribes unavoidable things to guilt; nor is he compelled by anyone to do only this much evil; with all, then, absolved, this one alone is found in crime, who with a marvelous brow imputes necessity to others, while he himself offends without necessity. Bravo for your virtue of prudence, most noble disputant, who by the steps of Punic dialectic, in order to commend gifts, overturned judgments; in order to feign grace, undermined justice; in order to defame nature, accused the Founder of human beings; and so accused him that not only by some sinner, but by the very law of sin, your God appears more harmful. And after these things you most profanely revile the catholic priests, so as to say that they deny the grace of Christ, whose equity they uphold; while we indeed praise the clemency of the remedies, but with the justice of the laws remaining.

*Augustine.* Would that you acknowledged the catholic priests, who, long before you began to exist, said that the concupiscence of the flesh, which lusts against the spirit, although the spirit too lusts against it, which is understood to be the law of sin, resisting the law of the mind after the sin which was perpetrated in paradise, has vitiated human nature; whence now no one is born without it; and the spirit is opposed to it in the saints, that they may live justly, by fighting against it, until, with the salvation of a human being made perfect, and the flesh fully concordant with the spirit, it is no more. Ambrose says that through the transgression of the first man the dissension of flesh and spirit turned into nature; and that dissension Cyprian shows—in whom, a Punic, you do not dare, I think, to mock Punic dialectic, which you dared to do in me: *There is*, he says, *between flesh and spirit a wrestling, and a daily encounter of those disagreeing against each other in turn, so that we do not do the very things we wish, while the spirit seeks heavenly and divine things, the flesh desires earthly and worldly things; and therefore we ask that between these two, by the help and aid of God, concord be made, so that, while the will of God is carried on both in spirit and in flesh, the soul which has been reborn through him may be preserved. Which the Apostle openly and manifestly declares with his own voice: "the flesh, he says, lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other in turn; so that you do not do the things you wish "*[^99]. This concord of flesh and spirit, which Cyprian says we desire and pray for from the Lord, you do not concede to have been even in paradise before sin; or if it was, why do you not confess that it perished through the transgression of the first man, with nature vitiated, and that wretched discord succeeded the blessed peace of soul and body? And you are indignant at us, that we, as it were, revile by our discourse catholic priests, that is, you, because we say that you deny the grace of Christ; when you most shamelessly and most profanely revile those truly catholic priests with these words, which against me, who follow and uphold their faith, you belch forth with undigested fury. The Apostle says: *Walk in the spirit, and you will not accomplish the concupiscences of the flesh*[^100]. I ask, why does he name them, if they are none? Why does he forbid them to be accomplished, if they are good? But he goes on to show what kind they are, and says: *For the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other; so that you do not do the things you wish*. To whom was he saying: *So that you do not do the things you wish*? Surely not to the Jews, according to your wonderful understanding, not yet placed under the grace of Christ; and not to those to whom he had said: *From the works of the law did you receive the Spirit, or from the hearing of faith?*[^101] To faithful Christians, then, he was saying that they do not do the things they wish, with the flesh lusting against the spirit. Why so, unless because they wished their lower part to consent to the higher, that is, the flesh to the spirit, and were not able to accomplish what they wished; and it remained for them not to consent to that vice, but to lust, against the flesh, with the spirit? But even if there was in them what you think, that on account of an evil habit they lived badly even though unwilling; what is it, then, that you say, that nature cannot sin without the will, when you confess that these men sin unwilling? But why the concupiscence of the flesh is called sin, or the law of sin, we have already said above; to which, if it is good not to consent unto unlawful things, surely it is itself an evil, by which unlawful things are desired, even if they are perpetrated by no consent, and by no work. Moreover, you overturn the judgments of God, who say that the miseries of the human race, beginning from little ones, come about without any deserving of sin; nor do you ask the gift of God, *that you may not enter into temptation*[^102], that is, that you may not sin; for you trust in your own strength, and that you have been noted and condemned in the holy Psalm[^103], either you, being blind, do not see, or, being stupid, you do not grieve.

**I. 73.** *Julian.* But let us now proceed to my words too, which, taken from the preface, he proposed for himself to assail: *I have, then, sufficiently warned that this man wished so to propose my words to himself as if to be refuted, that in one place, with the middle parts removed, he interrupted statements; in another, by not adding the final parts, he cut them short; and I have shown sufficiently why he did this. Now let us see, in the things which, as he wished, he proposed as ours, what he set down as his own*. *For his words now follow, and, as this man indicated, who sent you the little sheet, he first wrote out something from the preface, beyond doubt of those books* *from which he excerpted a few things. But it stands thus: The teachers, he says, of our time, most blessed brother, and the authors of the wicked sedition that still rages, have decreed to arrive, through the ruin of the whole Church, at insults and destruction of the men by whose holy zeal they are burned*; *not understanding how much honor they have conferred on those whose glory they have shown could not be torn away except along with the catholic religion. For if anyone says that there is either free choice in human beings, or that God is the founder of those being born, he is called a Caelestian and a Pelagian. Lest, then, they be called heretics, they become Manichees; and while they fear a false infamy, they incur a true crime; like wild beasts, which are surrounded with feathers, that they may be driven into nets; into which, since they lack reason, they are thrust by a vain dread into true destruction*. I recognize my own sayings, but not brought forth by you with integrity; and although the sum of the contest does not consist in these, because they are from the preface; yet, that your levity may be clear, *most blessed brother*, was not placed in that spot, but in the very first line of the book. Likewise, when I had said: *To the insults and destruction of the men by whose holy zeal they are burned*; I added: *since no other way lay open, they decreed to arrive through the ruin of the whole Church*. After which I also said: *Because the confessor of free choice and of God the founder is called a Caelestian and a Pelagian, by which the simple, terrified by an iniquitous speech, in order to be stripped of the odium of the name, desert even the sound faith; about to believe, beyond doubt, that there is neither free choice in human beings, nor that God is the founder of those being born, when they have deserted both of those things, which they had previously affirmed*. But all this was passed over by you. After which are those things which you set down, how true indeed, and how impregnable, it will be no difficult work to prove. Neither did I, then, say anything less of your sayings, nor did you report even the first chapter as it had been arranged by me. Which I therefore drive home, that the gravity of the Punic writer may stand out.

*Augustine.* He who transmitted from your books the things he wished excerpted, to be read by whom he wished, himself did what you impute to me; nor do I think that even you yourself believe otherwise. For in that same book of mine, against which you bark, I did not fail to state beforehand to what little sheet I was compelled to respond; but you seek by reviling what to say, feeling that you say nothing strong by disputing. Although even in these words of mine, which you presently propose to yourself as if to be refuted, you could have noticed what I say; for I would not say: *It is not so as you say, whoever you are who said these things*; if I were certain that you had said them, not he who sent the writing such as he wished, and to whom he wished, which, sent to me, I had undertaken to refute. But thanks be to God, that I so answered, with the Lord helping, your whole work, from which this man excerpted what he wished, as it pleased him, that I subverted all your contrivances, which, as a new heretic, you had erected against the most ancient catholic faith.

**I. 74.** *Julian.* Let us hear, then, what you have written back against these words of mine: *It is not so as you say, whoever you are who said these things, it is not so; you are greatly deceived, or you plan to deceive; we do not deny free choice; but: "if the Son shall free you—says the Truth—then you will be truly free "*[^104]. *This Liberator you envy, you who attribute a vain freedom to captives. "For by whom anyone has been overcome—as the Scripture says—to him also he is assigned as a slave "*[^105]; *nor is anyone loosed from this bond of slavery except through the grace of the Liberator, from which bond no human being is free. "For through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned "*[^106]. That the dignity of our business is very great, as the very consideration and weighing of the things which we uphold shows, so too does your terror; you who, fighting against the blood of our people by the pouring out of moneys, by the largesse of inheritances, by the sending of horses, by the sedition of peoples, by the corruption of the powers, both blush to profess your faith, which is assailed by us, and flee for refuge to the words of our statement, from which you have deviated.

*Augustine.* Either you calumniate knowingly, if you yourself lie these things; or you do not know what you say, since you believe those who lie; but let it now be enough that I have answered these things above to your vanity, or even malice.

**I. 75.** *Julian.* For so wicked is that which the shameful transgression of Babylon has undertaken, that when it is objected by us, it is denied by you; and so holy is that which is believed by us, that you desire to hide under its shadow, though with an adverse mind. For when I was expressing whatever of your disputation was held scattered piecemeal, and was forcing the sum of the question into brevity, so that the cause of the ecclesiastical dissension, how great and of what kind it was, might be set forth without smoke and clouds, I said that free choice is denied by you, and that God is the founder of those being born; furthermore, that both of these are defended by us, and that on this account you stir up an empty din to inexperienced ears about the names of the catholic men who, on account of the apostolic faith which we uphold, labor with us, so that those who feared to be called Caelestians by you might lose the dignity of the heavenly faith; and those who dreaded lest they be called Pelagians by you might be hurled headlong into the sea of the Manichees; and the inexperienced might think that they could not be called Christians, if the Traducianists called them Pelagians; while, on the contrary, the prudent decided that the odium and injury of the names was rather to be borne by themselves than the catholic faith to be abandoned. But lest you should glory that this very kind of reviling was invented by your talent; remember that we are wont to receive diverse names from all heretics indeed; but that in the Synod of Rimini it became more vehemently clear what, among leaden minds, either the ambiguity of a word or the threat of a new name for wickedness can obtain. For when, under an Arian prince, a man of great constancy, of the soundest faith, Athanasius bishop of Alexandria, had withstood the times, with nearly the whole world fallen away from the faith of the Apostles and made impious, and on this account had been driven into exile; of six hundred and fifty bishops, as is reported, scarcely seven were found to whom the precepts of God were dearer than those of the king; namely, that they should neither agree to the condemnation of Athanasius, nor deny the confession of the Trinity. But all that multitude of cast-down hearts, apart from the fear of injuries, was deceived chiefly by this threat of a name, lest it be called Athanasian, or by the cunning of the questioning.

*Augustine.* The Arians call the Athanasians, or Homoousians, Catholics, but not other heretics too. But you are called Pelagians not only by the Catholics, but even by heretics, those like you and those dissenting from you; just as the Arians are called Arians not only by the Catholic Church, but by the heresies too. But you alone call us Traducianists, as those call us Homoousians, as the Donatists call us Macarians, as the Manichees call us Pharisees, and the other heretics by diverse names.

**I. 76.** *Julian.* For when the Arians, who at that time were masters of affairs, proposed: Do you wish to follow the Homoousion, or Christ? they answered at once, as if religious in name, that they followed Christ; they repudiated the *homoousion*; and so they go out exulting, as those who believed in Christ, whom they had already denied, by denying the *homoousion*, that is, that he is of one substance with the Father. So, then, even now you fabricators of deceit terrify inexperienced ears, so that, if they are unwilling to be bespattered with the name of the men laboring for the faith, they should deny both free choice and God the founder of human beings. This, then, is established to have been objected by me in that place; and how not falsely, the present disputation will make plain. You answered, then, in this way: *We do not deny free choice*; and you subjoined nothing else of your own. For it would have been consequent that you fulfill the statement without evasion; and when you had premised that you do not deny the freedom of choice, you should add: But we confess that the freedom of choice given by God remains in the nature of human beings.

*Augustine.* How does freedom remain in those who, in order to be freed from the slavery by which they are assigned to the conqueror sin, need divine grace, except because they are themselves free too, but free of justice? Whence the Apostle says: *When you were slaves of sin, you were free of justice*[^107].

**I. 77.** *Julian.* For you had fulfilled something by this discourse, against which, if you had spoken further, you would have been too shameless; but if you had said it all together, you could appear, even if tardily, more corrected. But now you say that I deceive, who prove what I objected through yourself; and you straightway, in that which you think you have consequently set forth, lie. For you say: *We do not deny free choice*; and you subjoin the testimony of the Gospel: *but "if the Son shall free you—says the Truth—then you will be truly free "*[^108]. Since it is established that in that place this was not brought forth by our Lord Jesus with respect to free choice, deferring the exposition of which statement a little, let us explain, by definitions and divisions, what each of us thinks. For, according to the discipline of all teachers, the beginning of a disputation ought to be taken from a definition.

*Augustine.* The Apostles, then, who did not take the beginnings of their disputations from a definition, were not learned? And yet they were teachers of the Gentiles, and despisers of such learned men as you are pleased to be puffed up about. But as for what the Lord says: *If the Son shall free you, then you will be truly free*, you will try indeed to twist it to your meaning; but how it does not follow you, when you have begun to try, will appear.

**I. 78.** *Julian.* For every disputation about some matter that is undertaken by reason, as that one says, ought to set out from a definition, that it may be understood what that is about which it is disputed. So, then, we too, just as we discoursed above about the definition of justice and of sin, let us now also see what definition befits freedom of choice, so that it may be plain which of us agrees with it, which opposes it. The freedom of choice, by which a human being has been emancipated by God, consists in the possibility of committing sin and of abstaining from sin.

*Augustine.* You say a human being has been emancipated by God: and you do not attend to the fact that this is done to the emancipated one, that he be not in the family of the father.

**I. 79.** *Julian.* For he was made a rational animal, mortal, capable of virtue and vice, who could, from the possibility granted to him, either keep the commandments of God, or transgress them, or by natural teaching preserve the right of human society, and have it free to will either part, in which the sum of sin and of justice is. For when one bestows something according to virtue on those in need, either from the founts of mercy, or from the udders of justice; this justice itself now works outwardly, which the holy will within conceived and bore.

*Augustine.* Whether it conceived justice of its own, this is what is dealt with between us and you, who, being ignorant of the justice of God, wish to establish your own[^109]; since the holy will certainly conceives justice by a holy thought, about which it is written: *A holy thought will preserve you*[^110]; but the Apostle says: *Not that we are sufficient to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God*[^111]. If you understand this, you will understand that a choice praiseworthily free is nothing other than what has been freed by the grace of God.

**I. 80.** *Julian.* So too when, on the contrary, one decides to act perversely to another's injury, whether one is unjust or cruel; the activity by which he harms others proceeds outwardly from the wickedness which the evil will within sowed and begot; but when the faculty is lacking, through which the force of the secret will may burst out upon one's neighbors, yet in that will alone, which has accomplished something either of good or of evil, not by a brief impulse, but by thought and appetite, the account either of benignity or of malignity is fulfilled.

*Augustine.* Just as you understand the will to fulfill, even by thought alone, the account either of benignity or of malignity, if you understand in that way what the Apostle says, speaking of the good and holy thought, that we are not sufficient to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God; you will be able to be corrected, and humble, to receive grace, to which pride made you an enemy, while you wish to be among those *who trust in their own strength*[^112]; not among those who say: *I will love you, O Lord, my strength*[^113].

**I. 81.** *Julian.* Both the possibility of good, then, and of evil, is good; since to be able to do good is the hall of virtue; to be able to do evil is the testimony of liberty.

*Augustine.* God, then, is not free, of whom it was said: *He cannot deny himself*[^114]; and of whom you yourself also said: *God cannot be anything but just*; and in another place: *But God cannot*, you say, *be anything but just and pious*.

**I. 82.** *Julian.* Through this, then, it is granted to a human being to have his own good, through that by which it is in his power to be able to do evil. The whole fullness of the divine judgment, then, has its business so joined with this liberty of human beings that whoever has recognized one of these has known both. Whence it comes about that whoever violates one of these violates both. So, then, let the liberty of human choice be guarded, just as the divine equity is guarded. This is the understanding of free choice, which has subjected to the ecclesiastical truth both the opinion of fates, and the calculation of the Chaldeans, and the phantasies of the Manichees. This is the understanding which, along with those whom we have enumerated, shows that you too are estranged from Christ. The freedom of choice, then, is the possibility either of committing or of avoiding sin, free of compelling necessity, which has it in its own right, as it were, which of the rising parts it should follow, that is, either the steep and rough things of the virtues, or the sunken and marshy things of pleasures.

*Augustine.* As long as a human being stood in the good will of free choice, he had no need of that grace by which he would be lifted up, when he could not himself rise; but now in his ruin he is free of justice, and a slave of sin, nor can he be a slave of justice, and free from the dominating sin, unless the Son shall free him.

**I. 83.** *Julian.* That this may be briefly settled, it is a possibility keeping watch for this alone, that a human being be neither driven into sin by anyone, nor drawn away from sin, with the will captive; which cannot be captured, if it has not itself wished to surrender, fortitude testifies, whose arms in the contempt of pains have shone forth assiduously both through Gentiles and through Christians.

*Augustine.* This is certainly what your heresy does; for you add the Gentiles for this purpose, lest the work of pious fortitude be believed to have been able to be done, or to have been done, by Christians through the grace of God; which is proper to Christians, not common to Christians and Gentiles. Hear, then, and understand: worldly desire makes the fortitude of the Gentiles, but the love of God makes the fortitude of Christians; which *is poured out in our hearts*, not through the choice of the will, which is from us, but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^115].

**I. 84.** *Julian.* If, then, the freedom of choice is, as reason has shown, the repeller of necessities, so that no one is either good or evil to whom it is not free to be the contrary; how is it that you either confessed free choice, about to use such a testimony as befits captives, or subjoined such a testimony, after you had confessed free choice? For you say: *We do not deny free choice; but: "if the Son shall free you—says the Truth—then you will be truly free "*. It is manifest that Christ in that place addressed his words to a captive conscience; which he was showing was not free, but liable to that vengeance which condemns sins committed by a free will. Which statement, understanding it badly, that is, not understanding it, or perhaps understanding it in yourself, and dragging it here, its own nature resisting, you placed in that spot where it disagrees, in all its own propriety, from your discourses. For, that we may join the words themselves: What is freed is captive; what is captive is not free; what is free is not captive.

*Augustine.* One thing is the remission of sins, in those things which have been done badly; another is love, which makes one free for those things which are to be done well. In both ways Christ frees; because he both takes away iniquity by forgiving, and bestows love by inspiring.

**I. 85.** *Julian.* Confess here simply whichever you wish, and cease to quibble; either say with us that choice is free, and remove the testimony, which was brought forth fittingly in its own time; or, as in these books which you have now sent through Alypius to Boniface, say that choice is captive, which we say is free, and cease to deny that you are a Manichee.

*Augustine.* The Manichee, insane, mixes an immutable substance of evil into the nature of his God, and rather makes the same nature of God corruptible, and wants it to be captive under another nature; but the catholic faith says that the creature, good, but yet mutable, was changed for the worse by its will; and through this, with its own nature depraved and vitiated, it is guilty and held in slavery, not under another substance, but under its own sin. And through this, our opinion about the Liberator himself is much different from his. For the Manichee says there is need of a liberator, that he may separate from us another nature; but we, that he may heal and vivify our own. Show, then, that you are not a helper of the Manichee, if you can, you who, since the human race without doubt feels and without doubt agrees that they are born with the miseries of human beings, refuse to attribute these to the sin of our vitiated nature, and so bring it about that he attributes them to another nature mingled with us.

**I. 86.** *Julian.* But these two things which you join, free and not free, that is, free and captive, cannot indeed befit the matter about which we are dealing; but they testify that in you there is singular stupidity, new shamelessness, old impiety.

*Augustine.* We call free, for doing the works of piety, those to whom the Apostle says: *But now, freed from sin, and made servants to God, you have your fruit unto sanctification, but the end eternal life*[^116]. This fruit unto sanctification, which fruit beyond doubt is love, and its works, we can in no way have from ourselves; but we have it *through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us*. For about this very fruit the Teacher, God, was speaking, when he said to the branches abiding in him: *Without me you can do nothing*[^117]. But you revile us as having singular stupidity, because God is our strength, and we do not trust in our own strength; and new shamelessness, because we do not praise, with your brow, the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit; and old impiety, because we defend, with whatever small labor, the ancient catholic teachings, which those taught who ruled the Church of Christ before us in true piety in his grace, against your novel perversity. In yourself, then, acknowledge the stupidity, in yourself the shamelessness, in yourself the impiety, not old indeed, but new.

**I. 87.** *Julian.* But it is now time that the statement of the Gospel be discoursed upon. *Jesus was saying*, says the evangelist John, *to the Jews who believed in him: If you abide in my word, you will truly be my disciples; and you will know the truth, and the truth will free you*[^118]. That is, our Lord Jesus was speaking to those who believed in him, that, rejoicing in no nobility of the age, nor claiming glory for themselves from the seed of Abraham, they should strive and be zealous for the virtues, and serve no sins after the knowledge of Christ, so that they might retain true liberty with a rejoicing conscience, and by the hope of sure things, that is, eternal ones, be vindicated from the desire of all the others, which, on account of their fragility, are frequently called vain and false. Then the Jews answered him, not understanding of what liberty Jesus had spoken: *We are the seed of Abraham, and we have never served anyone; how do you say: You will be free?*[^119] For liberty is wont to be named in many ways, as in this place holiness, as resurrection in the Apostle, where he says the creature is freed *from the slavery of corruption into the liberty of the glory of the sons of God*[^120]; as the liberty which is better known is said for the distinction from slavery. But by this name the freedom of choice too is named. Let the cases, then, be divided, lest things very distant be confused by the commonness of a name. Here, then, the Lord does not say that the freedom of choice is to be freed; but, with it remaining whole, he addresses the Jews, that, receiving indulgence, they may be freed from their guilt, and may occupy that liberty which is greatest in God's sight, that they may begin to owe nothing to crimes. Finally, the Evangelist continues: *Jesus answered them: Amen, amen I say to you, that everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin. But the slave does not abide in the house forever; the son, however, abides forever. If, then, the Son shall free you, then you will be truly free*[^121].

*Augustine.* Attend to what was said: *Everyone who commits sin*. For he does not say: Who has committed; but: *who commits*. And you are unwilling that he free human beings from this evil; you are unwilling that he promise in this place such a liberty by which we may not commit sin; but that he free only for this reason, because we have committed it.

**I. 88.** *Julian.* He disclosed of what slavery he was here speaking. *Everyone*, he says, *who commits sin is a slave of sin*. But how vehement is this very thing against your error, because he says that no one is a slave of sin except him who has himself committed sin; nor can any sin cling to anyone which the one in question has not committed by himself, either by action, or even by will alone? And how does he show that the whole of the human race cannot now be possessed by the devil, since he makes a distinction between slave and son, that is, just and unjust? For here Christ separated, as himself, so also each of the saints, from the condition of slaves: as there were both before the Old, and in the Old Testament, those whom he declares to abide in the house of his Father, and to rejoice at his table[^122]. But this whole kind of exhortation would have been ineptly brought forth, if it did not befit human beings of free choice.

*Augustine.* It plainly befits those committing sin for this reason, because they are slaves of sin, that, having received the liberty which he promises, they may cease to commit sin. For sin was reigning in their mortal body, so that they obeyed its concupiscences, and presented their members as weapons of iniquity to sin[^123]. Against this evil, then, by which they committed sin, they needed the liberty which he was promising: *For everyone*, he says, not who has committed, but *who commits sin, is a slave of sin*. Why do you try to obscure lucid words with foggy disputations? They break forth altogether, and, though you resist, by their splendor conquer your darkness. *Everyone*, he says, *who commits sin is a slave of sin*. You hear: *who commits*; and you wish not to expound but to substitute: Who has committed. Let those, then, hear to whom he himself opens the sense, that they may understand the Scriptures; let them hear: *Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin*. And let them seek to receive liberty, that they may not commit sin, crying out to him to whom it is said: *Direct my steps according to your word, and let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^124]. But why do you mock the slow understandings, and so insinuate the words of the Lord, as if he had said that no one is a slave of sin except him who has himself committed sin? You act by fraud, he did not say this; for he does not say: No one is a slave of sin except him who commits sin; but he says: *Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin*. For there are slaves of original sin who do not yet themselves commit sin; and from this bond of slavery they are loosed by regeneration. Not everyone, then, who is a slave of sin commits sin; *but everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin*; just as not every animal is a horse; but yet every horse is an animal. Where is your dialectic, with which you are wont to puff yourself up? Why do these things escape you, so learned and acute? Or why do you lie in wait for the unlearned and slow, if these things do not escape you? But who of us says that the whole of human beings is possessed by the devil; when there are so many thousands of saints who are not possessed by the devil? But we say that those alone are not possessed by the devil whom the grace of Christ frees; of which grace you are enemies. For if you did not assail this grace, but understood it; without doubt you would see that even before the Old Testament, and in the time of the Old Testament, all the saints, separated from the condition of slaves, were freed by the same grace of Christ.

**I. 89.** *Julian.* Finally, that you may understand that he reproaches not their nature, but their life; there follows: *I know that you are the sons of Abraham*. Behold, of what dignity of origin those men had said they were free; now he shows to what slavery they are liable, saying: *You seek to kill me, because my word does not take hold in you. I speak what I have heard with my Father; and you do the things which you have seen with your father*[^125].

*Augustine.* What is: *My word does not take hold in you*, if nature, even such as now needs one who frees it, is already capable of his word, even if his grace does not open the understanding, as it opened it to the Apostles, that they might understand the Scriptures[^126]; as it opened it to that woman of Thyatira, a seller of purple, that she might attend to the things which were said by Paul[^127]?

**I. 90.** *Julian.* See how diverse is the account of nature and of will. Jesus did not deny that their flesh pertained to the seed of Abraham, but drives home to them that they had migrated to the devil, the father of their will, by the iniquity of their will; who is therefore called their begetter, because he is convicted to be the instructor of their crimes. *They answered*, he says, *and said to him*, the Jews: *Our father is Abraham. Jesus says to them: If you were the sons of Abraham, you would do the works of Abraham; but now you seek to kill me, a man who has spoken to you the truth, which I heard from God; this Abraham did not do, but you do the works of your father*[^128]. Do you see what divisions of words Wisdom makes? He denies that those are sons of Abraham whom above he had said were sons of Abraham; but since the treatment of nature and of will is diverse, he shows that one is the begetter of innocent flesh, the other the seducer of a wretched will.

*Augustine.* You speak the truth, unwarily. For in paradise the devil was the seducer of a blessed will, which by seducing he made wretched; but now, as you confess, he is the seducer of a wretched will. From this wretchedness, then (lest through it the will, now seduced more easily than it was then seduced into it, be still seduced by the devil), no one frees it except him to whom daily the whole Church cries out: *Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil*[^129].

**I. 91.** *Julian.* Here, then, where the Lord said: *If the Son shall free you, you will be truly free*, he promised indulgence to the guilty, who by sinning lost, not the freedom of choice, but the conscience of justice. But free choice even after sins is as full as it was before sins; since by its own work it comes about that very many renounce the hidden things of disgrace[^130], and, with the filth of disgraceful deeds cast off, are adorned with the insignia of the virtues.

*Augustine.* Hear yourself at least, where you say that, on account of the habit of sins, it was written: *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, this I do*[^131]. How, then, is it free after sins, when, even if not by propagation, because you are unwilling, yet certainly by habit—which alone you concede to this necessity of the overcome and twisted—it has so lost liberty that its groan strikes your ears, and dashes your brow, when you hear: *I do not do what I will*; and: *I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do*[^132]?

**I. 92.** *Julian.* Cease, then, to make yourself impious (if, however, any right or wrong remains to you), by so expounding the words of Christ that he may seem to have denied free choice, without whose integrity the equity of his own judgment cannot stand.

*Augustine.* Rather, for this reason *there is a heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam, from the day of their going out from their mother's womb*[^133], because the equity of judgment stands; which would be altogether unjust, if they did not draw original sin, through which *man is made like to vanity*[^134].

**I. 93.** *Julian.* Hear indeed where he shows the force of human liberty: *I came in the name of my Father, and you did not receive me; if another shall come in his own name, him you will receive*[^135]. And likewise: *Either make the tree good, and its fruit good; or make the tree evil, and its fruit evil*[^136]. And likewise: *If you will not believe me, believe the works*[^137]. And, more vehemently than all, that he says his intention was hindered by the human will: *Jerusalem*, he says, *Jerusalem, how often I wished to gather your children, as a hen her chicks under her wings, and you would not!*[^138] After which there does not follow: But, with you unwilling, I gathered; but: *Your house will be left to you desolate*[^139]; that he may show that those were indeed justly punished for their evil work, but ought not to have been recalled by any necessity from their own intention. For thus he had spoken also through the Prophet: *If you are willing, and listen to me, you will eat the good things of the land; if you are unwilling, and do not listen, the sword will devour you*[^140]. How, then, do you not deny free choice, which—not indeed by your discourse, but by the testimony of the Gospel, but by your sense—you have declared not free?

*Augustine.* You must be forgiven, because in a matter very hidden you, as a human being, are deceived. Far be it that the intention of the Almighty and one who foreknows all things be hindered by a human being. They think too little about so great a matter, or are not sufficient to think it out, who think that almighty God wills something, and, with a weak human being hindering, cannot do it. Just as it is certain that Jerusalem was unwilling that her children be gathered by him, so it is certain that, even with her unwilling, he gathered whomever of them he willed. For *God*, as his man Ambrose said, *calls those whom he deigns, and makes religious whom he wills*. But Scripture frequently addresses the will of a human being, that, admonished, he may feel what he does not have, or cannot do; and, being in need, may ask from him from whom are all good things. For if he is heard in that which we have been commanded to say: *Lead us not into temptation*[^141]; without doubt he will be deceived by no ignorance, overcome by no desire. For this reason, then, it was said through the Prophet: *If you are unwilling, and do not listen to me, the sword will devour you*; and other things of this kind; that, when they had found in themselves victorious desires, they might know from whom they ought to ask help to repel the evil. But for this reason it was said: *Behold, your house will be left to you desolate*; because there were there very many whom he had judged worthy of hardening and desertion, by a hidden judgment, yet a just one. For if, as you say: *A human being ought not to be recalled by any necessity from his own intention*—certainly an evil one—why was the apostle Paul, still Saul, breathing slaughter and thirsting for blood, recalled from his most wicked intention by a violent blindness of the body, and a terrible voice from above; and, from a prostrated persecutor, raised up to be a more laborious preacher than the rest of that Gospel which he was assailing[^142]? Acknowledge grace; God calls one in this way, another in that, whom he deigns; and: *The Spirit breathes where it wills*[^143].

**I. 94.** *Julian.* For in that work which I said above you recently sent to Rome, you have disclosed more boldly what you think. For in the first book, when you had likewise proposed to yourself what we object, that you deny free choice, you discourse thus, a most constant and most subtle disputant. *But who of us would say that by the sin of the first man free choice perished from the human race? Liberty indeed perished through sin, but that liberty which was in paradise, of having full justice with immortality; on account of which human nature needs divine grace, the Lord saying in his Gospel: "if the Son shall free you, then you will be truly free "*, *truly free for living well and justly. For free choice has so far not perished in the sinner, that through it they sin, especially all who sin with delight and love of sin, and to whom what they enjoy is pleasing. Whence the Apostle: "when you were—he says—slaves of sin, you were free of justice "*. *Behold, it is shown that they could not even sin, except by another liberty. They are not, then, free of justice except by the choice of liberty; but they are not made free of sin except by the grace of the Savior. On account of which the admirable Teacher distinguished even the words themselves: "for when you were—he says—slaves of sin, you were free of justice. What fruit, then, did you have then in those things in which you now blush? For the end of them is death; but now, freed from sin, and made servants of God*, *you have your fruit unto sanctification, but the end eternal life "*[^144]. *He said free of justice, not freed; but of sin not free, lest they attribute this to themselves, but most vigilantly he preferred to say freed; referring this to that statement of the Lord: "if the Son shall free you, then you will be truly free ". Since, then, the sons of men do not live well, except made sons of God, what is it that this man wishes to attribute to free choice the power of living well; since this power is not given except by "the grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord "*, *the Gospel saying: "But as many as received him, he gave them power to become sons of God "*[^145]. And likewise after a little: *Power is given, then*, you say, *that they may become sons of God, who believe in him. Which power, unless it be given by God, can be none from free choice; because that will not be free in good which the Liberator has not freed; but he has free choice for evil, in whom either the hidden or the manifest deceiver has planted the delight of malice, or to whom he has himself persuaded it. Not, then, as some say that we say, and this man dares moreover to write, are all driven into sin, as if unwilling, by the necessity of their flesh; but if they are now at that age that they use the choice of their own mind, both they are held in sin by their own will, and from sin into sin they are hurled headlong by their own will. But this will, which is free in evils, because it is delighted by evils, is therefore not free in goods, because it has not been freed. Nor can a human being will anything good, unless he is helped by him who cannot will evil*. In all these words of yours which I have set down, I so see the name of grace woven together with the denial of free choice that the evils of your sense cannot so be vindicated by good names, as the dignity of the names is cheapened by adherence to your teachings. You did not, then, honor yourself by these discourses, but befouled the very ornaments. Yet we separate the things that have been joined by you, so that the divinity of grace, disentangled from your distorted bindings, may neither be shaken by a response, and may be praised with the gravity of Catholics, not with the flattery of Manichees. We confess, then, the grace of Christ to be manifold. His first gift is that we have been made out of nothing. The second, that we excel by sense those that live, so by reason those that have sense; which is impressed on the mind, that the image of the Founder might be taught: to whose dignity the freedom of choice granted equally looks back. To grace itself too we reckon the increases of the benefits which it does not cease to bestow on us. Grace itself *sent the law as a help*[^146]; it belonged to its office to rouse, by manifold instructions, the light of reason, which the examples of depravity and the habit of vices were blunting, and to cherish it by its own invitation. To the fullness, then, of this grace, that is, of the divine benevolence, which gave a cause to things, it looked that the Word should be made flesh, and dwell among us[^147]. For God, requiring back from his image the return of love, made plain how wholly he had acted toward us with inestimable affection, that even late we might love in return him who, commending his love to us[^148], *did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us*[^149]; promising that, if we should henceforth be willing to obey his will, he would grant us to be coheirs of his Only-begotten[^150].

*Augustine.* Pelagian man, love wills the good, and love is from God; not through the letter of the law, but through the spirit of grace. In this the letter is a help to the predestined, because, by commanding and not helping, it admonishes the weak to flee to the spirit of grace. Thus those use the law lawfully to whom it is good[^151], that is, useful; otherwise, by itself the letter kills[^152]; because, by commanding the good and not bestowing love, which alone wills the good, it makes them guilty of transgression.

**I. 95.** *Julian.* This grace, then, which in Baptism not only forgives sins, but, along with this benefit of indulgence, both advances, and adopts, and consecrates; this grace, I say, changes the deserving of the guilty, it does not found free choice; which we receive at the time when we are created, but use at the time when we attain the strength of discernment between good and evil. We do not deny, then, that innumerable kinds of divine help are present to a good will; but in such a way that not through the kinds of help is fabricated the freedom of choice that was destroyed, nor, with it ever excluded, is the necessity either of good or of evil to be believed to lie upon anyone; but all help cooperates with free choice.

*Augustine.* If grace does not go before, that it may work it, but cooperates with a will already existing first, how is it true: *God works in you even to will*[^153]? How: *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^154]? How: *Love is from God*[^155], which alone wills the beatific good? Or if the knowledge of the law and of the words of God works love in us, so that not through the gift of God, but through the choice of our will we love what we recognize, with God teaching, ought to be loved; how is the lesser thing from God for us, and the greater from ourselves? Because without God giving the knowledge, that is, teaching, we cannot know; but without him giving the love which surpasses knowledge[^156], we can love. Thus none think but the new heretics and enemies, far too much, of the grace of God.

**I. 96.** *Julian.* This free choice, then, on account of which alone the Teacher of the Gentiles writes that we are made manifest before the tribunal of Christ, that each one may carry back the proper things of the body according as he has done, whether good or evil[^157], just as the Catholics indeed confess; so you deny, not only with the Manichee, but even with Jovinian, whom you dare to thrust upon us, by a diverse kind indeed, but by a similar impiety. That this may be made plainer, let the examination of divisions be applied. We say that by the sin of a human being not the state of nature is changed, but the quality of deserving; that is, that even in the sinner there is this nature of free choice, through which he can cease from sin, which was in him that he could deviate from justice.

*Augustine.* We know that you say for this reason that by the sin of a human being the state of nature is not changed, because you have deserted the catholic faith, which says that the first man was so made that he did not have the necessity of dying; but that by sin this state of nature was so changed that it is necessary for a human being to die, so far that the Apostle says even to those already regenerated and vivified in spirit: *If Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of justice; if, then, the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Jesus Christ from the dead will vivify even your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^158]. For this reason certainly he said: *He will vivify even your mortal bodies*, which we hope for in the resurrection of the flesh, because he had said: *The body is dead because of sin*; and you are unwilling that the state of nature was changed by sin. Yet when it had been objected to Pelagius in the Palestinian judgment that he said newly born infants are in the state in which Adam was before sin, he denied that he said it, and condemned it[^159]. And if he had done this with a truthful heart, perhaps your heresy would already have perished; finally, he himself at least would have been healed of that plague. Then I ask whether a sinful nature is free of vice; but if that is most absurd, it has, then, a vice; if it has a vice, without doubt it has been vitiated. How, then, has it not been changed, which, when it had been sound, was vitiated? And through this, even apart from that sin, about which John of Constantinople says: *Adam committed that great sin, and condemned all in common*; even apart from that, then, from which human nature draws condemnation originally, how can it soberly be said that the state of nature was not changed in a human being who says: *The law is spiritual, but I am carnal, sold under sin; for what I work, I do not understand; for I do not do what I will*[^160], and other things of this kind; even if he drew this change not in the condition of being born, but, as you wish, contracted it by the habit of sinning? Do you see that it is vain, what you say, that by the sin of a human being not the state of nature is changed, but the quality of deserving? Unless perhaps you say that not nature is changed by sin, but the human being. And what is this, but to deny that a human being is a nature? When would you say these things, if you considered with a sound mind what you say?

**I. 97.** *Julian.* The Manichee says that an evil will is inspired by that nature which cannot will good; but a good will is infused by that nature which cannot will evil; thus he certainly imposes a necessity on the natures of individual things, so that their own wills cannot will contrary things. Between us and him there is certainly a great chasm; let us now see how much you have receded from him. You say the will is free, but only to do evil, but that it is not free in this, to cease working evil, unless a necessity of willing good has been imposed on it by that nature which, to use your discourses: *cannot will evil*. You define, then, the race of human beings, through free choice, to do nothing other than sin, nor to be able to do anything else. By which you absolutely declare that human nature always desires one thing which is evil, and cannot will the contrary; but that the nature of God cannot will evil; and therefore, unless it makes the evil nature of human beings a partaker of its own necessity, there can be no good of action in it. After these things, then, whether you do not love the Manichee much, God will have seen in the secret of your heart; yet, so far as is shown by the kinship of the teachings, you have done absolutely nothing else than that, with the order changed, you establish the same thing which he had affirmed.

*Augustine.* Would that you destroyed the Manichee strongly, not helped him basely! For he, perverted by too much insanity, does not say that the nature of evil is compelled to do good by another nature of good, which cannot will evil; but that the nature of good is compelled to do evil by that nature of evil, which cannot will good; and through this, marvelously demented, he wants the nature of evil to be immutable, but the nature of good mutable. Accordingly, it is certain that the Manichee says that an evil will is inspired by that nature which cannot will good; but the Manichee does not say that a good will is infused by that nature which cannot will evil, as you more mildly suspect of him; since he in no way believes the nature of good to be immutable, which cannot will evil—the nature into which he believes an evil will is inspired by that nature which cannot will good; and so that it comes about through the nature of evil that the nature of good wills evil, which he wants to be nothing other than the nature of God. You, then, help him, by denying that human nature was vitiated by the sin of the first man, so that he may attribute to the nature of evil, which he invents, whatever of evils he finds in the most manifest misery of little ones. Then, when it displeases you that a human being cannot will good unless he is helped by him who cannot will evil, do you not see that you contradict him who said: *Without me you can do nothing*[^161]; and the Scripture, where it is read: *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^162]; and where it is read: *For it is God who works in us even to will*[^163]; and where it is read: *By the Lord the steps of a man will be directed, and he will will his way*[^164]? Wherein I wonder at nothing, except why you call yourself a Christian, when you contradict these so many and so clear divine voices.

**I. 98.** *Julian.* But with Jovinian you are joined in one part; for he says in the second book of his work that a baptized human being cannot sin; but before Baptism he can both sin and not sin. With you, then, he thinks that from the time of Baptism a necessity of good is imposed on human beings; which is equally false as what you think, that before Baptism there is in human beings a necessity of doing evil. For when you say: *A human being cannot will anything good, unless he is helped by him who cannot will evil*, you certainly want a human being to attain the possibility of doing good by participation in grace and in a good nature; which you say, however, can happen from the time of Baptism. Midway, then, between impiety and fear, you have fled for refuge to the consortium of Jovinian, but you have not yet left the brothel of the Manichee. Yet Jovinian is more innocent than you, by as much as the Manichee is more profane than Jovinian. For, that what we have done may be gathered still more briefly, the Manichee says: In all human beings the nature of darkness, the inspirer of an evil will, offends, and can do nothing else. You say: In all human beings the nature, infected by the darkness of the first sin, and through this the author of an evil will, offends, and cannot will good. Jovinian says: The will indeed of human beings offends, but up to Baptism; but afterward it cannot will anything but good. The Catholics, that is, we, say that from the beginning to the end, without any compulsion of natural things, the will offends, even before Baptism, in each one—a will which, at that very time when it sins, has the power to recede from evil, and to do good, so that the account of liberty may stand. The truth of the teachings, then, stands for none of you; yet, since you have all gone out from one beginning of error, it would have been less dishonorable, if you admitted what follows, and, because you say with the Manichee that one offends by an evil, that is, maimed, liberty, you should say with the same that it can in no way be cleansed (which indeed you affirm elsewhere); but that you should add what necessarily followed, that therefore there is no need for it of the sacraments of baptism. Or if, with Jovinian, you assert that from the time of receiving the faith good desires are impressed; you should say with the same that nature was good even before Baptism, which, although it had the possibility of evil, yet did not have the necessity, and through this, consecrated, arrived at an undisguisable good. For in this way, although you would contravene reason, yet you would in no way contravene the teachings of those whom you follow.

*Augustine.* You have forgotten what we say: recall it, I beg you. We are those who say, with you crying out against it, that even to the just, as long as they are in this life, there is not lacking that on account of which in their prayer they may say truthfully of themselves: *Forgive us our debts*[^165]. Since, if they say they have no sin, they deceive themselves, and the truth is not in them[^166]. What is it, then, that you vainly say, that I am joined with Jovinian in that part in which he says a baptized human being cannot sin? Far be it that we be so deaf and dumb that we do not hear the voice of those who are baptized, or do not say with them: *Forgive us our debts*. But from the time a human being begins to use the choice of the will, he can both sin and not sin; but one of these he does not do, unless helped by him who said: *Without me you can do nothing*[^167]; but the other by his own will, whether deceived by himself or seduced by another deceiver, or assigned as a slave to sin. But we know that human beings were helped by the Spirit of God, that they might will the things that are God's, even before Baptism, like Cornelius[^168]; but certain ones not even after Baptism, like Simon Magus[^169]. For *the judgments* of God are like *a great abyss*[^170]; and *his grace is not from works; otherwise grace is now not grace*[^171]. Cease, then, to revile us about the Manichee and Jovinian; for the things for which you revile us, who, following those whom we cannot deny, hold original sin, you would see, if you had eyes, and would be silent, if you had a brow. But you are so calumnious that you say I affirmed elsewhere that a human being cannot be cleansed from sins; when I so say that he can, that he is even led to where, most blessed, he cannot sin.

**I. 99.** *Julian.* But now, faithless toward all, you say that a necessity has been made in the nature of the flesh of sin.

*Augustine.* Deny that the Apostle said: *When you were slaves of sin, you were free of justice*[^172]. Or, if you do not deny that this was said by him, refute that he said it rightly. But if you do not dare, deny, if you can, that those to whom he said this had a free will in evils, when they were free of justice; or that they had a free will in goods, when they were slaves of sin; and dare to say that they were freed from this slavery by themselves, not by the grace of God, to whom it is said: *But now, freed from sin, you have been made servants of justice*[^173]. But if you say that they were freed by the grace of God from the guilt of past evils, not from the dominion of sin, by which one is not permitted to be just; but that, in order that sin might not have dominion over them, they could do this themselves if they willed, and did not for this need the grace of the Savior; where do you place him who says: *I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do*[^174]? For if he speaks these things placed under the law, not under grace; deny that he groans under the heavy weight of necessity; assert that he is free, by the choice of the will, to live well and to act rightly, while he cries out to you: You lie, or you are deceived; *I do not do what I will*. But if, as Ambrose better understood, the Apostle says this even of himself; then the just do not have in this life, for accomplishing the good, so great a freedom of their own will as there will be in that life, where it will not be said: *I do not do what I will*.

**I. 100.** *Julian.* So that, with the possession of eternity lost (which indeed it is established it never had by its origin), the will leaned perpetually toward desiring depravity. And you add: *The will which is free in evils is not free in goods*; with no smaller a profession of stupidity, plainly, than of profanity, you call free that which you say can will nothing but one thing.

*Augustine.* If nothing is free except what can will two things, that is, both good and evil; God is not free, who cannot will evil, of whom you yourself also said, and said truly: *God cannot be anything but just*. Do you so praise God as to take from him liberty? Or ought you rather to understand that there is a certain blessed necessity, by which God cannot be unjust?

**I. 101.** *Julian.* But although you stand on no side, yet let the prudent reader attend to what you have brought forward. Let us agree that a will can be called free which cannot will good; but this will, you assert, is freed in Baptism. I ask, in what manner is it freed? So that it is compelled always to will good, and cannot will evil? Or so that it can desire either? Here, if you answer: So that it is compelled always to will good; how much of a Jovinianist you are, you yourself even recognize. But if you say: How can a will be free, if it is compelled always to will good? I answer: How was the will called free before, if it was compelled only to will evil? If, then, you answer that after Baptism the will is made free in such a way that it can both sin and not sin; by this very thing you will declare that there was no free choice, when it could not do both. You are shut in on every side by the snares of your own disputation: choice was free before Baptism; it had the faculty of doing good, just as it had the faculty of doing evil; and the whole stage-set of your teaching, by which you persuade a natural evil, collapses.

*Augustine.* Whoever reads will find that I have already answered you above about the Manichee and Jovinian; but how you say that he, whose will we say is prepared by the Lord, is so made of good will that he is compelled to will good (which far be it that it be said by us), let your splendid understanding have seen. For if he is compelled, he does not will; and what is more absurd than that one should be said to will, unwilling, what is good? See, too, what you hold about the nature of God, you who say that a human being is compelled to will good, if he cannot will evil. For is God compelled to will good, because he cannot will evil, since he is altogether immutable? But human nature, although mutable, is good as far as concerns the fact that it was created; which was not only made without vice, but even when by vice it is evil, is capable of the good by which it may be good. This true opinion overturns the fallacious insanity of the Manichees. But in Baptism all sins are so forgiven in the grace of God—by which a human being is led even to Baptism itself with his will prepared by him—that thereafter, although his spirit lusts against the flesh that it may not consent to iniquity, yet the flesh too lusts against the spirit, so that he too does not do what he wills. For he wills also not to have this concupiscence of the flesh, but he cannot now; on account of which he still groans in himself, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of his body[^175], where he will have flesh in such a way that he can no longer sin. Now, then, not only can he sin after Baptism, but also, because, even fighting well against the concupiscence of the flesh, he is sometimes drawn by it to consent; and, although venial, he commits some sins; he has cause always here to say: *Forgive us our debts*[^176]. This too the catholic truth refutes the vanity of Jovinian. But both—that is, both what we say against the Manichee, and what against Jovinian—subverts your heresy and your calumnies.

**I. 102.** *Julian.* But if it was not free for good before, and was made so free after Baptism that it cannot do evil; the freedom of choice was indeed never present to it, and it is proved both to have sinned before without guilt, and afterward to possess the glory of holiness without care.

*Augustine.* Then there is not in God either the freedom of choice, because he cannot do evil, *as he cannot deny himself*[^177]—who is going to bestow on us too, by that supreme reward, that we, equal indeed not to God himself, but yet to his Angels, can no longer sin. For this is to be believed to have been granted to them, after the fall of the devil, for the deserving of the good will by which they stood in the truth, that afterward no one should become a new devil through free choice.

**I. 103.** *Julian.* From which gathered conclusion you are convicted of utterly contradicting your own teaching; so that you promise that you do not deny free choice, which before by the necessity of evil, afterward by the necessity of good, you overturn.

*Augustine.* You are going to say, as I see, that God is pressed by necessity, so that he cannot sin; who certainly neither can will, nor wills to be able, to sin. Rather, if it is to be called necessity, by which it is necessary that something either be or come about; this is altogether a most blessed necessity, by which it is necessary to live happily, and in that same life it is necessary not to die, it is necessary not to be changed for the worse. By this necessity, if it is even itself to be called necessity, the holy Angels are not pressed, but enjoy it; but for us it is to come, not present.

**I. 104.** *Julian.* Yet it profits the whole destruction of your teaching that we recall also the earlier definitions. If sin is nothing but the will to retain and to commit what justice forbids and from which one is free to abstain, no sin at all is found in things.

*Augustine.* That definition is of the sin which is only sin, not of that which is also the punishment of sin, by which the liberty of not sinning perished; from which evil no one frees us except him to whom we say not only: *Forgive us our debts*, but also: *Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil*[^178].

**I. 105.** *Julian.* For if justice does not impute except that from which one is free to abstain; and before Baptism there is a necessity of evil, since the will, as you said, is not free for doing good, and through this can do nothing other than evil; it is defended from the infamy of evil by the very necessity which it suffers; since with that justice it cannot be weighed down by its own works, which does not impute evil, except that from which one is free to abstain. But after Baptism, if there is a necessity of good, there can certainly be no sin. See, then, how that which reason demonstrates to be sin cannot be found in the seeds, when, according to your definitions, it is now not even found in morals.

*Augustine.* You greatly err, who either think there is no necessity of sinning, or do not understand it to be the punishment of that sin which was committed by no necessity. For if there is no necessity of sinning (to omit the force of that evil which is drawn originally; for you want this to be nothing), what, I ask, was he suffering who, according to your understanding, was pressed by so great a mass of evil habit that he said: *I do not do the good that I will, but the evil that I will not, this I do*[^179]? Then I judge that you consider with what labor it is learned what is to be desired, what to be avoided in the conduct of life. But those who do not know this suffer the necessity of sinning by the very ignorance of desiring good and avoiding evil. For it is necessary that he sin who, not knowing what he ought to do, does what he ought not. About which kind of evils God is asked, where it is said: *Remember not the offenses of my youth and of my ignorance*[^180]. Which kind of offenses, if the just God did not impute, the faithful man would not ask that they be forgiven him. Whence the servant of God, Job, also says: *You have sealed up my sins in a bag, and noted if I have committed anything unwillingly*[^181]. You too yourself, in the last book of those four which you published against my one, say, from the affections and passions of the mind: *That an affectional quality befalls human beings and so inheres that it is separated either by great efforts, or by none at all*. Whoever, then, sins by that fearfulness which cannot be separated from him, what else does he do than sin by necessity? But you concede that these sins come from those sins which were committed by no necessity—at least in him who says: *The evil that I will not, this I do*. For he who, in order to suffer this necessity, is pressed only by the habit of sinning; without doubt before he sinned, he was not yet pressed by the necessity of habit. And through this, even according to you, the necessity of sinning, from which one is not free to abstain, is the punishment of that sin from which one was free to abstain, when no weight of necessity pressed. Why, then, do you not believe that that ineffably great sin of the first man availed at least so much that by it the whole of human nature was vitiated, as much as a second nature now avails in one human being? For you thought we should be reminded that habit is thus called by the learned. Since, then, we both confess that there are in human beings those sins which are committed not by necessity, but by will, which are only sins, from which they are free to abstain; and that the human race is full of sins coming from the necessity of ignorance or of the affections, which are now not only sins, but also punishments of sins; how do you say that, by our definitions, sin is not found even in morals? But hear what you are unwilling to hear. From all sins, whether original or moral, whether they have been committed, or that they may not be committed, no one frees except the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, in whom we have been regenerated, and from whom we have learned to say in prayer, not only: *Forgive us our debts*, that is, because we have sinned; but also: *Lead us not into temptation*[^182], that is, that we may not sin.

**I. 106.** *Julian.* But with this sum of the disputation sealed in the mind of the prudent reader, let us examine what your Baptism—which you say was provided on account of the mere stirring of the genitals—carries out of the office entrusted to it. It professes that it expiates human beings from sins, but when the case of the will is tried before justice, that is not pronounced guilty which could not will otherwise. But with the odium of guilt vanishing, the pomp of the one indulging vanishes too; because it cannot forgive what it cannot by right impute. And through this, it is frustrated of the effects of its promise; since it neither finds crimes by whose remission it may be praised; nor has it debtors of this benefit, by which the bonds of sins are loosed; because it cannot convict of an evil will those placed in the asylum of necessity; by all of which it is caught out as superfluous. But because the grace which was provided by Christ is not superfluous; let its reasonable munificence be held, through which the guilty will of the sinner is convicted, which certainly could will good as much as it willed evil. The whole fiction of necessity, then, has vanished; and through this, there is no sin from the condition of nature; but free choice perseveres in the nature of human beings; which, as you with the Manichees deny, so we with the Apostles and all Catholics confess.

*Augustine.* It is necessary that he sin by whom justice is not known; is it for that reason that, when he has come to know justice, the sins are not to be remitted to him which he committed by the necessity of ignorance? Or, because he has now come to know how he ought to live, is he to presume of himself that he live justly, not of him to whom it is said: *Lead us not into temptation*[^183]? There is not, then, a security of impunity in the necessity of sinning, but, that this necessity may not harm, he grants it to whom it is said: *Rescue me from my necessities*[^184]. But he grants it in two ways: both by forgiving past iniquity, and by helping that we may not enter into temptation. *But each one is tempted, drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence*[^185]. Which concupiscence, as you have taken it up, is so pleasing to you that, when anyone is not drawn by it into consent, you think it is to be praised; as though the thing which impels into evil were not an evil, if the one who is impelled does not yield to it, but resists. Although you, even if it is consented to, contend with great empty talk that he who fell, not the thing which impelled; he who was drawn away, not the thing which drew away; he who was enticed, not the thing which enticed, is to be blamed; because, namely, as you define, he used a good badly; for you have so evil a spirit that the concupiscence by which the flesh lusts against the spirit seems to you good. But you think you have elegantly mocked our opinion about Baptism, most lyingly affirming that we say Baptism was provided on account of the mere stirring of the genitals. We do not say this, but we say that—which you try to subvert by a new and heretical perversity—the help of the second spiritual birth was divinely provided, which Christ instituted to come about in himself, because those born carnally according to Adam contract, at the first birth, the contagion of the ancient death. Behold, I have used the words of the Punic bishop Cyprian, against whom you bark even though he is a martyr, when you assail the most firmly founded faith of the Church, for which Church his blood was shed. For just as the apostle Paul said: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^186]; so too the bishop Cyprian, an interpreter of this Apostle, confessed that those born carnally according to Adam contract, at the first birth, the contagion of the ancient death. What is it, then, that you fallaciously join yourself to the Apostles and all Catholics; since you most plainly contradict the Apostle fraudulently, and the catholic bishop and martyr, in what he understood with the Eastern and Western catholic Church?

**I. 107.** *Julian.* The exposition, indeed, which you applied to the words of the apostle Paul, would have to be passed over with laughter, did it not terrify those ignorant of the Scriptures. *When you were*, says the Apostle, *slaves of sin, you were free of justice*. He certainly could not say: Freed; since this word of liberation is then fittingly and properly introduced, when a human being is freed from harmful things; but those can be called free of the virtues who decide they owe nothing to the virtues. He can, then, be called free both of good and of evil, who, serving one, is zealous to owe nothing to the other; but he cannot be called freed except from evil; because this word of liberation has in itself the indication of that anguish which is repelled. What question, then, could here be made to the Apostle about his words, when he, in the manner of the human race, called those free of good, freed of evil? *When*, then, he says, *you were slaves of sin, you were free of justice; what fruit did you have then in those things in which you now blush?*[^187] But lest you should reckon that we become slaves to sin by nature, hear the Apostle himself speaking in the same passages: *Or do you not know that to whom you present yourselves as slaves for obeying, you are slaves of him whom you obey, whether of sin, or of the obedience of justice?*[^188] You presented yourselves, he says, as slaves to sin; that you might understand that he imputes sin to the will, not to birth. For this reason alone, then, he said they had been free of justice, because they had refused to keep its precepts.

*Augustine.* If for this reason alone human beings were said to have been free of justice, because they had refused to keep its precepts; then before they received the precepts of justice, which they refused to keep, were they not free of justice, and slaves of sin? Who would say this? From this necessity of slavery, then, he frees who not only gives precepts through the law; but also bestows, through the spirit, love, by whose delight the delight of sin may be conquered; otherwise it perseveres unconquered, and holds its slave. *For by whom anyone has been overcome, to him also he is assigned as a slave*[^189].

**I. 108.** *Julian.* Finally, he immediately adds that they are slaves of justice, just as they had been slaves before of sin. Through which you can now, if you please, call them free of sin, when they serve justice; just as they were said by him to be free of justice, when they served the offense. Most ineptly, then, you wished to stir up a calumny against the apostolic simplicity. For he did not, as you think, most vigilantly intimate this which you reckon; but you most drowsily look at what he brought forth. For you argue that he preferred to say freed rather than free, that we might understand that by the liberty of choice one can indeed do evil, but that good cannot be done. But the very order of his words struggles against you. For if he had thought this which you do, that one sins only by liberty, he ought to have said: You were free of sin; not: *You were free of justice*, so that he might be said to be free for whom liberty itself was working. For if it pleases even to weigh the moments of the cases, he said they were free to this justice, not free from this justice. More consistently, then, we would be helped by this expression, if we too wished to press even such slight matters. But far be it: we understand the sense of the Apostle, and we are content with the office of expressions simply brought forth. The Teacher of the Gentiles said nothing other than: *You were free*, you did not serve, *justice*; you were freed, you received the pardon of sins, with the liberty of choice remaining; through which they could both before obey sin, and afterward obey justice.

*Augustine.* From this heretical sense, by which you say that liberation from sin does not come about through grace, except when pardon is received for the past; but not also that sin may not have dominion, when one is drawn by his own concupiscence into consent to it; you contradict even the prayers of the saints. For why is it said to God: *Lead us not into temptation*; if, that this may not come about, it is in the power of our free choice, which is naturally implanted in us? Why does the Apostle say: *But we pray to God, that you do no evil*[^190]; if God does not free from sins, except by giving pardon for past ones?

**I. 109.** *Julian.* Finally, he showed what his exhortation held, premising: *It is a human thing that I say, on account of the weakness of your flesh; for as you presented your members to serve uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present your members to serve justice unto sanctification; for when you were slaves of sin, you were free of justice*[^191]. Most orderly, indeed, he says that those were free of justice whom he had addressed, that they should keep their members in all sanctification. But, since we have lingered here not a little, in order to show that it is most certain what I had said—that human beings would deny free choice who had been frightened by your speech, and would be thrust by a vain dread into true destruction; and that you are the chief denier of free choice—let us return to that book which was directed to Valerius, that we may prove first that you denied God the founder; but now, that you deny it in one place, and in another confess it much more wickedly than you had denied it. And indeed, how absolutely you denied in that earlier book of yours that God is the founder of human beings, appeared sufficiently when it was examined; for you said that *the devil rightly plucked the human race as the fruit of a shrub, as it were, planted by himself*; and many other things which were placed by you in the place of arguments cooperate with this error. But in this second book, although by the whole of your teaching you do the same, yet you strive more perniciously to amend the opinion which you brought forth before.

*Augustine.* If the Apostle is too little for you, who says: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*; Ambrose certainly—not as a Manichee, by which name you accuse those of such a faith as he was; but as a Catholic—understood the Apostle, and, understanding him, said: *We are all human beings born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*. Behold from what, as from his own shrub, the devil rightly plucked the fruit; not from the nature which God created, but from the vice which he himself planted. For those who are born under sin cannot be except under the author of sin, if they are not reborn in Christ.

**I. 110.** *Julian.* But let us supply briefly the response which is owed to your chapter, which we set down above. Constantly and faithfully, then, I respond that we do not envy human beings the Liberator, the Lord Jesus Christ; whom we address, lest, believing you, they be broken by despair of amendment, and recede from the instruction of Christ, as though commanding things which the nature of mortals cannot grasp, being weighed down, of course, by a congenital evil.

*Augustine.* But death too is congenital; and yet he frees from it who *vivifies whom he wills*[^192]; to whom they ought to flee who wish to be freed from the congenital evil; and that they may do this, read in the Gospel who draws them.

**I. 111.** *Julian.* But let them run to him who cries out: *My yoke is sweet, and my burden is light*[^193]; who both bestows pardon on the evil will by inestimable liberality, and makes the innocence which he creates good better by renewing and adopting.

*Augustine.* These are the very human beings whom you envy the Liberator, whom you deny to have an evil from which they may be freed. How, then, do you constantly and faithfully respond that you do not envy human beings the Liberator, the Lord Jesus Christ; when you rather act pertinaciously and faithlessly, that little ones be not believed to be saved by him, just as he saves his people from their sins[^194]; on account of which we have learned, the Gospel speaking, that he was called Jesus? You will not, then, be able to teach that you do not envy human beings the Liberator Christ, because you can in no way show that little ones are not human beings.

**I. 112.** *Julian.* I marvel, then, that you dared to set down the testimony by which it is said: *For by whom anyone has been overcome, to him also he is assigned as a slave*[^195]. Which most manifestly makes for us, who assert that no one can be liable to the devil except him who, after the contest of the will, has been overcome by a degenerate surrender. But it ought not to have been used by you, against whom it vehemently fights, persuading that those being born are in the kingdom of the devil, who without their own will certainly could neither be overcome, nor sin.

*Augustine.* Those whom you say, being born, could neither be overcome nor contend, draw their origin from him in whom all sinned; who, the man—what is worse—was overcome without a contest. *For there was Adam, and in him we all were; Adam perished, and in him all perished*. Allow, then, little ones to be found by him who came to seek what had perished[^196]; otherwise you utterly envy human beings the Liberator Jesus, since they too are human beings, however much loquacity you believe should be used to cover the cruelty of this your error.

**I. 113.** *Julian.* And indeed, seeing that this avails very much against you, as if questioned by the very testimony: Through what, then, are little ones in the kingdom of the adverse power, if the Scripture is believed that each one, when he is overcome, is then a slave of the conqueror, and it is established that infancy, without the use of reason and will, could neither fight nor yield?, you add: *"For through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned "*[^197]. *But God is the founder of those being born in such a way that all from one go into condemnation, of whom he has not been the liberator of those reborn. For he himself was called "a potter, making from the same lump one vessel unto honor according to mercy, another unto dishonor according to judgment "*[^198], *to whom the Church sings: "Mercy and judgment "*[^199]. This which you say, that through one man sin entered, by which testimony of the Apostle you have nevertheless stirred the breasts of very many of the unlearned, although in the fourth book I have briefly shown how it was to be understood, yet, with Christ helping, we will expound more fully in this work, so that in the second book, with other things passed over, the apostolic passage itself may be discoursed upon most fully in its own context.

*Augustine.* To that which you say you briefly showed in your fourth book, you have been answered in our sixth; but what you promise in this work, when you have begun to render it, there it will appear how empty a talker you are.

**I. 114.** *Julian.* But here I would briefly admonish, since it cannot support you toward the confirmation of that opinion which all erudition, all reason, and the law of God convict as most unjust. Let the diligent reader, then, attend to what you have declared, that God is the maker of evils; and creates such beings as, without any deserving of their own will, all go absolutely into condemnation.

*Augustine.* This I said: because he makes the nature of human beings, who by the vice of evil are evil, which he himself did not make; and from which he himself does good, even when the human beings whom he makes are evil; because in so far as they are human beings, not in so far as they are evil, he makes them. For they would not become vessels unto dishonor, unless they were evil; who nevertheless, by the nature which God makes, are certainly good; but evil according to the vice which the enemy planted, against nature indeed, but yet in nature; so that from this nature was evil, that is, an evil human being. For there can be no evil except in some good; because there can be none except in some nature; but every nature, in so far as it is a nature, is a good. Attend diligently how those things are said which seem to be said as if contrary to each other, and are not; if, however, you have not utterly lost your eyes in the smoke of proud contention.

**I. 115.** *Julian.* And lest we should not know of what time you were speaking; from Adam, whom you say all were as one, to the end, those who are not baptized you declare to be found liable to condemnation and to the devil. In which opinion you strive to heal yourself much more perniciously than you had before wounded yourself. For, in order to remove the odium which was rushing upon you, that you said the devil is the founder of human beings; corrected, you confess God to be the founder, but of such beings as the Manichee ascribes to the prince of darkness.

*Augustine.* *We are all human beings born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*. This was said not by the unclean heretic the Manichee, but by the catholic saint Ambrose. Furthermore, the Manichee neither says that every nature, in so far as it is a nature, is good; nor does he say that the nature which he calls evil can in any way be healed and made good; which the catholic faith says about the human nature of the little and the great, both against the Manichees and against the Pelagians, both insane by diverse diseases, but equally.

**I. 116.** *Julian.* Indeed, believing human beings to be evil by the condition of being born, he assigned to them that author through whom the crime of the evil work might be separated from the good God; and since he had erred in the definition of sin, so as to think that to be natural which cannot be except voluntary; he consequently thereafter invented an evil artificer of an evil origin; here more religious toward God, more obstinate toward nature. But you say that human beings are indeed born evil, but that God is the author of the evils; you, more obstinate toward God, more honoring toward nature; for nature is defended by the majesty of its author, but the author is accused by the foulness of the work.

*Augustine.* Accuse God, then, if you please, by the foulness of the work; since some bodies are born so foul that some of them, by their excessive deformity, are even called monsters. For it is not another god, as the Manichee invents, nor lesser gods, as Plato errs, that are the founders of bodies; but certainly the good and just God works even such bodies; which, if you refer them to the heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam[^200], you will find him neither evil, such as the Manichee employs for fabricating bodies; nor overcome and entangled and mingled with evils, which the Manichee does not fear to believe about the good God; but plainly just on account of original sin, such as the catholic faith knows, from which your error has strayed. For if no one had sinned, foul and monstrous bodies would not be born even in paradise.

**I. 117.** *Julian.* You did not fear, most wicked one, to apply to God this which the Manichee, lest he apply it to him, invented another founder; both of you indeed are enemies of truth, but it was thought before you that he could not be surpassed in impiety.

*Augustine.* Before me was Ambrose, who was not a Manichee; before him Hilary, Gregory; before these Cyprian, and the rest, whom it is long to recall, who were not Manichees. And yet they taught the Church what they learned in the Church, that little ones draw original sin, and are to be breathed upon in the exorcisms, that they may be rescued from the power of darkness, and transferred into the kingdom of their Savior and Lord[^201]. Since, if Christ died even for them, which you too have been compelled to confess, *then all are dead; and he died for all*[^202], which the Apostle says; who certainly, if according to your words he too will be a Manichee, what then will you be?

**I. 118.** *Julian.* But you have justified, as the Prophet says, your sister Sodom[^203]; the Manichee will be thought acquitted, if he is compared with your blasphemies. I had gloried in the first book of my work that I was torn by that mouth by which the Apostles too had endured injury; but now I am terrified at the greatness of my felicity; I am reviled by one who accuses God.

*Augustine.* You are reviled by one who, with Ambrose and the rest of his associates, proclaims Christ the Liberator even of little ones; whom you not only accuse as a liar, where he says he came to save and to seek what had perished[^204]; but even contradict him, that he should not seek little ones to be saved.

**I. 119.** *Julian.* Whence did so great a dignity of insult befall me? You could have conferred nothing such upon me by praising. You say that my thoughts are to be reproved, but that the works of God are to be condemned; that I dispute badly; but you declare that God creates wickedly; you cry out that I err; that he rages; you assert that I do not know the law; but that God does not know justice; you proclaim that I am not a Catholic, because I say that Christ summons those whom he saves; but you swear that God creates those whom he condemns, and founds them for no other reason than that all may go into condemnation.

*Augustine.* This can be said also about the foreknowledge of God, which nevertheless cannot be denied by the faithful, and, I think, not even by you. Either deny that God foreknows that he will condemn many whom he creates, lest he seem to create those whom he condemns; and—what is more marvelous and more unsearchable—that he does not snatch from this life many, lest malice change their understanding[^205], whom he cannot be ignorant will be evil. Give honor to God: let the noisy ruin of your words—as if polished and sharp, but glass—yield to the height of his judgments.

**I. 120.** *Julian.* Between you, then, and the Manichee, the begetter of your thoughts, I see that, by the progress of your erudition, a great distance has been made. For he, although he had introduced two authors, yet left a hope of salvation from that part by which he said that the good God is most foreign to iniquity and to cruelty; but you, saying that there is one God indeed, but the same the founder of evils, have utterly taken away both the reverence of divinity and the hope of salvation.

*Augustine.* The Manichees invent a God cruelly weak, who handed over his own part, his own substance, the members of his own nature to be torn apart and contaminated by enemies, from whom he discerned that devastation threatened him; but you, who do not deny God to be most almighty, wish him to be believed unjust in the heavy yoke of little ones, by denying original sin.

**I. 121.** *Julian.* For there is no one to come to the aid of the guilty, when he himself, who is one, by a desire of creating miseries punishes even those in whom he recognizes nothing other than this which he himself made.

*Augustine.* He recognizes there also what he himself did not make; for sin he himself did not make. But another, vain like you, could say that God, by a desire of creating miseries, creates even those whom he could not be ignorant that he would condemn—incomparably more than those whom he foreknew he would free.

**I. 122.** *Julian.* With the abyss of your impiety, then, examined, although nothing more profane could ever be found; yet how this has no force, and what is to be gathered about those things which you had appended, will be shown by a brief disputation. God, who wished to be called by this name, is believed both most almighty and most just; of which, if one is lacking, neither will be present; as he is the most benign founder of human beings, so the most equitable assessor of deservings; everything that he makes is very good[^206]. And through this, no one is naturally evil, but whoever is guilty is accused by morals, not by origins.

*Augustine.* Why, then, is there a heavy yoke upon the origins of little ones, under a God most powerful and most just?

**I. 123.** *Julian.* And so evil is not natural, nor can God create the guilty, nor place them in the kingdom of the devil. With these things gathered, both you are shown to be a Manichee, or rather worse than the Manichee; and the entrance of humanity into this world without sin, and the fruit of fecundity under the right of God, not of the devil, and natural innocence have become clear.

*Augustine.* Then Ambrose too, who said: *The little ones who have been baptized are reformed from malice to the beginnings of their nature*, is shown to be a Manichee, or worse than the Manichee, as you revile, or you rave.

**I. 124.** *Julian.* With this sealed, then, attend to what follows from your progress. It is established that the Prophets and Patriarchs, and all the saints of the Old Testament, were without Baptism, but, founded by God, thereafter shone with their own virtues; under the kingdom of the devil, then, against the testimony of the law, they will be believed to be handed over to eternal punishments; because all from Adam are declared by you to be created unto condemnation.

*Augustine.* The same grace, against which you have declared war, freed those ancient just ones too; although they used different sacraments according to the time. Since what we believe about Christ, this they believed. *For there is one God, and one Mediator of God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus*[^207], whose coming in humility was foretold to them, but to us announced; but his coming in glory, which will be at the end, was both foretold to them and to us. Both their faith, then, and ours, in this one Mediator, is one, and the same spirit of faith is both in them and in us; whence the Apostle says: *But having the same spirit of faith, according to what is written: I believed, on which account I have spoken; we too believe, on which account we also speak*[^208]. But whence this faith is, lest we glory as though it were from ourselves, let us hear the same Apostle: *By grace*, he says, *you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves, but it is the gift of God*[^209]. And in another place: *Peace*, he says, *to the brothers, and love with faith from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ*[^210].

**I. 125.** *Julian.* But if you say it, even your patrons will be able to confess how openly you are a Manichee. But if you understand that so great an army of the true King fights against your opinion, and that you can bring no prejudgment against them; acquiesce that what you had built up has been destroyed, and through this that not all go from one into condemnation[^211]; but only those who, rebellious to the will of God, without penitence, without correction, are caught at the last end.

*Augustine.* Add also those begotten, if they have not been regenerated; because in one all sinned[^212].

**I. 126.** *Julian.* But as for the fact that God was called *a potter, making from the same lump one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor*[^213], it ought not to have been mentioned by you at all; because, as it is consistently expounded by us, so it wholly fights against you; for when some are said to be made unto honor, others unto dishonor, the sense of the Catholics is helped, by which, according to the diversity of the human will, a diverse outcome of the vessels too is proclaimed.

*Augustine.* Hear Ambrose, who says: *We are all human beings born under sin, and our very origin is in fault*. For thus he understood, with the rest of his fellow disciples and fellow teachers, without any doubt catholics, what is written about sin and death, that it entered through one, and passed into all human beings[^214]. And understand that this is the lump from which the vessels are made, whether those or these. For if the solution of this unsearchable question were that which you hold, according to the deservings of wills; it would be so manifest that the Apostle would be compelled by no difficulty of it to say: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^215] For it was about those not yet born, of whom, not from works, but according to his own purpose, God loved one, hated the other[^216]; whence it came to these words, that about the same lump and diverse vessels, and about the power of the potter, it was said.

**I. 127.** *Julian.* But you, who said above that all go into condemnation, with what brow did you set down a testimony by which it is declared that one goes unto honor, another unto dishonor?

*Augustine.* But grace frees, from the condemnation of the whole lump, those whom it frees; which, by denying, you are heretics. For as far as concerns the deserving of origin, *from one all go into condemnation*; but as far as concerns grace, which is given not according to deservings, whoever are freed from this condemnation are called vessels of mercy; but those who are not freed, *the wrath of God remains upon them*[^217], coming from the just judgment of God; which is not for that reason blameworthy, because it is unsearchable; and therefore they are called vessels of wrath, because God uses these too well, *that he may make known the riches of his glory, upon the vessels of mercy*[^218]. For what, with God judging, is exacted from the rest, this, with him having mercy, is granted to these; which unsearchable ways of the Lord, if you reckon they should be disapproved, hear: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^219]

**I. 128.** *Julian.* For nothing is so at odds as to say all, and not all. You say that all are made by God the potter unto condemnation; the Apostle says, not all unto condemnation, nor all unto honor; what dignity it has, I will settle in its own place. Yet in the very pronouncing of the opinion, it is established that there is great discord between you, and that the potter who fashions all things unto condemnation is not the same whom Paul says fabricates others unto honor; nor that you believe him who is proclaimed by the admirable teacher; since your potter fashions all unto condemnation, the Apostle's potter fashions very many unto glory.

*Augustine.* When all are said to go from one into condemnation, the lump itself is signified, from which the potter makes some vessels unto honor, that is, those taken up into grace, others unto dishonor, that is, those left to pay the debt; so that the sons of grace may know that this is granted to them which, if it were exacted, would not be unjust; and so they may glory not in themselves, but in the Lord[^220].

**I. 129.** *Julian.* And these things I would say, that it might at once appear that you are of singular inexperience or shamelessness, who, in place of your own, use contrary things; but piety and reason will explain that my God forms no one unto dishonor.

*Augustine.* If your God forms no one unto dishonor, he is not the God of the apostle Paul; for he said about the true God: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it: Why have you made me thus? Or does the potter of the clay not have power, from the same lump, to make one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor?*[^221] But you, of course, a great craftsman, bring forth from the Pelagian workshop a better god, who makes no vessel unto dishonor.

**I. 130.** *Julian.* But he founds his own image, that is, all human beings, good, who desires even to reform, by the largesse of remedies, those demolished by the depravity of their pursuits. To him indeed the Church sings *mercy and judgment*[^222]; because he is both benign to those who have offended in nothing, and punishes by a just judgment those who, founded good by God, sinned by their own will, and spurned the supports of mercy. This mercy, then, and this judgment the Church of the Catholics sings; but in yours nothing such can resound, which says that God, the founder of evils, without justice, without judgment, without mercy, forms those whom he punishes; and punishes for this, that he himself formed them from Adam.

*Augustine.* You have already been answered above to all these things; yet hear here too briefly. God does not withhold the good of his forming even from a damned stock; but if it displeases you that God creates those whom he condemns; contradict him, if you can, that he should not create those whom he foreknew would be evil, and would persevere in malignity to the end, and on this account, without doubt, were to be condemned; or suggest to him, if it seems good, that so many thousands of unbaptized little ones, whom he knows will live in ruin, and will go, with him condemning, into eternal fire with the devil, he should snatch from this life, while they are innocent and good; that, if not in his kingdom, at least in some place of a second happiness, which your heresy has built for such, they may hold everlasting life. You have still something to suggest, as a counselor of God, for his sons whom he regenerated, whom he adopted, and yet foresaw would be evil and to be condemned; that, before they come into a guilty life, they be deprived of life itself by being cut short, and pertain to his kingdom, not to eternal punishments. For when you think you say spitefully that God creates those whom he condemns; consider how much more spitefully another, vain in like manner, could say that God regenerates those whom he condemns; since it is in the power of his omnipotence, before they become damnable, to take them away from the temptations of this mortal life. But if you cannot say these things, nor contradict God, nor offer your counsel to that wisdom; *for who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?*[^223], cease to introduce to us another potter, who does not make vessels unto dishonor; and restrain yourself from reprehending this one who makes them, and acknowledge yourself; because, that you may not entangle yourself in this sacrilege, the Apostle says to you: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^224]

**I. 131.** *Julian.* But now let the dignity of the apostolic statement be asserted, lest he be thought to have held about some what you reckoned about all. The apostle Paul, then, disputing with the questions of the Jews, who, swelling with the dignity of their race, disdained that the faithful from the Gentiles be made equal to them, commends both the justice of God and his grace, disputing that it pertained to their munificence, both that first the knowledge of the law had ennobled the Jews, and that thereafter the preaching of Christ had adopted the Gentiles too. For there is one founder of both peoples, about to judge both the latter through the law, and the former without law, and the latter in the law[^225]; because he was not the God of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles, restoring to each his own without fraud, without favor, that is, without respect of anyone's person (which the name of favor expresses in the definition of justice), so that those coming from the seed of Abraham, when they live wickedly, he justly condemns, and drives out from his inheritance, just as also the Gentiles caught in a like working; and, on the other side, the good wills of both peoples and true faith and uprightness of actions he grants everlasting joys. The Teacher of the Gentiles, then, represses the swelling of the Jews, and shows that the distance is not in the seeds of the human race, but in morals; that they might recognize that, unless they had taken care to be faithful, they were to be vindicated by no prerogative of their circumcised race; since Jacob and Esau, conceived from one seed-emission, and poured forth by one force of the one giving birth, bore very diverse outcomes according to the diversity of their deservings.

*Augustine.* If you understood according to the Apostle, you would not mention in this place the deservings of Jacob, where he says that Jacob was loved not from works; that he may commend grace, which is given not according to our deservings; otherwise *the reward is not imputed*, as he himself says, *according to grace, but according to debt*[^226]. By which words what does he show, except that grace is not owed, but gratuitous? This, then, he was commending, where he said: *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might remain, not from works, but from him who calls, it was said: The elder shall serve the younger*[^227]. They are clear, the things you try to obscure; take away the smoke, attend to the light of the Scriptures. For grace goes before a human being, that he may love God, by which love he may work good things. Which the apostle John too most plainly shows, where he says: *Let us love, because he first loved us*[^228]. We are not, then, loved because we have loved; but, because we have been loved, let us love.

**I. 132.** *Julian.* For Esau, profane and a fornicator, selling his birthright for one meal, sought the blessing which he had spurned, and did not obtain it, although he had asked for it with tears[^229]. But Jacob, quiet and mild, obedient to the precepts of his parents, and most desirous of sanctifications, was advanced so far that, as God is called the God of Abraham and Isaac, so also of Jacob, among the holy people[^230]. Since, then, it was established by all examples that God, by a just judgment, does not deny his mercy to good minds in whatever nation, but allows evil minds to be defended by no nobility of stock; the Jews should understand that the faith of the Gentiles ought not to be despised by them; because, just as the lineage of the Israelites does not patronize crimes, so also the origin of the Gentiles is no hindrance to virtues. Doing all this, then, in that conflict, the Apostle yet, in certain places, to bend down the arrogance of the circumcised, under the name of grace prejudges only about the power of God.

*Augustine.* Then, to bend down the arrogance of the circumcised, under the name of grace the Apostle lies, for God chooses from works, not from grace. Who would so think, except a heretic, an enemy of grace, a friend of pride? The Vessel of election cries out, and the preacher of the grace by which he was made such, that Jacob was loved not from works, and you mention the works of Jacob, from which you contend that he was loved; and doing this, you reckon that you contradict me, when you are a new Antichrist, and most plainly contradict him in whom Christ spoke[^231].

**I. 133.** *Julian.* So that to those glorying in the observance of ceremonies and sacrifices, and through this reckoning that other nations, consecrated by no rites of the law, could neither suddenly nor ought to be admitted to fellowship with them, he might say that, even if in those observances were the sum of justice, yet God would have in his power to make a certain exchange of peoples, so as to reject whom he would, and take up whom he would. To which sense he answers under the person of the Jews, that nothing ought now to be exacted from the will of a human being; since God *has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills*[^232]. To which the Apostle replies: *O man, who are you to answer back to God?*[^233] And he brings in the testimony of the prophet Isaiah: *Does the thing formed say*, he says, *to him who formed it: Why have you made me thus?* And he adds of his own: *Or does the potter of the clay not have power, from the same lump, to make one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor?* And the sense is of this kind: Because I commended the will of God, and set forth the authority of his grace, saying that he would show mercy on whomever he had had mercy; you, O Jew, stirred up against me a calumny, as though the commendation brought forth by me of the divine will and power pertained to the destruction of that justice; and because I said: He does what he wills; you argued that nothing now ought to be required of the will of a human being, if God did everything by his own will; whereas the dignity of the person had excluded the place of the question. For if I had said: God does what he owes according to the laws of his justice, which judges about the deservings of individuals; you would certainly not have replied anything such as you now object; but now, because I said: God does what he wills, you thought I had stolen something from the dignity of justice. Both, then, are the same. For when I say of God: He does what he wills, I say nothing other than: He does what he owes; because I know he wills nothing other than he owes. Where, then, the will clings inseparably to equity, whichever of them I shall have named, I have marked both.

*Augustine.* In whatever way you say that God does what he owes; he owes grace to no one, and to many he does not render the punishment which he owes for their evil works, and bestows the grace which he owes for none of their good works. For what did he owe to that same Paul, when, still Saul, he was persecuting the Church? Was it not punishment? That, then, he prostrated him by a voice sent from heaven, that he blinded him, that he so violently drew him to receive the faith which he was laying waste[^234], without doubt he did according to grace, not according to debt, so that he might be among those remnants of the people of Israel, of whom he says: *So then, even at this time, a remnant has been made according to the election of grace; but if by grace, then no longer from works; otherwise grace is now not grace*[^235]. What did he owe also to those to whom he says: *Not for your sake do I do this, house of Israel, but for the sake of my holy name, which you have profaned among the Gentiles*[^236], except punishment? He says, then, that he does their good things in them, but for the sake of his name which they profaned, not for the sake of those who profaned it; for if he wished to do it for their sake, he would render them the punishment owed, not bestow grace unowed. For what he says he will do pertains to this, that they may do good things, not because they were good who profaned his holy name. Finally, he says most plainly that they will do good things; but with him bringing it about that they do them; for he says among other things: *And I will bring it about that you walk in my justifications, and observe and do my judgments*[^237]. To these works certainly the reward is imputed according to debt; for the reward is owed, if they are done; but grace, which is not owed, goes before, that they may be done. The good reward, I say, is owed to the good works of human beings; but grace is not owed, which works the human beings themselves good from evil. Finally, you who said that God does what he owes, and with high neck have aired human deservings, say, I beg you, by what deservings of little ones the kingdom of heaven is owed. You will perhaps say that he owes this to his grace, by whose help they were reborn. For having received this, they now deserve to come into his kingdom; but that very grace, which he shows to those to be regenerated, he owes to none of their deservings at all. For this reason your Pelagius, in the episcopal judgment of Palestine, was compelled, lest he himself be condemned, to condemn those who say that the grace of God is given according to our deservings; where he condemned, without doubt, both yourself and himself, who do not cease to say this[^238]. This grace, truly grace, that is, gratuitous, and owed to no preceding deservings, the Apostle was commending, when he said: *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might remain*. This is the election of which that too is said: *A remnant has been made according to the election of grace, but if by grace, then no longer from works; otherwise grace is now not grace*. Whence here too, when he had said: *That the purpose of God might remain according to election*; he immediately added: *Not from works, but from him who calls, it was said: The elder shall serve the younger*. Against this trumpet of truth you cry out, and say: *To bend down the arrogance of the circumcised, under the name of grace the apostle Paul prejudges only about the power of God*. Where what do you say other than: To bend down the arrogance of the circumcised, the Apostle lies, saying that Jacob was loved not from works; when he was loved from works, because he was *quiet, and mild, obedient to the precepts of his parents, and most desirous of sanctifications*? Nor do you understand that he was loved not for the reason that he was such, or was going to be such; but was made such, because he was loved. Blush: the Apostle does not lie; Jacob was loved not from works; *for if by grace, then no longer from works*, but loved by grace, and it was fitting that, by that same grace bringing it about, he should be strong in good works. Spare your soul, do not be an enemy of this grace.

**I. 134.** *Julian.* Therefore that pride, which wished to be idle, and to cloak its sloth on that account with the color of necessity, so as to cry out against the Gospel in the reception of the Gentiles; hears that, even if it were so as you contrive, yet you ought to supplicate God, not to stir up sedition; by which words he beats back the wickedness of the man who, hunting for the ambiguity of an expression, was trying to ascribe to divine necessity the diversity of deservings coming from the quality of the will, so as to assert that one of two things was necessary, that is, either that the Gentiles should not come to fellowship with the promise, or, if this were permitted to God, that the offices of free will should be extinguished. But because that did not suffice for the business; for by such a teacher, as the authority of God was commended, so justice was not to be left undefended; he adds most consequently that the vessels which are made in dishonor and those in honor have this from the wages of their own will. *For if God, willing to show wrath, and to make known his power, in much patience, in vessels of wrath, consummated unto perdition; and that he might make known*, he says, *the riches of his glory in vessels of mercy, which he prepared unto glory, whom also he called, namely us, not only from the Jews, but also from the Gentiles*[^239]. Certainly here he settles what the earlier conflict had covered, that wrath is not brought by God except upon those vessels which have been consummated unto perdition; but that glory is given to those which have been prepared for this. But by whom vessels of this kind are prepared for the reception of those things which we have said, the discourse of the Apostle himself made plain. *In a great house*, he says, *there are not only golden and silver vessels, but also wooden and earthen; some indeed unto honor, others unto dishonor. If, then, anyone shall cleanse himself from these, he will be a vessel unto honor, sanctified, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work*[^240].

*Augustine.* Then the vessels themselves so prepare themselves, that it was said in vain about God: *Which he prepared unto glory*? For you say this most plainly; nor do you understand that it was said: *If anyone shall cleanse himself*, so that the work of the human being too through the will might be shown; but, ungrateful man, *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^241]; therefore both are true, both that God prepares vessels unto glory, and that they prepare themselves. For that a human being may do, God does; because that a human being may love, God first loves. Read the prophet Ezekiel, whence I mentioned above what seemed enough; you will find these words too, that is, that God brings it about that human beings do his precepts, on whom he has mercy, not on account of their deservings, which he mentions there to be evil, but on account of his name; so that, with God bringing it about, without their deservings, that they do his precepts, they may begin to have the deservings of good deeds. This is the grace which you deny—not from works which are done, but that they may be done.

**I. 135.** *Julian.* Behold the office of free will: *If*, he says, *anyone shall cleanse himself* from the society of base vessels (by which name vices are denoted), *he will be a vessel unto honor, sanctified, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work*. These vessels, then, by their own pursuits, are prepared either for wrath or for glory; but God makes known his power in both, either by exerting severity upon the impious, or by bestowing blessing upon the faithful. It appeared, then, that this statement of the excellent teacher both brought no help to Manichaean meanings, and, on the contrary, consistently armed us.

*Augustine.* Why do you calumniate, why do you falsely accuse, and do not attend to whom, and what kind of teachers of the Church, you accuse? Not with the words of any Manichee, but of holy Ambrose, I answer you: *God calls those whom he deigns, and makes religious whom he wills*. This he does in truth, this Ambrose understood in the truth of the divine Scriptures; but the judgment, when he makes some, and does not make others, is hidden. On account of which it is said to a human being through a human being, but not from a human being: *O man, who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it: Why have you made me thus? Or does the potter of the clay not have power, from the same lump, to make one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor?*[^242] Take away your fogs from the serenity of these words, by which the judgment of God indeed is signified as hidden; but they are themselves so clear that they do not allow themselves to be overshadowed and obscured by your mist.

**I. 136.** *Julian.* And through this it is vehemently set against you too, saying that not all are fashioned unto condemnation, into which you declare all go. But you are wont to argue most absurdly, so as to say: But these are not fashioned unto condemnation, who are afterward freed; whereas not even thus can even the surface of the apostolic discourse befit you. For when you say: All are created unto condemnation by the law of being born, but some thence, though very few, are freed by the mysteries; you do not assert this which he does, who proclaims not only that some are freed of the condemned, but that not all are fashioned unto condemnation, but some unto dishonor, others unto honor.

*Augustine.* Where the Apostle said: *From one all unto condemnation*; he showed that very lump, which flowed wholly vitiated from Adam; but where he says that from it are made vessels unto honor, he commends grace, by which God even frees the human beings whom he creates; but where he says that from it are made vessels unto dishonor, he shows the judgment, by which, although he creates human beings, he yet does not free them. Which even you are compelled to confess about little ones, of all of whom you certainly cannot deny that there is one lump, of whatever kind you think it; from which, however, you confess that some are adopted into the kingdom of God, whom without doubt you concede to be vessels made unto honor; but that others are not adopted, whom, made vessels unto dishonor, unless you intelligently consent, you shamelessly deny. For even if this is not the punishment of damnation, as you wish, will it not at least be a dishonor of the image of God, to be separated from the kingdom of God? But if you perseveringly deny that grace, you will show that you pertain to this judgment; which certainly would be unjust in little ones, if there were no original sin.

**I. 137.** *Julian.* And as it became clear that the Apostle spoke about morals, so it appears with how great a lack of testimonies of the Law you labor, who from these statements seek help against the thunderbolts of reason—statements which disdain, or rather by their own nature cannot, come to your aid.

*Augustine.* Against the Apostle saying that from the same lump God makes one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor; and not saying, another neither unto honor nor unto dishonor, which he would certainly say, if he believed this about little ones which you do; against him, then, divinely thundering, your reason is not thunderous, but smoky.

**I. 138.** *Julian.* And these things indeed have been disputed as far as concerns the testimony of the Apostle. But in Isaiah, whence Paul took the statement itself, so far is God from deterring the rational creature from the consideration of his judgment, that, just as through the same Prophet he had said: *Cease to act perversely, learn to do well, come to the aid of the oppressed; and come, accuse me, says the Lord*[^243]; so also here, lest he seem to have done anything by power alone, not justice, he deigns to open the reason of his dispensations. For he announces to the people of the Jews afflicted in captivity that the time of redemption is drawing near, in which they would return to their own land; and he discloses the cause both of the preceding anguish and of the imminent joys. *Let the heaven feast from above, and let the clouds scatter justice; let mercy arise from the earth, and let justice arise together. I am the Lord God who created you, making you better; I prepared you like the clay of the potter. Will he who plows, plow the earth all day? Does the clay say to the potter: What are you doing, because you do not work, because you have no hands? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it: You have formed me wisely? Or who says to a father: What will you beget? And to a mother: What will you bring forth? Because thus says the Lord, the holy God of Israel, who made the things to come: Ask me concerning my sons and daughters, and concerning the works of my hands give command to me. I made the earth and the man upon it; I with my hand established the heaven; I gave command to all the stars; I raised up a king with justice, and all his ways are right; he will build my city, and will lead back the captivity of my people, not with reward, nor with gifts, said the Lord of Hosts*[^244].

*Augustine.* If you understood the words of the Prophet, you would understand that the king there, of whom it was said: *I raised up a king with justice, and all his ways are right*, is the Mediator of God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus; but you would understand as he is to be understood. For you will not dare to say that he was made the Son of God by preceding deservings of works, from the beginning, that is, from the Virgin's womb. By what grace, then, that man was made good from the beginning, by the same grace human beings who are his members are made good from evil. For you do not find what you may say about Christ according to the man, that is, according to that by which the Word was made flesh; that he who was God both remained God and was made man; that that man himself never was man in such a way that he was not the only-begotten Son of God, on account of the only-begotten Word. For he did not acquire this by the deservings of his own morals coming from his own will; but, as Ambrose truly said, as though born of the Spirit, he abstained from offense. Otherwise, according to you, many would be such, if they had wished to be; and that he was the only such one, was in the power of human beings who were unwilling to be such; which, if you attend with how great impiety they are said, or even believed by silent thought; just as you acknowledge the nature of the Only-begotten, that *in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^245]; acknowledge also the grace, that *the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us*[^246]. He, then, *calls those whom he deigns, and makes religious whom he wills*, who made the man whom he willed, one, without any preceding deservings of human will, the Mediator of God and human beings.

**I. 139.** *Julian.* In this place, then, this sense, as far as concerns the history, is contained, that God speaks to the people: Because I neither handed you over into captivity out of hatred, nor now have taken you from the bonds of the Babylonian captivity out of forgetfulness of judgment; but I, as far as is in me, ready to cherish you with continual benevolence, owed it nevertheless to my justice both to hand over the offenders to enemies, and to refresh and free the vexed. For just as one skilled in farming does not always press upon one work, so as to cleave the fields only with plowshares; but composes the lands by a diverse kind for the wished-for fertility; so I too vary the turns of my dispensations, that, now by pressures, now by consolations, I may be able to fit your will to the crops of justice. Finally, that you may understand with how great equity I act with you, I could by my power despise your mutterings, so that, as the thing formed cannot say to the potter: What have you done? so I too might impose on you the weight of silence; yet, against these examples I summon you, that you may ask me concerning my sons and daughters, that is, concerning yourselves and the works of my hands, and learn that I did everything justly, and never did anything cruelly.

*Augustine.* You say what you wish, not what Isaiah said; he speaks of grace, against which you speak.

**I. 140.** *Julian.* Both by the Prophet, then, and by the Apostle, the potter was brought forward as an example, about to furnish nothing other than the office of comparison; not, however, that human beings be shown to be as cheap in God's sight as clay is in the furnace and on the wheel of potters. With this exposition which we premised settled, we note that, according to the recent interpretation, in the same place something else shines out. *Drop down dew, you heavens, from above, and let the clouds rain the just one; let the earth be opened, and let it bring forth the Savior, and let justice arise together; I the Lord have created him. Woe to him who contradicts his fashioner, a potsherd of the earthen vessels of Samos; does the clay say to its potter: What are you doing? And your work is without hands?*[^247] By which words, although according to the history King Cyrus, yet according to the prophecy the incarnation of the Savior is expressed; who, since he was to be born of a Virgin, the obstinacy of the Jews and of all unbelievers is addressed, lest they be foes to the faithful signs. For when he had premised: *Let the earth be opened, and let it bring forth the Savior, and let justice arise together. I the Lord*, he says, *have created him*.

*Augustine.* Tell me by what works the man Christ Jesus deserved this; and by what justice of God he alone deserved this, dare to chatter; or if you do not dare, at last confess grace without deservings, not only remitting sins to a human being, but also working justice in human nature by the Holy Spirit. For did grace either dismiss sins to the man Christ too, or did grace not make him such that from the beginning he was always good, as from the beginning he was always the Son of God? Plainly, just as those who in the desert were being lethally vexed by the bites of serpents were admonished to attend to him who had been lifted up on the standard, lest they perish[^248]; so are those to be admonished who are poisoned by your disputations, that they attend to Christ, and in the justice of that man, the mediator, see grace without deservings, whence they may shut out from themselves the venom of your mouth. Finally, in the words of the Prophet, even according to the recent interpretation which you mentioned, where Christ was more evidently prophesied, you took thence nothing to dispute from, except that he was born of a Virgin, because it was said: *Let the earth be opened, and let it bring forth the Savior*; but about his justice, which here was likewise foretold, you wished to say nothing; although you yourself set down the prophetic words, where he says: *Drop down dew, you heavens, from above, and let the clouds rain the just one; let the earth be opened, and let it bring forth the Savior, and let justice arise together*. Tell me what just one the clouds rained, except that the Prophets and Apostles proclaimed Christ, who was born with justice from the very womb of the Virgin? Whence, when it had been said: *Let the earth be opened, and let it bring forth the Savior*; it was soon added: *and let justice arise together*. And through this, by that grace human beings who are reborn in Christ become just, by which grace Christ was born a just man. As, then, he is an example of life, that by imitating him we may act justly; so he is also an example of grace, that by believing in him we may hope to be made just thence by him, whence he himself was made, *who was made for us wisdom from God, and justice and sanctification and redemption; that, as it is written: He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^249]. Whomever, then, you have bitten with your deadly tooth, let them gaze upon this just one, and they will be healed; that is, let them believe that they receive justice thence, whence justice was inborn to Christ; and therefore let them glory not in their own choice, nor in their own deserving, but in the Lord.

**I. 141.** *Julian.* The earth is opened into a sprout before the work of the cultivator receives the seed; which is approved in the childbearing of the virgin, who by the gift of a mother forestalled and shut out the office of a wife. What, then, was not in use, that almighty God promises he will do; and adds, foreseeing the multitude of unbelievers: *Woe to him who contradicts his fashioner, a potsherd of the earthen vessels of Samos*. That is: Woe to those who, with God promising, contend that a virgin could not become pregnant; and although they themselves, though from seeds instituted by God, yet with the power of God intervening, are fashioned in the maternal viscera, with stubborn zeal affirm that flesh could not be fabricated from the flesh of a virgin without the ministry of a man. Such, then, is it that you, O obstinate ones, do not think that I could do this, and prescribe to me from the difficulty of the matter, when it is established that you yourselves were made by my hands; as if the clay should say to its potter, when it is handled by him: You have no hands; by which it was then being formed into some vessel. So, then, you too, who inquire who gave a son to the virgin without the seed of a man, recognize that he is the same who made you from seed. But now, since the exposition of the holy Scriptures may be various, yet each one is pious and religious in harmony, let the first book be ended; yet warning by its very ending that God be believed the maker of those being born, the guardian of the innocent, the rewarder of Catholics, and the condemner of the Manichees.

*Augustine.* That those who read intelligently may know how you tried to becloud the clear apostolic words, and to pervert the right ones, I must answer this disputation of yours from that same disputation of the Apostle. The blessed Paul, then, wishing to show that the things which God promised, he is able also to do; where grace is especially commended, of which you are enemies; for it is not in the power of human beings that God fulfill what he promised, and not in the power of him who promised; wishing, then, to show this: *The word of God cannot fail*, he says; *for not all who are from Israel are Israel; nor because they are the seed of Abraham are all his sons; but in Isaac shall a seed be called for you; that is, not those who are sons of the flesh, these are the sons of God; but the sons of the promise are reckoned as seed. For the word of the promise is this: At this time I will come, and Sarah shall have a son*[^250]. Hold in mind the sons of the promise, since he who promised is able also to do[^251]. *And not only this*, he says, *but also Rebecca, having Isaac of one intercourse, our father. For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might remain, not from works, but from him who calls, it was said: The elder shall serve the younger*[^252]. And here hold in mind the election not from works, which the later Prophet in a certain way expounded; which the Apostle, subjoining, says: *As it is written: Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*[^253]. Here occurs a question, which could disturb those not understanding the height of grace; and proposing it to himself, he says: *What then shall we say? Is there injustice with God? Far be it!*[^254] And to teach what he said: *Far be it! For Moses*, he says, *says: I will have mercy on whom I have had mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I have been merciful. Therefore it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*[^255]. If you attended to this, you would not exalt against grace the deservings of the will; where you hear: *It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*. God, then, did not have mercy because Jacob willed and ran; but Jacob willed and ran because God had mercy. *For the will is prepared by the Lord*[^256]; and *by the Lord the steps of a man are directed, and he will will his way*[^257]. Then, because this general statement was said on account of Jacob: *It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*; an example is given also about Pharaoh, on account of what was said: *But Esau I hated*; and it is subjoined: *For the Scripture says to Pharaoh: For this I have raised you up, that I may show in you my power, and that my name may be announced in all the earth*[^258]. After these things it is concluded as to both: *Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills*[^259]; but he has mercy certainly according to grace, which is given freely, not rendered to deservings; but he hardens according to judgment, which is rendered to deservings. For from a damned lump to make a vessel unto honor is manifest grace; but to make from it a vessel unto dishonor is just judgment. Hence now, subjoining the words of those to whom this displeases, he says: *You say, then, to me: Why does he still complain? For who resists his will?* And restraining them: *O man*, he says, *who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to him who formed it: Why have you made me thus? Or does the potter of the clay not have power, from the same lump, to make one vessel indeed unto honor, another unto dishonor?*[^260] See whether it does not accord with his earlier words, and dissents from yours; you who think this was said according to the deservings of wills, against what he himself says: *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might remain, not from works, but from him who calls it was said: The elder shall serve the younger*; and against what he himself says: *Therefore it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy*. And not only against these things which preceded, but also against those which follow. For he calls vessels of wrath those which are perfected unto perdition; which would be unjust, unless the lump were already damned, with all coming from one into condemnation; and he calls vessels of mercy those which he prepared unto glory; for it pertains to gratuitous, not owed mercy, to prepare from a damned lump vessels unto glory: *Not only from the Jews*, as he says, *but also from the Gentiles*, on account of which he sets down the testimony from the prophet Hosea: *I have called not my people, my people*[^261]; and from Isaiah: *On account of Israel a remnant shall be saved*[^262]. But that there should be these remnants, he teaches, by the testimony following, came about by the grace of God, through the same Prophet saying: *Unless the Lord of Hosts had left us a seed*[^263]. Then he shows that the Gentiles laid hold of justice from faith; but that Israel for this reason did not lay hold of it, because it was not from faith, but as it were from works. For faith holds what he afterward says at some point: *That everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved*[^264]. To which salvation it pertains that works should be good and that justice should be for us from God, not from ourselves. On account of which, about those who, not from faith, but as it were from works, stumbled on the stone of stumbling, he continues and says: *Brothers, the good will indeed of my heart, and my prayer to God for them, is for their salvation; for I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge. For, being ignorant of the justice of God and seeking to establish their own justice, they did not submit to the justice of God*[^265]. Which you too altogether do; for you wish to establish your own justice, to which God renders grace according to deservings; nor do you wish the grace of God to go before, which makes you have justice. Then, with the series of disputation connected with itself, he arrives at that where he says: *I say, then: Has God rejected his people? Far be it! For I too am an Israelite, of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin; God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. Or do you not know what the Scripture says in Elijah, how he appeals to God against Israel? Lord, they have killed your prophets, and dug up your altars, and I am left alone, and they seek my soul. But what does the divine response say to him? I have left myself seven thousand men, who have not bent the knee before Baal. So, then, even at this time a remnant has been made according to the election of grace. But if by grace, then no longer from works; otherwise grace is now not grace*[^266]. Then see what he adds. *What then?* he says, *that which Israel was seeking, this it did not obtain; but the election obtained it*[^267]. But look above what kind of election, where he says: *A remnant has been made according to the election of grace; but if by grace, then no longer from works*. And he refers this to that from which this disputation set out: *For when they were not yet born, nor had done anything good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might remain; not from works*. This is the election of grace, not from works, by which vessels are made unto honor, that they may do good works; because good works follow grace, do not precede it; since the grace of God brings it about that we do them; and that we do not establish our own justice, but that the justice of God be in us, that is, which God grants us. *But the rest were blinded*[^268]; this is the judgment, by which vessels are made unto dishonor; from which judgment it was said: *But Esau I hated*. From which judgment it was said also to Pharaoh: *For this I have raised you up*. Whence it appears that you, so understanding, or rather not understanding the Apostle, wish to glory from works against grace, and, wishing to establish your own justice, are not subject to the justice of God. But we indeed proclaim God the maker of little ones; but from the same lump a middle place for vessels, where they are made neither unto honor nor unto dishonor, which the Apostle did not give, we do not give; which, by doing, you would escape the judgment of God, if you could show him the condemner only of the Manichees, and not of all heretics.
