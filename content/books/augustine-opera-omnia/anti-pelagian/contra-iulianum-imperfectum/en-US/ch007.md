# Book Six

**VI. 1.** *Julian:* I do not doubt that an opinion of this kind has hitherto prevailed concerning our contest, such that it was believed to look rather to an involved question than to the sum of the faith. For those who carry on far from spiritual pursuits are moved only by the little breezes of reputation; and, dreading the odium of the times, and not holding the safeguard of an ascertained truth (as generally in fearful matters, no one is trusted less than oneself), they judge the safer road to be that which is more frequented.

*Augustine:* Our road is the more frequented in that it is the more ancient, because it is Catholic; but yours is the less frequented, the more it is novel, because it is heretical.

**VI. 2.** *Julian:* But this happens at present for two causes: because the opinion of the Manichees has been approved by the designs of criminals, and the stirred-up storms of persecutions deter needy spirits from favoring the truth.

*Augustine:* How is the road more frequented by the opinion of the Manichees, when they are exceedingly rare? Or how do you suffer persecution for the truth, you who withdraw little ones from the Savior?

**VI. 3.** *Julian:* A part, then, both consulting pleasure and fear, the luxury attended by the crowds of the arena, or the circus, or the stage, seeking in all shameful deeds to put forward the necessity by which the odium of what is committed is always lightened, and to escape the uproars of the world by transgression; these, then, are the things which make the defense of vices more accompanied. From which, however, the greatest part of the common crowd, as I said before, has believed that the reasonings even about God, of the Traducianists and of the Catholics, are in harmony.

*Augustine:* The innumerable multitude of the faithful, which was promised to Abraham[^1], is despised by you as though a common throng; because, namely, what you say can please only the few whom you make Pelagians, that is, mad with the poisons of a novel pestilence—the few who say that the so evident misery of the human race, which appears in the heavy yoke of the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^2], does not come from the merit of the sin by which, in the first man, human nature was vitiated. Whence it follows for you that, if no one had sinned, you are compelled to say that there would have been in paradise not only so many and so great burdens of troubles, which we see little ones bear; but also so many and so great faults of minds and bodies, with which very many are born. In which place of felicity and rest you also place your protégée, the lust by which the flesh lusts against the spirit; and us, who war against it as an adversary fault, with the spirit lusting in opposition, you accuse with closed eyes as though we were friends of pleasure and luxury; into which no one falls shamefully and basely, except one who consents to that protégée of yours, which we charge and you defend, as it solicits and persuades.

**VI. 4.** *Julian:* But because, both in the first and in the present contest, it has become clear by Augustine's argumentation that he is not the God of the Traducianists whom Christians venerate, with harmonious assertion, as just and the founder of all things; I now take heart, by which very many even of those who have fallen by blind error may, on recognizing these things, be corrected.

*Augustine:* Rather, on the contrary, once these things are recognized—the things which, against your deceitful empty talk, we answer in accordance with the word of truth—no one will be held in the error of your heresy except by excessive madness or obstinacy.

**VI. 5.** *Julian:* For the Manichee believes, against us, that mortals are by nature compelled into shameful deeds and crimes; he supposes that the origin of darkness furnished both matter to bodies and crimes to them; that the pleasure of the sexes is the corruption of the human race, the asserter of the devil's right, and the compeller of human beings into all disgraces. The Traducianist, following him in every way, as his heir and offspring, attests with manifold discourse natural crimes, an eternal necessity of evil from the dark nothing, and an affection assigned to the senses that defiles all the saints and sets the image of God in the kingdom of the devil.

*Augustine:* The Manichee, with singular madness, fables a nature of evil substantially coeternal with the good God, against Catholic truth; but Catholic truth confesses God alone to be eternal without any beginning—not only good, which he too says, but also unchangeable, which he does not say. This God, then, supremely good, and on this account altogether unchangeable, to whom no nature is coeternal—a nature which is not what he is, and would not be unless it had been made, not from him, yet by him, that is, not from his nature, yet by his power—we proclaim against the madness of the Manichees; and the nature that was made, which could have been nothing unless the omnipotent nature had made it (though not from itself), we know and say is indeed good, but not equal to him who made it. For God made all things very good[^3]; but not supremely good, as he himself is; which goods, however, of whatever sort, would not be unless he who is supremely good had made them; nor would any changeable goods be unless he who is unchangeably good had made them. And through this, when the Manichees ask us whence evil comes, wishing to introduce an evil coeternal with God—not knowing what evil is, and thinking it to be a nature and substance—we answer them that evil is not from God, nor coeternal with God; but that evil arose from the free will of the rational nature, which was founded good by the good one; but its goodness is not equal to the goodness of its Founder, since it is not his nature, but his work; and therefore it had the possibility of sinning, yet not the necessity. But it would not have even the possibility, if it were the nature of God, who neither wills to be able to sin, nor can will it. Yet if this rational nature, in this possibility of sinning, had not sinned when it could have sinned, it would have acquired for itself a great merit; the reward of which merit would have been even this: that, with a greater felicity, it could not sin. But when he hears this, the Manichee still presses on, and says: If evil is from the free will of the rational nature, whence are these so many evils with which we see those born who do not yet use the freedom of the will? Whence is the concupiscence by which *the flesh lusts against the spirit*[^4], and draws toward committing sin, unless the spirit lusts more strongly against it? Whence in one human being so great a discord of the two things of which he consists? Whence the law in the members fighting against the law of the mind[^5], without which law no one is born? Whence so many and so great faults, whether of minds or of bodies, with which very many are born? Whence the labors and calamities of little ones who do not yet sin by will? Whence, when the use of reason has arrived, so great a punishment of mortals in learning letters or any skills whatever, that to their toilsome efforts is added even the torment of beatings? Here we answer that these evils too draw their origin from the will of human nature, which, by sinning grievously, was vitiated and condemned along with its stock. Whence so many natural goods of this nature come from God's making, but evils from his judgment; which evils the Manichees do not see to be in no way natures or substances; but that they are called natural for this reason, that human beings are born with them from a vitiated origin, as from a root. But you, new heretics, contradict us; answer the Manichees, then; say whence are so many and so great evils with which human beings are born—and if you deny that they are born with them, where is your effrontery? If you confess it, where is your heresy? But contend that these evils are not evils, and fill paradise—not the true one, but yours—with labors, pains, errors, groans, weeping, mournings, even if no one had sinned. But if you do not dare to do this, lest you be laughed at even by the very children, and be judged by the rods of those who must be corrected, the Manichee concludes against you that these evils, which you are unwilling should be from a vitiated good nature, are from the commingling of evil; which evil he says is a nature coeternal with God and contrary to him. And through this, where you try to be more removed from the Manichee, you become his helper.

**VI. 6.** *Julian:* But now they hurl the darts of their accusations against God himself, with an equal end, though an unequal beginning. For the Manichee says: The good God does not make evil; but he adds that, for natural faults, he destines souls to eternal burning; which is of manifest monstrousness: and through this, by the end of his opinion, he contaminates with plain iniquity the one whom he had called good. Furthermore Augustine, as though trusting in a patron in him to whom he writes, with a bolder daring looks down on the trepidation of his master, and does not hesitate to begin where the Manichee arrived; and he pronounces that God does and creates evil, that is, sin—whom it is established does not befit that God whom the faith of the Catholics worships. Let this above all sit in the mind of the reader: that the contest has lain more heavily on us than on any of the faithful ever; and that everyone who thinks nature itself the necessity of crime has no fellowship in the worship of the God of Christians; which, since it has been frequently driven home—for the usefulness of the principal cause has demanded repetition—let us now come to the discussion of the first human beings, in whose appraisal the Numidian, like a light-shield bearer, runs to meet our battle line.

*Augustine:* What the Manichee says about the commingling of good and evil substance, I would believe you did not know, except that I know you have read those things which we wrote against that same error; for from the book in which I refuted their opinion of two souls established in a human being, one of which was good, the other evil[^6], you brought forward certain testimonies, thinking they were against me. The Manichee, then, contends that there are in one human being two souls, or spirits, or minds—one proper to the flesh, and this not evil by a supervening fault, but coeternal with God, evil by nature; the other indeed good by nature, as it were a particle of God, but stained by the commingling of that evil one; and from this he wishes it to come about that the flesh lusts against the spirit, namely the good one, through its own evil soul, so as to hold it entangled; but the spirit lusts against the flesh, so as to be freed from that commingling. And if it cannot, even in the final conflagration of the world, then he says it is fixed to the globe of darkness, and held in such punishment forever. Not, then, as you say, does the God of the Manichees *destine souls to eternal burning for natural faults*, but souls good by nature, on account of the commingling of an alien evil nature, with which evil he himself commingled them—souls he could not free from it—not by eternal burning; for the Manichee supposes no eternal burning; but, as I said, he fixes them to the eternal globe of darkness, in which the mind of darkness will be enclosed. But the Catholic faith, which you abandoned in order to found, as though against the Manichees—not, as you think or pretend, an opposing sect, but rather a helping novel sect—when it hears or reads what the Apostle says: *The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other; that you may not do the things you wish*[^7]; does not recognize two natures, namely of good and of evil, contrary to each other from eternity, and commingled by a later war, as the Manichean heretic supposes; but, as the Catholic teacher Ambrose does[^8], recognizes that this discord of flesh and spirit passed over into nature from the transgression of the first man; so that this is understood not as the nature of the human being as first established, but as a penalty turned into nature in the condemned. This faith is not the Numidian light-shield, over which you insult us as though wittily; but it is a truthful shield, in which we extinguish all the fiery darts of the wicked one, as the Apostle exhorts[^9]. With this shield he was armed, when, already meeting you who were to come, that man—not indeed a Numidian, yet a Carthaginian, Cyprian—came armed; by which name again your empty wordiness leaps upon us; armed, I say, with this shield, that Carthaginian said, in the book On the Lord's Prayer, that we pray this, when we say: *Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven*[^10], namely that between these two, that is, flesh and spirit, there may be, with God's help, concord. There the excellent soldier of Christ extinguishes the fiery darts of the wicked one hurled both by the Manichees and by you; for which wicked one all heretics fight, and whose camp you thought should be increased by your enlistment. For by seeking the concord of spirit and flesh, he teaches against the Manichees that both the natures of which we consist are good, if the evil of the discord is healed by divine mercy; but he resists you, because you say the concupiscence of the flesh is good, by whose harassment this discord exists, which he asks to be healed; and it then exists when we act well, so that, with the spirit lusting in opposition, we resist the harassing concupiscence of the flesh. For if we consent, the concord of spirit with flesh becomes not desirable, but blameworthy, or even damnable. He is also against you, because you assign to free choice what he understands must be asked from God, that it may come to pass in the human being. But you who, not knowing what you say, object to me that I say God creates sin, resist the Manichee saying that in the discord of flesh and spirit there appear two natures, of evil and of good, contrary to each other. For there is one thing we may answer, that this pestilence be conquered: namely that this discord passed over into our nature through the transgression of the first man; which you, by denying, try to make them win; and you show yourself clearly a false assailant of the Manichees, a true helper.

**VI. 7.** *Julian:* For he urges throughout all his writings that Adam and Eve were founded by God only good, that is, made over to no natural crime, and that they sinned by free will, but so grievously that they overthrew all God's institutions in their nature. *For*, he says, *the devil fixed in him a sin far greater and deeper than are those sins known to human beings. Whence, by that great sin of the first man, our nature there, changed for the worse, not only became sinful, but also begets sinners; and yet that very weakness, by which the strength of living well perished, is not nature, but a fault. That sin, then, which changed the human being himself for the worse in paradise, because it is much greater than we can judge, is drawn from everyone being born*[^11]. See how openly he expounded what he held: Those first human beings, he says, had a good nature, but committed a sin so monstrous, so inestimable, that they killed the strength of living well, extinguished the light of free choice, and made for the remainder a necessity of sinning; so that to no one born of their stock would it be possible to strive after the beauties of the virtues, and, with vices set aside, to become a partaker of holiness.

*Augustine:* You seem to yourself, or even to your Pelagian associates, to say something, when, the divine authority abandoned, you are tossed about by human vanity, and by the arguments of your heart you oppose and clamor against the truth of the Holy Scriptures. For surely, if with a Christian and Catholic mind you attended to what the Apostle says: *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^12], you would surely understand that the first man sinned so grievously that, by that sin, the nature not of one man, but of the whole human race was changed, and, fallen from the possibility of immortality into the necessity of death, has collapsed; so that even those who are converted to God, through the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus, do not at once take on immortality of the body; but it is now promised them, to be given afterward through the Spirit of God by whom they are now inhabited. Which the same Apostle in the same place thus explains: *If anyone*, he says, *does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to him. But if Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness. If, then, the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Jesus Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^13]. The body, then, is dead because of sin; since it has the necessity of death even in the living. And what sin, but the first man's? Since through the righteousness of the second man, that is, Christ, to the same body which is now called dead, a blessed making-alive is to come. Whence Christ was called both the second man and the second Adam; although between Adam, made a man, and Christ, born a man, we see so many generations of human beings to have passed, nor in their order could anyone be called the second man, except Cain; but because the first is the death of the body, which came about because of Adam's sin, in which this age runs its course; the second is the life of the body, which will come about because of Christ's righteousness, and has already come about in the flesh of Christ, in which life the age to come will abide; therefore he was called the first, either Adam or man, but this one the second. And do you refuse to understand that the sin of the former was so great that it propagated an age of mortals; but the righteousness of the latter so great that it propagated an age of immortals? And do you object to me the greatness of the first man's sin, which was the cause of so great an evil of all human beings, as though I alone or first said this? Hear John of Constantinople, a priest of excellent glory: *Adam*, he says, *committed that great sin, and condemned the whole human race in common*[^14]. Hear too what he says at the raising of Lazarus, that you may understand that even the death of the body came from that great sin: *Christ wept*, he says, *because the devil made those mortal who could have been immortal*[^15]. Where, I ask you, did the devil make all human beings mortal, except in him on whom he inflicted, by transgression, so great a sin, by which the human race was cast from the blessedness of paradise into so great a misery as we see and feel? Which not only the death of the body, but also of the soul itself, which the corruptible body weighs down, is attested by so many and so great evils, and the heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^16]; under which yoke is also that which is read in the Psalm: *Every living man is altogether vanity*[^17]. These evils you, unwilling to attribute to that great sin of the first man, what do you do, except either bring them into the paradise of so great a felicity—as though they would be there too, if no one had sinned—while the Manichees attribute them to the race of darkness, not confounded by your accusing, but emboldened by your helping, unless they be transfixed along with you, as if by a most unconquerable sword, by Catholic truth? But we do not say, as you allege: *To no one born of the stock of the first human beings is it possible to strive after the beauties of the virtues*. For many strive, in whom God works also to will; nor, with him helping, do they strive without the fruit of attainment. But if the corruptible body did not weigh down the soul, they surely would not have to strive. And through this, in paradise (if no one had sinned, and there were no heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam) they would obey their God easily and happily, without striving.

**VI. 8.** *Julian:* He thinks, then, that the praise of those first human beings, that is, of only two, aids him toward maintaining the distinction between the Manichees and the Traducianists; than which opinion hardly anything either more eminent or more shameless can be found. The liberty, he says, of choice, after it began to use itself, lost its strength. And, that, turning to him, we may search through everything step by step; you confess, surely, that the first man was made of free choice, and founded good by God, polluted by no taint of sin from the beginning; but that, of an innocent condition, having afterward of his own accord transgressed, he brought an unavoidable compulsion of sinning upon all who arise from him. This is certainly your dogma, which we attest is expressed from the mire of the Manichee, who supposes that even the nature of Adam himself—although compounded of the flower of the first substance, far better than those who followed—is yet naturally evil.

*Augustine:* What was said above sufficiently indicates both our Catholic dogma and your heretical dogma concerning the first human beings and their descendants: those founded upright by God, but these, although by the same founder, yet arising, from a nature vitiated by sin, with the bond of sin, and cast down, by the condition of their origin, from the salvation in which the human being was first made, into the weakness of contagion and the necessity of death. On which account they need the help of the Savior, who first makes safe by the forgiveness of all sins; afterward also by the healing of all weaknesses[^18]. For to those already baptized and to those who had already received the Holy Spirit the Apostle said: *The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other; that you may not do the things you wish*[^19]. Where you who deny that free choice lost its strength by sinning, that is, by using itself badly, what will you answer, hearing that, with the flesh lusting against the spirit, the faithful too do not do the things they wish, even those whose sins were forgiven in Baptism do not do the things they wish; even those whom the Apostle says received the Holy Spirit from the hearing of faith[^20] do not do the things they wish; finally, those whom the same Teacher of the Gentiles says were called in liberty[^21] do not do the things they wish through free will? Then, you yourself, so eloquent a defender of lust, who, like a distinguished patron, dare so much on behalf of your protégée that you do not hesitate that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which it lusts against the spirit, existed even in paradise before sin; do you not see that you are compelled to this: to say that not even then was there an effective free will in those first human beings? For if even then the flesh lusted against the spirit, they surely did not do the things they wished. But since, through free choice, which then had the most unimpaired strength, they did beyond doubt the things they wished—that is, they served the divine law not only with no impossibility, but even with no difficulty—your protégée was not there, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit; through which it comes about that human beings, now even converted to God through faith, now baptized, sanctified, called into liberty, do not, in extinguishing a vicious delight, do the things they wish. And that is most true which, through the bishop Ambrose, the Catholic faith said: that this fault, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, passed over into our nature through the transgression of the first man[^22]. By which unavoidable and insuperable dart of truth, both the Manichee and you are cut down. For both of you in this matter—see which of you the worse—yet altogether err; you for this reason, because you contend that this pestilence is not an evil; but he, because he indeed recognizes the evil, but does not know whence it is, and, destitute of the Catholic faith, composes a fable full of lies and shameful things, about the commingling of two natures, namely of good and of evil. Now, then, this is our righteousness: that, justified by faith, we have peace with God[^23]; but against the concupiscence of the flesh assailing us, by the help of God himself, with the spirit lusting in opposition, we fight. It is not, then, the righteousness of this life to have no fault; but to lessen the faults by not consenting to them, and, by resisting them, to live temperately and justly and piously. But to have no fault to resist is of the later life, which is the reward of the present one well lived; by the healing of our own nature, not (as the Manichee, whose helper you are, madly holds) by the separation of an alien one. Behold, our dogma is not expressed from the mire of the Manichee, as you charge; by which dogma, if you have not lost all sense, you see the Manichee crushed along with you.

**VI. 9.** *Julian:* For us, then, at present, the dullness of your understanding must first be convicted; then it must be shown, as we have often already done, that you do not depart even by one foot from the hovels and brothels of the Manichees. At the outset, then, it is utterly senseless to think that the business of choice is mixed into the seeds, and that the offices of wills have grown into conceptions; so that, the clearest and vastest distinction of natures and pursuits being removed, the will of the first human beings is said to have engendered itself into their descendants. Against which error everything in reality struggles. For never have the sons of the eloquent brought forth the charm of their father's art in their wailings, or has the offspring of actors stretched out its hands to words with learned movements; nor have the sons of warriors demanded the trumpet of the people. One may, in this way, pursue examples clearer than thunder, harmonious in agreement. The whole world will answer that the limits of nature are one thing, those of pursuit another; and that the conditions of seeds cannot be open to the appetites of pursuits. Most slow-witted, then, and of an abject stupidity it appeared, to believe that what you confess to have been voluntary was turned into nature. But that other thing is far and away more deformed: that you say the possibility of working was assumed at the beginning of working; that is, that free choice (which is nothing other than the possibility of sinning and not sinning, subject to the violence of neither part, but having the faculty of standing by its own judgment on whichever side it wills), after it began to will one of the two, lost the power of both.

*Augustine:* Do you really not see that you support the Manichee—ignorantly indeed, yet earnestly—with this inflated and foaming din of your wordiness? For if, when he asks us whence evil comes, by which question they are wont to disturb untaught hearts, we answered that it arose from the free will of the rational creature; and he said: Whence, then, are these so many evils, which befall, not those already born and using the choice of the will as age advances, but with which all, or very many, are born? For congenital to all is the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, even in one imbued with right faith and the teaching of piety; congenital to all is also a certain slowness of mind, by which even those who are called talented yet learn, not without some toil and trouble, whether any skills, or even those called liberal, or attain a richer knowledge of religion itself. Some too are born with a deformed and sometimes monstrous body, many forgetful, many slow and dull at understanding, many irascible, many lustful, some even altogether senseless and foolish. What else would the Catholic faith answer, except that all these evils, from the time the human being sinned and was cast out of paradise, that is, the place of felicity, arise from a nature vitiated by the contagion of sin? For neither, if no one had sinned, would either such things, or any other faults, be born in paradise. Hearing which, he would recite to us your words, if he had them, where you say: *That it is utterly senseless to think the business of choice mixed into the seeds, and the offices of wills to have grown into conceptions*; and whatever else you added to this sentiment, trying to prove it from this, that neither the eloquent are born sons of the eloquent, nor actors of actors, nor warriors sons of warriors. By this help of yours the Manichee uses this to assail what we say: that by the sin of the first man human nature was vitiated, even in his descendants, who were in him by seminal principle, when he sinned in that great transgression; and when he has assailed this, he introduces the commingling of his two natures, and asserts that from the commingling of the evil nature those evils with which human beings are born exist. But you, to resist me, are compelled to say a most absurd and most detestable thing: namely that these evils of those being born would have arisen even in paradise, if no one had sinned. Here the Manichee will press you, to say whence they would have arisen. Where, hemmed in by great straits, if you say that these evils would have arisen from the very natures of those being born, without the merit of any will, you will surely charge the Creator; and lest you do this, you will flee to the merits of evil wills. But he will ask: Whose? For there is no will of seeds, or of little ones being born. What, then, will remain, except that you understand with us—if you wish to escape or overcome the Manichee—that in the hidden recesses of origins are latently entangled even the seeds of those being born, and the merits coming from the evil will of those begetting; but that the sin of the first man was so great that it condemned the whole human race in common, to use the words of holy John[^24]? From which it is concluded that these evils would not have arisen, if no one had sinned; nor could they have been in paradise, from which those who had sinned departed before they begot. By which Catholic dogma is emptied out what you thought should be subjoined about the arts, reminding us that no one is born with his father's art. For it is one thing to sin in morals, by which one lives rightly, which is wont to be punished either by the laws or by the divine judgment; another to sin in arts, whether they be honorable or base, where something is said to be done against the art; which sins are neither reprehended by the divine law, nor punished with severity; but by those human beings to whose judgments these things are subject, and especially by the masters of them, when they teach boys, under fear or pain of punishments. In which matter, however, we ought to consider that, if in paradise anything were learned which it was useful for that life to know, the blessed nature would attain it without any toil or pain, either with God teaching, or by itself. Whence who does not understand that, in this life, even the torments of those learning pertain to the miseries of this age, which was propagated from one into condemnation[^25]? Where this too is wretched: that wretched minds are unwilling to do what is good; or, if the will is already prepared by the Lord[^26], that he who lives in this age yet still cries out: *To will is present with me; but to accomplish the good, I do not find*[^27]. If you hold this, you will conquer the Manichees; but because you do not hold it, this faith conquers both of you.

**VI. 10.** *Julian:* Now, then, let what we said be approved: that your dogma differs in nothing from the Manichees'. It is altogether beyond doubt that the very nature of Adam was made the worst, if it was formed on this condition, that it had the necessity of evil, which it did not have of good; that is, that crime, even if it had been conceived by will, yet became natural in it, in which goodness did not become natural; and it is falsely said that he sinned by will, who labored under the prejudgment of a most wicked condition. For it appears how bound by evil he was, who was to be stuffed with an inseparable crime. For what worse substance could I find than one which was made so that it could fall into iniquity, and could not withdraw from iniquity? If it had suffered such violence on the side of good, if it had lost free choice, yet it would have accused its author in nothing; because no one would raise a question with it about the resources of his kindness; but when on the side of evil such domination is set up, it charges no one more than the very founder of the human being, and such a God is flattered by the superfluous adulation of his accusers, that is, of you, who, most friendly to malice, is convicted by the foulness of his creation. For who can be persuaded that he did not destine the first man to crimes, if he deprived him of the faculty of emendation; if he affected him with so depraved a mind that his error could not displease him, that he had no return to honorableness, nor became more corrected after his experiences, and—lest perhaps he should ever feel any affection for recovering uprightness—tore out from him the very possibility of correction? Indeed, if his condition was such that, as long as he was in this life, by one fall he lost the strength of emendation, he was established for no other reason than to fall; nay, it is more truly said that he never fell at all, but always lay prostrate, who, as far as morals are concerned, is not allowed to rise. What sort, then, was that liberty which is believed first conferred on him, if of two contrary qualities he took the worse from necessity, the better from the changeable; nay, occupied by the tyranny of crime, he was despoiled of the faculty of coming to his senses? Most foul, then, was the state of the first man from the very beginning, if he was so unhappily established by God that, about to fall into crime, he was bound by a perpetual necessity of sinning.

*Augustine:* You say things which, if you do not neglect to consider them when we at least admonish you, you would, however shameless you are, blush at even before yourself. For why do you not attend that, if the nature is made worst which by an unjust will runs into evil, but by a just penalty cannot run back into good, then not only the human nature you object to us, but the angelic nature too was made worst? Unless perhaps you will say that the devil too, fallen by will from good, will return, if he wills and when he wills, into the good he forsook, and you will set up for us the error of Origen. But if you do not do this, then correct, being admonished, what you said incautiously; and confess that the nature was founded good, which fell into the evil it did, compelled by no necessity, but collapsed by its own will. But into the good it left, it can be recalled only by the grace of God, not by the will of liberty, which it lost by the merit of iniquity. For another too, erring like you, can say: What worse substance could I find than one which was made so that it could go into eternal punishment, and from there could not return? And surely God almighty can rescue from punishment whom he wills; but he cannot lie, who threatened that he would not do this, when he called that punishment eternal. But that you may think emptily about this matter, your definition deceives you, by which, in the prosecution above, to which we have already answered—as you often do elsewhere too—you defined free choice. For you said: *Free choice is nothing other than the possibility of sinning and not sinning*. By which definition you first took away free choice from God himself, whom you do not deny—because you both often say this, and it is true—cannot sin. Then the saints themselves will lose free choice in his kingdom, where they will not be able to sin. But here you must be admonished what you ought to think about this matter now in hand: namely that punishment and reward must be regarded as contraries to each other, and that two other contraries cleave to these contraries; so, then, in the punishment is the inability to act rightly, as in the reward will be the inability to sin. Attend to the Scriptures, from which, to be pitied, you wander off, and, a vagabond, are tossed by windy wordiness as if by a storm; and see how it is said: *What Israel sought, this it did not obtain; but the election obtained it: the rest, however, were blinded; as it is written: God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that they should not see, and ears that they should not hear, to this very day. And David says: Let their table become a snare, and a trap, and a stumbling block, and a recompense to them; let their eyes be darkened that they may not see, and bend their backs forever*[^28]. Look too at that in the Gospel: *For this reason*, he says, *they could not believe, because Isaiah said again: He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart, lest they see with their eyes, and understand with their heart, and turn, and I heal them*[^29]. These things I have mentioned that you may understand, if you can, that by a punishment beyond doubt just it comes about that human beings do not believe with a blinded heart; while by mercy it comes about that they believe by free will. For who does not know that no one believes except by the free choice of the will? But the will is prepared by the Lord; nor is it at all rescued from the evil servitude due to its merits, except when by gratuitous grace it is prepared by the Lord. For if God did not make the unwilling willing, surely we would not pray for those who are unwilling to believe, that they may be willing. Which the Apostle showed that he did for the Jews, where he says: *Brothers, the good will of my heart, and my prayer to God for them, is for salvation*[^30]. This salvation they surely could not obtain except by a believing will; this, then, blessed Paul prayed that they might will. And the bishop Cyprian understands what is said in the Lord's Prayer, *Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven*[^31], in this sense too, that we have been admonished to pray for our unbelieving enemies; that, as we believe, who are now heaven, by bearing the image of the heavenly man, so they too may believe, who are therefore earth, because they bear only the image of the earthly man[^32].

**VI. 11.** *Julian:* A covenant remains, surely, between you and the Manichees, that you—they by profession, you by argument—assert that the nature even of the first man was evil; which, although it is wholly full of follies and lies (to pass over the legions of the saints, let us seize the first example of righteousness after the sin of Adam), is refuted by the marvelous holiness of Abel, who, born of sinners, showed that he did not lack the virtue of living well, by the very effect of that virtue; yet, these things passed over, it helps to press the opinions of the Traducianist nation. What sort, then, do you maintain was the free choice which you confess was conferred on the first human beings? Surely such that the motions of the mind could alternate, and either do evil, or withdraw from evil, either forsake or keep righteousness. The will of sinning, then, would not have been, unless the possibility of willing had gone before. After they began to use this by their own will, that is, by a motion of the mind with nothing compelling, you say free choice perished; than which what can be thought more frenzied? For, that the force of your argument may be held expressed, you say that thing perished on account of will, which was given only on account of will; for sin is nothing other than an evil will; but liberty was given for this alone, that it should not compel, but permit, the will to arise; and this liberty you say lost its condition by the work of the will, so that it is believed to have perished through the very thing by which alone it is proved to flourish. The evil will, then, is not indeed the fruit, but the testimony of liberty. But liberty is nothing other than the possibility of good and evil, but voluntary. How, then, could it perish through that by which it is shown to have been established, when an evil will and a good will are not the destructions, but the heralds of liberty? And through this, as far as offices differ from distinctions, so far does your opinion differ from the rationale of free choice, for which you think a destruction has been prepared out of its own praises. What new, what unhoped-for thing, then, happened when the human being transgressed, by which God's institutions collapsed? He was made one who could sin and not sin; when he sinned, he did what he indeed ought not, yet could; through what, then, would he lose that faculty, which had been established for this, that he could will, could not-will, what he willed?

*Augustine:* You say the same things again and again, to which whoever reads will see that I have already answered above. But here too, to you persisting and asserting that the liberty of acting well or badly cannot perish by its own bad use, let the blessed Pope Innocent also answer, bishop of the Roman Church; who, writing back in your case to the African episcopal councils, says: *That man, once enjoying free choice, while he uses his goods too inconsiderately, falling, plunged into the depths of transgression, found no way by which he could thence rise; and, deceived forever of his liberty, he would have lain crushed by this ruin, had not the coming of Christ afterward, by his grace, raised him up*[^33]. Do you see what the Catholic faith holds through its minister? Do you see that the human being had the possibility of standing and falling in such a way that, if he had fallen, he would not rise by the same possibility by which he had fallen, namely with punishment following the fault? On which account the grace of Christ, to which you are wretchedly ungrateful, came to raise up the one lying prostrate. In another letter too, which he wrote back about you yourselves to the Numidians: *Therefore*, he says, *they try to take away the grace of God, which it is necessary that we seek, even when the liberty of our former state has been restored to us*[^34]. You hear that liberty is restored, and you contend it did not perish; and, content with the human will, you do not ask for divine grace, which our liberty, even restored to its former state, understands to be necessary to it. But I ask you whether the liberty of him who says, *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*; and: *To will is present with me, but to accomplish the good I do not find*[^35]; and to whom it is said: *The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; that you may not do the things you wish*[^36]—has now been restored to its former state. I think you are not so foolish as to say there is in these the liberty of the former state; and yet, if there were none in them, they could not even will what is holy and just and good. For there are those whom it so delights to sin that they are unwilling and hate righteousness; which no one can even will, unless the will is prepared by the Lord, so that for accomplishing righteousness the desire of the will may go before, and gradually the effect of power accrue—to some sooner, to others later, to each as the Lord has given, who alone can repair the salvation of the human being, and increase it once lost, and bestow also this, that it can no longer perish. In which number of the freed is also holy Abel, who, you say, did not lack the virtue of living well. He did not lack it plainly, but after it began to be in him; before, however, who is *clean from filth? Not even an infant whose life on earth is of one day*[^37]. They are redeemed, then, whoever are redeemed, by him who came to seek what had perished, who, even before he came in the flesh, redeemed by that faith by which he was believed to be coming. And they are redeemed into the everlasting liberty of blessedness, where they can no longer be slaves to sin. For if, as you say, the possibility of voluntary good and evil alone is liberty, then God does not have liberty, in whom there is no possibility of sinning. But if we seek the congenital and altogether unlosable free choice of the human being, it is that by which all wish to be blessed, even those who do not wish the things that lead to blessedness.

**VI. 12.** *Julian:* Then, to follow the precipices of your opinion, you argue that free choice was so ordained that it would lose its force by the merit of the will that followed, and would for the remainder sustain the necessity of the quality it had chosen. From this, then, attend to what is reported by us. Do you think the human being was so created that he would suffer this necessity of the chosen quality on either side, that is, that if he had willed good, he could no longer sin; and if he had chosen evil, he could no longer correct himself? Or that the necessity of evil should follow only the evil part, but on the good part nothing of the sort should happen, but it should always lie open to the perils of variations? Take whichever you please of these two: if you say a nature was made such that it suffered only the necessity of evil, no doubt will remain that it is defined most foul, to which the violence of the worse part alone is attributed; and it is proved that Adam too was of an evil nature; and there will remain no shadow of anything voluntary committed, under which you may lurk. But if you profess that the same would have followed on the good part too, that is, that if he had willed good, he could no longer sin; I report: Why, then, did he sin? Why did he suffer no necessity of good, so as to appear impenetrable to the snares of the devil—he who is found to have been obedient to God for some time before he sinned? For it was not immediately, when the clay grew warm by the entrance of the soul, that he was inflamed into the appetite of an evil will. Rather, it is read that, having been transferred to the cultivation and keeping of paradise, he received a precept from God, that he should eat of all the fruits, but abstain, obedient to the word, from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Before, then, the body of his wife was formed from his side, he remained obedient to the command, an innocent cultivator of the pleasant countryside; but after this, he earned the help of a consort like himself. The trustworthiness of Scripture, surely, has driven home these distinctions of times. But after he now saw the woman prepared for him, he served the precept of God with so diligent a duty that he even intimated to the woman the law which he had received. Not only the keeper, then, but also the preacher of the divine command, he thoroughly taught Eve both the reverence of the one commanding, and the kind of service, and the cause of fear. Hence it is that the woman too, when the devil approached, struggled anxiously—she to whom God had commanded nothing—and at the very first refuted the lies of the serpent, and says, not abstinence from all the trees, as he had feigned, but, with permission of all fruitfulness, the prescribed caution about one little tree only, and the fear of death set before her, which would justly follow transgressors. Not for a short time, then, does it appear that Adam kept the command; but also that Eve had a care of devotion, who collapsed through the love of knowledge and divinity. Why, then, did that righteousness, that devotion, which flourished long in Adam, for some time in Eve, not snatch away the possibility of sinning, so that the necessity of good should render them impenetrable to guilty persuasions? They were, then, obedient as long as they willed, nor yet did they lose, by the merit of devotion, the faculty of transgressing; finally, they fell[^38]; therefore, even after they sinned, it is manifest that they could in no way lose the strength of correction. And so, as in all places, here too you have lost the whole of what you had feigned; because that sin of the first human beings neither made a necessity of any crimes, nor passed into nature; just as the righteousness that preceded neither brought a necessity of virtues, nor claimed for itself the ways of the seeds.

*Augustine:* The whole of what you said so long and with so great a perplexity of words can be said briefly thus. Why, you say, did Adam, by acting badly, lose the ability to act well, and, by acting well first, did not lose the ability to sin? And you wish it understood that, if this is so, the human being was made of a nature not good, but evil, in which the evil action availed more, to make him unable to act well, than the good action, to make him unable to act badly. You could in this way say that the human being was made badly with eyes, because, when he has put them out, it comes about in him that he cannot see; but by seeing it was not brought about in him that he could not put them out; or that the whole body of the human being was founded badly, because he has it in his power to kill himself, and does not have it in his power to raise himself up again; and it comes about in him through death that he cannot bring himself to life, but it does not come about in him through life that he cannot kill himself. But if you do not say this, since you see how insipid it is, why do you say God created the nature of the human being evil, if an evil will brought it about in him that he could not return to good, although a good will did not bring it about in him that he could not go into evil? For he was created with free choice in such a way that he could not sin, if he willed not to; not that, if he willed, he could sin with impunity. What wonder, then, if, by transgressing—that is, by changing, by depravation, the uprightness in which he was made—it followed, with punishment, that he could not act rightly? But as long as he stood in the same uprightness, in which he could not sin, he did not for this reason receive something greater, that is, the inability to sin, because in what he had he was unwilling to remain to the end of reward. For what the saints are to receive, who in the age to come will be in a spiritual body, Adam would have received without the intervention of death, so that, ascending from being able not to die, he would arrive where he could not die; and ascending from being able not to sin, he would arrive where he could not sin. For he was made not in a spiritual body, but in an animal one, although, unless he had sinned, by no means destined to die. Because, as the Apostle says: *Not first what is spiritual, but what is animal; afterward the spiritual*[^39]. Whence blessed Ambrose says that Adam was made in the shadow of life, from which he could fall, not by necessity, but by will[^40]. In which, surely, if he had remained, he would receive that life of which it was the shadow, which the saints are to receive, from which they can in no way fall. This mortality too, in which this age runs its course, he understands on the contrary as the shadow of death; but the death of which this is the shadow, he mentions as that which is called the second death[^41], from which no one, when he has come into it, will return. But whoever are freed from this shadow of death are prepared not to return to the shadow of life, but to go to that life from which no one can ever depart. There Adam himself too will be; for it is rightly believed that, by the Lord's coming and descent into hell, he was already loosed from the bonds of hell; so that the first work fashioned by God, who had no begetter, but only God as creator, and the first father, according to the flesh, of Christ, should not remain longer in those bonds, nor perish by eternal punishment. But where mercy exalts itself above judgment[^42], merits are not to be thought of, but grace: whose depth is so great and so inscrutable and unsearchable that, after that sentence by which it was said, *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of God*[^43], we see sometimes that to well-deserving faithful their own children are not granted, that they may be in the kingdom of God with their parents; but unregenerate little ones depart from this life, while sometimes—even with their own parents ardently desiring it, and the ministers of the Sacraments eagerly hastening—the death of these little ones is not put off even a little by the most omnipotent and most merciful God, so that the children of Christians might depart hence reborn, and not perish from the kingdom of Christ and from their own begetters; but before they are baptized, they breathe their last; while sometimes to the infant children of unbelievers, even of those blaspheming the grace of Christ, brought into the hands of Christians by God's marvelous governance, this grace is granted, that they may come into the kingdom of God, separated from their impious parents. Where, if it be asked of you what this justice is, surely in that dialectical and philosophical discourse, in which you seem to yourself to have most diligently disputed about the justice of God, you do not find it. For the Lord knew the thoughts of the wise, that they are vain[^44]; and hiding these things from the wise and prudent, he revealed them to little ones[^45], that is, to the humble, and to those trusting not in their own virtue, but in the Lord; which you either never, or not yet, deign to be. If, then, you seek where or when it is given to the human being to be unable to sin, seek the rewards of the saints, which they must receive after this life. But if you do not believe that the free choice of the human being, by which he could and ought to have acted rightly, failed by the malice of sin, at least attend to him who says: *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do*[^46]; whom you are unwilling should labor under a vitiated origin, but under a prevailing evil habit; and so you too confess that free choice, by using itself badly, can fail; and you are unwilling that, by that so great sin, which was greater and worse than every evil habit, free choice could be vitiated in human nature; in the depraving of which you say that an evil habit alone has such power that the human being cries out that he wills to accomplish good, and cannot. But the unchangeable liberty with which the human being was created and is created is that liberty of the will by which we all wish to be blessed, and cannot not wish it; but this does not suffice for anyone to be blessed, nor to live rightly by which he is blessed; because the unchangeable liberty of the will by which one wills and is able to act well is not so congenital to the human being as is congenital that by which he wills to be blessed; which all will, even those who are unwilling to act rightly.

**VI. 13.** *Julian:* What, then, has been accomplished? That one of two things is necessary: that either you confess Adam was made of a good substance, and his nature not destroyed by a voluntary quality, and you abandon natural sin; or, if you contend that he, as you have hitherto opined, is the cause of natural evils, you pronounce that he too is of a most evil substance, and belongs to your God, that is, yours and the Manichee's.

*Augustine:* That what you think has been accomplished has not been accomplished, our earlier answer declares. For when the question was turned between us, whether by the bad use of the free choice with which the human being was created, this liberty could be vitiated, so that he who lived badly would not be fit for living well, unless healed by the power of grace—to pass over the rest, which was said much in that same answer—we found a human being saying, on the supreme authority of the divine Scriptures: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*. In which words it evidently appears that free choice has been vitiated by its own bad use. For not before sin, by which the human being used free choice badly, when he was established in that felicity of paradise and in great ease of acting well, could he say this. But you attribute this, not to the human nature vitiated in the first man, but to the evil habit of each one, which, prevailing over him, the human being, willing but not able to conquer, and not finding his liberty whole for accomplishing good, is compelled to say these things; as though, indeed, he suffered an insuperable force of evil habit, so that he asks to be freed from it by the grace of God, unless the nature were weakened. For he who said these things, when he had reached the point where he says: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive under the law of sin which is in my members. Wretched man that I am*, he says, *who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^47]. Interpret the body of death as you wish: yet, with free choice weakened, with nature weakened, he said these things, and desired, through the grace of God, to be freed from the body of death, where he did not do the good he willed, but did the evil he hated. But you are conquered by a more evident proof, that the sin of the first man was so great that it was greater and worse than all the violence of habit, when there are brought forward to you the evils of children, which surely would not have been in paradise, if, so as not to be driven from it, the human nature had remained in that felicity of uprightness in which it was made. For, to be silent about such things, which we have already said in several places, and to pass over not only the untaught but even the unteachable infancy; would not a boy, receiving from a teacher something to retain in memory, willing to hold it and not able, if he could speak, say most truly: "I see another law in my soul, fighting against the law of my will, and taking me captive under the law of the rods that hangs over my members? *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? For the corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^48], so that what it wishes to hold in memory, it cannot." And from this corruptible body who frees, except the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord? Whether when, stripped of this body, the soul redeemed by the blood of Christ rests, or when *this corruptible shall put on incorruption*[^49], and, after the troubles of the body dead because of sin, our mortal bodies too shall be made alive through the Spirit of Christ dwelling in us. Against whose grace you defend the free choice of the will, the will that is the handmaid of sin. But we are far from the Manichee, who confess both the fault of a good nature, whether in the grown or in little ones, and the physician.

**VI. 14.** *Julian:* I have disputed thus far as the manner of our faith demanded; but, not content with this part alone, I will deal kindly with you, and deliberately put on the persona of one who seems to favor the opinion of your preceptor. But this will be done for this reason, that you may be compelled to assail the Manichee, if you disagree with him. For it will be shown by this very thing that there is no craftiness in our assertion, when the Traducianist does not find a way to resist the Manichee. And so it will appear how great a conspiracy there is between you, how you procure all things by mutual gains, since a quarrel could not be stirred up between you. By what counsel, then, I have shown that I have seized upon this, let it sit in the mind of the reader; now let us discharge the discourse of the persona we assume. They err altogether who think this concretion of the body fit for righteousness; the base nature of flesh and blood is opposed to all good pursuits. Whatever there is of incitements by the allurement of the senses slides toward the perturbation, nay, the overthrow of the mind, which, cast down into this mire by some unhappiness, loses its noble flashings by the muddy admixture. It strives, as far as in it lies, toward its own place, that is, toward higher things; but it is afflicted by its earthly prison-house. Finally, when it wishes to fly up to chastity, it experiences the glue and birdlime of obscene pleasure from its scorched bowels. Now if it longs for the liberality of munificence, it is bound by the tightest manacles of avarice, which is covered under the garb of frugality. But indeed if it wishes to stand in a certain even serenity of constancy, it is at once overwhelmed by the hail of fear, and the storms of grief, and, growing bloodless at all uncertainties, is not allowed to remain master of its own counsel. Add to this the night of unknown things, by which it is surrounded as by filth. What praiseworthiness shall we suspect in that living being, which neither has vigorous eyes for the choosing of useful things, and amid the tempests of desires and dangers does not suffice to number its own shipwrecks? Nor yet let anyone falsely say that these things happened by the free will of a depraved substance; the very establishment of the first human beings is found liable to these troubles. For, that this too may be proved by the testimony of Moses, whom the Catholics venerate, the first human beings experienced the butchery of fear, whom the threatening of danger, unless they obeyed, terrified; and, as far as is to be measured from the comparison of things, we suppose them to have been more fearful than their descendants, whom a punishment not yet known to anyone vexed. For why would they be shaken by the threatening of death, who did not know what harm destruction brought? They were assuredly troubled by mere suspicions of evils. In what tranquillity, then, would that mind stand, which so harsh a winter of dread agitated? Moreover, how deep the ignorance, and how hard its condition of suffering, that it could not be freed from it except by transgression; since he would by no means take hold of the knowledge of good and evil without a condemnable daring. This animal so blind and troubled, you will say, an innate desire too rendered restless, which the very beauty and sweetness of the forbidden thing irritated. All of which, as though it were too little to express his unhappiness, is exposed to the assault of a higher nature. Who, then, would be so foolish as to think there was anything good there, where he confesses so many instruments of miseries were established? Of a most evil condition, then, and a most evil nature, this flesh in these first human beings put forth what it held from the beginning. But this so evil a substance the good God could not have fabricated. What, then, remains, except that we confess that one is the giver of the soul, another the founder of the clay? The battle line of the Manichee, whose persona I had put on, stands surely arrayed. What we await, you understand: namely that he who is opposed to him should, by a consequent assault, demonstrate it. Let your dogma, then, contend with him; it will appear whether, without its own ruin, it can be moved even slightly. He has pronounced, surely, that not only are all born criminal from the commingling of bodies, but that Adam himself brought the necessity of faults from the concretion of his bowels, and from the filth of the clay of which he was made. The nature of the flesh, he says, in the first human beings was noxious; it shut in, drenched, extinguished the very spark of the mind shining faintly with the zeal of honorableness. The Catholics are altogether silly, who lean on the testimonies of sinners, and do not acquiesce in their own proofs, and, seeing that they do not do the good they will, but work the evil they execrate, yet think that there is no necessity of evil in the flesh. With these things, then, so invidiously said, let the Traducianist engage, if he can; meanwhile I stand by as a spectator, and await the outcome of your conflict. What, then, will you answer to one swearing that an evil nature was in the first human beings too? Without doubt you will report that God the formator of human beings could not found the evil which he created, and therefore, because God, who does not make evil, made human beings, they are by no means proved to be naturally evil. You have said something, and truly: but see whether you ought to have brought this forward with me hearing. For by what strength you have shaken the Manichee I care little; meanwhile you have crossed wholly into my jurisdiction. Now that you are captured, it delights me to mock you; for I receive your profession with great applause, and warn you to remember it. For by the dignity of the author, that is, of God, who does not make evil, you have pronounced that his works ought to be asserted good. All human beings, then, generated from the commingling of the sexes instituted by God—do you think they are made by God, or by the devil? If by God: how do you dare to pronounce them guilty and evil, when you have said the sole testimony is that Adam could not be made of an evil nature, because he is shown to be founded by God, who is best? If, then, it is perhaps an argument by which it is believed that the substance of the first human beings was not founded guilty—this very thing, that God founded it, whom we confess good—there remains, for the destruction of the propagation-theory, that it be proved by the same testimony that all begotten of marriage too cannot be founded wicked, because God founded them, whom we confess good. But if, even after this, your raging shamelessness should persist in swearing both that little ones are made by God, and yet that they are naturally evil; to the Catholic indeed, as also to our God, no prejudice is gained by these lies; yet it is established that the Manichee has not been assailed by you, who gladly embraces what you charge against God; content that this much has perished for you, by which you had striven to prove that Adam was of a good condition.

*Augustine:* When you propose to me a contest to be entered against the Manichee, and to yourself a contest to be watched, with eloquent blindness, you improvidently undermine your own side; and how, with the pestilential breath of your dogma, you aid that most foul plague which the deadly error of the Manichee brought in, you have made plain even to those human beings who are slowed by their wits, that they might be able to understand. For when anyone has heard or read those things which you said copiously and eloquently about the miseries of this mortal and corruptible life, he will recognize that you prosecuted true things, not only in your discourse, but also in human affairs themselves. For to the Manichee, to whom you spoke words as though speaking against us, it was nothing great or difficult to behold, in this mortal life—which both was cast out and cast down from the felicity of paradise by the merit of sin—those things which were said by you about it; and either as you, or more richly and copiously, yet to chatter those same things which are so manifest that they are even read in very many places of the divine Scriptures, coming from the burden of the corruptible body, and from the weighing-down of the soul that comes from it. Whence even in the saints, struggling in the contest of this life, *the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*[^50]; when the spirit, as the most glorious Cyprian says, seeks heavenly and divine things, the flesh desires earthly and worldly things[^51]. Hence is that conflict which the said martyr, in the book he wrote On Mortality, diligently and eloquently explains, saying among other things that we have a constant and troublesome struggle with carnal vices and worldly allurements[^52]. And indeed blessed Gregory sets this contest, which we have in the body of this death, so before our eyes that there is no athlete of this struggle who does not recognize himself in his words as in a mirror. *Within ourselves*, he says, *we are assailed by our own vices and passions, and day and night we are pressed by the fiery goads of this lowly body and of the body of death; now secretly, now even openly, with the allurements of visible things provoking and irritating us everywhere, this clay of dregs to which we have clung breathing out the stench of its mire through its wider veins; and with the law of sin, which is in our members, fighting against the law of the spirit, while it strives to lead captive the royal image which is within us; so that whatever it is that flowed into us by the benefit of the divine and first creation should yield as spoils to it*[^53]. These words of the man of God I set down both in the second book of those six which I published against your four[^54], and in this work, when I answered your first volume, where you thought the body of death, from which the Apostle says he is freed by the grace of God[^55], must be understood otherwise. And holy Ambrose, when he had said: *All human beings are born under sin; whose very origin is in fault, as you have read, David saying: "For behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^56]*,"* at once added: *Therefore Paul's flesh was a body of death, as he himself says: "Who will free me from the body of this death?*[^57]*"*[^58]. What wonder, then, if the Manichee, beholding the evils of this life, and the body of this death which weighs down the soul, and the discord of flesh and spirit, and the heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all[^59], says such things even through your mouth, as if against us, as we see Gregory said against you? Whence it is established that the evils of this life, which is a temptation upon earth[^60]—with which evils, in the human race, on account of the heavy yoke of the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all[^61], the world is full—both Manichees and Catholics confess; but whence these come, both do not say the same, and there is here a great difference between them; for the Manichees attribute them to an alien evil nature, but the Catholics to one both good and our own, but vitiated by sin and deservedly punished. You who are unwilling to say what we say, what do you yourself say? How do you answer the Manichee as to whence these evils come, with which human beings are born, and which would not be born in paradise if no one had sinned, and where our nature, not depraved, but upright, as it was founded, would remain? If the fault is congenital, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, and is not from a nature vitiated in its origin, say whence it is. If the fault is congenital, in which the human being cries out: *I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, the good; for to will is present with me, but to accomplish the good I do not find*[^62]; and is not from a nature vitiated by the transgression of the first man, say whence it is. But if these faults are not congenital, say whence they are. From the habit of sinning, you will say, which each one made for himself by free will. Where you confess, meanwhile, what you do not wish, namely that the liberty of the will could perish by its own bad use, because, by accomplishing evil, it was made less fit for accomplishing good. But is anyone dull of heart by will? Is anyone forgetful by will? Is anyone foolish by will? These and other faults of the mind itself and of the soul, with which no one doubts that human beings are born, if you say they are not from a vitiated origin, say whence they are. For you will not say that paradise, if no one had sinned, could have had these. Finally, that the corruptible body weighs down the soul—under which wretched burden all who are not utterly senseless groan—say whence it is. For you will not say that the first human beings were so created that the soul of any of them was weighed down by a corruptible body; or that, after that great sin of theirs, anyone is born without such a body. Why, then, do you, most wordy one, bring in the Manichee speaking against us, when you, by denying what we say, cannot answer him? To whom Cyprian answers, showing that flesh and spirit so disagree that yet the concord of both must be prayed for from God the Father[^63]; to whom Gregory answers, who, when he had said such things about the flesh as you composed for the Manichee to say against us, yet attests that both must be recalled to God, with God himself favorable, that is, spirit and flesh; to whom Ambrose answers, who, when he had said that the flesh must be joined to the arbitrament of a more moderate soul: *Such was it*, he says, *when it took upon itself to inhabit the secret places of paradise, before, infected by the venom of the pestilent serpent, it knew a sacrilegious hunger*[^64]. For by these sayings of theirs the Catholic bishops have taught clearly enough that the nature of the flesh is not evil, but its fault; which being healed, it returns to this, that, as it was first established, it weighs down the soul by no corruptions of its own, and has no discord with the spirit by lusting against it; by which discord the Manichee was deceived, so as to feign that an alien evil substance was commingled with us. If you were willing to follow with us the faith of these Catholic bishops, you would overthrow the Manichees, not aid them; but now you try not to destroy them, but rather to build them up; for by denying the evils which human beings being born draw from a vitiated origin, you do not bring it about that we believe there are no natural evils, since they are too manifest; but rather you bring it about that these are thought to be from an alien evil nature, which the madness of the Manichees fables to be commingled with us; and they are not shown to flow from our own good nature vitiated by the transgression of the first man, which the soundness of the Catholics declares. But the Manichee, you say, detests even the very flesh of the first man, such as it was before he sinned, so as to strive to show it evil. So indeed he is introduced by you as speaking, that he may force the business of answering, of whatever sort, not only upon me, but also upon you. For where he says it was made by an evil founder, we at once answer him that so good a creature, which could not sin if it willed not, although it is not equal to its Creator, yet could not but have a good Creator. But where, concerning the fear of death, with which, if he sinned, God threatened him, he calls him wretched even before he sinned, we at once answer that, for the human being who could never have sinned if he had never willed to sin, the avoidance of that punishment which would have followed sin was a tranquil caution, not a turbulent dread. This indeed we can answer the common adversary, as has been said, in common; but I, against the Manichee, increase the praises of that rational creature, which not only was tortured by no fear, but even enjoyed great gladness; since it had it in its power not to suffer the evil of death, which the hearts of all, or of almost all, the faithful shrink from. Since your error is opposed to this our faith—by which you think Adam, whether he sinned or did not sin, was to die—what do you here answer the Manichee saying that a wretched nature was founded, which, whether sinning or not sinning, impending death tortured with fear? For if you say it was so founded that it did not fear death, which would surely come at some time; you will surely confess that this nature, which is in his descendants, is born wretched, in which we see the fear of death so congenital that even those who, with faithful hope, long for the joys of the future life, yet in this life struggle with the fear of death; for they do not wish to be stripped, but to be further clothed, so that, as far as pertains to their will, this life may not be ended by death, but the mortal may be swallowed up by life[^65]. From which it comes about that, if you place the fear of death in paradise before sin, you are conquered by the Manichees, who think and wish it to be thought that, even in the first man, human nature was founded wretched; but if you answer that the fear of death, by which the mind of mortals is goaded not without misery, was not there before sin, you are conquered by us, since it would not be changed for the worse unless the nature were vitiated. Again, in that which you make the Manichee say against us—that an innate desire rendered that blind and troubled animal restless, which the very beauty and sweetness of the forbidden thing irritated—recognize, Julian, the shipwreck of your dogma, as if upon an unavoidable rock. For we say that in that blessedness there was no desire which resisted the will. Moreover, if those human beings desired what they rather wished to abstain from, beyond doubt desire resisted their will. This, then, you have made the Manichee say through your words, not against me, but against you. For if they were such that in them desire resisted the will, then already the flesh lusted against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; where the fault of the flesh is most manifestly understood, on account of which the Apostle said to the faithful: *For these are opposed to each other: that you may not do the things you wish*[^66]. For there is no one of the saints who does not wish to act so that the flesh may not lust against the spirit; although he resists it so as not to accomplish the concupiscence of the flesh by his consent, hearing the same Apostle, where he says: *But I say: Walk by the Spirit, and you will not accomplish the concupiscences of the flesh*[^67]. He does not say: Do not have the concupiscences of the flesh as adversaries; since he saw that perfect peace of flesh and spirit could not be fulfilled *in the body of this death*[^68]; but he says: *Do not accomplish the concupiscences of the flesh*; where he set before us rather a contest, which we ought to wage against the flesh as our adversary, so that we do not accomplish its concupiscences by consenting, but conquer them by resisting. But peace, where we do not suffer them as adversaries and opponents, was in the body of that life, which we lost by the nature vitiated through the transgression of the first man. For if even there, before sin, there was no peace of flesh and spirit, and it is false what Ambrose says, that the discord of both passed over into our nature through the transgression of the first man[^69]; then that sentence will be true—which God forbid—which you made the Manichee speak against us, namely that the first man was founded a wretched animal, whom an innate desire rendered restless, which desire the very beauty and sweetness of the forbidden thing irritated. But we say that that man before sin was so blessed, and of so free a will, that, observing God's precept with the great strength of his mind, he suffered the flesh resisting him in no contest, nor felt anything at all from any desire that he did not wish; and that his will was first vitiated by the venomous persuasion of the serpent, so that there arose a desire which would follow the will rather than resist the will; and, the sin being committed, then, as a punishment, the concupiscence of the flesh too fought against the weakened mind. And through this, unless the human being first did by sinning what he willed, he would not suffer by desiring what he did not will. Behold how we conquer the Manichee, trying to introduce an evil founder of the human being's nature; but you, who chose for yourself the place of a spectator while we contend, by your own arbitration—by these very words of yours, which you thought should be given to the Manichee against us—by what art, I ask, by what strength, will you dare to resist, you, a human being, who say that the concupiscence of the flesh, such as it now is, which we see fighting against the spirit, was such even in paradise before sin? We depose you, then, whether you will or not, from the seats of the theater into the arena, and from a spectator we make you a wrestler. Enter the contest, and conquer, if you can, the common adversary; since you too profess that you worship God the creator even of the flesh. Conquer, then, the enemy, trying to persuade that the God who founded the flesh is evil, whose concupiscence already resisted the spirit not yet depraved by transgression, and made the human being wretched by its conflict with him. Or will you say: He had also such a concupiscence, and yet was not wretched? Is this to overcome the adversary, or rather is this to aid the Manichee and to rebel against the Apostle? Have you so forgotten who it was that said: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*; and after such words subjoined: *Wretched man that I am*[^70]? If, then, Adam, when he willed to obey the divine precept, was irritated by desire to eat the forbidden food, and the concupiscence of the flesh, such as you say he had even then, resisted the spirit lusting in opposition, would he not say most truly, if he wished to say it: *I delight in the law of God according to the inner man; but I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*[^71]? How, then, was the man not wretched, when, after such words, the Apostle says: *Wretched man that I am*? Finally, how was he not wretched, and yet had a free will, if, with the flesh lusting against the spirit, by the testimony of the Apostle himself, he surely did not do what he willed? Since, then, if you say that the concupiscence of the flesh was, before sin, such as it now is, the Manichee will beyond doubt conquer you; cross over into my opinion, and let us both approve Ambrose saying that, through the transgression of the first man, the discord of flesh and spirit passed into nature, that we both may conquer the Manichee. Who, by your words, which you composed for him as though about to recite them, and, as is wont to be practiced in the schools of the rhetoricians, about to say things not his own, said that Adam was created not only troubled, but also blind; whence blind, except because he did not know sin?—which was said in the praises of Christ[^72]. For whatever evils are learned not through wisdom, but through experience, are happily not known. But this, against the Manichee slandering about the ignorance of the first man, you too perhaps say with me; but what you would answer him about the death of the body and about the concupiscence of the flesh, according to what we have now answered you, inquire. For by these two most manifest matters it appears that the condition of the first human beings, who were begotten of no parental stock, was one thing; but that of those who are so created by God that they are also procreated by human beings is another; for from him they receive the mode of their establishment, from these they draw the merit of their origin, owing their conformation to the work, their obligation to the judgment, their liberation to the benefit of the Creator. Whose evils, with which they are born, the Manichees, beholding, try to introduce an evil maker of the human being; whose maker is God (to be silent about the soul, which is the life of the flesh), from whom are all good things whether heavenly or earthly, as the very framework of the flesh attests; which is so great a good that the blessed Apostle took from the concord of its members a likeness for the chief praise of love, by whose peaceful bond among themselves the good faithful, as members of Christ, are knit together[^73]. So it comes about that both those first ones, made without fault, and their descendants, born with original fault, on account of the most evident good of nature, could not be created except by a good founder.

**VI. 15.** *Julian:* But lest we press the first part of the contest too hard, let us allow you, by the progress of the opinion, to prove Adam's nature good. You surely say: The just God would not set a law of devotion for the human being, if he knew him to suffer a necessity of sinning; because if he exacted the righteousness of the will from one whom he knew to be of an evil nature, then, when he transgressed, he would not be charging him as guilty, but publishing himself an enemy of righteousness. But the just God set a law for the human being, whom he pledged he would punish, if he transgressed. It is established, then, that he, being good by nature, could not sin except by will alone. Do you see, surely, how lawful is the conclusion brought forth by me in your name? It is altogether the very sword which, flashing in the hand of the Catholics, lays waste Manichees and Traducianists. But I added "too hard" on account of your name, in whose place I had for the present wished the answer to seem given. A solid answer, then, has shaken the Manichee. I follow up the august reasoning with worthy praises: but with the oil of this praise behold the sword sharpened against you. Repeat, then, I beg, what you had said. The just God, you say, would not set a law for the human being, if he were evil by nature; but he who is just set a law; it now appears that he could keep what the most equitable one commanded; since, unless he had the strength of obeying, there would never be a rationale of the command in the one commanding. O most charming little chapter! In my presence, with me watching, it affirms the goodness of the nature for which a law is set, from the justice of the one promulgating the law; nor does it see that, before the Manichee feels the little wound, it has brought destruction upon the Traducianists. For that you may understand that I snatch from you, half-dead, your bloody arms, and that your dying eyes may carry victorious truth[^74], I will drive you against your own weapons. If the just God could not give a law to Adam, unless he knew that he, free, without any compulsion of evil, could observe what is just; without doubt, in the times that followed too, with the same gravity of justice, a law—even handed down in writing, more diffuse by multiplication, more marked by distinction, and more reverend by increased punishment—would not have been given to human beings, if they were either feeble for doing good without the possibility of righteousness, or guilty, that is, of evil, born from the womb; since, as the pretext of necessity would excuse them in every transgression, so the immoderation of the precepts, the impotence of the sanctions, the iniquity of the judgments, would turn into a stain upon the author. Therefore this second part too is closed by the same kind as the first; that is, you must confess either that the justice of God could not command except what it weighed could be done by its subjects—and by the testimony of the first command the Manichee, but by the testimony of the laws afterward following the Manichee and the Traducianist, is consumed; or, if impiety leaves this aside, then the Manichee, not even lightly struck by you, will show, with the world as witness, that he is your father, your prince, and that with you he has a single contest against us.

*Augustine:* This is surely what you have prosecuted, not with eloquence, but with much speaking: namely that the earlier law, which was given in paradise, is a testimony of the good nature, which was founded with free choice; otherwise the law would be given most unjustly to a human being not having free choice. Whence also the later law, you say, which was most copiously promulgated in writing, is a testimony of the good nature, which is created from parents, likewise without fault, with free choice. Disputing these things, you seem to yourself to say something, because you follow either your own or human subtleties; but the divine words, from which you think to prescribe against us, you do not care to read; or, if you care to read, you do not wish, or are not able, to understand; but if perhaps you understand while we dispute, do not be such as the Scripture marks, saying: *A hard servant will not be corrected by words, for even if he understands, he will not obey*[^75]. Although the heart of stone too, by which the divine words even when understood are not obeyed, he can take from you, if he wills, who promised this to that hard people, with holy Ezekiel prophesying[^76]. For in paradise the human being received a law, who was made upright, that it might be taught that obedience is either the sole or the chief virtue of the rational creature. But by the transgression of that same law, he was made depraved by himself. And since he could be vitiated by himself, but not healed, afterward too, at the time and in the place where and when the wisdom of God judged it must be done, he received a law even when depraved—not one through which he could be corrected; but one through which he might feel that he was depraved, and that, even with the law received, he could not be corrected by himself; and so, with sins not ceasing by the law, but increasing by transgression, his pride being cast down and crushed, with a most humble heart he might desire the help of grace, and, slain by the letter, be made alive by the spirit. *For if a law had been given which could make alive, then surely righteousness would be from the law; but Scripture enclosed all things under sin, that the promise by faith of Jesus Christ might be given to believers*[^77]. If you recognize the words of the Apostle, you surely see either what you do not understand, or what, when you understand, you neglect. Not, then, is the law which was given in writing through Moses a testimony of free will; for, if it were so, he would not pertain to it who says: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I hate, that I do*[^78]; whom you certainly contend to be not yet under grace, but still under the law. Nor is the new law itself, which was foretold as coming forth from Zion, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem[^79]—which is understood to be the holy Gospel—nor is it, I say, a testimony of a free will, but rather of a will to be freed. For there it is written: *If the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^80]. Which is said not only on account of past sins, by whose forgiveness we are freed, but also on account of the help of grace, which we receive that we may not sin—that is, we are made free in such a way, with God directing our paths, that all iniquity may not have dominion over us[^81]—the Lord's Prayer attests, where we say not only: *Forgive us our debts*, on account of the evils we have done, but also: *Lead us not into temptation*[^82], on account of this surely, that we may not do evils; whence the Apostle too says: *But we pray to God that you do nothing evil*[^83]. But if this were so in our power, as it was before sin, before human nature was vitiated, it surely would not be sought by praying, but rather held by doing. But since, after the former ruin so grave that we fell into the misery of this mortality, God first willed us to contend, granting us to be led by his Spirit, and to mortify the works of the flesh, and, with him giving the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ, afterward to reign with him in eternal peace—surely, unless God is present, no one is fit to contend with vices; lest he be dragged off by them without a contest, or lest, already contending, he be conquered in the very conflict with them. Therefore in this struggle God willed us rather to contend by prayers than by strength; because even the strength itself, as much as it befits us to have here, he supplies to those contending, whom we ask. If, then, those whose spirit already lusts against the flesh need the grace of God for individual acts, lest they be conquered, what sort of liberty of the will can those have who, not yet rescued from the power of darkness, with iniquity ruling, have not even begun to contend; or, if they have willed to contend, are conquered by the servitude of a will not yet freed?

**VI. 16.** *Julian:* I do not indeed know whether here, now compelled by want, you contrive something so inept, so feeble, as to say that you can by no reasonings teach that Adam was founded good by God, but are content, for believing this, with the authority of the reading alone, by which, after the human being was formed, on the sixth day it is reported in common of all the creatures: *And God saw all that he made, and behold, it was very good*[^84]. Which, not for the dignity of the maker, not for the weighing of his justice, but for the testimony by which the things created are said in common to be well made, you would judge that Adam too was established not unjust. Moreover, although this strikes the Manichee so lightly that he laughs, yet it exposes the Traducianist to us bound. For, that we may gather peoples of no divine testimonies on this matter, we prescribe by the authority of the Apostle alone, who, when he foresaw your foul error, thundered against you with lofty mouth, saying: *For every creature of God is good*[^85]. If, then, for proving that the first man was formed of a good nature, this suffices which Moses says, that God founded all things well, and through this you contend that Adam could not be created by God with sin, because he is read to have been established good among the rest; by the same lines we will report that through this no one can be born with sin, since by the Apostle every creature of God is defended to be good. What, then, has been accomplished by these disputations? Namely, that what reason had tracked down, the choice of a battle proclaimed between you and the Manichee should also publish; so that, the contest being set, since you could not brandish even one weapon against your preceptor without your own ruin, it might shine forth much more manifestly that you and the Manichees have, by a base concord, grown together into one body of impiety. For what can be so joined as that which is not separated by the assault of dangers? With every destruction of the Manichee, the dogma of the Traducianists dies along with it; there is nothing that strikes him and leaves you. You have one set of institutions, one set of mysteries, and one set of perils; and do you grumble, if you be called the offspring of old Manes?

*Augustine:* You say that I can by no reasonings teach that Adam was founded good by God, as though I contend with you about that. Do not both I and you—not I alone, nor you, but both—say that Adam was founded good? For both of us say that the nature was good which could not sin if it willed not; but since I assert it better than you do, since I say that it could not even die if it had willed not to sin; what is it that you say, that Adam cannot be taught by me by any reasonings to have been founded good by God, when by my reasonings he is shown to be more good than by yours? For by mine he is shown to have been able not only not to sin, if he willed not, but also not to die, if he had willed not to sin; but by yours, made so mortal that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die. Which, when it had been objected to Pelagius in the episcopal judgment of Palestine, lest he be condemned, he himself condemned it[^86]; and he is condemned by himself, which the Apostle says of a heretical person[^87]. I say also that Adam did not fear death, since it was in his power not to die; but you say he had a necessity of dying, even without any necessity of sinning. If you say that he feared death even before sin, what do you say but that he was created wretched? But if, lest he be wretched, he did not fear death, although it was to come; surely his offspring, to whom he engendered the fear of death, he without doubt begot wretched. For who would deny that human beings so fear death by nature that a rare greatness of soul scarcely makes some not fear it? Likewise I too add to the goodness of Adam's condition, that in him the flesh did not lust against the spirit before sin; but you, who say that such concupiscence of the flesh as now exists would have been in paradise, if no one had sinned, and that there was such in him even before he sinned, add to his condition this misery too, through the discord of spirit and flesh. Since, then, by so many and so great reasonings I show Adam to have been founded better and happier than you say him to have been; what seemed good to you, to rave with such senselessness, that you would say: *that I can by no reasonings teach that Adam was founded good by God, but am content, for believing this, with the authority of the reading alone, because it is written that God made all things very good*? I am not so much duller than a pestle, as you revile, that, in refuting the Manichee, I would object to him the authority of this divine book, by which he is not bound. I object it to you, when the matter demands, since it is common to me and you. But I convince the Manichee that these creatures are good, not from God's making, which he himself denies; but rather I compel him, from their goodness, to confess that they have a good maker. But as for what the Apostle says, whom the Manichees profess to receive: *Every creature of God is good*—since it is plain of what creature he was speaking, it would be a strong testimony against them, were it not that they contend that certain false sentences have been mixed into the canonical Books too, which they receive. And through this they must always be pressed about the goodness of the creatures, that they may confess God, whom they deny, to be the good author of them. Moreover, all creatures are so good that even those which are created with faults, reason demonstrates to be good, by the attestation of the very faults; because a fault is against nature; for unless the nature itself rightly pleased, the fault of it would in no way rightly displease. This, against the Manichees, who think that even faults themselves are natures and substances, is disputed more copiously in certain little works of ours, and it is shown that a fault is not a nature; and that, because it is against nature, it is therefore evil; and through this, that a nature, insofar as it is a nature, is good; whence it is gathered that there is no creator of natures except the creator of good things, and through this a good one; but, by a great difference and the highest goodness, better than his creatures, who cannot at all be vitiated—not by the receiving of grace, but by the property of nature. Created natures, then, whether those which are without fault, or those which, born, are vitiated, or those which are born vitiated, cannot have any creator except him who creates good things; because, insofar as they are natures, they are good, even whichever are vitiated. For the creator of them is the author not of the faults, but of the natures. For even the author of faults himself is by nature good, which God made; but by fault he is evil, by which he himself fell away from the good founder by an evil will. And so this true reasoning convicts the error of the Manichees, who are unwilling to receive the authority either by which it was said: *God made all things, and behold, they were very good*[^88], when there was yet no evil; or by which it was said: *Every creature of God is good*[^89], when this evil age already was, since God is surely the maker of all ages. But you, who receive this authority of the divine words, so that you can be deservedly pressed by it, why do you not attend that, in that book where it is read that God made all things very good, the best of all places, paradise, was planted, where God so willed nothing evil to be that he did not even permit his image to be there, after he had sinned by his own will[^90]? And yet you do not hesitate to introduce, into a place of so great felicity and beauty—where it must be believed that there could be no fault of tree, or hay, or fruit, or of any crop or beast whatsoever—all the faults of human bodies and minds, with which we let you grieve, not deny, that human beings are born. For it is necessary that you grieve, when you do not find what to answer, and are unwilling to change so depraved an opinion, which compels you, by an unavoidable necessity, to set in a place of so great blessedness and beauty the blind, the one-eyed, the bleary-eyed, the deaf, the dumb, the lame, the deformed, the distorted, the scabby, the leprous, the paralytic, the epileptic, and those vitiated in various other kinds, and sometimes even monstrous by excessive foulness and a horrible novelty of members. What shall I say of the faults of minds, by which some are by nature lustful, some irascible, some timid, some forgetful, some slow of heart, some senseless and so foolish that a human being would rather live with certain beasts than with such human beings? Add the groans of those giving birth, and the wailings of those being born, the torments of those in pain, the labors of the ailing, the many torments of the dying, and the far more numerous perils of the living. All these, and other such things, or worse, which who can suffice to mention even briefly in fitting words?—according to your error, but plainly against your shame, either with a most shameless brow, or with hand set against the brow, you are compelled to place in the paradise of God, and to say that these would have been there too, even if no one had sinned. Say it, say it: for why do you fear to dishonor with so many and so great faults and calamities a place which by your nefarious dogma you make alien from you? For if you ever intended to enter there, you would never place such things there. Or if shame conquers in your hearts, and you blush, shudder, fall silent at setting up such things in such a place, and yet you obstinately cling to your error, by which you do not believe that human nature was vitiated by the transgression of the first man, answer the Manichees as to whence these things are, lest they conclude that they are from the commingling of an alien and evil nature. For when this is asked of us, we answer that these are not from the commingling of an alien nature, but from the transgression of our own nature, through him who was cast down in paradise, and cast out of paradise, lest a condemned nature should remain in a place of blessedness, and lest the faults or punishments which were deservedly to follow his descendants should be in that place, where no evils are allowed to be. But you, while you deny that these deformed or troublesome things come from the merits of our vitiated nature, permit the commingling of an alien one, and thus, wretched, are both compelled to aid the Manichees, and your error recalls those evils into paradise, whence your shame had taken them away.

**VI. 17.** *Julian:* But on the contrary, see how true is our conflict against you and the Manichee, in whose ruin your own always entangles you, and how swift the triumph over it. All those things which he vomited out in disparagement of the divine work, we circumscribe at once with the plow of the first definition; and we compel him to explain what he thinks sin is, which is clear to be nothing other than the will desiring what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. This established, all those brambles of objections, which had pricked the concretion of bodies, are shown, as he says, to be plucked out by the roots[^91]. But now the affection of fear and the sense of pain, through which he thought a storm was stirred up into the shipwrecks of human beings, are thoroughly taught to be not only the compellers of no evil, but, when they have met the barrier of a good will, the helpers and conveyers of righteousness. For who would dread judgment, except at the prompting of fear? Who would be aided by the groans of repentance, except by the expiation of pain and inner sickness? What, finally, would the severity of the one judging avail, unless it punished voluntary sins by the trouble of inflicted torment? By the testimony of all which it appears that sin is nothing other than a free will despising the precepts of justice; and that righteousness does not otherwise stand, than if it impute as sin that of which it knows there was a free caution; and through this that by no law can those things which are natural be reckoned as fault; nor does anyone take on a crime which he himself did not commit, when he could avoid it. By which power the Manichee and the Traducianist are consumed, who, with the eyes of all understanding dug out, tries to bring the matter of the will to the seeds.

*Augustine:* To these errors of yours we have already often answered: whence those who read them, and commit them to memory, do not everywhere desire my answer, wherever you repeat your wordiness. But lest anyone complain, while those quicker of wit pardon me, I ought not to be lacking even to the slower. Behold, here too I answer you concerning the definition of sin, by which you think yourself much aided. That sin which is so sin that it is not also the penalty of sin, that definition determines, by which it is said: Sin is the will desiring what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. Which especially pertains to him from whose great sin there arose for his descendants the misery through the heavy yoke from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb, and through the corruptible body which weighs down the soul. For he, having received a very brief law, knew what justice forbade; and from it surely it was free for him to abstain, with the flesh not yet lusting against his spirit, on account of which evil it was said even to the faithful: *That you may not do the things you wish*[^92]. The blindness of heart, then, by which it is not known what justice forbids, and the violence of concupiscence, by which even one who knows from what he ought to abstain is conquered, are not only sins, but are also the penalties of sins. And therefore they are not included in that definition of sin, by which only sin alone was defined, not what is also the penalty of sin. For when someone is ignorant of what he ought to do, and therefore does what he ought not to do, it was not free for him to abstain from that which he did not know he ought to abstain from. Likewise he who, as you say, is pressed not by origin, but by habit, so as to cry out: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^93]—how is it free for him to abstain from that evil which he does not will and does, hates and does? But if it were in the power of human beings to be free of these penalties, God would not be asked, both against blindness, where it is said to him: *Enlighten my eyes*[^94]; and against iniquitous desire, where it is said to him: *Let no iniquity have dominion over me*[^95]. Moreover, if these were not also sins for the reason that one is not free to abstain from them, it would not be said: *Remember not the sins of my youth and my ignorances*[^96]; it would not be said: *You have sealed up my sins in a bag, and noted if I have admitted anything unwillingly*[^97]. By that definition of sin, then—such as Adam committed, who knew what justice forbade, and did not abstain from that from which it was free for him to abstain—the Manichees are conquered; but by us, because we say that from this is the origin of human evils, by which we see even little ones weighed down, and on account of which this too was written, when sin was being treated: *Because no one is clean from filth, not even an infant whose life on earth is of one day*[^98]. But you, denying this, try, by a pernicious defense, to press our nature more, lest, wretched, it seek a liberator; but the Manichee, when it is asked whence evil comes, you permit to introduce an alien nature coeternal with God. For he does not, in order to blame human nature, allege the affection of fear and the sense of pain; which two you thought should be praised against him, because, namely, *fear and pain are thoroughly taught to be helpers and conveyers of righteousness*, while the human being does not sin out of fear of judgment, or grieves that he has sinned with the goads of repenting. This is not asked of you, but what the penalty of fear is in little ones, who do not flee sins, and why they are afflicted with such great pains, who do not commit sins. You surely said: *What would the severity of the one judging avail, unless it punished voluntary sins by the trouble of inflicted torment?* By what justice, then, are little ones, whose voluntary sins are none of their own, punished by the trouble of inflicted torment? In these surely it appears that your praises are empty and inept, by which you praised fear and pain. For these are grievous punishments, which freshly arisen and new images of God would not suffer from their beginning, under the most just judgment and omnipotence of God, if they did not draw the merit of original and old sin. Finally, in paradise, if no one had sinned, and the fruitfulness of the married had come about from that truthful blessing of God, far be it that anyone there, whether of the great or of the small, should suffer these torments. For not only pain, which is very manifest; but also fear, by the testimony of the divine Scripture, has torment[^99]; far be it, then, that in the place of that felicity there should be any torments. Accordingly, at any age, with no one terrifying, what would they fear? With no one harming, what would they grieve over? But in this present evil age, which, that it might be passed through in our miseries, we were cast out from the paradise of delights, even in those whose sins have been forgiven, the trouble of fears and pains remains, so that our faith concerning the future age, where these will be none, may be proved not only in our own troubles, but also in those of our little ones; because we do not for this reason wish them to be regenerated, that they may not suffer these evils; but that they may be conveyed into the kingdom, in which there will be no such things. To this true and Catholic faith, which you spit out, and try to refute with the empty noise of your cheeks, when the Manichee has brought in the question whence the evils of little ones are, all your wordiness will fall silent; since, when you deny original sin, he will soon dash your face, and introduce an alien nature of evil. But the Catholic faith does not fear what seems so to you, *that the matter of the will cannot be brought to the seeds*; since it hears God saying that the sins of the fathers are repaid in the third and fourth generation of the sons[^100]. Surely the matter of the will, by which the fathers sin, is brought to the seeds, when it is avenged in the sons; and father Abraham, that he might be tithed by Melchizedek, gave tithes to that priest by his own will; and yet his sons too, because they were in his loins, the Sacred Scripture attests were then tithed[^101]; which surely would not happen, if the matter of the will could not be brought to the seeds.

**VI. 18.** *Julian:* But, by the faith of our God and of human beings! That such prodigies of inventions could be found, that with set purpose and lavished zeal they try to assert all things perversely! For what is so prodigious as what the Carthaginian utters? Those things, he says, which were natural, were not perpetual; and those which were taken up by zeal, clung to the first coagulations of the members. Adam, he says, was made good, he had a natural innocence; preferred to the other creatures too by a peculiar nobility, he shone with the likeness of his Founder. He received free choice in his condition, that he might move himself to whichever side he willed by his own judgment, and he obtained, by the very condition of his making, in which he stood before the rest, the faculty of going to good or to evil, or of withdrawing from both. But since, with his judgment emancipated, he usurped the depraved will of his free mind, he overthrew all those things which were established as inborn; and sin alone and the necessity of sinning clung inseparably to him. This is what I called a prodigious invention. For it is an unheard-of deformity to say: A living being was well established, in which even natural goods were losable, to which voluntary evils clung inseparably.

*Augustine:* When it is said: *A living being was well established, in which even natural goods were losable, to which voluntary evils clung inseparably*, you think it *a prodigious invention*; and that these things are said by us, you are so vehemently and sharply moved that you cry out by the faith of God and of human beings, as though you suffered violence because these things are said. But, I beg, lay aside your terrible onslaughts, and more calmly attend to what I say. If anyone blinds himself by will, will he not lose a natural good, that is, sight, and will a voluntary evil, that is, blindness, cling to him inseparably? Was the living being therefore badly established, to which a natural good was losable, and a voluntary evil inseparable? Why, then, do I not rather cry out: By the faith of our God and of human beings! That these things, so manifest and set before the eyes, should not be seen by a human being who wishes to seem very acute, and learned, and a philosophaster, and a dialectician? For who, if he cuts off any member by will, does not lose the natural good of soundness, and take on the inseparable evil of mutilation? But perhaps you will say that these things can happen in the goods of the body, not of the mind. Why, then, when you said *natural goods*, or *voluntary evils*, did you not add "of the mind," lest your headlong and inconsiderate sentence be destroyed by the goods and evils of the body? Or did you perhaps forget? Let us allow it: it is human; but there comes forward into the midst that man who cries out: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^102]; and shows you that certain goods of the mind so perish through an evil will, that they cannot return through a good one, unless God do what the human being cannot, who can both restore eyes to one blinded by will, and members to one mutilated by will. Then, what will you answer about the devil himself, who lost a good will irreparably? Will you say it can be repaired? Dare, if you can. Or will you rather confess that these things too did not come into your mind, and that the forgetfulness of them made you precipitate a rash sentence? At least, then, with me reminding you, correct yourself. Or does obstinacy not permit you to correct what inconsiderate rashness said, and does shame at emendation confirm the lapse of error? God, as I see, must be entreated for you; whom the Apostle entreated for the Israelites, that he might heal those who, ignorant of the righteousness of God, and wishing to establish their own righteousness, were not subject to the righteousness of God[^103]. For such are you too, who wish to establish your own righteousness, which you make for yourselves out of your own free choice; you do not ask for and take from God the true one, which is called the righteousness of God; not that by which God is just, but that which is given from God; just as salvation is the Lord's[^104], not by which the Lord is saved, but by which he saves. Whence the same Apostle says: *That I may be found in him, not having my own righteousness, which is from the law; but that which is from faith, the righteousness from God*[^105]. This is the righteousness of God, which the Israelites did not know, and wished to establish their own, which is from the law; which Paul, in destroying, did not surely destroy the law, but their pride, who thought the law sufficient for them, as though fulfilling the righteousness of the law through free choice, and were ignorant of the righteousness of God, which is given from God, so that what the law commands is done with him helping, whose wisdom carries the law and mercy on its tongue[^106]: the law, because it commands; mercy, because it helps, so that what it commands is done. Long for this righteousness of God, O son Julian, do not trust in your own virtue; long, I say, for this righteousness of God; may the Lord grant you to long for it, may the Lord grant you also to have it. Do not, puffed up by your earthly stock, spurn this Carthaginian admonishing or warning you. For not because Apulia bore you, should you think the Carthaginians are to be conquered by race, whom you cannot conquer in mind. Flee rather punishments, not Carthaginians; for Carthaginian disputants you cannot flee, as long as it delights you to trust in your own virtue; for blessed Cyprian too was a Carthaginian, who said: *One must glory in nothing, since nothing is ours*[^107].

**VI. 19.** *Julian:* Here someone may say: What then? Do you deny that that innocence, in which he had been founded, was corrupted by the admission of spontaneous sin? For although the possibility of returning to good does not perish when iniquity is committed; yet it is certain that the merit of the innocence with which the human beginning comes forth perishes by the fault of the will. But I do not deny that this is so; but this is what I wish illuminated by these examples: that, since the condition of the qualities by which we are called good and evil is such that they move under the right of the will; and this is so seriously established that not even innocence—which, for the dignity of the author, goes before the office of the will and is natural—yet guards itself by no power in a resisting mind; far and away more does this right avail on the side of evil, that fault taken up by will seizes no tyranny for the destruction of reason. And if the good quality with which the human being was made was not unchangeable (for the human being would be falsely called free, if he could not vary his own motions), much more could the evil quality not be made, as it were, unchangeable and rational; lest that rationale of liberty feel a prejudice on the side of evil, which, on account of its state, it had not suffered on the portion of good.

*Augustine:* Behold, you too have found—and, though rather late, yet at some time it came into your mind—whence your rash sentence is destroyed; for you said that a natural good, which is innocence, can perish by the fault of the will; and through this you have shown that so great a good, which so pertains to the nature, not of the body, but of the mind itself, that God made the human being with that very good, is losable; which, if it had come into your mind before, you would not think it prodigiously and too deformedly said: *A living being was well established, in which even natural goods were losable*; for you thought losable things to be voluntary, whether goods or evils; but natural things you are wont to proclaim unlosable. For you too say elsewhere that *natural things persevere from the beginning of the substance to its end*[^108]; to this end, that you may contend that free choice, which God gave to the human being when he created him, cannot be lost; especially asserting that natural goods cannot perish by voluntary evils. And therefore you say that we try to assert all things perversely, as though we said that voluntary evils cannot be lost, but natural goods can; which indeed we do not say; for we say that both can be lost; but the evils which are brought in by free will can be lost through divine indulgence, or through a human will, but freed by God, and prepared by the Lord. But you who say that voluntary goods, not natural ones, can be lost through an evil will—behold, you have found, and you yourself have said, that innocence, which is a natural good, can be lost through a voluntary evil. And innocence, if you attend well, is a greater good than free choice; because innocence is a matter of good things, but free choice is of both good and evil things. But whether an evil will so destroys innocence that it can be repaired by a good will, is no contemptible question. For just as, if the members of the body are amputated by will, they are not likewise restored by will; so it must be seen whether, in a thing indeed dissimilar, that is, in the mind, something of the kind happens in the loss of innocence, and it can perish by a voluntary motion, but cannot return. For sacred virginity too, if it perishes through an unchaste will, one can return to chastity, but cannot to virginity. But to this it is still answered that the integrity of virginity is in the body, not surely of the mind, but of the body; whereas, when innocence is being discussed—and yet it must be considered whether he who has sinned returns by will to righteousness, not to innocence; as that one returns to chastity, not to virginity. For just as injustice is, as it were, the contrary of justice, so to innocence is opposed, as its contrary, not injustice, but guilt, which is not taken away by the will of the human being, even if it was made by his will. For he does not see the truth who thinks that the penitent takes away guilt from himself; although God also gives the penitence itself, which the Apostle confirms, saying: *Lest perhaps God give them penitence*[^109]; but God most openly takes away the guilt, by giving the human being indulgence; the human being does not take it away from himself by doing penitence. For we ought to recall him who found no place for penitence, although he sought it with tears[^110]. And through this both he did penitence and remained guilty, because he did not receive pardon; and those who will say among themselves: *Doing penitence, and groaning through anguish of spirit: What did pride profit us?*[^111]—they will surely remain guilty forever, not having received pardon; just as also he of whom the Lord says: *It will not be forgiven him, but he will be guilty of an eternal sin*[^112]. Behold, innocence is found to be a great good of the human being, and so natural that, although the first man was founded with it, and, as you say, every human being is born with it, yet it can be lost by the will of the human being, but cannot be restored; and guilt is found to be a great evil and contrary to innocence, which yet the power of the human being, since it is voluntary, can bring in, but cannot take away. Do you see how that general rule of yours is broken, by which you thought we do not lose a natural good by will; when there has been found that which not only perishes, but does not even return by will, at least human will? But God can, the guilt being taken away, recall the human being to innocence. Why, then, do you not believe that the liberty of acting well could perish by human will, and could not return except by divine will, when you hear a human being saying: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*; and after such words crying out: *Who will free me?* and subjoining: *The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^113]? *But falsely*, you say, *would the human being be called free, if he could not vary his own motions*[^114]. Nor do you see that you take away liberty from God himself, and from us, when we shall have begun to live immortal with him in his kingdom, nor will it be possible for us then to vary our motions, now to good, now to evil; and yet we shall then be more happily free, when we shall not be able to be slaves to sin, as neither can God himself: but we by his grace, he indeed by his nature.

**VI. 20.** *Julian:* Then, what parasite ever extolled the strength of a boastful soldier with flatteries, as the Traducianist did the devil's? Which may be recognized from the weights of the affections. For God made the human being, handling the very matter of the clay with venerable condescension, that, subdued, it might follow the hand of its author into the form of a human being. The image had now stood, completed, but pale and cold, awaiting the spirit by which it might shine and be vigorous. Then, by the most august breath of its author created and inspired, the mind moved the filled bowels; then all the senses were roused to the apparatus of their proper functions. The indweller gave color to the entered bowels, heat to the blood, vigor to the members, sheen to the skin. See what a business the divine piety undertook in forming and animating the human being. But the familiarity of the Founder did not leave him even when perfect; he is transferred to a more pleasant place, and the one he had mercifully made, he munificently enriches. Yet not content to have bestowed this, he illumines him by the imparting of conversation; he gives him a precept, that, understanding his liberty, he might see that there was at hand for him that by which he might become more friendly to his Founder; which precept is not stretched into many things, lest he should feel any burden from a multiplied law; but by the prohibition of one little apple, a testimony of devotion is sought. Afterward too, that he might have a consort through whom he might become a father, again his hand by which he was founded is ennobled by his touch; and presently too he is cherished and honored by divine conversation. These things, then, bestowed by God—so long, so many, so great, institutions, gifts, precepts, conversations—made for the human being no necessity of good; but on the other side the devil, no less timidly than craftily, mingled a few words with the woman; and they are said to have had such force that they were at once turned into the condition of natural things; nay, that they overthrew all things inborn, made a perpetual necessity of evil, and set the devil as lord and possessor over the image of God. What, then, is stronger, what more excellent, what more magnificent than the adverse power, if by a slight conversation alone it did as much as God could not obtain either by his works or by his gifts? It is manifest, then, that you stand on his side, whose power you so immoderately proclaim; and that you have no fellowship in the worship of our God, whom we confess to be as most just so most omnipotent, who is mighty, and truth is round about him[^115]; who humbles like a wounded one the proud[^116], that is, the devil and the Manichee, and you their followers slandering nature, lest you confess that you sin of your own accord. Our God himself, then, in the strength of his arm scatters his enemies[^117], by which neither you nor the Manichees could bring forward anything that would not leap apart at the thunderbolt of his truth.

*Augustine:* We are not flatterers of the devil, nor do we extol his power, which is subject to the power of God, with the adulatory proclamation of praise, as you revile us. But would that you were not his soldiers, as all heretics are; whose dogmas he, through your tongues, hurls like deadly weapons against those whom he can. The Apostle says: *Giving thanks to the Father, who has made us fit for a share in the lot of the saints in light; who rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^118]; and you forbid us to give these thanks for little ones, asserting that they are not under the power of the devil; for what reason, except that they may not be rescued thence, lest the gains of the devil be lessened? Jesus says, who according to this name of his saves his people from their sins[^119]: *No one enters the house of the strong man to snatch his vessels, unless he first binds the strong man*[^120]; and you contend that in this people of Christ, which he saves from their sins, little ones are not, whom you wish to be bound neither by their own nor by original sins; and while by your deceitful discourse you lessen the strength of him whom Truth called strong, you by your error make him stronger for holding little ones. Jesus says: *For the Son of man came to seek and to save what had perished*[^121]; and you answer him: There is no need for you to seek little ones, for they had not perished; and thus, while you repel the Savior's search from them, you increase the power of the wounder against them. Jesus says: *The healthy do not need a physician, but those who are ill; I came not to call the just, but sinners*[^122]; and you say to him: You are not, then, necessary to little ones, because they are sinners neither by their own will, nor by human origin. When, then, you forbid those to be saved who are to be saved to come to the physician, the diabolical plague exercises its dominion over them more powerfully. How much more tolerable, then, is it for you, as parasites and flatterers, to soothe the devil with false praises, than, as soldiers or attendants, to aid him with the falsehoods of dogmas? With copious and ornate eloquence you describe how God formed the human being from clay, animated him by breathing, enriched him with paradise, supported him with a precept, and took such care, lest he should burden him in anything, that he did not stretch that same precept into many things, lest the human being, whom he had founded with so great kindness, should feel any burden from a multiplied law. Why, then, does the corruptible body now weigh down the soul[^123]? Why, then, is there a heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^124]? Since he was unwilling to weigh down Adam himself even by a multiplied law. Surely you perceive that, if no one had sinned in paradise, the fruitfulness of the married would so fill that place of so great felicity with the human race that neither would a corruptible body weigh down the soul, nor a heavy yoke press born human beings, nor labor and pain instruct the pitiable little ones. Whence, then, are these things, which surely are not from some evil which the Manichee feigns or believes to be alien to us and commingled with us, except from our nature vitiated by the transgression of the first man? But you wonder, an acute and prudent man, and do not think it must be believed that the devil's few words mingled with the woman are said to have had such force that they perverted all the natural goods; as though the words of the speaker did this, not the consent of the hearer. For not, as you say, were the few words of the serpent turned into the condition of natural things: but the will of the human being lost a good, which could be restored not by the will of the human being, but of God, when and to whom he, most just, most powerful, and most merciful, shall judge it must be restored; just as in the body, as we have already said, the sight of the human being can be taken away by his will; which if it happens, blindness follows, which necessity bears, the will does not take away; and in the mind the will of the human being can lose his innocence, and cannot restore it. Rather consider this: that the evils with which human beings are born, which could not be engendered together with human beings in the felicity of paradise, surely, unless a vitiated nature had gone out of paradise, would not now be engendered together with them. These things, which are manifest, look at. For we do not conjecture in obscurity the evils of mortals from the day of the coming out of the womb of the mother of the sons of Adam; but we discern them in the clearest light. These, because they are not from the commingling of an alien evil nature, are beyond doubt from the depravation of our own. Nor let it seem unworthy to you that the image of God is subject to the devil; for this would not happen, except by the judgment of God; nor is this condemnation removed, except by the grace of God. For he who by the excellence of his nature, that he might be the image of God, was made to the likeness of God, it is not to be wondered at that, by the depravation of his nature, he was made like to vanity, whence his days pass away like a shadow[^125]. Do you say why innumerable images of God, at a little age committing no sins, are not admitted to the kingdom of God, if they are not reborn. For they have something whereby they deserve to lie under the devil, on account of which they do not deserve to reign under God; to whose light if you adhered, you would not, with so great arrogance, compare your words to the thunderbolt.

**VI. 21.** *Julian:* It is clear, then, most absolutely, that Augustine differs in nothing from his preceptor, but that by his disputations the nature of Adam no less than of the rest is defined as most evil. Finally, that we may still discuss something with the same man about the things we have treated, it appears that you do not think even the first sin of the same kind as you think the rest of the faults. For when you say that the shameful deeds of later times cannot pass into nature—for example, that of an embezzler, of a parricide, of an incestuous man, sons born are born liable to the sins of their begetters; and that there is no crime which is mingled with the seeds, except that one alone—you show most openly that you do not think that disobedience of the same kind of which the rest also are. See, then, how brief and clear is our question. If the sin which Adam committed was taken up by will, and could become natural, why are not these sins which happen daily, which a criminal will commits, heaped into the deformity and prejudices of the seeds? But if these, no less atrocious than more numerous, cannot be engendered, by what law, by what condition, by what privilege is that one alone avenged as inborn? If the sins are of one kind—those which we know, which the law charges, which equity punishes, and that of the first man, which was committed by will and punished by equity—why do we not either understand these from that, or that from these? Or, if they cannot be a mutual testimony to each other, with what shamelessness is it denied that that transgression was of another condition, that is, brought forth not from will, but from a natural taint? Finally, dare, with the assertion of propagation, to define any sin whatever—I do not say that first one, but for the present even of those which now happen, for example, sacrilege, a shameful deed, or any evil crime—that is, explain what definition these sins have. You will say without doubt: It is the will desiring what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. For if there were not an evil will, there could not be sin. Attend how reasonably we rise to this. O dullness, O shamelessness not to be borne! You define sin to be nothing but a free will, forbidden by justice, when the opinion of natural evil prescribes that there is a sin, with which the human being is born, that is not voluntary. It is not true, then, that there is no fault except what is committed of one's own accord; because there is a crime, and the greatest, which is taken up not of one's own accord, but by being born. Dismiss, then, the definition of sin, which, friendly to the Catholics, does not turn aside to you even by the right of hospitality; and that being dismissed, prove that you are not the comrade-in-arms of those who assail the very substance by the malice of depravity. And that what we have done may be gathered: either no sin will be taught to be voluntary, if there is any natural sin; or there will be none natural, if every sin is defined voluntary; and it is concluded from these that either you deny that sin can be born, and cross over into the faith of the Catholics; or, if you persist that not some, but the greatest crime is taken up without will through nature, you surrender your name even to the Manichee, to whom you render total obedience.

*Augustine:* You think you bring great odium upon me, when you say that I differ in nothing from my preceptor; but I take your revilings for my praise; and, recalling to my faith not what you think, but what you sound forth in words, I understand, as it behooves me to understand. For you say what is true and do not know it; just as Caiaphas the high priest, the persecutor of Christ, was thinking of crimes, and, not knowing it, was saying salutary words[^126]. I rejoice altogether that, in this question which is turned between us, I differ in nothing from my preceptor; first, because the Lord himself taught me that little ones are dead, unless he himself give them life, who died for all; which the Apostle, expounding, says: *Therefore all have died, and he died for all*[^127]. And you contradict, denying that little ones are dead, lest they be made alive in Christ, although you confess that Christ died even for little ones. This John too, the Apostle of the Preceptor of all, teaches me, who says: That the Son of God came for this, to destroy the works of the devil[^128]; which you deny are destroyed in little ones, as though he had not come for them, who came for this, to destroy the works of the devil. My preceptors too I ought not to deny, who aided me to understand this by their literary labor. My preceptor is Cyprian, who says that the infant, born carnally according to Adam, contracted the contagion of the ancient death by his first birth, and for this very reason approaches more easily to receive the forgiveness of sins, that there are forgiven him not his own, but another's sins[^129]. My preceptor is Ambrose, whose books I not only read, but heard him speaking the words too, and through him received the laver of regeneration. I am indeed far unequal to his merits; but I confess and profess that in this matter I differ in nothing from this my preceptor. To whom far be it that you dare to prefer Pelagius your preceptor; whom yet I hold against you, concerning Ambrose, as my witness. For Pelagius said that not even an enemy dared to reprehend his faith and his most pure sense in the Scriptures[^130]. Whom you so dare to reprehend that what he says—that the discord of the flesh passed into our nature through the transgression of the first man[^131], and whatever else he held and said about human nature vitiated through Adam—you affirm to be the invention of the Manichee. You do indeed keep in part the testimony of your preceptor concerning this so great man, in that you do not dare openly to reprehend him: but when you revile me by name with a slanderous tongue and a brazen brow, you surely charge both him and the others who held and said the same things—the great and famous teachers of the Catholic Church—the more wickedly the more obliquely. I, therefore, against you, defend both my faith and theirs, whom you openly fear to have as enemies, and unwillingly endure as judges. But far be it that before such judges your argumentations should avail anything, in which you compare the sins of later times to that great sin, that is, the transgression of the first man, and think that if, by the crime of the first man, the nature of the human race was changed, then now too the crimes of parents ought to change the nature of the sons. For in saying these things you do not consider that those sinners, after they committed that great sin, were both dismissed from paradise, and forbidden from the tree of life with so great severity. Are the wicked of these times cast down into some lower lands away from this orb of the earth, when they have committed here the very crimes, however great? Are they forbidden the tree of life, which is none in this misery? But the place and life of the human race persists, in which even the most impious human beings live; whereas we see that the place and life of those impious ones, as it was before sin, could not stand after sin. But, according to your opinion, little ones bound by no guilt ought, as soon as they are born, to be carried, as innocent images of God, by the angels into the paradise of God, and there to be nourished without any labor and pain, so that if any of them sinned, he would deservedly be cast thence, lest sins should grow by imitation. But now, when only he who sinned in the felicity of paradise heard: *Thorns and thistles the earth will bring forth for you, cursed in all your works; and in the sweat of your brow you will eat your bread*[^132]; we see no human being free from the penalty of labor, which labor surely was not going to weigh down the blessed inhabitants of paradise. And when only his wife heard: *In sorrow you will bring forth children*[^133], we know no woman in childbirth immune from this punishment. Are you, then, so absurd that you either think human beings would have suffered these troubles in paradise, if no one had sinned—which it is most manifest God inflicted then only on those transgressing human beings; or now deny that their descendants, exiles of paradise, suffer them, enduring so many and so great miseries everywhere on earth; or are you going to say that, the more anyone is a sinner and impious, the more his fields bring forth thorns and thistles, and the more he sweats with labors; and the more iniquitous any woman is, the more grievous pains she suffers in childbirth? As, then, the penalties of human miseries, which the sons of Adam suffer in common from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb, are therefore of all, because the parents are common to all, from whose transgression these things came; so the transgression of those two ought to be understood to be so great a sin that it could change for the worse the nature of all born of man and woman, and bind them with a common guilt as with the bond of a hereditary debt. Whoever, then, says that the condition of any offenses whatsoever which are now committed ought to have been such as was the condition of that offense which was committed in so great a felicity of that life, and so great an ease of not sinning, ought also to equate the two lives—namely that which is now lived, and that which was being lived in those holy and blessed delights. But if you discern that this is most foolish, cease to wish to prescribe from the sins of the present age, lest that great sin should have its own force and its own singular merit. Although even in this life the omnipotent and just one who says: *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the sons*[^134], shows clearly enough that the descendants too are entangled by the guilt of the parents, and, although by a milder bond, yet become hereditary debtors, unless—as we have already disputed in the earlier parts of this work—not your argument, but the New Testament, and not the nature of generation, but the grace of regeneration, absolve them from the bond of that proverb which is wont to be said: *The fathers ate a bitter grape, and the teeth of the sons were set on edge*[^135]. But that definition of sin, where it is understood as the will desiring what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain, is the definition of that sin which is only sin, not that which is also the penalty of sin; which I know not how many times has already been answered to you concerning these things. For he who says: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^136], is not free to abstain from this evil, and therefore he invokes a liberator, because he has lost liberty.

**VI. 22.** *Julian:* The time admonishes us to pass to other matters; but I am compelled by indignation to dwell a little on this same point. Would you dare to say that Adam sinned by will? Whence came this dream to you? Because it was iniquitous, you say, that God should impute as sin except what he knew it was free to abstain from. What then? Had that prince of darkness whom you worship credited him with this justice for a moment, and, soon demanding it back, stripped this God of all equity, so that he who had understood from the beginning that nothing was to be imputed as sin except what it had been free to abstain from, knew, through all the remaining time, that for all those being born it was not free to abstain? Finally, whence do you know that this alone was just, that in Adam nothing but a voluntary crime could be punished; if you do not know that it is unjust that there be imputed to anyone as a crime what you confess was taken up without will? Either, then, you will think the opinion of propagation just, so that it can agree with the sentence of God, when he imputes to a little one a sin which was committed by no will of his, and you will be compelled to profess that this too is just and agreeable to the judgments of God, that he imputed to Adam as sin what he knew was brought forth by him not by will, but by the deformity of his substance; and through this very thing there will be no propagation-theory, nor will the nature be reprehended as depraved by the choice of the one acting, but as badly established from the beginning, and you will confess that you are a Manichee. Or, if, coming to your senses, you say it is unjust that Adam should be held guilty for the faults of his nature; it follows irrefutably that it is most wicked, if Abel, Enoch, Noah, and the whole human race be reckoned liable to original crime. Which crime of judgment, if you bring it upon your God, he alone will remain guilty for all; and it will appear, as always, that he is not the one whom the Catholics venerate as most equitable in the Trinity. But if you desist from accusing God, you will condemn the dogma of the Manichean propagation, by which you have hitherto been transfixed, even when revived.

*Augustine:* Hence it is that you err vehemently, hence you are heretics, hence against the Catholic faith, which, avoiding heretics, follows the divine words and is fortified by them, you dare to compose novel engines from human and empty argumentations; since you do not know, and what you cannot understand you refuse to believe, what force the bonds of the seeds have in the series of generation, and, in the creatures which God willed to be born one from another according to their kind, how great and how ineffable are the natural rights of propagation, and how also they are penetrated by no sense, comprehended by no thought; whence is this affection implanted in the human race, that all, as far as pertains to themselves, wish to have certain sons; to which thing the fidelity of the conjugal covenant in chaste women avails; on account of which the philosopher Plato rightly displeased, because he judged that women should be used in common, in that city which he forms by disputation as it were the best—he too wishing what, except that the elder might render to all the younger that love which he saw nature owed to sons, when each one might think that he could be his son whose age he saw to be such that, born of his seed from any unknown woman whom he had used indifferently, he might not without reason be believed to be his? What? Did not Cicero utter that voice from the bowels of all fathers to his son, to whom, writing, he says: *You alone are the one of all by whom I would wish to be surpassed in all things*[^137]? Did not those very natural rights of propagation, which we have said are most hidden, and yet are known to avail more than is credible, bring it about that two twins, not only not yet begetting, but not even yet born, still in the womb of their mother, were called two peoples[^138]? The same natural rights of propagation brought it about that Israel is said to have served in Egypt[^139], Israel to have set out from Egypt, Israel to have entered the land of promise, Israel to have obtained the goods or experienced the evils which God either bestowed on or inflicted upon that people. Of whom also it is written: *There will come from Zion one who will rescue and turn away impiety from Jacob; and this is for them the covenant from me, when I shall have taken away their sins*[^140]; whereas that man who first and alone received these two proper names, having died long before, did not see these goods or evils. These natural rights of propagation brought it about that the same people was tithed in Abraham, for no other reason than that it was in his loins, when he himself was tithed by his own will[^141], but that people not by its own will, but by the natural right of propagation. But how the same people was in the loins of Abraham, not only from him up to the time which is written in the Letter to the Hebrews, but also from him up to this time, and from this up to the end of the age, as long as the sons of Israel are generated one from another; how, then, there could have been in the loins of one man so innumerable a multitude of human beings, who can explain in speech, who can even find out in thought? For not even the very seeds, whose quantity is corporeal, although each is tiny, from which individuals are born—if those from which so many human beings have been and are born, and will be born to the very end, were heaped together—could be contained in the loins of one man. There is, then, some force, invisible and intangible, implanted in the natural secret places, where the natural rights of propagation lie hidden; on account of which force, however, it is not falsely said that as many as could be propagated from that one man by the successions and multiplications of generations were in the loins of that father. And not only were they there, but also, with him knowing and willing being tithed, they themselves were tithed neither knowing nor willing, since those who could know and will did not yet exist. This, moreover, the author of that sacred Letter said for this reason, that he might set the priesthood of Christ, which the priest Melchizedek prefigured, who tithed Abraham, before the Levitical priesthood; teaching that even Levi himself, who tithed his brothers, that is, received tithes from them, was tithed by Melchizedek in Abraham; because he too was in the loins of Abraham, when Melchizedek tithed him, that is, received tithes from him. And through this he does not wish Christ, to whom it was said: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^142], to be understood as tithed; that he may be deservedly preferred to the Levitical priesthood[^143]; for Melchizedek tithed Abraham, was not tithed, as Levi was in Abraham. But if it be asked how Christ was not tithed, since he too, which is manifest, was, according to the origin of the flesh, in the loins of Abraham, when that father was tithed by Melchizedek; nothing occurs except that Mary indeed his mother, of whom he took flesh, was born of the carnal concupiscence of her parents; but she did not so conceive Christ, whom she procreated not of male seed, but of the Holy Spirit. Christ, then, did not pertain to the principle of the male seed, through which those were in the loins of Abraham whom the sacred Scripture attests were tithed in him. Moreover the concupiscence of the flesh, through which the casting of carnal seeds is provoked, either was none in Adam before sin, or was vitiated in him through sin. For either without it the genitals could be moved suitably, and the seed poured into the bosom of the wife, if there was none then; or it too served the bidding of the will, if there was. But now, if it were such, never would the flesh lust against the spirit. Either, then, it itself is a fault, if there was none before sin; or it itself is without doubt vitiated by sin; and therefore from it original sin is drawn. There was, then, in the body of Mary the carnal matter whence Christ took flesh; but in it carnal concupiscence did not sow Christ. Whence he was born of flesh with flesh, yet in the likeness of sinful flesh[^144], not, like other human beings, in sinful flesh; therefore he dissolved original sin in others by regeneration, he did not himself contract it by generation. Therefore that first one is Adam, this one the second; because that one was made without carnal concupiscence, this one was born; but that one only a human being, this one both God and human being; and therefore that one could not sin, not as this one could not sin. In vain, then, do you try either to equate, or even to prefer, the sins of his sons, however great and horrible, to the sin of that one. The more loftily his nature stood, the more grievously it fell. That nature was such that it could not even die, if it had willed not to sin; that nature was such that it had in itself no discord of flesh and spirit; that nature was such that it contended against no faults of its own; not because it yielded to them, but because there were none in it. Then, therefore, you ought to equate the sins of his descendants to his sin, if you can find a nature equal; but then to say they are even greater, if you can find a better nature. For a rational nature, the higher it itself is, the worse its ruin, and its sin, the more incredible, the more damnable. Therefore the angel fell irreparably, since from him to whom more is given, more is required[^145]; and so he owed the more of voluntary obedience, the more he had in the goodness of nature; whence he was so punished, by not doing what he ought, that he can no longer even will it, being destined also to eternal torments. But Adam, in his so many descendants, that no one can number them, is freed from everlasting punishment by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, and in himself too—although some thousands of years after his death, when Christ, who died for us, descended to the places of the dead, not by necessity, but by power, and loosed the pains of hell[^146]. For so must it be understood that Wisdom led him out of his offense[^147], because, through the holy flesh of the only-begotten Son of God which she brought forth, the father of the human race, and so also the father of Christ, who for the salvation of human beings was made a human being, was then loosed from those bonds—not by his own merit, but by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord—the Church not vainly believes. This, then, God imputed as sin to the first Adam, whence it was free for him to abstain; but the first Adam himself was of so excellent a nature, since it was not vitiated, that his sin was so much greater than the sins of the rest, as he himself was far better than the rest; whence his penalty too, which immediately followed his sin, appeared so great that immediately he was held also by the necessity of dying, who had it in his power not to die; and was immediately sent out of the place of so great felicity, and immediately forbidden the tree of life. But when this happened, the human race was in his loins. Whence, according to those things which we spoke of before—the too hidden and greatly availing natural rights of propagation—it was consequent that those who were in his loins, about to come into this age through the concupiscence of the flesh, should be condemned together; just as it was consequent that those who, by that right of propagation and by the principle of the seed, were in the loins of Abraham, should be tithed together. And so all the sons of Adam were sprinkled in him with the contagion of sin, and bound by the condition of death. And through this, although they are little ones, and do nothing of good or evil by will; yet because they are clothed with him who sinned by will, they draw from him the guilt of sin and the punishment of death; just as little ones who are clothed with Christ, although they have done nothing of good by their own will, take from him a participation in righteousness, and the reward of eternal life. Thus Christ is shown by contrast to be the form of the one to come, on which account the same Apostle says: *As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of him who is from heaven*[^148]. Since these things are so, let him say that those who are born are not clothed with the sin and death of the first Adam, whoever dares to say that those who are reborn are not clothed with the righteousness and life of the second Adam; although neither did the former commit a sin from which it was free to abstain, nor the latter a righteousness which it is free to do.

**VI. 23.** *Julian:* *That sin, then, which changed the human being himself in paradise for the worse, because it is much greater than we can judge, is drawn from everyone being born*[^149]. Who told you that the sin of Adam was much greater than Cain's? Much greater too than the Sodomites'? Much more monstrous, finally, than yours and the Manichee's? Certainly in history no occasion of this vanity is found. He had been bidden to abstain from the eating of one tree; raw, unskilled, incautious, without experience of fear, without example of righteousness, at the suggestion of the woman he usurped a food whose sweetness and beauty had enticed him. See, here was a transgression of a command. One transgression was committed among the rest, which the zeal of sinners has perpetrated at various times; it was no more than when the people of Israel used forbidden animals. For the cause of the sin lay not in the quality of the apple, but in the transgression of the command. What, then, did Adam do such that you charge his sin to have existed beyond the estimation of human beings? Unless perhaps you too, according to the mysteries of the Manichee—who restrains his hand from the plucking of apples and of all growing things, lest he tear a part of his God, which he supposes enclosed in barks and grasses—think that Adam grievously sinned, because by the eating of the apple he tore the substance of your God. O frenzy! *Because that sin is much greater than we can judge, it is drawn*, he says, *from everyone being born*. A greater crime, then, was it to eat an apple when it was not lawful, than for fratricidal envy to stab holy Abel, than to violate the rights of guests and of the sexes in Sodom, than to immolate one's own sons to demons already under the law, than finally to subject innocents conscious of no will, the fresh work of God, to the kingdom of the devil, and to join them to its merits, than to charge God with iniquity, than to assign honorable marriage to the prince of darkness, finally to make little ones, because they are born through the begetting of their parents—I do not feign, but gather; for you have so said that that sin is greater and more grievous than all crimes that it can be equaled by no guilt. And you assert that little ones come full of this so great evil, which outweighs all vices. Well, then, have we understood: that, the more they are partakers of a greater sin, the more they are preferred, in condemnation, to all the wicked.

*Augustine:* On account of my words, which you set down from my book, as if to be refuted, if you could, where I said: *That sin, then, which in paradise changed the human being himself for the worse, because it is much greater than we can judge, is drawn from everyone being born*; you ask me who told me that the sin of Adam was much greater than Cain's, much greater too than the Sodomites'. Which I indeed did not express in my words, but you so understood them; for I said that that sin is greater than we can judge; I did not say greater than Cain's or the Sodomites'. For the usurpation of the forbidden apple, since it was so avenged that the nature which had the power of not dying should have the necessity of dying, beyond doubt exceeds all human judgments. For to eat an apple forbidden by the law of God would seem a slight sin; but how greatly he who cannot be deceived esteemed it, appears well enough by the greatness of the punishment. The sin of Cain the fratricide indeed appears monstrous to all, and is established to be a horrible crime; which, if, as you do, it be compared, by a human appraisal, with an apple unlawfully plucked, the comparison will be judged ridiculous; yet that fratricide, although destined to die at some time, was not punished by the death by which such crimes are wont to be avenged in human judgments. For God said to him: *You will work the earth, and it will not add its strength to give you fruit; groaning and trembling you will be upon the earth*. And when he, hearing that the earth would not give him fruit according to his labor, and that upon it he would be wretched with groaning and trembling, was shaken rather by the dread of death, lest anyone do to him what he himself had done to his brother; God set a sign on him, lest anyone, finding him, should kill him[^150]. Here again a huge fault, and a light penalty; but it seems so to the judgments of human beings, who neither know these mysteries, nor can weigh the faults of human beings with so clear and whole an appraisal as God can. The Sodomites indeed, with fire coming upon that land from heaven, with a punishment fitting their deeds, were consumed[^151]; but there too were little ones, by your patronage, pure and free from all contagion of sin, and yet the just and merciful God did not snatch away so many of his innocent images beforehand, by the ministry of the angels, which would have been most easy for him, from the burning of Sodom; nor did the omnipotent one furnish to them, as to those three men in the furnace[^152], harmless flames, by which their parents were being burned. Consider these things, consider them diligently and piously; and seeing in this age the small subject, along with the great, equally to such miseries as could in no way exist in the paradise of God, if no one had sinned; recognize original sin, and the just heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^153]; and do not weigh them down further by your defense, denying to the sick or dead Christ the Savior and life-giver. For if you ask who told me how great a sin Adam committed, it is he who told you too; but if you have ears to hear, you will hear him; but you will have them, if you do not attribute them to your own choice, but receive them from him who said: *I will give them a heart to know me, and ears that hear*[^154]. For who, except one who lacks such ears, does not hear the Scripture saying without any obscurity or ambiguity to the first sinful human being: *You are earth, and into earth you will go*[^155]? Where it is evidently shown that he was not even going to die in the flesh, that is, to go into the earth, whence his flesh had been taken, by the death of the flesh itself, unless he had merited, on account of sin, to hear and suffer this; whence the Apostle afterward said: *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^156]. Who does not hear, except one who has not such ears, God saying of Adam himself: *Lest perhaps he stretch out his hand, and take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever; and the Lord sent him out of the paradise of pleasure*[^157]? Where surely, without any labor and pain, he would live forever. For that pleasure of paradise—which you must confess, if you have not yet forgotten the Christian name—is to be thought of not as of baseness, but of blessedness. This penalty, then, which Adam merited, that he should not live forever, and therefore was dismissed from a place of so great blessedness, where, if he were, and had not sinned, he would beyond doubt live forever—how great a penalty it is, so great a sin must we understand, which deserved to be avenged by that penalty. What, then, are you doing, I beg you, when you try with such insistence to extenuate the sin of Adam, except that you charge God with monstrous and horrendous savagery, who punished this with so great—I do not say severity, but cruelty? Which, if it is wicked to think of God, why do you not measure the greatness of the fault—about which human beings cannot judge, with an incomparably just judge judging—from the greatness of the penalty, and restrain your tongue from sacrilegious wordiness? But I do not charge God with iniquity, whose just yoke I say is upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^158]; but you rather make God unjust, who think that they suffer this without any merit of any sin whatsoever. And not on account of the work which God made, but on account of the fault which the enemy implanted, I say that those who are born of the first man are under the same enemy, if they are not reborn in the second. In which matter you accuse the Catholic Church of the crime of treason, if, as you say, they are not rescued from the power of darkness when little ones are baptized, and it, before they are baptized, exorcizes and exsufflates so many images of God. Nor do I assign honorable marriage to the prince of darkness, which I purge from all harm of lust, if they use it well with the intention of propagation. But you do not shudder to set up in paradise the evil by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, that is, in a place of so great peace, so great quiet, so great honorableness, so great felicity. Nor do I judge little ones, having only original sin, to be worse than all the shameful and wicked, as you slanderously claim. For it is one thing to be weighed down by a sin committed by oneself, another to be sprinkled by the contagion of another's, however great. On account of which little ones approach the more easily to the forgiveness of sins, as the Carthaginian, your punishment, Cyprian, says, for this very reason, that there are forgiven them not their own, but another's sins[^159]. But you, when you say that little ones not only—which we too say—have done none of their own will, but also have drawn no sin from their origin, beyond doubt make God unjust—which has already often been said to you, and must more often be said—he who imposed on them a heavy yoke from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb. But, that you may understand how little ones, born of Adam, are both bound by participation in the sin of that man, and yet are not equated to his guilt, attend to Christ, whom you have read to be the form of the one to come[^160]; and see how little ones, reborn in him and made partakers of his righteousness, you dare not compare to his merits. You too, in the second book of this work of yours[^161], said that in Adam the form of sin, because Eve sinned first, was not the first, but the greatest; just as in Christ the form of righteousness was not the first, but the greatest, because there were just men even before him; which, if you had not forgotten that you said it, you would not here extenuate the sin of Adam, in whom you confessed the greatest form of sin existed.

**VI. 24.** *Julian:* But why should odium be stirred against you concerning enmities against innocence, when the petulance and rabidity of your obscene mouth is not even bridled by the honor of divinity? For you accuse little ones, but together with God; you assail innocence, but with injury to equity; you deny the truth, but with the slander of him whom you confess to be your God. And through this, even if we lacked the help of reasoning, yet the propagation-theory of sin would abundantly collapse by the deformity of its own asserters.

*Augustine:* Your abusive wordiness has objected to me the rabidity of an obscene mouth. Am I the defender and praiser of lust? Have I dared to enrich the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, even with the possession of paradise? Into which place of so great beauty and peace you have at once introduced either war, in which one laudably resists the one impelling toward sin, or disgrace, in which one basely yields to it. Why, then, do you so contumeliously rise up against me, and do not look back at yourself? For I do not accuse God; but you, who say that little ones draw no sin from their origin, on whom he imposed a heavy yoke. Nor do I assail innocence with injury to equity; but you do injury to equity, who say that little ones have so great an innocence; whom yet, if equity knew that what you yourself think were true, it would not punish with a heavy yoke. Nor do I deny the truth, nor charge God; but you rather. For the Apostle said truly: *The body is dead because of sin*[^162]; which you deny. But how do you not charge God, to whom you impute the miseries, which you cannot deny, of little ones, having no original sin worthy of misery? And through this your conclusion, which charges us with deformity, follows the truth of no reasoning.

**VI. 25.** *Julian:* But why do we think the reason of truth alone must be so stubbornly followed; when the phalanx of our enemies leans on the very perils of things, and rises against us fortified by the suffrages of troubles? For he wishes, by the shame of the one having intercourse, the pain of the one giving birth, the sweat of the one laboring, to prove the transmission of faults and penalties into the seeds; so that by these signs, namely, of difficult births, of sweating farmers, and of brambly fallow fields, a natural crime may be believed, by the merit of which the human race is exercised through so many troubles; which some suppose was made mortal through the sin of Adam. But I said "some," because their prince Augustine indeed blushed to say this. Finally, he writes to Marcellinus that Adam seems to have been made mortal; but, with his accustomed elegance, he subjoins that death was the wages of iniquity; and the one whom he confesses was established mortal according to nature, he pronounces could not have died. Those sentences, then, which are read in Genesis are wont to be brought forward against us, by which Adam and Eve are harassed, about which it is now time to discourse. *The Lord God said*, he says, *to the serpent: Because you have done this, cursed are you above all the cattle, and above all the beasts which are on the earth; upon your breast and belly you will go, and you will eat earth all the days of your life; and I will put enmities between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. She will lie in wait for your head, and you will lie in wait for her heel. But to the woman he said: Multiplying I will multiply your sorrows and your groaning; in sorrow you will bring forth children, and your turning will be to your husband, and he will have dominion over you. But to Adam he said: Because you have heard the voice of your wife, and have eaten of the tree, of which alone I had commanded you that you should not eat of it, cursed is the earth in your works; in sorrow you will eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it will bring forth to you, and you will eat the hay of your field; in the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*[^163]. These sentences, then, you take as testimony of inborn iniquity, and you harangue that women would not have been going to suffer pain in giving birth, unless, with the sin of Eve, the trouble of that fruitfulness had passed to them. You wish, then, the very punishment to be the indication of sin, so that what the first of women merited through her offense, no woman either is believed to feel without the same iniquity. For there would not be, you say, pain in the one giving birth, unless in the one being born there were sin. How, then, astonished, I am to meet these things, I scarcely measure. For your opinion has applied so many goads in this place that I scarcely deign to be moved to the contest; for there are more sins of understanding in these objections than syllables.

*Augustine:* However glibly and wittily you mock the troubles of the human race, or pretend to mock them, those very troubles have driven you into these straits, that you are compelled to affirm that the paradise of God, even if no one had sinned, would have been full of troubles; which, if, ashamed, you are unwilling to do, you will be pressed by your own dogma to do; which dogma, unless, corrected, you cast away, you will not escape these pressing straits that thrust you into a horrible precipice. For it is asked of you whence you suppose these troubles, which we behold both in the grown and in little ones, flow forth. You answer, from your dogma, that the human race was so divinely established from its beginning. And it is reported to your answer: Then they would have been in paradise too, if no sin had arisen there. Here you will either go headlong, or change your dogma; either lost in brow, or corrected in mind. For either you will fill the place of most famous felicity with a troublesome life, and will not find eyes with which you may dare to look at any Christians whatever; or, cast down into more horrible precipices, you will impute these troubles of the human being to an alien evil nature commingled with us, and will be swallowed up by the tartarean depth of the Manichee; or you will confess that this penalty of troubles exists from a vitiated nature, by the judgment of God punishing, and you will breathe again into the Catholic air. Indeed you also say that *some suppose the human race was made mortal through the sin of Adam*; and you add that you therefore said *some, because I, their prince, blushed to say this; but wrote to Marcellinus that Adam seems to have been made mortal*[^164]. Those who have read or read these things of yours and those of mine, if they are not Pelagians, surely see how your tongue has embraced deceit. For I never thought, never at all said, as you say, that Adam was made mortal so that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die. These very words were objected to Caelestius in the episcopal judgment of Carthage. These were objected also to Pelagius in the episcopal judgment of Palestine[^165]. For this is the question that is turned between us and you concerning this matter: whether Adam, whether he sinned or did not sin, was to die. For who is ignorant that, according to the definition by which he is called immortal who cannot die, but mortal who can die, Adam could die, because he could sin; and therefore could die by the merit of fault, not by the necessity of nature? But according to that definition by which he too is called immortal who has it in his power not to die, who would deny that Adam was founded of this power? Since he who had the power of never sinning, surely had the power of never dying. That, then, is what is said against you—this dogma of yours, by which you think Adam, whether he sinned or did not sin, was to die, is altogether most false. Since these things are so, in what way would I say—what you lie that I said—that Adam was established mortal according to nature, as though he were pressed by the necessity of dying; when he could not be pressed into death except on account of sin? Or how do I pronounce that he could not die, when I know that he died; and surely, if he could not have died, he would not have died? But plainly I pronounce that he could not-die. But it is one thing to be unable to die, another to be able not to die; the former is of a greater immortality, the latter of a lesser. If you distinguish these two, you discern both what you say about Adam, and what we say against you. For you say: Whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die; but we say: As long as he did not sin, he was not to die; and if he had never sinned, he would not have died. Then you recall what is wont to be said against you from Genesis; and about the pain of the one giving birth, by which penalty Eve first was punished, you say something which you wish to be thought, or think, that we say. For we do not say that women would not have been going to suffer pain in giving birth, unless with the sin of Eve the trouble of that fruitfulness had passed to them; since to them passed the trouble not of fruitfulness, but of iniquity. For although a troublesome fruitfulness was made, iniquity, not fruitfulness, did this; for the trouble of the one giving birth descends from the iniquity of the human being, but fruitfulness from the blessing of God. Or, if you did not wish the trouble of fruitfulness to be so understood as though fruitfulness made it, but received it, this is our opinion. But we do not say that even in paradise women giving birth would have suffered pain; nay rather, from this we gather that this pain is a penalty of sin, because it would not have been in that place, where whoever had sinned would not be allowed to remain; which you do not strive to refute, except that you are compelled, with hand set against brow and eyes shut, to fill the paradise of God not only with the lusts of human beings, but also with their torments. But what wonder? Since you wish to fill that place of memorable felicity even with the deaths of human beings, of which few or almost none come to pass without some torment of the body. And since your dogma compels you to say these monstrous things; those who—far be it that they say them, because they hold rather what was anciently handed down to the Church of God, whence it was said: *From a woman was the beginning of sin, and on account of her we all die*[^166]—you dare moreover to mock, and insultingly call me their prince, against your knowledge and conscience. For in no way are you ignorant how many and how great learned men in the Church, and teachers of the Church, before us said that human nature was so divinely established that, if it had not sinned, it would not have been going to die. How, then, am I called the prince of these, whom I do not lead, but follow? But you I do not call the prince of those who assert that Adam was made mortal in such a way that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die, and so try to fill the paradise of holy pleasure, where there was so great rest of soul and body, with the torments of the dying, the funerals of the dead, the griefs of the mourning. You are not their prince: Pelagius and Caelestius, who said these things earlier, hold the principate of this nefarious dogma; whom would that, as you do not lead, so you did not follow!

**VI. 26.** *Julian:* For this very thing, how insane it is, which you say—first, that the pain of giving birth is the companion of sin, when it so plainly looks to the condition of the sexes rather than to the avenging of crimes, that all animals, stained by no sin, suffer these anguishes, these groans, in parturition. By which it appears that it is not an argument of sin, which can be found even without sin. Then, advancing, you bring forward another thing, much more inept. The woman would not feel pain, you say, unless she were a partaker of crimes. But you at once subjoin: But this sin, on account of which the woman feels pain, is detected not in the one giving birth, but in the one being born. For therefore even baptized women, free, you say, are driven from sin; but for the iniquity of the sons whom they bring forth, they are afflicted by the difficulties of their fruitfulness. By which opinion the propagation of sin now no longer runs down from the mother into the offspring, but is poured back into the parents from the one being born. For if the baptized woman feels pains for this reason, that iniquities are found in the little one, the propagation begins to be backward, not forward. But she is not tortured for this reason, that the son has iniquity; but for this reason, that she herself had carried it with her, when she was born. But this evil you had said was taken away from her by grace; if, then, the pain of the one giving birth clung to sin, the removal of sin ought to have healed the torment of childbirths. Or, if this torment cannot be without iniquity, which yet is found in women after Baptism; then iniquity too is not taken away from them by grace, and the pomp of Baptism has become worthless. But if in these mysteries there was that virtue and truth which we believe, not which you feign, and every sin was taken away, yet the pain remains, which is generated by the difficulty of birth; it is manifest that that groan is an index of nature, not of fault; which, with you too consenting, those women suffer whom you confess freed from the sin of the Manichees. Moreover this appeared from the examples of things alone; but if we inspect also the very words of the sentence, lo, they will dissolve your fogs with a brighter brightness than the rays of the sun. For it was not said to the woman: Pains will arise in you, or: I will generate groans for you; so that after the fault their feeling would seem to be established; but: *Multiplying*, he says, *I will multiply your sorrows*. He shows that there was already in the natural rationale that which he promises, in the sinful person, not to found, but to multiply. For things are never multiplied unless they exist; but before they are, they are properly called to be made, but to be increased prematurely. Finally, lest this seem to be ours rather than the very truth's, here the order of words is reported to be kept in all living things. Of the human being too, before he was made: *Let us make*, says God, *the human being to our image and likeness*[^167]; and again of the woman: *God said*, he says, *It is not good for the human being to be alone; let us make for him a help like himself*[^168]. But afterward, when they were made: *He blessed them*, he says, *saying: Increase and multiply, and fill the earth*[^169]. Before they were founded, it was not said: Let them be multiplied; but: Let the human being be made; after there existed one who might receive increases, he consequently adds: *Increase and multiply, and fill the earth*. By this order, then, the groan of childbirths, which was according to nature, so established in the bodies of human beings as in the cattle, is not founded in Eve, but increased, so that she should be afflicted by a peculiar greatness of aroused anguishes. Nor yet would these reach the women of later ages, except by natural moderation and the variety of bodies. There, then, not that the woman should feel pain in giving birth was from sin, but that she should feel too much; just as also at various times we read that debilities of body befell certain people for their sins; but that enlargement of miseries did not overturn the sparingness of the natural measure. Nor yet are all the things contained in that same sentence closed by the vigor of one observing. But a certain part there indicates merit, a certain part designates duty. *Multiplying*, he says, *I will multiply your sorrows and your groaning; in sorrow you will bring forth children*. Thus far the punishment, which not nature, but the person merited. Now hence simply the duty of the later sex is shown: *Your turning*, he says, *will be to your husband, and he will have dominion over you*. This surely does not look to penalty, which, if it were not so, would look to fault; for that the woman should be subject to her husband with a modest affection is an order, not a punishment. For the head, according to the Apostle, of the woman is the man[^170]; because the man was not created for the woman, but the woman for the man[^171]. If, then, she venerates her head with fitting modesty, she keeps the institutions of nature, she does not suffer the torments of sin; to which order if she rebels, she is guilty. There is not, then, in misery the performance of a duty, the neglect of which is in fault.

*Augustine:* We say that the pain of giving birth is a penalty of sin. For we know that God said this without any ambiguity, and said it only to the transgressor of his command, and said it for no other reason than that he was angry that his command had been transgressed. But that you might empty out and frustrate this anger of God, you said that this penalty is so far from being of sin that animals, whose sins are none, suffer groans of this kind and pains in giving birth. The animals indeed did not tell you whether that groan is of those singing or of those in pain. We see that hens about to give birth are more like those singing than those in pain; but after the birth they utter such cries as they are wont in fear; moreover, when they give birth they are in the deepest silence, like doves, and other birds, which become known to those observing. Who, then, knows of mute living things, which cannot indicate what is done within them, whether at the time of giving birth their motion and sound have not only nothing of pain, but even something of pleasure? But what is it to me in this matter to scrutinize the obscure things of nature, when our cause does not hang from it? If mute animals suffer nothing of pain in giving birth, your argument is none; but if they suffer, this very thing is a penalty of the image of God, to be compared to the condition of cattle: but a penalty of the image of God could not be just, unless fault went before. But far be it that I say what you thought must be refuted, as if I said it: that the mother feels pain when she gives birth not by her own, but by the merit of the one being born, and therefore that even faithful women whose sins have been forgiven feel pain by the urgency of the necessity of giving birth. Far be it, I say, that I say this. For because we say that death is the punishment of sin, ought it therefore to be said that it ought not to come about after the forgiveness of sins? For these things, which in a nature vitiated by transgression we say are penalties of sin, therefore remain even after the forgiveness of sins, that the faith we believe concerning the age to come, where these will be none, may be proved. For it would not be faith, if we believed for this reason, that immediately the reward of not grieving and not dying were rendered to us. By which reason being given, that is, that the evils contracted by sin are left to us for the contest of faith, although the guilt of sins is already loosed by Baptism, what force has what you said: *If in the mysteries every sin is taken away, and yet there remains the pain which is generated by the difficulty of birth, it is manifest that that groan is an index of nature, not of fault*? For this, which has no force against us, you surely would not say, if you either had or attended to the force of faith, which is the stronger the more we hope for the things we do not see, and through the patience of miseries await the fullness of blessedness[^172]. *But the very words of God*, you say, *by which he did not say: Pains will arise in you, or: I will generate groans for you, so that after the fault their feeling would seem to be established; but he said: "Multiplying I will multiply your sorrows"; show that there was already in the natural rationale that which he promises, in the sinful person, not to found, but to multiply*; and you add, as though a definitive and general sentence, saying: *For things are never multiplied unless they exist; but before they are, they are properly called to be made, but prematurely to be increased*. Where first I ask of you, how you say that the pains of Eve already existed, which she had not suffered; how, I say, did she already have pains, who felt no pain? But if pains did not exist in her, because she who felt no pain did not have them; then even those which were not could be multiplied; and rightly is what was said received: *I will multiply your sorrows*, that is, I will make them many; which can come about whether something has already begun to be, or has not even begun. In vain, then, did you say: *For things are never multiplied unless they exist*; because behold, in Eve after sin pains were multiplied, which before she sinned were none. And through this, not for this reason did God say: *I will multiply your sorrows*, because some sorrows had already begun to be in her; but because they were going to be manifold, from the time they should begin to be. *But they were*, you say, *in the natural rationale*. If, then, in the natural rationale there already are even those which have not yet arisen, what does it help you that you said: *For this reason God did not say: Pains will arise in you, but: "I will multiply your pains," because they were already in the natural rationale*? For it is answered to you: He could say: They will arise in you; because he was going to multiply those which were already in the natural rationale, not those which had already arisen. Or will you perhaps say: They had already utterly arisen in the very natural rationale? How much more manifestly, then, and more worthy of acceptance, is it said to you: The sons of Adam were already in Adam by natural rationale, when, as blessed John the bishop says: *He committed that great sin, and condemned the whole human race in common*[^173]; or, as his colleague Ambrose says: *Adam was, and in him we all were; Adam perished, and in him all perished*[^174]; if you dare to say, not only: They already were; but also, what we do not dare to say of the sons of Adam, when that sin was committed: The pains of Eve had already arisen, when God threatened that he would multiply them! Yet the pains of Eve were not already in the natural rationale, which those giving birth must of necessity suffer; because it was not necessary for her to suffer them, when she had begun to give birth; for they came by the condemnation of fault, not by the condition of nature. Which you, by denying, what do you do, except that, in the place of that blessedness, where human beings were not permitted to be who already ought to be tortured, you set up the torments of human beings, even with no one sinning? Which I know not with what brow you do; unless because, opposed to paradise, it delights you in a way to dwell against paradise; because Adam too, cast out of paradise, was set over against paradise[^175]. Now, then, opposed to paradise, attend with how great vanity you argue against paradise. Things do not seem to you to be multiplied, except those which in some part already exist; but, before they are, you think they must be said not to be multiplied, but to be made. No multiple things, then, are born, if they are not multiplied by additions, that is, made multiple. And through this, that one who is said in Wisdom to be a manifold spirit[^176], who did not begin to be, but is so from eternity, was not rightly called manifold, because no additions made him manifold. What too will you say about the answer of God to Abraham, where he says: *Multiplying I will multiply your seed like the stars of heaven*[^177]? Where we see God multiplied even the stars of heaven, as he promised he would multiply the seed of Abraham? Did the stars of heaven, then, in order to be multiplied, first begin to be fewer, and were not established manifold in their number, from the time they were established? Why, then, do you not so receive what was said: *Multiplying I will multiply your sorrows*; as though he had said: I will make your sorrows manifold—unless because you act so that, into paradise, against which you are set, you may, if you can, introduce pains, and proclaim in a place of so great blessedness, before sin, that miseries were established? For that Eve, as you affirm, received parturition with pains naturally, before she sinned, you call this natural sparingness; which you do not wish to seem overturned by the fact that more accrued of penalty to the woman, with God avenging, when she had sinned. For you so speak as to say: *But that enlargement of miseries did not overturn the sparingness of the natural measure*. Accordingly, with you teaching, that women in childbirth feel moderate pain is natural sparingness; but what was added to Eve by the merit of sin is an enlargement of miseries. Nor do you see, when you say these things, that if the miseries were enlarged through sin, they had already been established through nature, and the woman, to whom the miseries were enlarged by the merit of sin, was already naturally wretched before sin. Although you call her, by natural sparingness, moderately wretched; yet, whether you will or not, her to whom you say an enlargement of miseries accrued, you surely confess wretched even before this accrual. Behold what the nature of the human being first established by God merits from you; behold what the paradise of God merits from you. For, cast out, and set over against it, you have made such returns, opposed to it, that you say miseries were divinely established in a place of blessedness—and not begun, but grown by sins. What is so contrary as misery to blessedness, and blessedness to misery? Or what does it signify, that the sinner, excluded from paradise, was set over against paradise; except that he was set in misery, which is contrary, without anyone's contradiction or doubt, to blessedness? And what does nature so flee as misery? What does it so seek as blessedness? Finally, the free choice we have concerning this matter is so naturally implanted in us that by no misery can it be taken from us that we are unwilling to be wretched, and wish to be blessed. So much so that even those who by living badly are wretched, indeed wish to live badly, yet do not wish to be wretched, but blessed. This is the free choice immovably fixed in our minds, not by which we wish to act well, for in human iniquity we could lose it, and by divine grace we can receive it; but the free choice by which we wish to be blessed, and are unwilling to be wretched, neither the wretched can lose, nor the blessed. For we all wish to be blessed, which even the philosophers of this age, and the Academics doubting of all things, by the testimony of their patron Tully, were compelled to confess; and they said this one thing was that which needs no disputation, which there is no one who does not seek. This free choice is aided through the grace of God, that what we naturally will, that is, to live blessedly, we may be able to have by living well. And you say that miseries—although moderate, yet miseries, contrary to blessedness with no one denying, no one doubting—were naturally established in the first works of God, with no one's sin preceding, so that, namely, the penalty of the sinful woman, on account of which it was said: *Multiplying I will multiply your sorrows*, is not the establishment of miseries, which, as you say, was already in nature; but the enlargement which accrued from the penalty. What now shall I deal with you concerning the following words of God, where, after he said what pertained to penalty: *In sorrow you will bring forth children*; he at once added: *And your turning will be to your husband, and he will have dominion over you*[^178]? What need is there that I contend with you thence, whether this dominion of the husband is a penalty of the woman or the order of nature? Which order, however, God did not mention when he founded, but when he punished. But, as I said, what need is there to linger over that which, whichever it be, does not impede our cause? Granted that God, from the punishment which he was inflicting on the woman, was suddenly turned to commanding, and said, by commanding, not by condemning: *Your turning will be to your husband, and he will have dominion over you*; what is this to the question which we treat in this disputation concerning the punishment of the sinful woman? I deal with you concerning the miseries, which, cast out of paradise and opposed to it, you try to place in paradise; and to attribute them not to the merits of those sinning, but to God the founder of natures, as though he established them too naturally, with your shame perishing, and your mouth blaspheming. Say now what you strive to persuade also concerning the man's punishment; since it is already enough how this woman, who before sin was naked and was not confounded, has stripped and confounded you.

**VI. 27.** *Julian:* But let these things about the woman suffice; let us pass to the duties of the man. *To Adam*, he says, *he said: Cursed is the earth in your works; in sorrow you will eat it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it will bring forth to you, and you will eat the hay of your field; in the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*[^179]. Here it was not said: I will multiply your thorns, or your sweats; but they are said as though then first created. But the odium of this too is dissolved by a labor no greater than that of his wife; for at the beginning it is not the seed of the human being, but the soil that is cursed: *In your works*, he says, *the earth will be cursed*. What had the fields deserved (which surely could have nothing of the propagation of Adam), that for the sin of another's will they should receive the reproach of a curse? Or was it that, even by the example of the very turf, it might be taught that there can be a curse where there is no fault? For if in the man there is sin, in the crop a curse, it is manifest that crimes do not always cling to detriments. If, then, the earth is cursed for this reason, that he who had sinned might be afflicted, and yet there is not iniquity there where the curse is contained; why would it not follow, on the same condition—even if something of troubles were taught to have been brought upon our nature after the sin of the first man—that yet it would follow that those being born are not wretched for this reason, that they should be convicted as guilty; but that, by the commemoration of the first sin, an affliction succeeding to those whom it had not made guilty might indicate a caution against evil imitation? Since the earth too would be shown for this reason to have borne the reproach of a curse, that it might charge the evil of another's will, not that it might engender a participation in its crime; unless perhaps we believe the earth dearer to God than innocence, so that, while it does not suffer the clod to be polluted by another's wickedness, it yet permits infancy to be condemned. The curse, then, is directed against the earth, nor yet is this very thing left wrapped up. For it is set forth with what end such a sentence was aimed, or how the soil was called cursed. *In sorrow*, he says, *you will eat it all the days of your life*. By what kind, then, it explains itself, take note; the earth is said cursed, not because anything could be punished in it, but by this name the opinion of a grieving mind is shown; so that, since he knew it was barren for the merits of its cultivator, the sickness of the hungering worker might impute to the earth what he himself had merited, and, afflicted, call it cursed, whose richness was therefore shut off, that that transgressor might confess neither nature, nor the earth, but his will and his person to be liable to the curse. *Thorns*, he says, *and thistles it will bring forth to you*. He was not content to say: *Thorns and thistles it will bring forth*; but he added: *to you*. For among other shrubs and bushes of brambles it had once, when bidden, brought them forth; but then, that the man might be pricked, it is promised to be more thorny than usual. Which could vehemently chastise Adam, whom, after the fountains and meadows of paradise, even one bramble could offend. But *in the sweat of your face you will eat your bread*; I do not see that it pertains much to trouble; since indeed it is even a natural aid that the limbs of those working be refreshed by sweat. But that labor lay upon the cultivation even before sin, the reading itself attests. For so it says: *And the Lord God took the human being whom he made, and put him in paradise, to work it and to keep it*[^180]. If, then, even in paradise God was unwilling that he should take his food exactly without labor, but, by the imposition of work, roused the industry which he had implanted; what new thing do we believe befell him, if he felt sweat, who experienced labor? But there follows: *Until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*. This last part of the sentence, surely, like that of the woman, looks to an indication, not to a punishment; nay rather, as the matter indicates, it consoles the human being by the end promised. For because above he had mentioned pains, labors, sweats, the feeling of which nature, the excess the person had taken on; lest this seem to be extended forever, the indicated term mitigates the sickness; as if he said: But you will not always suffer these things; but *until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are*, he says, *and into earth you will go*. Why, after he said: *until you turn into the earth from which you were taken*, did he not subjoin: Because you have sinned and transgressed my precepts? For this ought to have been said, if the dissolution of bodies pertained to crimes. But what did he say? *Because earth you are*, he says, *and into earth you will go*. He showed the cause why he was to return into the earth: *Because earth you are*, he says. But how he was earth, the earlier discourse had made plain: *Because from earth*, he says, *you were taken*. If, then, God said this was the reason for returning into the earth, that he had been taken from the earth, and that he should be taken could not look to iniquity; without doubt it was not of iniquity, but of mortal nature, that he who was not eternal should be dissolved in the part of the body. That barrenness of trees, then, that fertility of brambles, that increased calamity of painful birth, were brought upon the persons of human beings, not upon the race. Finally, when Cain and Abel were already born, of one nature both, but of different wills, neither did it profit Cain, sinning of his own accord, that the sins of his father had not pressed him, nor did it harm Abel that his parents had transgressed; but each of his own judgment, by a varied purpose, with a varied end, published that no natural prejudice of virtue or of vice had been in him. For, performing the office of priests, they offered victims to God their founder. But an equal obedience, an unequal care, distinguished them. This the divine sentence made plain, which showed the gift of Abel pleasing to itself; but, offended with Cain, opened the cause of its indignation, saying that he had offered well, but divided badly[^181]. And without delay the profane mind flared into envy, and, weighed down by the holiness of his brother, gratified his envy with parricide. Thus on the first occasion it became clear that death is not an evil, since a just man first of all dedicated it. Nor yet did the audacity of the guilty mind escape the anger of God. He is questioned about his brother, convicted of crime, sentenced to punishment, and, beyond that terror which lay upon him for his notable cruelty, is punished also by the curse of the earth: *Cursed*, he says, *are you from the earth, which opened its mouth to receive the blood of your brother from your hand; for you will work the earth, and it will not add its strength to give to you*[^182]. Lo, again the barrenness of the earth is decreed in the punishment of the cultivator. But innumerable torments of the race are promised in Deuteronomy. What then? Did the brambles of our lands, for the cutting away of which the cultivator keeps watch armed with a hook, germinate through the parricide of Cain? And because every lord of a thorny field is by you thought liable to the sin which was punished by the fertility of thorns; will all little ones now be said not only to have eaten apples, although they are born without teeth, but also to have shed the blood of Abel? It certainly appears to what frenzy the propagation-theory of the Manichees comes; which, since it has nothing but madness, the gravity of the Catholics laughs at your arguments; but the affection of the same weeps over your ruins.

*Augustine:* Surely your so long and most laborious disputation about the penalty of the first man does nothing, except that, this being extenuated, the fault itself too may be extenuated, which was condemned by the infliction of this penalty. And you do this on account of the words of my book, to which you reply, which you propose to yourself as if to be refuted, where I said: *That sin, then, which changed the human being himself in paradise for the worse, because it is much greater than we can judge, is drawn from everyone being born*. So, then, lest that great sin seem to be such that the nature could be changed for the worse by it, you contend that the punishment which it merited is light and almost none. Hence it is that you twist the earth cursed in the works of the transgressor to the depravity of your dogma; hence it is that you assert that thorns and thistles were established even before the human being sinned; whereas God does not name these among his first institutions, but threatens them in the punishment of the sinner; hence it is that the sweat of the one laboring, lest it should seem to pertain much to trouble, you said was even a natural aid, namely that the limbs of those working be refreshed by sweat; as though God, in saying these things, did not inflict a punishment for sin, but bestowed in addition a reward. Although we would say this fittingly, if you so praised the sweat of labor as to say it was then established; but now you affirm that the human being was placed in paradise even before sin in such a way that he was not without labor in working the earth; as though that firmness, and no infirmity of the body, could not work, not only without labor, but even with pleasure of the mind, that which could delight. But why you said this you could not conceal; for you speak most openly, when you add: *What new thing do we say befell the one who felt sweat, who experienced labor?* Did it so please you to send into the most quiet place of the blessed not only the sorrows of parturition in women, but also the sweat of labor in men, that you would say nothing new befell the one condemned, with God condemning? Do you so mock and despise the severity of God, that what he inflicted as a penalty, you assert was bestowed naturally? If you say nothing new befell the human being, to whom God said: *In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread*[^183]; deny that God said this in condemning. Or will you say: He indeed condemned the human being by these words, but nothing new befell the human being from this? Then he condemned, but this one was not condemned? Was the impetus of vengeance frustrated, as though God hurled a weapon and could not strike whom he willed? Nay, you say, he was both condemned, and nothing new befell him. Here it is difficult to hold back laughter. For if he was condemned, and nothing new befell him; then he was wont to be condemned, and through this he was wont also to sin; for he would not be condemned unjustly. Or, because no one doubts that he then first sinned, was he wont before to be condemned unjustly? For you have not confessed, as you said of the one giving birth, that at least this new thing befell the human being, that, as to the woman the pain of parturition, so to the man the sweat of labor was added; for in this way, by this addition which was not before, you would grant that something new befell him. But when you say: *What new thing befell him?*—whom yet you confess was condemned; what else do you affirm but that he was so wont to be condemned? Or if we do not say a thing is wont to be done, except what we know was done continually; surely you must grant that he was at least once condemned before, to whom you assert that nothing new befell, because he was so condemned. Where you see into what precipices you have advanced. Return, then, from the precipice of your laborious disputation, and do not bring labors and pains into the seats of happy joys, and into the place of ineffable rest. What of the fact that you try also to introduce the death of the body into paradise in such a way that you say it was promised, or rather indicated, to the transgressor as a benefit, where God said: *Earth you are, and into earth you will go*[^184]; as though the human being did not know that he had been so established that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die; and God gave him this knowledge then, when he condemned him for the iniquity committed? For disputing about these words of God, where he says: *In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread, until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*. *This indeed*, you say, *last part of the sentence, like that of the woman, pertains to indication, not to punishment; nay rather, as the matter indicates, it consoles the human being. For because above*, you say, *he had mentioned pains, labors, sweats, the feeling of which nature, the excess the person had taken on; lest this seem extended forever, the indicated term mitigates the sickness; as if he said: But you will not always suffer these things, but until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for "earth you are, and into earth you will go."* Saying these things, you try to persuade that the human being was so created that, even if he remained in the uprightness of life in which he was created, yet, mortal by the necessity of nature, he would at some time die; but that this was not indicated to him except at the time of his condemnation, so that his trouble, lest it be thought eternal, might receive consolation from the promised end. Adam, then, would think he was not to die, if God had not indicated this to him: but God would not have indicated this to him, unless the sinner had to be condemned; he would remain, then, in this error, by which he believed himself eternal, or never to die, unless, by the merit of sin, he came to the wisdom by which the human being knows himself. Do you perceive what you say? Take another. Beyond doubt Adam, not knowing that he was to die—which, unless he had sinned, he surely would not know—yet, if he had been unwilling to sin, even not knowing this, would be blessed, and, believing things contrary to truth, would not be wretched. Do you hear what you say? Take a third too. If, in the time of his righteousness, Adam believed he was not even going to die in body, if he had not violated God's precept, but learned that he was to die then, when he violated it; this we believe, which, when he was just, he believed; but this you believe, which he did not deserve to know except when unjust. Our error, then, stands on the side of righteousness, and your wisdom on the side of iniquity. Do you understand what you say? Take a fourth too. If God did not indicate to that blessed and just human being that the death of his body would be, but indicated it to the wretched and the sinner; it is more fittingly believed that he wished to torture him also with the fear of death, judging him, namely, worthy of this torment too. For, as nature itself cries out, death is more feared than labors; for all human beings labor that they may not die, if such a choice be set before them, that they die at once if they do not labor; but how rare is one found who would rather die than labor? Finally Adam himself, through so many years, preferred to labor, rather than, by not laboring and dying of hunger, to end at once both life and labor. For by what other natural sense did even Cain fear death more than labor? By what other sense do judges, by judgments not unjust nor inhuman, condemn lesser crimes to the labor of the mines, greater ones to death? But whence are the martyrs proclaimed with such great glory, that they died for righteousness, except because it is a greater virtue to spurn death than labor? On which account the Lord did not say: *No one has greater love* than that he labor; but: *that he lay down his life for his friends*[^185]. If, then, it is greater love to die than to labor for friends; who is so blind as not to see that the penalty of labor is less than that of death? Or if the human being ought to fear labor more than death; how is not nature itself wretched, which fears death more than labor? And you, not considering these things, say that the human being, his death being indicated to him, was consoled, lest he think his labor eternal; whereas, if your dogma were true, by which you affirm that Adam, even if he had not sinned, was to die, therefore this ought not to have been indicated to him before he began to be afflicted by the penalty of condemnation, lest God torture him with the fear of death before he had sinned; but then, when, after he sinned, he judged him most worthy of penalty, he would indicate also that he was to die, so that the just God, the avenger of the evil committed, might increase the same penalty even with the fear of death. Whoever, then, understands these words of God, by which Adam was punished, when it was said: *Earth you are, and into earth you will go*, according to the Catholic faith, and does not wish to introduce into paradise especially the death of the body, lest he introduce also diseases, by whose pitiable variety we see the dying afflicted, and be compelled to fill the paradise of holy pleasure and of spiritual and corporeal felicity, to which you are not ashamed to be opposed, with pains, labors, griefs; as you are compelled, and find no way by which you may go out, as long as you are unwilling to change so impious a dogma; he, then, who, as I said, receives and understands these words of God according to the Catholic faith, as he discerns the penalty of labor in that which was said: *In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread*, so he discerns also the penalty of death in that which follows: *Until you turn into the earth from which you were taken; for earth you are, and into earth you will go*; and thus receives the saying, as though it were said: I indeed took you from the earth, and made you a human being; and surely I could have brought it about that the same earth which I made living should never be compelled to lack the life I gave; but because you are earth, that is, you willed to live according to the flesh which was taken from the earth, not according to me, who took you from the earth; you will labor in the earth, until you return into it; and therefore you will return into the earth, because you are earth; and by a just penalty you will go into the earth from which you were made, because you did not obey the spirit by which you were made. This sound and Catholic understanding is especially recognized from this, that it does not compel us to fill with deaths the earth of the living, and the earth of the happy with all the most laborious and most grievous evils which human beings suffer in this corruptible body, and, by not bearing them, are forced to die. For you cannot say that human beings would have died gently in paradise, if no one had sinned; because this too is against you. For if they then died gently, and now die so harshly, human nature has been changed through the sin of the human being; which you, denying, are consequently compelled to send into the place of that so great felicity and joy such deaths altogether as now are; and through this also innumerable kinds of diseases, so grievous and so intolerable, that by them human beings are driven into death. Which face of paradise, if it pours over and confounds your faces with any shame; you who are unwilling to confess that our nature could be changed through sin, change rather your sentence; and confess with the Apostle that the body is dead because of sin[^186]. Say with the Church of God: *From a woman was the beginning of sin, and on account of her we all die*[^187]. Recognize with the Church of God that *the corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^188]. For before sin, in paradise, the body was not such that the soul should be weighed down by it. Sing with the Church of God: *The human being became like to vanity, his days pass away like a shadow*[^189]. For he who was made to the likeness of God would not become like to vanity except through sin, so that, by the course of his ages and the incursion of death, his days should pass away like a shadow. Do not pour over the most serene light of truth the clouds of your error; the paradise of God, which the hearts of the faithful ought to love, they ought not to embitter. For what offends you, I beg, what offends you that memorable place of the blessed and the quiet, that you should wish to fill it with the deaths of human beings, and through these with all the evils with which we discern the straits and necessities of the dying to abound—with eyes shut, with a most shameless brow, a most obstinate mind, a most talkative tongue; lest you be compelled to confess that, into these miseries with which you see the human race crammed, from the wailings of little ones to the gaspings of the decrepit, human nature collapsed through the greatest sin of the first man? And because you discern that it is unjust that the penalty of parents should pass into their descendants without fault, grant that the fault too passed. For that the fault of the first man was the greatest—which you for this reason, as much as you could, tried to extenuate, lest on account of it human nature should be believed able to be changed—therefore that that fault was the greatest, I prove not only by the very miseries of the human race, which begin from the cradles of infants, but also by you yourself. For you too, in the second book of this work of yours, set down the greatest form of sin in the first man, so that, on the contrary, the greatest form of righteousness might be commended in Christ[^190]; which you seem to me to have forgotten you said; for if you had remembered, you would surely never strive so wordily to lessen the sin of Adam. But I demonstrate that that sin was the greatest by the very greatness of the penalty; for it is not greater, than that he should be cast out of paradise, and separated from the tree of life lest he live forever; with the anguishes of this life added too, so that his days both groaned in labors, and passed away like a shadow. Indeed the very hereditary calamity of the human race, from infants to the old, is a witness; which miseries would not have the condition of punishment, unless they were drawn by the contagion of sin. About which contagion you obstinately fight with us, both that it be not believed, and you extenuate both the sin itself of the first man, and his penalty, and try, with a most shameless and most impious contention, to introduce pains, labors, and deaths into paradise. You say too: *If the earth is cursed for this reason, that he who had sinned might be afflicted, and yet there is not iniquity there where the curse is contained; why would it not follow, on the same condition too, even if something of troubles were taught to have been brought upon our nature after the sin of the first man, that yet it would follow that those being born are not to be wretched for this reason, that they should be convicted as guilty; but that, by the commemoration of the first sin, an affliction succeeding, to those whom it had not made guilty, might indicate a caution against evil imitation?* I see by what straits you are pressed. You are compelled to confess the miseries of those being born, since the evidence of things does violence to you, which, set before the eyes of all, you are not allowed to deny; but that these miseries of those being born would have been even in paradise, if no one had sinned, you indeed wish to assert; but you discern that you cannot persuade this to human beings having any sort of heart. There remains, then, that on account of the sin of the first man you confess the human race made wretched; but, dreading to say this absolutely: *If something of troubles*, you say, *were taught to have been brought upon our nature after the sin of the first man*. What is: *If something were taught*? Is really a most manifest thing, which you too are now compelled to feel, not taught? Or must we return to that point, whence you meditate gradually to flee through these words; understanding by what intolerable absurdity it is believed that the miseries of those being born would have been even in paradise, if no one had sinned? Which if you shudder to say, since truly it is vehemently to be shuddered at; why do you say: *even if it were taught*; when, without any doubt, it is taught, not that something of troubles, but that all the troubles of those being born were brought upon our nature after the sin of the first man, nay, on account of the sin of the first man? But: *Not*, you say, *for this reason are those being born wretched, that they may be convicted guilty*. Nor do I say: For this reason are those being born wretched, that they may be convicted guilty; but I rather say: For this reason are they convicted to be guilty, since they are wretched. For God is just—which you assiduously say against yourself, and do not know; God is just, I say; and therefore he would not allow those being born either to make or to be made wretched, unless he knew them guilty. Whence the Catholic faith understands not otherwise what the Apostle says: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^191]. Which you, unwilling to refer to the bond of origin, try to twist to the example of imitation; from which it comes about that, when it is said to you: Why, then, do infants, who have sinned by no imitation, attest the miseries of the human race in the very beginning of their age, by their innumerable and various evils? You, as by a most grievous and unavoidable pressure of the stomach, burst out into these vomits, and say: Not because they are guilty are those who are born wretched; but that, admonished by this misery, they may beware to imitate the sin of the first man. For so I thought it should be said more plainly and explicitly, what you said more obscurely and more perplexedly. But however you say this, who does not see that, in your zeal for defending your opinion, you altogether refuse to attend to what you say? Were innocent human beings, I ask you, to be punished by misery, not because they had any sin, but that they might not have it? Eve, then, ought first to have been made wretched rather than guilty, that by her misery she might be admonished not to consent to the serpent. Adam too ought first to have been punished by the evil of misery, lest he consent to his wife seduced to the evil of sin. For it pleases you that crimes be guarded against by preceding punishments, not avenged by following ones; and thus, inversely, not because there is sin, but lest there be sin, not guilt, but innocence is punished. Correct, I beg, this perverse and preposterous sentence; for you would surely correct your tunic, if perhaps you clothed the right side in the left. This I have said, because you wish sins to be guarded against by preceding punishments; whereas preceding sins are wont and ought to be avenged by following punishments. Then tell us how we may admonish calamitous infants to behold their misery, lest they imitate the sin of the first man; who can neither yet imitate anyone, nor be admonished. For the cursed earth (whence you take an example for this raving of yours, that those being born could so be made wretched that they might beware the sins of their begetters, although they do not draw from them original sin, just as the earth was cursed on account of the human being's penalty, although it itself does not have fault)—why do you not attend that, as it does not have fault, so from that curse it does not have penalty; but when the earth is cursed for the sinning human being, it itself becomes his penalty? But those being born, when they are wretched, themselves feel their miseries; themselves drawing no sin, as you suppose; without merit, if this is so, they beyond doubt sustain penalties, who can neither yet, as I said, be admonished anything, nor imitate the sin of the first man, on account of which they ought to be admonished. Or must we wait until, by growing, they reach free choice, when they may feel the one admonishing, and, by beholding their misery, not imitate another's fault? But where do we place so many who, to the day of their death, do not know who Adam was, or whether he was, or what he committed? Where do we place so many who die before they reach the age in which they may feel the one admonishing? Where do we place those who are born of so insipid and foolish a wit that not even when grown can they be admonished such a thing with any fruit? Surely all these are punished with so great a misery without any merit, without any usefulness. Where is the justice of God? Which if you considered, you would never believe that those being born are so wretched without any merit of original sin. But since you spoke with a condition; for you did not say: Since it is taught, but you said: *If something of troubles were taught to have been brought upon our nature after the sin of the first man*; you are ready, as I think, to say: It is not taught. And through this it remains that you say that the evils which we see little ones suffer would have been even in paradise, if no one had sinned, lest you confess that they arose on account of the sin of the first man. So, while you seek to escape these knots, and to become slippery from our hands, you will stand immovable against paradise; to which you are so opposed that, for disturbing its felicity and quiet, you send in, with a most audacious mouth and a most abandoned brow, both the pains of those giving birth, and the labors of those working, and the tossings of the ailing, and the diseases of the dying. But in praise of death you think you have found something great to say: namely *that on the first occasion it became clear that death is not an evil, since a just man first of all dedicated it*. Then render an account how the just man would not have what he might dedicate, unless the unjust man built it. For the author and effecter of that death was Cain, not Abel. He therefore dedicated it who fabricated it. For the death of a good man was the evil work of an evil man. But this man, who suffered evil for good, dedicated not death, but martyrdom, bearing the figure of him whom the people of the Jews, like an evil carnal brother, killed. For this reason, then, Abel is glorious, not because he took anything of good from his brother, but because, by patiently dying for righteousness, he used his evil well. For just as transgressors are punished by using the good of the law badly; so, on the contrary, the martyrs are crowned by using the evil of death well. Accordingly, if you do not disdain to say what I see you do not know, death is an evil in all the dying; but in the dead, an evil to some, a good to others. This those followed who committed to writing even praiseworthy disputations about the good of death. The death, then, of the just Abel dwelling in rest is not only not an evil, but is even a good. But you have brought into paradise not the rest of the good dead, but the torments of the dying, lest there be there the rest of the good. Or if you say: If no one had sinned, in paradise human beings would die without torment; at least because outside paradise hardly anyone dies without torment, at last, convicted and contorted, confess that human nature was changed for the worse by the sin of the first man.

**VI. 28.** *Julian:* Finally, what shall be said about the serpent itself? Do you think the devil was punished by that curse, or this common reptile animal? That is, that sentence which says to the serpent: *Cursed are you above all the cattle, and above all the beasts which are on the earth; upon your breast and your belly you will go, and you will eat earth all the days of your life*[^192]—do you think it was fulfilled in the punishment of the devil, or of this beast which is led out of its caverns by the warmths of spring? If in this serpent, whose form is stretched into a smooth body, and you say that by the merit of that fault it was made earth-eating; then the propagation of iniquity lies even upon all the beasts, which you think cannot be drawn down except through the lust of those copulating; and it comes about that you assert that even the lust of serpents, and thence of irrational animals, was planted by the devil, and, the opinion of the Manichee being uncovered, you clang your song at us. But if you profess that whatever is said to the serpent was intelligibly fulfilled in the devil; you will without doubt grant that neither is this which is there brought forward for vengeance now an indication of guilty serpents, nor does the devil eat earth corporeally; but although then he seized the ministry of the dragon, and afterward fatherly severity broke this as it were a weapon, by which he had used to wound the human being; yet the sin resided in the will of the agent alone. But that foods, and thorns, and sweats were first established naturally, afterward in some increased judicially, but came to our age without the wonderment of that sin; these things are so plain that they need no further assertion at all.

*Augustine:* What is it that you twist your viperous cunning even about the serpent? For who, fittingly understanding these words of the divine book which you mentioned, does not see that that sentence was brought forth rather against the devil, who used the serpent for what he willed, as he could, than against the animal itself, of whatever earthly kind? But because the devil worked his seductive words not through himself, but through the serpent, therefore God spoke to the serpent what would befit the signifying of the devil's malice, and the nature of this one was a figure of that one. Accordingly, what God said to the serpent: *Cursed are you above all the cattle, and all the beasts which are on the earth; upon your breast and your belly you will go, and you will eat earth all the days of your life*, and the rest; these things are understood and expounded the better, the more fittingly they are received of the devil. But since, even according to a right faith, these things are wont to be disputed in many ways, nor does it pertain to the cause now which of them I should rather choose to bring forth, it is enough that I answer you that the nature of the devil in no way pertains to the connection and succession of propagation, where the question of original sin is turned. But about thorns and the sweat of the one laboring, how shamelessly you affirm that they were established before there was sin, I believe our earlier answer persuades those who read. For you wish to make such a paradise that it can by no reasoning be said to be God's, but yours. Yet, although you said thorns were established before sin, you did not dare to introduce them into paradise; nay rather, you expressed that they were not there; where, however, you wished to set labor, whose burden, even if it does not prick, presses. But yet, if it pleases you too that thorns do not befit paradise; would it really befall human nature so that it was first there where thorns are not, and now is there where they are, with no change of blessedness into misery; or would it have so befallen without any merit of sin? Let it, then, compel you to recognize original sin, at least such as you could not deny the punishment to be, unless you believe God to be unjust.

**VI. 29.** *Julian:* Yet, lest we seem to have negligently passed over anything, take another point. It is found that this pain of parturition is varied according to the bodies and strengths of the parents. Surely the women of barbarians, and of shepherds, who are hardened by exercise, give birth in the midst of journeys with such ease that, without interrupting the labor of traveling, they care for their offspring, and at once carry them—they who, withering not at all from the difficulty of giving birth, transfer the burdens of the belly to their shoulders; and generally the poverty of lowly women does not require the work of midwives; but on the other side the rich are softened by delicacies, and the more attendants each has solicitous about her, the more she both learns and longs to be sick, and thinks she needs as much service as she abounds in. The hands of rich husbands, surely, never experience the trouble of the first man among brambles; nay rather, trusting in their resources, they think it unworthy to spend any moment on the prayers of fertility, and, beyond the fear even of hunger, settling themselves by the enlargement of their possessions, they sometimes command, as the poet says: *Unyoke the oxen, while you cast in the truffles*[^193]. If, then, both the pain of those giving birth looks to the institutions of nature—which is taught both by the example of brute animals and by the property of the divine sentence; if the interception of fruits, and the springing up of thorns, was indeed created among the rest, yet became denser and more troublesome for certain persons; if, finally, as the very difficulty of the one giving birth, so also the thorniness of the clods is varied according to bodies and regions; finally, if that anguish persists after grace in the bowels of women, but the luxury of the wealthy is utterly ignorant of it; if the reason why the dissolution of the body would follow was expressed, and applied to skills rather than to errors; here too it appears that all things harmonize with the truth of the Catholics, and that neither the women nor the thorns profit you anything.

*Augustine:* When you were disputing about the penalty which God inflicted on the first sinners, you had long ago so passed away from the woman that you said: *Let these things about the woman suffice*. Why, then, did you not keep the faith of your promise? Behold, after so many things you return to her; behold, those things you had said do not suffice for your wordiness—those things which you had said about the woman should suffice. But unless you were so wordy, whence would you fill the eight books which you render to my one? But say what you please; behold, after the sufficiency you promised, we have patiently heard your abundance too. For why should you lose such pretty things, which afterward came into your mind? Although that too ought to have come about, that from your book, which was still in your hands to be completed, you should take away what you had said: *Let these things about the woman suffice*; lest you be read so unbecomingly contrary to your own promise. But go on, bring forward into the midst, against what you promised, the things you afterward thought of. Say that the labor of parturitions is varied according to the bodies and strengths of those giving birth; and so describe barbarian and rustic women in the ease of giving birth, as though they did not even seem to give birth; and through this that they feel in giving birth not a small pain, but none. But if it is so, what does it help you? Do you not speak against yourself, you who said that these pains are so natural that without them Eve could not give birth, even if she had persisted in paradise with no iniquity perpetrated? Will you say that your barbarian and rustic women are happier in this matter than that first woman, so that in these troublesome lands they give birth without pain, which in paradise, if she gave birth, she could not? As though in these the womanly nature were changed for the better than it had been established, and human exercise availed more in changing it than divine working in creating it. But if in your words you do not wish it understood that fierce and rustic women give birth without pains, and so grant them a birth tolerable and easy, yet in such a way that you confess that they feel pain when they give birth; is it therefore no penalty, because it is less? Whether, then, these women feel less pain when they give birth, or pains equal to other women, or even, for some of them, greater pains, which, by a strength gathered through exercise, they bear marvelously, nor are wearied and weakened by that torment; yet they feel pain, and surely all who feel pain, whether they feel more or less pain, suffer without any doubt greater or lesser penalties, but penalties nonetheless. But if you thought of yourself, I do not say as a Christian, but as a human being of any sort, you would more easily contend that there is no paradise of God, than make it penal by your sacrilegious disputation. Elegantly indeed you defend rich men from the hereditary labor of the first man, ignorant—or pretending not to know—that the rich labor more bitterly with thoughts than the poor with works. For by the name of sweat the sacred Scripture signified labor in general, from which no human being is immune, since some labor with hard works, others with harassing cares. To the same labors pertain also the studies of all who learn. And what bears these, except this earth, which its former, when he founded the first man, had not made burdensome? But now, as it is written: *The corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the mind that ponders many things; and with difficulty do we estimate the things that are on earth, and the things that are in our sight we find with labor*[^194]. Whether, then, the human being studies to learn useful or useless teachings, with the corruptible body weighing down the soul, he must labor. Wherefore even so this earth brings forth thorns for him. Nor let the rich be said to be free from these thorns; especially since those thorns in the Gospel, by which the seeds cast are choked lest they reach fruitfulness, God the teacher expounds to be the cares of this life and the solicitudes of riches[^195]; who surely calls not only the poor, but also the rich, saying: *Come to me, all you who labor*. But why does he call them, except what he says a little after: *And you will find rest for your souls*[^196]? When will this be, except when the corruption of bodies, which now weighs down souls, will be no more? But now the poor labor, the rich labor, the just labor, the unjust labor, the great labor, the small labor, from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all. For this age is so malignant that, unless we have gone out from here, the promised rest cannot be present to us; although through the transgression of the first man labor came upon his descendants; yet, even with the guilt of that transgression which we drew already loosed, labor remains for the contest, that the testing of faith may be exercised. For we must contend with vices, and in the very contest labor, until it be granted us to have no adversary. Wherefore, that as good fighters we may be brought to the rewards, the punishments are turned into battles. For little ones too, with original guilt loosed, labors remain for this reason, although they are taught to have arisen from that very guilt; because even so it pleased God to prove the faith of the elders, by whom they are offered to him to be regenerated. What sort and how great would the faith of invisible things be, if an invisible reward followed at once; and if, rather than the promised rest being deferred, the business of faith were carried on with the heart, not with the eyes; and so the age to come, where there will be no labors, which we do not yet see, might be both more sincerely believed and more desirably sought? And through this God, by a marvelous kindness, turns our labors, that is, our penalties, to our advantages. Wishing to refute these things, you labor uselessly; for you labor by bringing forth thorns, not by plucking them out; but we labor that we may pluck out your thorns, as far as the Lord grants. Unless perhaps you boast that you do not labor, since you write so many books with great ease of wit, and, like those barbarian and rustic women their offspring, so you too bring forth thorns without difficulty. But I think you boast emptily of your ease of wit; you labor altogether: for how do you act so as not to labor, you who even try to introduce labors into paradise? For surely, the more impossible this thing is proved, the more your labor is found both more abundant and more empty.

**VI. 30.** *Julian:* I will surely not assail those who maintain that Adam, if he had been obedient to the word, could have been transferred to immortality as a reward. For we read that Enoch and Elijah were transferred, so as not to see death. But the institutions of nature are one thing, the rewards of obedience another. For the merit of one is not of such weight as to disturb all the things that are naturally established. The inborn mortality, then, would have exercised itself in the rest, even if that first man had migrated from longevity into eternity. The matter is held not by indubitable conjecture, but by a sure example, since the sons of Enoch could not, by the immortality of their parent, be claimed from the condition of dying. Nor perhaps let it be thought to occur, that if not the sinners, yet the just at least would all escape to immortality without the intervention of bodily dissolution: since Abel, the first of the just, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the bands of the saints, both in the New and in the Old Testament, taught both their merit by their virtues, and their nature by death. A thing set in the absolute the authority of Christ too confirmed. For when the Sadducees brought to him a question by the example of the woman married seven times, asking, if the rousing of bodies were believed, by which husband especially she was to be claimed, he answered: *You err, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God; for in the resurrection they will neither marry nor take wives; for neither will they die*[^197]. Conscious of his own work, he expressed why he had instituted marriages, namely that offspring might supply the losses of death; but that bountiful fecundity would at once cease, when greedy death had ceased. If, then, with Christ who founded it as witness, fertility was created for this, that it might struggle with frailty, and this condition of marriage was ordained before sin; it appears also that mortality looks not to transgression, but to nature, to which marriages too are read to look. That law, then, which was promulgated, that is: *On whatever day you eat of the forbidden thing, you will die the death*[^198], is understood as the penal death, not the bodily; threatening sins, not seeds; which none incurs but transgression, none escapes but emendation. But that it is said to be inflicted on the day of sin, is the custom of Scripture, which is wont to call the condemned one the one to be condemned. Hence it is that in the Gospel the Lord says: *Everyone*, he says, *who does not believe in me is already judged; because he has not believed in the name of the only-begotten Son of God*[^199]. Not that unbelief denying Christ was, before the time of judgment, joined to perpetual punishment, since all who come to faith were first unbelievers; but that the censure of the one commanding might appear, sins are said to be already punishments. Finally, that Adam himself was expiated, with emendation following, both that book which is called Wisdom, and the opinion of many, assert[^200].

*Augustine:* If you do not assail, as you say, those who think that Adam, if he had been obedient to the word, could have been transferred to immortality as a reward, distinguish immortality, namely the greater from the lesser. For that too is not absurdly called immortality, by which anyone can not die, if he does not do that whence he may die, although he can also do it. In this immortality was Adam: this immortality he lost by the merit of transgression. This was supplied to him from the tree of life, from which he was not prohibited when he received the good law, lest he sin, but when he sinned by an evil will; for then he was cast out of paradise, lest he stretch out his hand to the tree of life, and eat, and live forever. Whence it must be understood that from this tree of life he was wont to take a sacrament; from the rest, nourishment. For only from the tree which was called of the discernment of good and evil was he commanded not to eat. Why, then, should it be thought that he did not eat of the tree of life, since it was both far more excellent than the rest, and, that one alone excepted in which he sinned, he had received the power of eating from all? For these are the words of God commanding: *From every tree*, he says, *which is in paradise, you shall eat; but from the tree of knowing good and evil, you shall not eat of it*[^201]. And likewise these are the words of him condemning: *Because you have heard*, he says, *the voice of your wife, and have eaten of the tree, of which I had commanded you, of that one alone, not to eat*[^202]. What, then, was the cause that he did not take care to eat especially of the tree of life, since he was prohibited from none but that one alone, by whose usurpation he sinned? Nay rather, if we understand vigilantly, just as he sinned by eating of the forbidden tree, so he would sin by not eating of the tree of life; since he would begrudge himself the life which was present from that tree. But that immortality in which the holy angels live, and in which we too are to live, is beyond doubt greater. For it is not such that the human being has it in his power not to die, as not to sin, but yet can also die, because he can sin; but that immortality is such that everyone who is or will be there cannot die, because he can no longer even sin. For there will be there so great a will of living well, as there is even now the will of living blessedly, which we see misery could not take from us. Into this immortality, which no one doubts is greater, if you say that Adam could, from that lesser one, by the reward of obedience, with no death interposed, be changed; you will say something which a right faith ought not to disapprove; but if you so praise the former that you deny the latter; you will surely be compelled to fill the face of paradise with deaths, and with the disasters of the dying, by which, not bearing them, they are driven into death; and so to discolor your own, that you would wish to flee yourself, if you could look at it in a mirror. For why would even the descendants of the first man, born in paradise, and not only good, but also blessed, be compelled to die, if no fault compelled them to go out of paradise, where there was the tree of life, and the highest power of living from it, but no necessity of dying? From which necessity Enoch and Elijah were transferred: for they were in these lands, where the tree of life was not, and therefore the common necessity of death for all urged them to the end of this life. For where must we believe they were transferred, except where the very tree of life is, whence they have the power of living, and no necessity of dying; as it would be in paradise for human beings in whom no will of sinning arose, which would not allow them to be there, where no equity compelled them to die? Wherefore the examples of Enoch and Elijah aid us rather than you. For God in these two showed what he would bestow even on those whom he dismissed from paradise, if they had been unwilling to sin; for from there these were cast out, whither those were transferred. Where we believe this too was conferred on them by the grace of God, that they should not have whence to say: *Forgive us our debts*[^203]. For in these lands, where the corruptible body weighed down their souls[^204], they so struggled in a great contest with vices, that yet, if they said they had no sin, they would deceive themselves, and the truth would not be in them[^205]. Who indeed are believed to be going to return for a short time into these lands, that they too may struggle with death, and pay what is owed to the propagation of the first man. Whence it must be understood that those who had no sin, and their sons, if the inhabitants of paradise remained in the same uprightness, would rather persist in that immortality, until, with no death interposed, they passed into the ampler one—if we confess that so great a longevity of life was conferred on those who were just in these lands outside paradise in such a way that they could not say they had no sin, that is, on Enoch and Elijah. *But the Lord*, you say, *asked about that woman married seven times, confirmed by his answer that marriages were instituted for this, that offspring might supply the losses of death; but that bountiful fecundity would cease, when greedy death had ceased*. You err altogether, who think that marriages were instituted for this, that the departure of the dead might be supplied by the succession of those being born. For marriages were instituted that the chastity of women might make sons certain to fathers, and fathers to sons. For even by the mixed and indiscriminate use of any women whatever, human beings could be born; but a certain bond between fathers and sons could not exist. But if no one sinned, and on this account no one died either; with the number of the saints fixed, as great as would suffice for the future age, this age would be ended, where there was the faculty both of not sinning and of sinning, and that one would succeed, where no one could sin. For if souls stripped of bodies can be either wretched or blessed, and yet cannot sin; what one of the faithful would deny that in the kingdom of God, where there will be an incorruptible body, which will not weigh down the soul, but adorn it, nor will need food any more, this disposition will be, that no sin of anyone could be there, no will bringing it about, but a good one? The Lord, then, where he said, when he was speaking of the resurrection: *They will neither marry nor take wives; for neither will they die*; did not say it for this reason, that, on account of the dying, he might show marriages were instituted; but because, with the number of the saints filled, there would be no need for anyone to be born, where it would be necessary for no one to die. *But*, you say, *Adam is asserted, with emendation following, to have been expiated from his offense, both by the book of Wisdom, and by the opinion of many; and yet*, you say, *he died, that we might know that the death of the body does not pertain to the punishment of that sin, but to the institutions of nature*. As though indeed David did not so expiate those two grievous crimes, that is, adultery and murder, by repenting, that the prophet himself who had terrified him attested that pardon was given him; and yet we read that those things which God had threatened followed in such a way that we understand that that pardon profited to this, that the man who had committed such great evils should not be punished for them by everlasting punishment. There was, then, even for the first man, a thing to which repentance might profit, that is, that it might punish him with a long, yet not eternal, penalty. Whence it came about, what is most rightly believed, that his son, that is, according to the human being, the Lord Jesus, when he descended to hell, loosed him from the bonds of hell. For then, according to the book of Wisdom, he must be understood to have been led out from his offense, so that that book is shown to have said not a thing done, but to have foretold a thing to come, although through a word of past time; for so it says: *She led him out from his offense*[^206], just as it was said: *They dug my hands*[^207], and the rest, which there through words of past time are said to be future. And through this both by the death of the body it came about that he paid a temporal penalty for sin; and by repentance something was accomplished, that he might escape the eternal one; where the grace of the liberator availed more than the merit of the penitent. There is no place from which you may fortify yourself against the assault of truth, by which you are laid low with your engines in the clearest light, nor are you permitted by any reasoning whatever to send into the paradise of God both deaths and innumerable diseases, full of deadly torments. Believe God saying: *On whatever day you eat of it, you will die the death*[^208]. For on that day they began to die, on which, separated from the tree of life—which, surely set in a corporeal place, supplied life to the body—they took on the necessity and condition of death. Certainly *the losses of death*, and *greedy death* are your words; let at least these words, so hard and horrible, admonish you to spare the paradise of God. Does that most famous place of the blessed so offend you, that by you both death, and that ruinous and greedy, should be sent into it? O enemies of the grace of God, enemies of the paradise of God, how much further will you be able to go, than to fill the sweetness of holy delights with the most bitter penalties, and to wish paradise to be nothing but a lesser hell?

**VI. 31.** *Julian:* But now enough things about Genesis. Let us pass to the apostle Paul, whom the Manichee and the Traducianist suppose to be of their opinion. For when he was discoursing about the resurrection of the dead, he said: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^209]. This testimony you usurped, with whom our cause lies; but what you thought was accomplished by it, because you kept silent, although I suspect it, yet I can scarcely affirm. For what does it look to, as regards propagation, if all are said to die in Adam; when Adam is the name of a human being, but propagation is the indication of sin and of the Manichean taint? Unless perhaps you confess that Adam himself is and sounds nothing but sin; so that by this appellation the Apostle may seem to have pronounced that all die in sin? But this is openly insane. What new thing, then, if, because Adam, a Hebrew word, indicates nothing but human being (for its interpretation expresses this), the Apostle said: *In Adam all die, and in Christ all will be made alive*; that is, those who die according to the nature of the human being are roused from the dead by the power of Christ? Whoever denies this sentence is insane; for by the power of the same author, who in this life instituted both fecundity and mortality, all will be roused from the tombs, that each may receive the proper things of his body, according as he has done, whether good or evil[^210]. By this saying of the Apostle, then, by which he says: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*; do you think the bodily death, which is common to the just and the unjust, or that penal death assigned to the devil and the impious, is suggested? If this simple natural death—which even in the saints is precious[^211], which befalls not only the good and the evil, but even human beings and cattle equally—this death, I say, the Apostle indicated here; it is manifest that by the name of Adam he showed the nature of humanity, but by the name of Christ the power of the creator and rouser. But if what he says: *In Adam all die*; you wish here to be understood as crime, not nature; an exposition is at hand as clear as it is certain, namely that he said: *As all*, that is, many, die by the imitation of Adam; *so all*, that is, many, are saved by the imitation of Christ. Either, then, he spoke of common death, and indicated nature; or of sin, and accused imitation. For so he subjoined a little after: *As we have borne*, he says, *the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*[^212]. Surely the taking on of an image could not be commanded, if it were believed natural in either quality.

*Augustine:* Who is so negligent of the apostolic words as not to see that the Apostle was discoursing about the resurrection of the body, where he says: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*? But you, to expand not your words, but your empty talk, where there is no question, bring in a question, and ask of me about that which was said: *In Adam all die*; of what death it was said. It was altogether said of the death of the body, namely that by which the good and the evil must die, not that by which those are said to be dead who, by the same death, are evil; which two deaths the Lord embraces in one brief sentence, saying: *Let the dead bury their dead*[^213]. There is also a death which in the Apocalypse is called the second death[^214], by which both body and soul will be tortured by eternal fire; which the Lord threatens where he says: *Fear him who has the power to destroy both body and soul in hell*[^215]. Although, then, many deaths are found in the Scriptures; yet there are two chief ones, the first and the second; the first is that which the first man brought in by sinning; the second is that which the second man will bring in by judging. Just as in the holy Books many covenants of God are mentioned, which those who read diligently can notice; yet there are two chief ones, the Old and the New. The first death, then, began to be when Adam was cast out of paradise, and separated from the tree of life; the second death will begin to be when it will be said: *Depart from me, you cursed, into eternal fire*[^216]. When, therefore, the Apostle was speaking of the resurrection of the body, he said: *Through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead; for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^217]. We ought not, then, to inquire of what death it is treated in this place; for it is manifest that it is treated of the death of the body; but we ought rather to attend through whom is the very death of which it is treated, whether through God who founded the human being, or through the human being who, by sinning, became the cause of this death. But this, as I said, we ought to attend as something set in the open, not to seek as though hidden. For the Apostle removed this question too, saying most openly: *Through a human being death*. And who is this, except the first Adam? For he himself is that one of whom it was said: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death*; to whom, on the contrary, the second Adam is opposed, who is the form of the one to come[^218]; whence here too it was said: *Through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead*. So, then, must we receive what he says: *In Adam all die*; that we do not forget what he says: *Through a human being death*. For therefore *in Adam all die*, because through a human being death; just as therefore in Christ they are made alive, because *through a human being the resurrection of the dead*. A human being, then, and a human being; as, therefore, this one is one, so that one is one; and through this, because this one is the second human being, that one is the first human being. We know that Adam, as you mention, is in the Hebrew tongue called human being; but it is not hence effected what you most shamelessly try to persuade, namely that in that which the Apostle said, *In Adam all die*, he be believed to have signified every mortal human being; so that, namely, we should suppose all to die, not in that first human being, but in the fact that they are mortal. Do not obscure clear things, pervert right things, entangle simple things; in that one all die, through whom death; just as in that one they are made alive, through whom the resurrection of the dead. And who is this one, except the second human being? Who, then, is that one, except the first human being? Accordingly, who is this one, except the one Christ? Who that one, except the one Adam? *And so, as we have borne the image of the earthly human being, so let us bear also that of the heavenly one*. The former is indicated, the latter commanded; for the former is already present, the latter is to come. And therefore we have borne that image by the condition of being born, and by the contagion of sin; but we bear this one by the grace of being reborn; we bear it, however, for the present in hope, but in reality we shall bear it by the reward of rising again, and of reigning blessedly and justly. Since these things are so, the death indeed of the human being so established, and set in that place, that he would not die unless he sinned, is without doubt penal; but with God, by his grace, turning our penal evils to good use for us, in the sight of the Lord the death of his saints is precious. Through it they contend, as through a discipline: *For discipline*, as it is written, *seems for the present to be not of joy, but of sorrow; but afterward it renders a richer fruit of righteousness to those who have contended through it*[^219]. But you, who contend that the death of the body would have been even in paradise, even with no one sinning, are an enemy of the grace of God, and an enemy of the saints, whose death is precious, who through it contend to enter and dwell in paradise. For not only death, that is, the dissolution of the soul from the body—of the soul, however, unwilling to be stripped, but to be further clothed, that the mortal may be swallowed up by life[^220]—but also all diseases, and all kinds of evils, which human beings, not being able to bear them, die of, as far as pertains to you, you send into the place of so great felicity and quiet; which by how great an error you do, I see, but with what brow, I do not know.

**VI. 32.** *Julian:* But let us winnow the whole context of the passage itself. *If*, he says, *Christ is preached as having risen from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead? But if there is no resurrection of the dead, neither has Christ risen; but if Christ has not risen, our preaching is empty, and your faith is void; we are found also false witnesses of God, because we testified against God that he raised Christ, whom he did not raise. For if the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen; but if Christ has not risen, your faith is vain, for you are still in your sins; and those, then, who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished. If in this life only we hope in Christ, we are more pitiable than all human beings. But now Christ has risen from the dead, the firstfruits of those who sleep, since indeed through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. But each in his own order: the firstfruits Christ, then those who are Christ's, who are at his coming; then the end*[^221].

*Augustine:* That you undertook to winnow this whole passage in the apostolic writings about the resurrection of the body for this reason, that you might have an occasion by which to spread out your much-speaking with a copious—if it can be said—poverty, and procure for yourself spaces of wandering for filling so many books, will become clear in your very prolix and superfluous disputation.

**VI. 33.** *Julian:* The excellent master built up his disputation most keenly, and instilled in us hope concerning the fellowship of the Mediator, asserting that that human being, as far as pertains to the substance by which he is joined to us, had nothing excepted, and that the opinion which unbelief was decreeing could prejudge Christ no less than us. So, then, he mingles the business of Christ and of human beings, that it is necessary that in both parts be believed what is judged in the one. For human beings at the same time had supposed that the resurrection of the dead would not be; yet they did not deny that Christ had risen. The Teacher of the Gentiles seizes this, and pronounces that it is necessary that both parts be open to one prejudgment, and that either all human beings be believed to be going to rise, or not even Christ be believed to have risen. Surely the Apostle would not have this force in disputing, if, according to the Manichees and their disciples the Traducianists, he distinguished the flesh of Christ from the communion of our nature.

*Augustine:* They are not Manichees who distinguish the flesh of Christ from the communion of our nature; but those who contend that Christ had no flesh. And so, by joining the Manichees to us, who are to be anathematized and condemned along with you, you even lift up their cause too, saying that they distinguish the flesh of Christ from the communion of our nature; as though they confessed that Christ had flesh, which they in any way distinguished from our flesh. Dismiss them far from us, and far from you too, differing in this question about the flesh of Christ; do with us what you do; because with us you confess the flesh of Christ, though dissimilarly. For neither do we distinguish it from the communion of the nature and substance of our flesh, but from the communion of its fault. For our flesh is the flesh of sin; on account of which his was called not the likeness of flesh, because it is true flesh; but the likeness of sinful flesh, because it is not the flesh of sin. If, then, the flesh of sin were not our flesh, how, I ask you, would the likeness of sinful flesh be the flesh of Christ? Or are you so foolish as to say that something is like, but that there is not that to which it is like? Hear Hilary, the Catholic bishop, whom surely, whatever you think of him, you cannot call a Manichee; who, when he spoke of the flesh of Christ: *Therefore when he was sent in the likeness of sinful flesh*, he says, *he did not, as he had flesh, so also have sin; but because all flesh is from sin, namely derived from Adam the parent, he was sent in the likeness of sinful flesh, there being in him not sin, but the likeness of sinful flesh*[^222]. What will you say to these things, most wicked, most wordy, most contumelious, most slanderous one? Is Hilary too a Manichee? But far be it that I disdain to receive your injuries, not only with Hilary and the other ministers of Christ, but even with the very flesh of Christ, to which you do not dread to do so great an injury, that you dare to make it equal to the rest of human flesh, which is established to be the flesh of sin; if it was not falsely said that Christ came in the likeness of sinful flesh[^223].

**VI. 34.** *Julian:* For never would he say: *If the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen*; to whom it could be reported: But Christ, since he was born of a Virgin, rose in a peculiar way; but human beings, because they are born of a diabolical commingling, do not rise. But he would at once report: And what was the vanity of rising, if it was prepared neither for our hope nor for instruction? For what would be the reason of teaching, what the gravity of the example, if a nature in us dissimilar lacked both the hope of reigning together and the force of imitating? Far, then, and very far, does the faith of the Apostle stand from this opinion. Full of the same Spirit by which Peter too was, he knows that Christ died for us for this, that he might give us an example, that we should follow his footsteps[^224]. And because he knows that the cause of so great a mystery was that it was a sacrifice and an example, he does not hesitate to pronounce, nay diligently drives home, that the human being Christ did not approach in anything by which any of us is barred by the prejudices of his nature. *If*, he says, *there is no resurrection of the dead, neither has Christ risen. But if Christ has risen from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?* That is, if you confess that he was, according to the human being, of the same nature of which we are, by what reasoning do you think either the resurrection accomplished in him, or that it will not be in the rest? With the conditions premised, he completes the sentence from every side: *But now*, he says, *Christ has risen from the dead*; there will be, then, a resurrection of the dead.

*Augustine:* To those thinking that there is no resurrection of the dead, and yet believing that Christ has risen, it is said for this reason: *If there is no resurrection of the dead, neither has Christ risen*; because Christ rose for this, that he might build up faith in the resurrection of the dead, showing that human beings will rise in the flesh, just as he himself, made a human being, rose in the flesh. And through this it was consequent that those should deny that Christ had risen, who did not believe there was a resurrection of the dead. Whence, because those with whom it was dealt could not deny that, they ought also, the fog removed, to confess this. For if, on account of some difference of Christ, human beings rightly seem to themselves to deny the resurrection of the dead, and yet not to deny the resurrection of the Lord; they can say other things too, of which they find many, by which they may seem to themselves to defend their error. For what if, when they have heard: *If the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen*, they answer and say: But he is not only a human being, but also God; which none of other human beings is? He, according to the human being himself, was born of the Holy Spirit and of the Virgin Mary; which none of other human beings is: he had the power of laying down his soul, and of taking it again[^225]; which none of other human beings is: what wonder, then, if he could rise from the dead; which none of other human beings will be able to do? If, then, they say these things, because they grant that Christ alone rose, but are unwilling that the rest do; shall we therefore deny those so great differences of Christ from the rest, in order to persuade them of the resurrection of other dead too from Christ's equality with them? So, then, neither, denying that difference by which we confess that the flesh of Christ alone was not, like that of others, the flesh of sin, but the likeness of sinful flesh; not for that reason do we defend that he alone rose, but that the rest too will rise, and so defend it that we say what the Apostle said: *If the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen*; but Christ has risen; therefore the dead rise. For it is not because the merits of origin are not the same in both flesh, that therefore the earthly and mortal substance is not the same. The likeness of sinful flesh has indeed its difference, by which it is distinguished from the flesh of sin; but far be it that Christ should make himself unequal to them in rising, to whom he willed to make himself equal in dying. We ought not, then, to make the likeness of sinful flesh equal to the flesh of sin, as far as pertains to the difference of pertaining to sin and not pertaining to it, because he was unwilling that there be between either flesh a difference of rising and not rising, who was unwilling that there be a difference of not dying and dying. But the imitation which you prop up, where there is no need, what does it avail to this cause? For imitation is in the will; but when it is good, the will is prepared, as it is written, by the Lord[^226]. No one, then, imitates, unless he wills; but the human being dies and rises, whether he will or not. And indeed imitation itself does not always take place then, when the nature of the one imitating is the same as that of the one imitated; otherwise we could not imitate the righteousness and piety of the angels, whose nature is different; which yet that we ask of the Lord in prayer, when we say: *Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven*[^227], you yourself too have confessed. Nor would we imitate God the Father, whose nature is far different from us; yet the Lord says: *Be like your Father who is in heaven*[^228]; and through the Prophet it is said: *Be holy, for I am holy*[^229]. Not, then, for this reason can we not imitate Christ, because he was in the likeness of sinful flesh in this age, but we in the flesh of sin.

**VI. 35.** *Julian:* Let us put forth these forces against the propagation-theory, and say: If Christ, who was made a human being, did not have natural sin, how do some among you say that an inborn depravity has dominion over the image of God? But if there is evil in the nature, then Christ too, who is found in the same nature, is established under the kingdom of the devil. But if he is believed guilty, our preaching is empty, and your faith is void; and the Apostles are found false witnesses, because they testified against God, that he formed his Son, from the seed of David according to the flesh, harmless and holy, if the crime of the accursed seed infected him. Surely, if we hope in such a Christ, we are more pitiable than all human beings. But now Christ, no less true human being than true God, generated from the stock of Adam, made of a woman, and made under the law, did no sin, and therefore had no sin. It appears, then, that crime is of the will, not of the seed.

*Augustine:* From this, surely, as from a foundation, the whole structure of your reasoning rises up, which you set down first, saying: *If Christ, who was made a human being, did not have natural sin, how do some among you say that an inborn depravity has dominion over the image of God?* And this proposition being torn up and dug out, whatever you afterward added as following is overturned by an easiest collapse. For it does not follow that, if Christ, who was made a human being, did not have natural, that is, original, sin, then no inborn depravity has dominion over the image of God; since it does not follow that, if the likeness of sinful flesh had nothing of depravity, then that to which it is like, that is, the flesh of sin itself, has nothing of depravity; nay rather, this follows, that if there is the likeness of sinful flesh, there is also the flesh of sin. Because everything like must necessarily be like to something; and if Christ alone had indeed true flesh, like other human beings, but yet not the property, but the likeness of sinful flesh; not only is it necessary that there be another to which this is like, namely the flesh of sin, but that the flesh of all other human beings be nothing but the flesh of sin. Wherefore, although there is evil in the flesh of sin, yet it is not in Christ, who came in true flesh, yet not in the flesh of sin, but in the likeness of sinful flesh, to heal the flesh of sin. Not, then, is he believed guilty, but by him our guilt is loosed, both the original and the added. Whence neither is the preaching of the Apostle empty, who would not preach in Christ the likeness of sinful flesh, unless he knew the flesh of others to be of sin. Nor is our faith void, by which your heresy is voided. Nor are the Apostles found false witnesses, who distinguish the likeness of sinful flesh from the flesh of sin, which your heresy does not do; and so evangelize Christ from the seed of David, that yet they assert him born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, not of carnal concupiscence, so that he might have the likeness of sinful flesh, but could not have the flesh of sin. Nor are we more pitiable than all human beings by believing these things; but it is a great misery to believe that the flesh of Christ is not distinct from the flesh of sin. Accordingly, you determine your argumentation with an empty conclusion, saying: *It appears, then, that crime is of the will, not of the seed*. This you inferred altogether emptily; since the earlier things, from which you think it is made, I have shown not to be consequent; and beyond doubt, because they could be in paradise, there would be no faults of those being born after paradise, unless the seeds too were vitiated by the evil will of the first begetters. Why, then, is it not rather said to you (that we may use truthfully the same form of reasoning which you used deceitfully): If the human being Christ was sent to human beings in the likeness of sinful flesh, how do, not some among you, but all of you say that the other flesh, to which this is like, is not flesh of sin, if there is no other flesh of sin? If Christ did not have the likeness of sinful flesh, empty is the preaching of him who said this, empty too is the faith of the Catholic Church which believed it; and the Apostle is found a false witness too, who testified against Christ that he had the likeness of sinful flesh, which he did not have. But if we believe this, we are not associated with faithful human beings. But now Christ was sent in the likeness of sinful flesh; because he alone so had true flesh that it was not the flesh of sin, but its likeness; and through this it is necessary that we confess that the flesh of other human beings is of sin, to which the true flesh of Christ was made like, but not the flesh of sin.

**VI. 36.** *Julian:* The Manichees are surely transfixed, both believing in natural sin and denying the resurrection of the flesh. *But now*, he says, *Christ has risen from the dead, the firstfruits of those who sleep; since indeed through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead*[^230]. Here the Apostle speaks not of the general resurrection, which even the wicked, even profane human beings will experience; but only of theirs who will be transferred into glory. He shows the blessed resurrection, then, by the pure name of resurrection, in comparison with which that of the impious is passed over as though it were not a resurrection. Here, then, the Apostle drives home not only the resurrection, as I said, which is common to the good and the evil, but the blessed resurrection; and although resurrection and the blessedness of resurrection are not one, just as neither are resurrection and the misery of resurrection one; yet, because eternal blessedness is not without resurrection, by its name that felicity too, which makes the rousing not to be regretted, is marked. As if someone, praising diligence, strengths, and diverse pursuits, should wish to show them by the abridgment of life, for example, calling that one's life learned, this one's elegant, another's tireless; he would not surely remove the distinction, so that life would seem the same as diligence, as elegance, as fortitude; for it is one thing to live, another to study; yet unless you live, you will not study at all. So too resurrection is not the same as blessedness; for there is a wretched rousing of ashes; yet you will reign at all only if you have first risen. The death of the body, then, and the resurrection of the body stand opposite; if death were universally penal, the resurrection too would be universally a reward; but now there is a penal resurrection of all who are assigned to the eternal fires; therefore death too is not penal, but natural. For just as the death of the body does not generally make one regret having died; so the resurrection does not generally make one pleased to have lived again. But the good of the latter is in the reward of those rising, and the bitterness of the former in the burning of the punished; both of which follow according to merits. It appears, then, that the Apostle discourses not of natural death, but of that of the criminal, which perpetual punishment makes unhappy; nor of the common resurrection, but of that which eternal glory makes blessed; nor, where he disputes about persons, does he bring prejudice upon the creatures, but, the distinctions always preserved and the wide limits of natures and wills, so sometimes mingles the terms, that the particularity of things may not perish by confusion. *Through a human being*, then, *death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead*: he announces here not that death was founded by the human being, but that it appeared in the human being; just as also he does not say that the resurrection of the dead was made by a human being, that is, by Christ, but in a human being, as the same master says to the Philippians: *He became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross; on which account God also exalted him, and gave him the name which is above every name*[^231]. The assertion of the apostle Peter too concurs in this: *Men of Israel, hear: Jesus the man of Nazareth, approved by God among you, you killed by the hands of the wicked, whom God raised up, the pains of hell being loosed*[^232]. And again: *This Jesus God raised up, of which we all are witnesses*[^233]. There, then, as it was proper to the human being to undergo death without injury to the deity, so it was proper to the divinity to rouse that human being from the dead. But what God is pronounced to work, from the person of the Word, Christ himself also does. For so he had said: *I have it in my power to lay down my soul, and I have it in my power to take it up*[^234]. Since, then, the person of the Son is one, yet by a lawful distinction one thing is applied to the flesh, another to the deity. *Through a human being*, then, *death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead*—manifested, not created; for the rest, both were instituted through God; but in Adam the condition of death, in the person of Christ the firstfruits of resurrection, became clear. Here, then, where the Apostle says: *Through a human being death*; if you say through his will, it does not pertain to nature; if through nature, it does not look to fault. For he set human beings opposite, one of death, the other of resurrection, unwilling to subject the second to the first. And it follows: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*. This, then, that he says: *All will be made alive in Christ*, does he say it even of the impious, or only of the faithful? If of the impious, that all are made alive in Christ, then no one is in punishment; if of the faithful, then not all are made alive in the faith of Christ, but only the faithful, although absolutely all are roused by his power, by which they had been founded. If, then, he speaks of the death of the body: *In Adam all die*, no guilt is indicated by this name, when in the same Adam Christ too is found dead; for the truth of resurrection would not have followed, unless the truth of death had preceded. Therefore the Apostle pronounces that all die in Adam. If this death indicates nothing but the dissolution of the body; it surely looks not at all to natural sin, nor does it prejudge the innocent, if they are said to die in Adam, in whom Christ too died. But if this which he says: *In Adam all die*, you wish to pertain, by the force of mind, to sin, and not only to death, but to a guilty and wretched death, that is, the one which the punishment assigned to crimes follows; but in that hell, namely, neither Christ could be, nor the saints; therefore it does not prejudge the innocent either, who, as they have nothing good, so have nothing voluntarily evil; but retain this alone, that they were made by God; whom by the right of Baptism we consecrate, that he who made them good by founding may make them better by renewing and adopting. What, then, the Apostle says: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*; is as far from this suspicion of the Manichean propagation as Christ is from sin, who both had no iniquity, and had nothing less of human nature.

*Augustine:* What is it that, disputing against us, you say: *The Manichees are surely transfixed, both believing in natural sin and denying the resurrection of the flesh*? Do we, like them, either attribute sin to an alien nature, or deny the resurrection of the flesh? Surely let the Manichees be transfixed by you, who are transfixed by us along with you, even when they are aided by you. For to their aid you deny that the discord of flesh and spirit must be attributed to the sin of Adam; so that they, seeking or rendering the cause of this evil, may conclude that an alien nature of evil, coeternal with the good God, is commingled with us. You expound then the following words, where the Apostle disputes about the resurrection of the flesh, and you say: *That he does not speak of the common resurrection, namely of the good and the evil; but only of theirs who will be transferred into glory*. So it is indeed: yet he speaks of the resurrection of the body. To this, then, on the contrary, the death of the body is opposed, and to these, that is, to the death of the body and the resurrection, individual authors are distributed, two human beings: *Since through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead*. And the two human beings themselves are expressed even by their proper names, so that of whom this was said may evidently appear; and it is added: *For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*. *They die*, he says; he does not say: They will die; but on the other side he does not say: They are made alive; but: *they will be made alive*. For now they die from penalty, then they will be made alive from reward. He does not, then, now speak of that death which will be for those who, with soul and body, will be tortured by eternal fire; otherwise he would set a word of future time on either side, and as he said: *they will be made alive*, so he would say: They will die. But since he said: *they die*, which surely now takes place; but: *they will be made alive*, which will then take place; he shows well enough that he is treating of that death, in that which he says: *Through a human being death*, which dissolves the soul from the flesh; although those too pertain to that future death, which is called the second death, who do not loose by regeneration through Christ the guilt which they drew by generation through Adam. Now accordingly, because he speaks of the resurrection of the body, which is to come, to which he opposes on the contrary the death of the body, which now takes place; and these two contraries have individual authors, Adam death, Christ the resurrection of the dead; as that resurrection is understood as a reward, so this death must be understood as penal. For to a reward, on the contrary, not nature, but penalty is opposed. And therefore in this place, where the resurrection of the body is opposed to the death of the body, the Apostle does not treat of the common resurrection, which pertains to the just and the unjust; but rather of that in which will be those who will be made alive in Christ, not those who will be condemned by Christ; although he makes both rise, whose *voice all who are in the tombs will hear; and they will come forth, those who did good, into the resurrection of life; but those who did evil, into the resurrection of judgment*[^235]. For this reason, then, as I said, I wished to commend that resurrection which pertains to the benefit of Christ, not also that which pertains only to judgment; so that, since this is a reward, the death of the body too, which on the contrary is opposed to it, may be shown to be penal. For as death is contrary to life, so penalty to reward; through which penalty, that is, the death of the body, since the holy martyrs contended and conquered, their death, in which they now sleep *in the sight of the Lord*, is precious, not by its own kind, but by his gift[^236]. For even the penalties of the saints are without doubt precious; but not because they are precious are they therefore not penalties; just as not because they are penalties are they therefore precious; but because they were undertaken for the truth, or borne with piety. If you held this sound and Catholic sense, you would not send into the paradise of God, that is, into the place of holy delights, not only the penalties of deaths, but also of deadly sicknesses. But what is every penalty of the human being, except the penalty of the image of God? Which if it is inflicted unjustly, surely he by whom it is inflicted is unjust. But who would doubt that a penalty is inflicted unjustly upon the image of God, unless fault merited this? For the sole mediator of God and human beings, the human being Christ Jesus, bore a penalty without fault, namely that he might empty out both our fault and penalty; not that penalty which was to be paid in this malignant age, but that which was owed us eternal. And yet he too, taking on our affection in himself, with death approaching: *Father*, he says, *if it can be, let this cup pass from me*[^237]. Who surely had the power of laying down and taking up his soul; but that death, which with no fault preceding he took on for us by will, not by necessity, God the master shows by these words of his to be a penalty, which he alone bore for us without any iniquity of his own. If, then, this glory of the mercy of Christ, by which he bore a penalty for us without fault, is singular, so that not indeed in the flesh of sin, but on account of the likeness of sinful flesh, yet in Adam, from whom the flesh of sin descends, he too should die; without doubt other human beings, in this malignant age, which is outside paradise, suffer whatever penalties from birth to death, in which penalties they surely suffer death too, for their sins either drawn by being born or added by living badly, deservedly and worthily suffer them under a just and omnipotent judge; who surely would neither inflict, nor allow to be inflicted, penalties upon his images, without whose will not even a sparrow falls to the ground[^238], unless he knew it inflicted justly. And what is justly, except by the merit of sins, or by the testing of virtues; so that, even with sins forgiven, the pledge of eternal salvation which the reborn receive may profit them in the age to come, but here they pay whatever must be paid in the penal vanity and malignity of this age? What is it, then, that you say: *That little ones are baptized for this, that those whom God had made good by founding, he may make better by renewing and adopting?* He indeed made them good, because every nature, insofar as it is a nature, is good; but those whom he made good, he would not unjustly make or allow to be wretched. Although you too, who say they are renewed, confess, incautious and not knowing it, that they carry the oldness of the old man, when they are surely new by being born. You are compelled, then, to choose one of these three: that you either fill paradise with the penalties of human beings; or say God is unjust in the penalties of his images, which the innocence of little ones suffers; or, because these two are detestable and damnable, acknowledge original sin; and so understand that all who die by the death of the body die in Adam, because through that human being death, that is, through his fault and penalty; and all who are not condemned by the resurrection of the body, but are made alive, are made alive in Christ, because through that human being the resurrection of the dead, that is, through his righteousness and grace. For because the death of the body is penal, therefore we see opposed to it, on the contrary, only the resurrection of the body that is a reward (although there is another that is penal too).

**VI. 37.** *Julian:* *But each in his own order: the firstfruits Christ, then those who are Christ's at his coming, then the end*[^239]. The same elsewhere too: *Who is the firstborn*, he says, *from the dead*[^240]. *Then those who are Christ's*: that is, the saints are caught up in the clouds[^241]. *After this the end*: since these will go into the eternal kingdom, but the impious into eternal fire[^242]. *When he has handed over the kingdom to God and the Father, when he has made void all rule, and all power, and might; for he must reign, until he puts all enemies under his feet; for he has subjected all things under his feet; but the last enemy, death, will be destroyed. But when he has said: All these things are subjected, without doubt except him who subjected all things to him; for when all things have been subjected to him, then the Son too will be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all*[^243]. It is the kingdom of God and the Father, that, with the number of the saints fulfilled which is contained in his foreknowledge, all rule and all might of the adverse power may perish. For this efficacy of so great a mystery must be such that all the enemies of righteousness are set under the divine feet. Which will then come about, when eternal death will behold itself destroyed and conquered by all the saints. But when all those kinds of powers have been subjected, both to Christ and to his body, by the manifestation of the kingdom, much more will the whole glorified congregation of the saints not cease to be subject to God; but the whole body worthy of the kingdom of heaven, which is built up under Christ the head, will cleave to the divine will with a perfect affection, so that, every desire of faults extinguished, God may both contain and complete all.

*Augustine:* In this part of your disputation you have prosecuted almost nothing that pertains to the cause which is dealt with between us. For why did you think this whole passage, where the Apostle disputes about the resurrection of the body, must be inserted, except on account of that which he says: *As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*? Because, namely, you are unwilling to attribute the death of the body to the sin of the first man, but to nature; which you say was so established in that first human being that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die. Whence I think enough has been answered you. Wherefore, those things being passed over, in expounding which you wished superfluously to expand yourself, that which the Apostle says: *The last enemy, death, will be destroyed*, must perhaps be discussed, of what death it was said; whether of this which now is, by which the soul is compelled to go out of the body; or of that by which the soul is not allowed to go out of the body, that both at once may be tortured by everlasting fire; which death surely is not yet, but is to come; nor will it then be destroyed, but rather then begins to be; which now, who doubts that it is not yet? But this, which is then frequent in all dying in body, to which the resurrection of the body is contrary—about which, that he might say all these things, the Apostle was disputing—this, I say, which now takes place, the usual death of the body known to all, will then surely be destroyed last, when this corruptible will have put on incorruption, and this mortal will have put on immortality[^244]. Which we perceive was beyond doubt said about the resurrection of the body which will then be, against the death of the body which now is. Wherefore, if eternal death, which never was, cannot then be destroyed, when it rather begins to be, nor will ever be destroyed, because it will always be; it remains that this which now is, this very one, last, that is, at the end, be destroyed, when it will come about by the resurrection of the flesh that it be not. Moreover, how would it be an enemy, if it were natural in such a way that it were not penal? But penal, under a just and omnipotent judge, it would in no way be, unless it had happened by the merits of sin. Now at last, we ask, correct your sense; and the paradise of the blessed, which you had contaminated with the penalties of human beings, cleanse. It cannot be said, however, how much it pleases me, what you said: *That in the kingdom of heaven it will come about that, every desire of faults extinguished, God may both contain and complete all*. O would that by the admonition of this sentence of yours you correct yourself, and the desire of faults, which in our flesh now, even when restrained, does not cease to fight against us, but then, as you most rightly confess, extinguished, will be none; that it may no longer please you to praise it as some good, but rather to accuse it as an evil! For it is that very protégée of yours, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, so that it is necessary that the spirit too lust against the flesh[^245], lest that be committed whence the human being may be condemned. Which evil of discord, of both things good and founded by the good God, that is, of flesh and spirit, passed over into nature through the transgression of the first man, neither a Manichee, nor a helper of the Manichees, but the destroyer Ambrose proclaims[^246].

**VI. 38.** *Julian:* *Otherwise*, he says, *what will those do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead do not rise at all? Why are they baptized for them? Why are we too in peril every hour? I die daily by your glory, which I have in the Lord. If, according to a human being, I fought with beasts at Ephesus, what does it profit me, if the dead do not rise? Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*[^247]. If, he says, this hope of future glory, by which God will be all in all, is shaken by impious unbelief, and the resurrection of the dead is denied, what will those do who are baptized for the dead? Hence arose the error of some, thinking that in the beginnings of the Gospel there was a custom by which people, standing in for corpses, would make profession, and would pour the water of Baptism even over the members of the dead; which it appears came about from inexperience. For this which the Apostle says: *They are baptized for the dead*, indicates nothing other than what he said to the Romans: *We were buried with him through Baptism into death*[^248]; that is: With what grace, with what mind do we approach the receiving of Baptism, that we may mortify our members for the future, and live altogether for the dead, if there is no hope that we live after death? But why, he says, am I subjected also to daily perils, falling continually into the death which persecutors bring, that I may be able to glory before God of your advances, if the dead do not rise? But why too did I, according to a human being, fight with beasts at Ephesus, that is, why did I endure the bestial fury of the seditious, if it still wavers that the dead rise? *Do not be deceived; evil conversations corrupt good morals. For some have ignorance of God; I say it to your shame*[^249]. The love, he says, of sins persuades you to distrust about future things; it is thought that there is no judgment, that one may sin with greater audacity. They altogether, he says, do not know how to esteem God, who deny the resurrection. Not only, then, do you deny the reward, but the power of the divinity, whence you ought to be exceedingly ashamed. I say it to your shame, that such people can be found among you.

*Augustine:* And here too you wished to say nothing at all that pertains to the matter which is turned in our discussion. Accordingly, to the words of the Apostle, which you tried to explain to your capacity—because the things you said, although in some you did not hold the sense of that author, yet are not against the faith—I have no need to answer your much-speaking.

**VI. 39.** *Julian:* *But someone will say: How do the dead rise? And in what body do they come? Foolish one, what you sow is not made alive unless it first dies. Not all flesh is the same flesh; one indeed of human beings, another of cattle; another the flesh of birds, another of fishes; and there are heavenly bodies and earthly; but one indeed is the brightness of the heavenly, another that of the earthly; one the brightness of the sun, and another the brightness of the moon, and another the brightness of the stars; for star differs from star in glory; so also the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption, it will rise in incorruption; it is sown in dishonor, it will rise in glory; it is sown in weakness, it will rise in power; an animal body is sown, a spiritual body will rise. So also it is written: The first man Adam was made into a living soul, the last Adam into a life-giving spirit. The first man from the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly; as the earthly, such also the earthly ones; and as the heavenly, such also the heavenly ones. As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*[^250]. He tames the difficulty of the matter by examples, and says that nothing is impossible, when omnipotence promises the effect. But as he commends the resurrection of bodies by the comparison of seeds, so he opens the diversity of those rising by the varieties of creatures; yet he speaks the whole about the resurrection of the blessed. *It is sown*, he says, *in dishonor, it will rise in glory; it is sown in weakness, it will rise in power; an animal body is sown, a spiritual body will rise*. This surely cannot be fulfilled except in the saints; the impious too are roused not into glory, but into reproach, as the Prophet asserts, everlasting[^251]. Here opportunely he makes the distance between nature and grace, and recalls the old testimony by which it was said: *The first man Adam was made into a living soul*; and adds from his own: *the last Adam into a life-giving spirit*. And he shows that the gifts of immortality pertain to the life-giving spirit; but the soul only living, to the nature at some time dying. There is not, then, he says, this living the same as life-giving; the life-giving is he who confers immortality, which he applies to Christ; but the living is he who uses life, but does not exclude mortality. So he distinguished each sentence by its end, that he might show that Adam was made living, but not immortal; but Christ into a spirit not only living, but even conferring resurrection, glorious to his own, eternal to all.

*Augustine:* Was Adam therefore going to die, even if he had not sinned, because he was made in an animal body, not a spiritual one? You err altogether, if you think it therefore necessary for us to fill the paradise of God with the deaths and penalties of the dying, and finally with the dishonor, weakness, corruption in which now the animal bodies of human beings are sown. For the tree of life, which God planted in his paradise, defended even the animal body from death, until, by the merit of persevering obedience, it should pass, with no death intervening, into the spiritual glory which the just who rise are to have. For it was just that the image of God, darkened and rendered obsolete by no sin, should be set in such a body, although created and formed from earthly material, that the stability of living supplied to it from the tree of life should remain; and that it should meanwhile live through a living soul, which no necessity would separate from it; and thence, on account of obedience kept, should reach the life-giving spirit, in such a way that this lesser life should not be taken from it, where it could both die and not die, but that ampler one should be added, where it should live without the benefit of any tree, and could not die. For I ask of you, in what kind of body do you now think Elijah and Enoch are; an animal, or a spiritual one? If you say: An animal one, why are you unwilling to believe that Adam, and Eve, and their descendants, if they had violated God's precept by no transgression, although in an animal body, would have lived as these now live? Since those were there, whither these were transferred; and there these live, whence those were cast out so that they might die. For the corporeal tree of life supplied life to animal bodies, just as the spiritual tree of life, which is the wisdom of God, supplies the life of salutary teaching to holy minds. Whence some Catholic treaters of the divine words too preferred to commend a spiritual paradise; not, however, resisting the history, which more evidently shows a corporeal one. But if you answer that Enoch and Elijah have already a spiritual body, why do you not confess that the animal body of the first human beings, and of those who would be born of them through the series of succession, if no sin existed which deservedly separated them from the tree of life, could pass into a spiritual one without death intervening, lest you be compelled to fill the paradise of God, the place of happy joys, with the penalties of deaths and of the dying, and with innumerable torments of deadly diseases?

**VI. 40.** *Julian:* *But not first*, he says, *that which is spiritual, but that which is animal; then, that which is spiritual. The first man from the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly; as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*[^252]. He openly, surely, passes over to pursuits, and wishes there to be as much difference between our past and present conversation as there is between mortality and immortality. *The first man*, he says, *from the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly*; by the name of substances he indicates the diversity of purpose. For neither did Christ, whom he calls the heavenly man, bring down his flesh from heaven, which he took from the seed of David, from the seed of Adam, and within the woman from the woman's flesh. He refers, then, earthly and heavenly to virtues and vices. Finally it follows: *As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*. So too to the Romans: *It is a human thing that I say, on account of the weakness of your flesh; for as you presented your members to serve uncleanness, and iniquity unto iniquity; so now present your members to serve righteousness unto sanctification*[^253]. But following the tenor of the exhortation undertaken, he subjoins what, unless it be understood, will seem to overturn all his sayings: *This, then, I say, brothers, that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God, nor will corruption possess perpetuity*. Surely in these passages, in which he had labored at nothing but affirming the resurrection of the flesh, which he had said was to be set in the glory of the kingdom, now he pronounces: *that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God*. If flesh does not possess, if blood does not possess, where is that whose pomp above the resurrection of the dead regenerates? But, by the custom of Scripture, by flesh and blood he named the vices, not the substance. Finally he made this same thing plain: *Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise, but we shall not all be changed*. The excellent master understood that above he had claimed the name of resurrection only for the future blessedness; and therefore, lest that remain ambiguous, he resolved it: *We shall all indeed rise*; behold the common rousing: *but we shall not all be changed*; behold where the resurrection of the blessed is. The change into glory, then, is owed only to those who merit not the wrath of God, but his love. *In a moment*, he says, *in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet, and the dead will rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed*. Again here he passes to those saints whom that day will find in the flesh; and he says that in a moment as brief as can be the last end of sounds, both those who had been dead are roused incorrupt, that is, whole; and those who shall be found are changed into glory. *For this corruptible must put on incorruption; and this mortal put on immortality. But when this mortal has put on immortality, then will come about the word that is written: Death is swallowed up in your victory. Where, O death, is your sting? Where is your victory? But the sting of death is sin; and the power of sin is the law*[^254]. He shows, as often, that he was speaking only of the resurrection of the saints; therefore, passing over that rousing of the impious, he pronounces it worthy that in the bodies of the saints corruptibility be devoured by the eternity of glory. But when this, he says, has been fulfilled, then it will be permitted to insult the devil, and perpetual death, which had made this natural corruption seem evil; then the joys of the saints will rebuke, seeing the sting of death crushed by themselves, and will say: *Where, O death, is your sting? Where is your victory? But the sting of death is sin; and the power of sin is the law*. That is: O you, eternal death, you had sin as a sting, by which you wounded the deserters of righteousness; since, if you were not armed with this sting, that is, with voluntary sin, you would have harmed no one at all. This sin and this sting you see broken by the forces of faith, our reward bearing witness, from which you tried to lead us away; for your sting was sin, and the power of your sin was the law; since *where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^255]. Or certainly, although your sting was sin; yet it was made stronger, at least in the minds of transgressors, after the death of the law was applied to it; which yet had not been given for this, that it should sharpen. For *the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just and good; but sin, that it might appear sin, worked death for me through the good, that sin might become sinning beyond measure through the commandment*[^256]. This power, then, which your sting attained by the help of the spontaneous iniquity in us, is proved conquered and broken by the virtues of the faithful, by the crowns now of the faithful. We insult you, therefore: *Giving thanks to our God, who gave us this victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^257].

*Augustine:* About the image of the earthly, and about the image of the heavenly human being, enough has already been disputed above; and we answered you that the image of the heavenly can now be borne by faith and hope; but it will be borne, presented and bestowed in reality, when the spiritual body shall have risen, which is now sown an animal one. For these two images, namely the one of the earthly human being, the other of the heavenly, he attributes to individual things; the former, namely, to the animal body, the latter to the spiritual. For above he said: *But not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animal; afterward, that which is spiritual*; and at once subjoined: *The first man from the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly, as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*. Who is that one, except Adam, through whom death? And who is this one, except Christ, through whom the resurrection of the dead? *Since through a human being death, and through a human being the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*; that is, whoever will be made alive, will be made alive only in Christ; whence we have already spoken above. There is surely no ambiguity to which two things these two images are referred; for the former is referred to death, this to resurrection. The former, then, to the death of the body; because this to the resurrection of the body; the former to the animal body, which is sown in dishonor; this to the spiritual, which will rise in glory; with the former we are clothed by being born, with this by being reborn. But since we are born under sin, and reborn in the forgiveness of sins: *As we have borne*, he says, *the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of that heavenly one*. He mentions the former as done, this he exhorts that it be done. For no one can bring it about that he was not born in the penalty by which his body is sown in dishonor; but unless he is reborn, and perseveres in that in which he is reborn in grace, he will not reach the disposition of the spiritual body, which will rise in glory. What is it, then, that you say: *He openly, surely, passed over to pursuits, and wishes there to be as much difference between our past and present conversation as there is between mortality and immortality?* When rather the Apostle passes over to nothing else; but pursues that which he had begun about the resurrection of the flesh, to which on the contrary he opposes the death of the flesh. He does not, then, wish two conversations, namely an evil and a good one, to be understood in this place; but asserts the resurrection of the flesh to be coming through Christ, just as through Adam the death of the flesh was made. Let the man of God do what he does; you follow him, do not try to make him follow you. For he does not follow, however much you try. He openly opposes the death of the flesh to the resurrection of the flesh; he openly distributes to these individual things individual authors, to the death of the body Adam, to the resurrection of the body Christ; he openly, comparing on the contrary between themselves two images, one of the earthly human being, the other of the heavenly, attributes the former to the animal body, which through Adam merited to be sown in dishonor; this to the spiritual, which through Christ will merit to rise in glory. Who too according to the flesh was called a heavenly human being, not because he took it from heaven, but because he also lifted it into heaven. If a good pursuit and a good conversation bring it about that they reach a glorious resurrection; was it by an evil pursuit and an evil conversation of this life, which, born, and increased by the advance of age, we have led, brought about that we are born in an animal body with the propagation of death? For who by an evil pursuit or by any pursuit procured for himself the beginning of a troublesome birth? Who by an evil conversation brought it about that, however he has lived, it is necessary for him to die? Plainly, if these two, that is, the image of the earthly human being, which pertains to the animal body, and the image of the heavenly, which pertains to the spiritual body, we wish to refer also to conversations; as we set the resurrection of the spiritual body on the side of righteousness, so we ought to set the death of the animal body on the side of sin; because as this resurrection will come about in the righteousness of Christ, so that death came about in the iniquity of Adam. But if you understand this, and consent to this most open truth; I grant what you say, that the earthly and the heavenly are referred to vices and virtues; for just as the virtue of Christ will bring it about that the spiritual body rise, so the fault of Adam brought it about that the animal body die. Not, then, does that sentence of the same Apostle to the Romans agree with this: *For as you presented your members to serve uncleanness, and iniquity unto iniquity; so now present your members to serve righteousness unto sanctification*[^258]. For there he was speaking of evil and good morals; but here he speaks of the resurrection of the body and the death of the body. But since those who already use reason cannot reach the glorious resurrection, which will then be, when the spiritual body rises, unless they both believe and hope this; therefore, mentioning that we have borne the image of the earthly human being, in which is through a human being death; he exhorts that we bear the image of the heavenly human being, in which is through a human being the resurrection of the dead; so that, as through Adam's sin we came into the death of the animal body, so through Christ's righteousness we may come into the resurrection of the spiritual body. Then he subjoins: *But this I say, brothers, that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God*. Where we do not reprehend you for having believed that by the name of flesh and blood was signified carnal prudence, not the very substance of the animal body, which is sown indeed in dishonor, but yet will rise in glory, and will beyond doubt possess the kingdom of God. Although this too can be understood otherwise, so that in this place, by the name of flesh and blood, corruption is signified, which we now see in flesh and blood; which corruption surely will not possess the kingdom of God, because this corruptible will be clothed with incorruption. Whence, when he had said: *Flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God*; as though expounding what he had signified by these names, lest the very carnal substance be believed, he added: *Neither will corruption possess incorruption*. And according to this sense he rather seems to weave the rest. But whichever of these the author of those same words wished to signify, neither is against the faith, which holds itself thus, that it does not doubt that the family of God gathered from all nations will possess the kingdom of God in incorruptible flesh. Not, then, do we reprehend this, which even before us very many Catholic treaters of the divine Scriptures said, that flesh and blood here can be taken as human beings who are wise according to flesh and blood, and therefore will not possess the kingdom of God; for so the same Teacher of the Gentiles says: *To be wise according to the flesh is death*[^259]. But that you are unwilling that the death of the animal body happened through the sin of the first man, when you hear the same Apostle saying: *The body is dead because of sin*[^260]; and dare not deny that the resurrection of the spiritual body, which is opposed on the contrary to the death of the animal body, will be present through the righteousness of the second man; and this you are unwilling for, that you may fill paradise, the memorable place of blessed delights, with the bodies of the dead, and through these even with the torments of the dying; this we reprehend, this we detest, this we judge worthy of anathema. For what death will be insulted at the end, when it will be said: *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?* Except either the devil, the author even of bodily death, or the death of the body itself, which the resurrection of the body will swallow up? For this word will then come about, when this corruptible will have put on incorruption, and this mortal will have put on immortality. For without ambiguity the Apostle says: *When this corruptible will have put on immortality, then will come about the word that is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?* To what death will this be said, except to that which will be swallowed up in victory? And what is this, except that which will then be swallowed up, when this corruptible and mortal body will be clothed with incorruption and immortality? Of this bodily death, then, the sting is sin; because to this death it will be said: *Where, O death, is your sting?* Which sting he said is sin; the sting, namely, by which death was made, not which death itself made; just as the cup of death is that by which death is made, not that which is made by death. How, then, as you suppose, will not this, but perpetual death be insulted? Will it itself be swallowed up in victory, when this mortal will have put on immortality? Does it itself contend against the saints, so that its fear may be conquered by the contest of those whom it formerly held conquered, when, by fearing it, they sinned? Did not the Lord die for the sake of conquering it, and empty out him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and free those who through the fear of death were all their life subject to the guilt of servitude[^261]? Were they guilty by the fear of eternal death; when rather those who do not fear it become guilty? On which account the Lord, that this death of the body should not be feared, whose fear makes guilty, but rather that everlasting one, which by not fearing they become guilty, says most openly: *Do not fear those who kill the body, and afterward have nothing they can do; but fear him who has the power to destroy both body and soul in hell*[^262]. This is surely the second and perpetual death, against whose fear the saints do not contend, but rather against this temporal one of the body. For that they may conquer this, they fear that one; because when they have conquered this for piety and righteousness, they will not feel that one. This one, then, not that one, they will insult, saying: *Where, O death, is your victory?* which elsewhere is written: *Where, O death, is your strife?*[^263] Since, therefore, the sting of this death is sin; with what brow do you dare to say that it was not by the sin of the first man brought about that in him we were separated from the tree of life, and punished even by the death of the body? What is it, I ask you, that, against the evidence of the divine words, you bark with the incredible rabidity of foaming wordiness, as though your soul could not obtain life in the paradise of God, unless you introduced thither the death of the body, with so many and so great bodily diseases, butchers, forerunners of death? Attend rather to yourself, lest, by sending the penalties of the body into the place of holy delights, you yourself pay, in the place of perpetual sorrows, the penalties both of soul and of body.

**VI. 41.** *Julian:* In this place, surely, Augustine supposes the sting of death to be that ancient sin, not understanding what follows, that is: *the power of sin is the law*; which law he tries to assert to be the precept that was set for Adam. But that law was not the power of sin, but truly the kind of sin. For it is one thing to give power to an existing thing, another to procreate what did not exist. The eating of that tree, then, if it had not been forbidden, would not have been evil; but after it was forbidden, and usurped by transgression, there was born there, from the one forbidding and the one despising, sin; although the law had not been given for this, that there should be transgression; but yet, by doing the same, that is, by eating of the tree, the human being would not have transgressed, because the tree was good, unless the tasting of it had been forbidden by the law. That, then, which is depraved in itself, for example parricide, sacrilege, adultery, and is recognized to be evil even without a law passed, is well said, among transgressors, who are made more desirous by the prohibition, to have acquired power through the law; but that which is not iniquitously usurped unless it be forbidden, the kind there properly, not the strength, is established to be conferred on it from the occasion of the law. But since here I have been rather long, at the end of this book too I admonish my reader to inspect vigilantly, that no occasion of Manichean impiety is found in the law of God; but that, if any things are thought ambiguous, he should not doubt that they can both be expounded according to the rules of truth and reason, and agree with righteousness. And so both those who say: The resurrection of the dead will not be through Christ, and these who assert, equally against the Apostle, that Christ did not have a body of our nature, by which they venerate the decrees of the Manichees; we condemn with the same vigor that agrees with the law of God.

*Augustine:* I never said that the law which was given in paradise was what the Apostle signified, where he says: *The power of sin is the law*. In vain, then, against me, as though I had said this, have you said many things. For I always understood that law to be the power of sin—of sin, that is, already existing, but working less—about which the same Apostle says: *What then shall we say? Is the law sin? Far be it! But I did not know sin except through the law. For I did not know concupiscence, unless the law said: You shall not covet. But sin, taking occasion, worked in me, through the commandment, all concupiscence*[^264]. Behold how the law is the power of sin: for sin worked less, when it did not yet work transgression, because the law had not yet been given: *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression*[^265]. There was not yet, then, all concupiscence, before, by prohibition, it so grew, and became so strong, that it burst the bond of the very prohibition by which it had grown. This you too showed that you yourself know, disputing many things to this sentiment; although, that you might show this, you applied rather other apostolic testimonies, not this which I have now used; perhaps for this reason, lest you confess that concupiscence is sin. For he most openly demonstrated that it is sin, who said what I mentioned: *I did not know sin except through the law*. And, as though we asked what sin: *For I did not know concupiscence*, he says, *unless the law said: You shall not covet*. This concupiscence, then, surely evil, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, was not yet before that great sin of the first man; but then began to be, and vitiated human nature as though in propagation, whence it draws original sin. For with it every human being is born; nor is the guilt of this concupiscence loosed except in those being reborn, so that after this absolution he is defiled by it only who consents to it for perpetrating an evil work, with the spirit either not lusting against it, or not lusting more strongly. Sins, then, add strength to this same concupiscence, sins which accrue by the proper will of those sinning, and the very habit of sinning, which is not without reason wont to be called a second nature; but not even then is it all concupiscence. For it still has whereby it may grow; since it is less, as long as it is sinned not by one knowing, but by one ignorant. Therefore he does not say: I did not have, but: *I did not know concupiscence, unless the law said: You shall not covet. But sin, taking occasion, worked in me, through the commandment, all concupiscence*. For it is all concupiscence, when forbidden things are coveted more ardently, and sins now known, the excuse of ignorance removed, and transgression of the law accruing, are committed more monstrously. Whence the law of God, among those whom the grace of God through the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world does not aid, was called not the correction of the one sinning, but rather the power of sin. And therefore, when he had said: *The power of sin is the law*, as though it were answered: What then shall we do, if sin is not taken away by the law, but increased? At once subjoining where there ought to be hope for those contending: *Thanks*, he says, *be to God who gave us the victory*; or, as other codices have, which the Greek also has, *who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*[^266]. Most truly altogether: *For if a law had been given which could make alive, then surely righteousness would be from the law; but Scripture enclosed all things under sin, that the promise by faith of Jesus Christ might be given to believers*[^267]. For these are the sons of the promise and the vessels of mercy, to whom the promise by the faith of Jesus Christ was given for this reason, that they obtained mercy that they might be faithful, which the Apostle says even of himself[^268]; lest even the very faith, from which begins, and to which is referred, whatever we do temperately and justly and piously, be so attributed to the choice of our will, as though it were not granted us by God's mercy; by whom even the will itself, as it is written, is prepared[^269]. Whence holy Church, through the mouths of supplicating priests, prays not only for the faithful, that in that which they believe they may not fail by persevering piety, but also for the unbelieving, that they may believe. For from the time that, through human free choice, Adam committed that great sin, and condemned the whole human race in common[^270], whatever human beings are freed from this common condemnation are freed only by divine grace and mercy; and whatever the law of God commands is fulfilled only with him who commands aiding, inspiring, granting; who is asked that the faithful may remain, may advance, may be perfected; who is asked also that the unbelieving may begin to believe. These prayers of holy Church, growing and fervent throughout the whole world, those wish to oppress and extinguish, who, against this grace of God, exalt rather than defend the choice of the human will, so that they hurl it down more grievously from on high. Among whom you hold the contentious place, either alone or chiefly, you who are unwilling that Christ Jesus be Jesus to little ones, whom you contend to be tainted by no original sin; whereas he was for this reason called Jesus, because he saved his people, not from bodily diseases, which he was wont to heal even in a people not his own, but from their sins[^271]. Although, then, the Apostle, in that which he says: *But the sting of death is sin*, expressed without ambiguity that death which was opposed to the resurrection of the body, of which he was speaking, that is, the death of the body; for it itself will be swallowed up in victory, when, with the spiritual body rising, it will be no more, because there will be immortality even of the body, which can be lost by no sin: yet in that which he added following: *But the power of sin is the law*, he did not signify the law which was given in paradise; for that could not be the power of sin, which as yet was none; but he said that law to be the power of sin, which entered in that sin might abound, and might work all concupiscence; that is, not only that which, having arisen in paradise, mortified even the body, with which every human being is born; nor only that which grew by sins accruing through the evil morals of each one; but also that which, more ardently aroused by the prohibiting commandment, reached even to transgression; so that the victory, by which not only the desire of sin, but also the fear of bodily death would be conquered, and finally the weakness of mortality itself swallowed up, might be bestowed not by the law given through Moses, but by the grace made through Christ. Accordingly the Apostle so said: *But the sting of death is sin; but the power of sin is the law; but thanks be to God who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*; as though he said: The sting of death indeed is sin, because by sin this death of the body too was made; whose author, or even death itself, will be told at the end by those who, rising in glory, will swallow it up: *Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?* But this sting, that is, sin, entering through one, and passing into all human beings with death, multiplied by others added too, not even the law holy and just and good could take away; for it became rather its power, when, the concupiscence being forbidden, it burned more, and reached the accumulation of transgression. What, then, remained, except that grace should come to the aid? *Thanks*, therefore, *be to God who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ*; who, by forgiving our debts, and by not leading us into temptation, brings us to the final victory, by which even the death of the body is swallowed up; so that *he who glories* may not trust in his own virtue, but *glory in the Lord*[^272]. This right and Catholic faith, in which we have also learned and hold that the death of the body too was made by that sting which is sin, is as far diverse from the error of the Manichees, and is rather declared adverse to it, that they rather say with you than with us that Adam was so made mortal that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he was to die. Nor yet do we therefore call you Manichees, because you too say this; nor yet do you see that neither ought we to be called Manichees by you, because both they and we say that the concupiscence by which the flesh lusts against the spirit is an evil. But you differ from them, in that which you say together, by a dissimilar indeed, yet by another error, you who do not, like them, attribute the death of the flesh to an alien nature commingled with us, but impute it to our own nature vitiated by no sin; and so unhappily and indecently fill the paradise of most honorable and most happy pleasure with the funerals of the dead and the torments of the dying. But we, in that which we say with the Manichees that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which it lusts against the spirit, is an evil, and is not from the Father[^273], differ from them not by another, though dissimilar, yet by the same heretical error, but by Catholic truth; because we discuss that this discord of the two concupiscences of flesh and spirit happened to us not, with them, through the commingling of an alien nature coeternal with God and evil, but, with the Catholic Ambrose and his associates, passed over into our nature through the transgression of the first man[^274]; and against both of you we confidently assert: and the flesh of Christ we proclaim, not, like them, none, nor, as you slanderously claim, alien from the nature of our flesh; but immune and altogether whole from this our fault, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit. But you, by denying that those things are evils which are evils, and by referring their origin not to the sin of the first man, do not bring it about that they be not evils; but that they be believed to be from an evil nature coeternal with the eternal good, and by a detestable blindness you give your vote to the Manichees, and accuse them in vain, because you pitiably aid them.
