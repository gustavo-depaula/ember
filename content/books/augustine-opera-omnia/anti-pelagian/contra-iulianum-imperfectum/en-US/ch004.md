# Book Three

**III. 1.** *Julian.* It ought indeed to have been the case that among the human race a reverence for all the virtues should flourish; it ought to have been the case that crimes should always be resisted by a wise mind, and the favor of the Creator earned by good pursuits; finally, it was worthy that—since here the first and happier step of continual devotion seems both rare and exceedingly arduous—at least vices long cherished should be spat out, and a return made to the safeguard of amendment and penance. It ought certainly, at least in this, that an inviolable reverence for God should remain, that it should not be necessary for us to vindicate the divine law with so great a contest; but because the fury of sinners has reached the point that with the greatest labors we must teach that God is just, presuming on the help of that very justice whose cause is pleaded, let us render what we promised in the preceding book.

*Augustine.* You seek the help of God that your empty books may be completed, and you do not seek that your perverse meanings may be corrected. Yet I could wish you would say for what you ask the help of God in this work, since it is in your free choice either to do this or not to do it. Or is it that those things may be at hand for you which are not in your power and without which this cannot be accomplished—as are, to omit others, food itself and leisure? But these God supplies to us almost always through the wills of others. You see, then, that what you ask of almighty God, when you ask help in order to complete your books, is that he work in the wills of men what helps you and what does not hinder you. For if men should be unwilling to supply you food and fitting means, if finally they should be unwilling to cease from disquieting and hindering you, you will not be able to write or dictate these things. You hope, then, that by the help of God the wills of the men among whom you live are so dealt with that nothing necessary is lacking to you. For—what you do not believe—*the will is prepared by the Lord*[^1]. Either, then, now correct your teaching, or cease to ask divine help for defending it.

**III. 2.** *Julian.* Since in the first volume it had been established by clear definitions that God is so just that, if it could be proved that he is not just, it would be proved that he is not God, and since on this no doubt had remained, it became clear also that justice is nothing other than the virtue that never judges anything iniquitously, does nothing iniquitously, but renders to each his own without fraud, without favor—that is, without partiality of persons.

*Augustine.* *Without fraud* you say truly, lest the undeserving be punished; but *without favor*—if this were the justice of God, Christ would never have died for the ungodly, that is, for those who merited nothing good and much evil; finally, he would never have adopted into his kingdom the little ones, whose good works and will did not go before, nor, in the same case, would he have defrauded other little ones of participation in the same kingdom—he who *never judges iniquitously, does nothing iniquitously, and renders to each his own without fraud*. Acknowledge, then, the little ones who are taken up into the kingdom of God to be vessels for honor through grace, and the other little ones who are not taken up into that honor to be vessels for dishonor through judgment, and at last at some time, lest you make God unjust, confess original sin.

**III. 3.** *Julian.* But the reason of that virtue holds then, if he punished none of his subjects except for those offenses which it was established were committed by free will.

*Augustine.* That too was committed by free will—the will of him in whom human nature was condemned, from which men are born liable to condemnation, unless they are reborn in him who was not born liable. This Christian teaching you want overthrown, but, while it stands, you are overthrown.

**III. 4.** *Julian.* Nor would he command men things which he knew could not be kept through their nature, nor would he judge anyone guilty for natural things.

*Augustine.* But *there was Adam, and in him we all were*, when he so sinned as to ruin in himself all, except those whom he would free from there—he who came to seek what had perished[^2].

**III. 5.** *Julian.* Nor would he impute the sins of some to others, and on this account would not adjudge to eternal punishments innocent children on account of their parents' iniquities—children who had done nothing of themselves, whether good or evil, by which they could be taught to have imitated their parents' crimes. From which gathered points it is established that God both is and is just—whom it had become clear would, if he did anything unjust, undergo in his divinity as great a loss as he had suffered in equity.

*Augustine.* You say truly, and on this account he does nothing unjust when he weighs down the sons of Adam with a heavy yoke from the day of their coming forth from their mother's womb[^3]—which would surely be unjust if there were no original sin.

**III. 6.** *Julian.* And yet—O the unhappiness of human error! For weighing the very reason of our conflict, I am shaken with excessive grief—could this have come into doubt, and could this cause have needed assertion? Could it, I say, be doubted in the churches which professed to believe in Christ, whether God judges justly, that is, reasonably!

*Augustine.* Because this is not doubted, therefore it is written that there is a heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam from the day of their coming forth from their mother's womb[^4]; for Pelagian wisdom is not better than ecclesiastical wisdom.

**III. 7.** *Julian.* But I have too much forgotten, in my very reverence for the matters, the contention that is at issue. For I wonder that it could be in doubt concerning the equity of God, when it is established that in the synagogues of the Traducianists nothing is doubted concerning his iniquity.

*Augustine.* Because there is no doubt concerning the equity of God, therefore the heavy yoke upon the little one is believed to be just; and because this is believed to be just, therefore the little one is not believed to be without original sin. Accordingly, in the catholic Church, from which the Pelagians went out from us, it is not, as you say, nothing concerning the iniquity of God, but rather nothing concerning his equity that is doubted—where even an infant, whose life upon the earth is of one day, is taught and said not to be without the stain of sin[^5], and therefore in the evils which he suffers, God is acknowledged not unjust, but just.

**III. 8.** *Julian.* Which is certainly so much the worse, by how much the pursuit of evils is graver than the neglect of goods, by how much an intention toward profanity is more pernicious than a doubting of the truth, by how much, finally, it is more wicked to dare to accuse God than to be unwilling to venerate him.

*Augustine.* But it is you who accuse God, while you deny that the little ones, whom you discern to be weighed down by his judgment with a heavy yoke, have any sin at all.

**III. 9.** *Julian.* *The fool indeed had said*, as the prophet David is witness, *in his heart, that there is no God*[^6]; yet he had not said: There is indeed a God, but nevertheless an unjust one; so harmoniously did the voice of all nature resound that justice cleaves inseparably to God, that one would more easily be found who denied his substance than one who denied his equity; there could exist one who thought that not to be which he did not see, but none had been found who called iniquitous what he believed to be divine.

*Augustine.* And you yourself have been found; for to whom, except such a one, does he say: *You suspected iniquity, that I would be like you?*[^7] But since catholic Christians know both that God exists and that he is just, therefore they cannot doubt that men who are born, if they die in their little age without being reborn, although they are images of God, are nevertheless not unjustly—but by the merit of original sin—not received into the kingdom of God.

**III. 10.** *Julian.* That fool, then, by denying God, seemed to have stood at the outermost edge of crimes. But the nation of the Manicheans and Traducianists has been found, by whose profanities he is outdone.

*Augustine.* Knowing how clear and approved Doctors of the Church of Christ believed concerning original sin and the justice of God what I believe, taught what I teach, defended what I defend, I ought to hear your insults just as I hear my own praises.

**III. 11.** *Julian.* But to return whence we digressed: it had become clear that by him whom we confess to be the true God nothing can be done in judgment that would conflict with justice, and on this account that no one is held guilty for the sins of others, and therefore that the innocence of the newborn is by no means condemned for the iniquity of their parents, because it would be unjust that guilt should be handed down through the seeds.

*Augustine.* Why, then, was it said: *Their seed is accursed from the beginning*[^8]? For it was not said as: *The seed of Canaan and not of Judah*[^9], where it was shown to whom they had been made like and from whom they had degenerated; but he said that the seed of those very ones was accursed whom he wanted understood to be naturally evil, as are all the sons of Adam, of whom by grace they become sons of God. For where it is said: *Not being ignorant that their nation is wicked and their malice natural, and that their thought could not be changed forever, for their seed was accursed from the beginning*[^10], I think it is nature, not imitation, that is convicted; and how nature, except corrupted by sin, not created so in the first man? From what beginning, then, was the seed accursed, except from that in which through one man sin entered into the world? By themselves they could not be changed—not by almighty God, who nevertheless surely did not change them by a most just, though most hidden, judgment. For the Apostle knew that he had been changed from this mass not by free choice but by the grace of God, when he said: *We too were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest*[^11].

**III. 12.** *Julian.* Although this stands in so great a light that nothing is found either more absolute or more true, nevertheless I had promised to prove this very thing by the testimony of the divine law—that is, that it is most iniquitous if the crimes of those who beget should be imputed to the children who are born, and that God so opposes this that he prescribed even in his own law that the deformity of judges should perpetrate nothing of the kind. This, then, I had pledged myself to render; but since the second book is occupied in expounding the sentences of the apostle Paul, it is fitting that faith with the promise be fulfilled in the first parts of this volume. We read, then, in Deuteronomy, in the catalogue of precepts by which the life and conduct of that people is ordered, that this too was most openly commanded by God; for, that it may be understood from the preceding and following things among which it is placed: *You shall not defraud the wage of the poor and needy, of your brethren or of the proselytes who are in your cities; you shall render his wage daily, let not the sun set upon it, because he is poor and has his hope in it; and let him not cry out against you to the Lord, and it be a sin in you. The fathers shall not die for the children, and the children shall not die for the fathers; each one shall die in his own sin*[^12].

*Augustine.* He said this of children already born, not of those condemned in the first parent, in whom all have sinned and in whom all die. And this precept indeed he gave to men judging, that a father should not die for a son or a son for a father, when only the father or the son was found guilty. But God did not bind his own judgments—whether when he judges by himself or when he judges through men to whom he gives the prophetic spirit—by this law. For when, except Noah with his own, he destroyed all the rest by the flood, he did not set apart the infants, who had not yet imitated their parents; nor did that fire take up the Sodomites without their little ones[^13]—which, if he had wished, the Almighty surely could have done. And that Achan, found the one transgressor of the precept, was nevertheless killed along with his sons and daughters[^14]. What of so many cities conquered under the same leader Joshua, the man of God? Were they not all so slain that no one breathing was left[^15]? What evil, then, had the little ones done? Did they not, through the sins of their parents, of which they could not yet be either conscious or imitators, undergo by divine judgment the common penalty? God judges, then, in one way, and commands man to judge in another, since God is without any doubt more just than man. These things you ought to have thought of beforehand, lest you should dwell on examples not pertaining to the case.

**III. 13.** *Julian.* *You shall not turn aside the judgment of the proselyte and the orphan and the widow; you shall not take the widow's garment as a pledge, because you were a servant in the land of Egypt and the Lord your God freed you from there; on account of this I command you to do this word*[^16]. When God instituted the form of judging, he took care at once to sanction this, that neither should parents be struck in the crime of their children nor children in the crime of their parents. The beginning, then, and the entrance of the justice which he commanded to be kept in judging, he showed to be this: that kinship should not burden the innocent, and that the hatred which a person had merited should not run out into his very lineage. In the case of acts, then, justice distinguishes those whom relationship joins; which it surely would not do, if there were one reason for will and seed, or if the work of choice passed to posterity by fruitfulness. We have, then, sufficiently and more than sufficiently taught, by this one testimony, that that most foul depravity of judgment, which the new error embraces, is crushed by the ancient authority of the promulgated law. Which sentence is certainly so brought forward into the case that it has left no room for doubting.

*Augustine.* God, who said in the book of Leviticus, resists you: *And those who remain of you shall perish for their own sins and for the sins of their fathers*[^17].

**III. 14.** *Julian.* For God, instituting what form ought to be kept in examinations, prescribed that innocence should not be joined to the perils of its own kinship, and, as he separated the father from the punishment of a sinning son, so he separated the son from the condemnation of the father, showing surely, by the like outcome of each person, that the sins of parents can no more pass to the children than the children's pass to the parents.

*Augustine.* The little ones choke you, who are read to have been so often slain not even for their own sins, but for the sins of their parents alone.

**III. 15.** *Julian.* He, then, who against this sentence says that there is a transmission of sin, let him also say that there is a return of sin, so that, if sins descend from parents to children, they may ascend from children to parents, since the authority of the divine law has shown that the crimes of parents harm children just as little as the children's harm the parents.

*Augustine.* The authority of the divine law was unwilling, in human judgments, that children should pay penalties for parents—not in divine judgments, where God says: *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the children*[^18]. You ought so to read the words of the law you want, that you consider you are going to hear the things you do not want.

**III. 16.** *Julian.* Therefore, striving to go against this sentence, let him assert that the thing happens alike which was commanded alike not to happen. For the law of God can more easily be denied than emended; and, although the denial is profane, the correction is nevertheless more profane and more absurd. For if of his two decrees you should venerate one and execrate the other, ungrateful in the part you accept, you are compelled to obey even that which you reject, since by the dignity of the one that is dear to you, even that which you opposed is defended; and most absurdly does anyone believe that he venerates those precepts, part of which he dares to assail. Whence the whole law can more consequently be denied than corrected; but no one will attempt to correct it except the impious; by the religious and prudent, therefore, the whole is accepted, the whole is praised. Nor indeed let it move anyone that he sees the rite of the old sacrifices have ceased in the age of the New Testament. There is not one reason for virtues and for victims: the perennity of precepts is one thing, the temporality of sacrifices another. Yet with Christ coming, who was figured by the ancient victims, the things that had been instituted were fulfilled, not condemned; for they are not foretold as practiced in their own times, but, with the perfection succeeding which had been promised by their offices, they came to rest.

*Augustine.* What does this matter to the case? God said he would repay the sins of fathers upon children, not sacrifices; and although parents can even imitate evil children, God nevertheless never said: I will repay the sins of children upon fathers, but wherever he said this—and he said it often—he said *of fathers upon children*, where surely he shows himself prosecuting the defects of generation, not of imitation.

**III. 17.** *Julian.* But the precepts in which piety, faith, justice, and holiness are contained not only have not ceased but have even been heaped up. And this law of justice to be kept in judgments, which we brought forth from Deuteronomy, looks not to the age of ceremonies but to the perennity of precepts, and did not depart with circumcision, but perseveres with justice.

*Augustine.* It has already been said to you that these are judgments commanded to men, not prejudgments laid down for God. Finally, if a man judging should say: I will repay the sins of fathers upon children, he says it most unjustly and contradicts the divine command; nor yet for that reason is God either a liar or unjust when he says this.

**III. 18.** *Julian.* It is settled, then—if Moses, through whom God speaks, is believed rather than Augustine, through whom Mani speaks—that children are not held guilty by nature for the sins of their parents.

*Augustine.* That I defend against you the faith which the catholic, holy, and illustrious Doctors who were before us learned and taught in the catholic Church, you yourself know; but since, if you should dare to tear them, not even your own people bear with you, therefore you have chosen me alone, whom, assailed with the reproach of a false charge, you may persuade men to flee as one to be shunned, so that that faith may be fled which, when defended, condemns you. I have already said to you above too: when, for the defense of the catholic faith, I hear insults from heretics, I count them as praises. Why do you labor to proclaim to us what we know? Moses said the truth, but you say nothing. *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the children*[^19]—it is not a man who says this, but God; nor did God command in this passage that a man should do this, but he indicated what he himself does.

**III. 19.** *Julian.* And so the crimes of those who beget do not pass to posterity, although they are born of them, just as neither do the crimes of children pass to parents, who could by no means have been begotten of their own children. Thus, then, one's birth can no more stand in the way of innocence than it can be a hindrance, since it does not exist.

*Augustine.* Yet you cannot deny that parents can imitate their own children, nor that God ever said: I will repay the sins of children upon fathers. When, therefore, he says *of fathers upon children*, he convicts not imitation but generation—not in the way that, from that one in whom nature itself was changed for the worse (so that on this account it is even necessary for man to die), but yet in some way, certain sins of various fathers are repaid upon the children, punished not by imitation but by generation; therefore he does not say *unto the third and fourth* imitation, but: *generation*[^20]—you indeed being unwilling, but nevertheless you too, whether you will or not, hearing it.

**III. 20.** *Julian.* The matter is indeed done; but yet, that the reader may be attentive to what I bring forward, I give warning. If anyone should exist who professed with freedom of words what the Traducianist tries to accomplish by arguing—that is, that he should declare war on the law of God, and despise without any fear the sentence we brought forward, and assert in whatever ways he could that both of these things are false which God willed to be kept, and undermine the sentence of which we speak from both sides, as far as in him lay, reckoning utterly that parents are wont, as well as ought, to be condemned for the sins of their children, and children for the sins of their parents—not even such a man could assert a transmission of sin, even according to his own opinions. Why? Namely, because, even if it were established that the sentence of the law is false which testifies that such kinships cannot be sprinkled with mutual crimes, nevertheless it remained unshaken that there is no transmission of sin. For by the very fact that the guilt both of parents returned to children and of children to parents, it was established that it was not of generation that the sins of those begetting reached the children, since they had also returned from children to parents, where generation could not be the cause. Let it appear, then, what I have accomplished here: that the authority of the divine law is inviolable and such that no arguments of impieties can overthrow it, but that by its sanction it was most signally and most absolutely prescribed that it is a foul opinion and a perversity of judgment—which he had commanded to be vehemently guarded against—if children should be pronounced guilty for the sin of their parents; by which thunderbolt the structure of transmission has burst asunder; but yet that the faith we maintain is fortified by such safeguards of truth that it is not shaken even by that profanity which can deny the law of God.

*Augustine.* You seek where you may range about, with wandering garrulity—not copious, but odious to those who, clinging to the matters, despise superfluous words. For you are conquered by the adversaries you have, and you set before yourself, to be conquered, adversaries you do not have. For who tells you that what God willed to be kept in human judgments is false, since now fathers and children have their own proper cases, pertaining to the life of each, which is led separately, so that neither children for fathers nor fathers for children are punished? No one opposes either the law or you when you say these things. But do not grow deaf against God saying: *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the children*[^21]—and, while he says this constantly, nowhere saying that he repays the sins of children upon fathers—so that you may understand that it is regarded not whom they imitate, but from whom they are begotten.

**III. 21.** *Julian.* Now, therefore, let the discourse look back to him with whom we deal. Do you acquiesce in the law of God (in profession for the time being—but what you do by arguing, we know), or do you resist it? If you acquiesce, the contention is removed; if you resist, the consent is removed; if you acquiesce, the perfidy of the Traducianists is extinguished; if you resist, that of the Manicheans is revealed—provided only it is established that it by no means agrees with your opinion and the law of God.

*Augustine.* I acquiesce in the law of God, but you do not acquiesce. I do not deny that neither a son for a father nor a father for a son ought to be condemned, when they have their own separate cases; but you are unwilling to hear in Leviticus: *They shall perish for the sins of their fathers*[^22], and in the book of Numbers: *Repaying the sins of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation*[^23], and in Jeremiah: *Repaying the sins of the fathers into the bosom of their children after them*[^24]. These and other like things you do not hear; in these and such testimonies of the law you do not acquiesce, and yet you do not cease to speak and to charge catholics with being Manicheans.

**III. 22.** *Julian.* Unless perhaps you say that God indeed commanded this, but does not do what he commanded—nay, does things contrary to those he commanded to be done.

*Augustine.* And how unwisely you think this, why do you not attend? For God sometimes does the contrary of what he commanded to be done. Nor is there need that I recall many things, lest it be long. Behold, I say what is known to all: divine Scripture commanded man, saying: *Let not your own mouth praise you*[^25]; nor yet is God to be called arrogant or proud, when he does not cease to praise himself innumerable times; and as to the matter at issue, I have already shown above how God, without any iniquity, for the sins of the parents slew the little ones along with them, although he commanded man not to condemn children for the sins of fathers when he judges. If you would notice these things, you would not say those things; or if you do notice these things and yet say those things, notice also that what you say is empty.

**III. 23.** *Julian.* Although how great a profanity this is now vehemently appears, though scarcely indicated, nevertheless—with the leave of that very divinity whose equity we maintain—let us see, by even a light discussion, what kind of thing this is. That he himself, then, should become a transgressor of his own law: is he compelled by the necessity of affairs that press upon him, or by his own weakness? Or, because neither of these is the case, by the mere lust of offending? But this not even Mani could say, and therefore he invented that your God endured a grave battle.

*Augustine.* You toss out words when you are pressed by the matters; he is not a transgressor of his own law when God, as God, does one thing, and commands man, as man, another.

**III. 24.** *Julian.* If, then, neither calamity nor weakness nor lust of transgression presses upon him, how can it be that he should destroy by judging that form of justice which he commended by commanding—nay, that he should rage not against this justice but against his own reverence (for so great is the power of equity that it both convicts those who deviate and is diminished by no authority of those who flee from it)—finally, that he should will just things to be done by us, and himself do what is unjust? Does he wish us to seem more just than he is himself—nay, not more just, but us equitable and himself iniquitous?

*Augustine.* What is this that you say, man, who are very unwise? By how much loftier, by so much more unsearchable is divine justice than human, and by so much more distant from this. For what just man permits a crime to be perpetrated which he has it in his power not to permit? And yet God permits these things—God, incomparably more just than all the just, and whose power is incomparably greater than all powers. Think on these things, and do not compare God the judge to men the judges—God, who is not to be doubted to be just even when he does what seems unjust to men, and what, if a man did it, he would be unjust.

**III. 25.** *Julian.* Or is that indeed just which he himself does—that is, imputing the sins of some to others—while he commands us what is unjust, namely, that we should pronounce each one guilty for the offenses of his own will?

*Augustine.* Read what was answered to you above, and learn, if you can, how original sins are understood to be both another's and ours—not another's for the same reason as ours; for they are another's because no one committed them in his own life, but ours because *there was Adam, and in him we all were*.

**III. 26.** *Julian.* And whence in him either so great envy or so great malignity? For it is envy, if by commanding he deceived his creature to this end—that it should not try, as far as it could, to imitate his virtues; but malignity, nay cruelty, if he punishes mortals for unjust works which they perpetrated when obeying his law.

*Augustine.* I have already shown above that God does justly certain things which, if a man does them, he does unjustly. For God avenges even his own injuries justly; but to men it is said: *Not avenging yourselves, dearly beloved, but give place to wrath; for it is written: Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord*[^26].

**III. 27.** *Julian.* Or perhaps he does not punish—which indeed he does prudently—but even rewards servants who have obeyed his precepts, though they teach injustice? And what did it profit him to envy, if even by doing unjust things mortals arrived where they would have arrived also by keeping justice? And since, for those whom he circumvents, nothing of happiness perishes, he himself is nevertheless deprived at once of the conscience of kindness and justice and of his honor. How much more tolerable it would be to withdraw one's neck from the profession of religion than to lead one's bereavement through such precipitous, such deadly ways?

*Augustine.* You follow yourself, saying nothing. Distinguish divine justice from human, and you will see that God justly repays the sins of fathers upon children—which, however, if a man should usurp it for himself in his own judgment, he is unjust. Do not you stray from the just way, so that, when you hear that the sins of fathers are avenged upon children, you either want God not to do it, or want a man to do it, by resisting either the divine testimonies or commands.

**III. 28.** *Julian.* Therefore, since God does not allow even his servants to commit any such thing as you assert is perpetrated by him, it is manifest that you have fled no less from his honor than from human reason; and on this account we are not, as you say, deceived by a Pelagian error, but directed by the law of God, so as to assert that it is iniquitous for the crimes of parents to be imputed to children.

*Augustine.* Not once but often God said that he repays the sins of fathers upon children, where surely he did not say that he repays the sins of children upon fathers, or of brothers upon brothers, or of friends upon friends, or of citizens upon citizens, or anything of the kind, that we might know, when that is said, that generation is convicted, not imitation; which you too could understand in the divine words, if you were not hindered by the Pelagian error.

**III. 29.** *Julian.* Which transmission of sin—the daughter of the Manicheans, your mother—it is lamented to have given birth to prodigiously in this storm.

*Augustine.* You do not reason, but you revile and slander. Reread the ancient expounders of the divine word and see that not in this storm, but long before us, in the words of the Apostle, this was understood—what he most openly said, that is, that *through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all human beings*[^27]—to pertain to generation, which regeneration heals, not to imitation, which it is rather you who have given birth to in this storm. Whence the storm of your novel teaching has cast you out from the face of the catholic Church like dust, which the wind casts out from the face of the earth[^28].

**III. 30.** *Julian.* That this which we assert was commanded by God has become clear; and although the open kinship of his sentence, conciliated to the understanding of all, admits in itself the blemish of no obscurity, nevertheless, lest you take refuge in the slowness of our talent and say that we do not understand what was commanded, let us teach also by another testimony—not now of a precept, but of a work carried out according to the precept—how the law was understood. We read in the fourth book of Kings concerning Amaziah, son of Joash, king of Judah: *It came to pass*, it says, *after the kingdom was established in his hands, he killed his servants who had killed his father; but their sons he did not kill, according to the covenant of the law of the Lord, in which it is commanded: The fathers shall not die for the children, nor the children for the fathers*[^29]. You see how the faith of history has shown the justice of the king judging, who, although he had been devout, nevertheless—since he is reported to have halted in mind in some matters—for the confirmation of his judgment the authority of the cited law of God came in; for lest this deed have too little weight from the consideration of the doer, it is commended as fulfilled according to the law and covenant of God.

*Augustine.* This judgment God willed to be of men, not his own—he who said: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^30]. Which he did even through a man, when through Joshua son of Nun he killed not only Achan but also his sons[^31]; or when through the same leader of his people he condemned the sons of the Canaanites—even the little ones who had not yet imitated their parents' sins by their own conduct—to perish along with those same parents, by a severity surely not unjust[^32]. Do not, then, garrulously and emptily multiply your writings, but attend diligently to the Scripture of God, lest what you think you have opened in one part of it you find closed against you in another.

**III. 31.** *Julian.* By two and three witnesses it is wont to be believed even against the blood of a man; how much more, by two sacred witnesses—the law contained in Deuteronomy and the history that embraces the deeds of the kings—should it be believed, for the honor of God! How he willed to be judged, he himself prescribed; how what he had commanded ought to be received, the judgments brought forth according to his law attest; and is it still doubted that a transmission of sin cannot be proved by the authority of the Scriptures? Surely these are contraries for which we contend in such long conflicts—that is, one thing defended by you, another by us, and so contrary and conflicting that you contend by persecuting, we by disputing; you by fury, we by reason. Both sides, then, agree that these things differ from each other and conflict—that is, that children are punished for the sins of parents, and that children are not punished for the sins of parents; that there is a natural crime, and that there is no natural crime; that it is prescribed by the law of God that the sins of parents be imputed to children, and that it is prescribed by the law of God that the sins of parents not be imputed to children. That these opinions and sentences, warring against themselves, cannot be alike true is manifest. For even by the rules of learned disputation it is shown that, when a twofold opinion arises concerning uncertain matters, both can be false, but both cannot be true—which indeed can happen in diverse species and in things said to have a mean, yet not in those which are contrary but lack a mean. These matters are known to dialecticians; but for the reader inexperienced in this discipline, let us unlock it by some example.

*Augustine.* You so seek how to fill your most garrulous books that you try even to teach dialectic, where there is no need, to their readers—not considering how the Church of Christ casts out the dialectician whom it perceives to be a heretic. For who does not understand that you are doing this—that in the wisdom of word you may empty the cross of Christ[^33], who shed his blood for the remission of sins, for all for whom he died, among whom, by your own confession, are the little ones?

**III. 32.** *Julian.* Let Goliath, for example, come into question—of what color his complexion was—and let one affirm that he was black, another that he was white; this diverse opinion can be both false, but cannot be both true. For it cannot be true that he was black, if he was always white, or that he was white, if he remained black at every age. Both these opinions, then, which cannot be true at the same time, can be false at the same time in this way: if he was neither white nor black, but his color was tawny, or moderately tempered from the contraries, with whiteness broken yet without blackness infused. More easily, then, can diverse and contrary things be alike denied than alike affirmed. But these contraries which have no mean—as, for instance, good and evil, just and unjust, innocence and guilt—as they cannot meet at one time in one and the same thing, so it is necessary that, when one of them is posited, the other be denied—that is, as a precept or a counsel or a help cannot at one and the same time be both just and unjust, so too a man cannot at one and the same time be both guilty and innocent, and both good and evil.

*Augustine.* No one asks of what color Goliath was; but you ask, in order to lie in wait, with what colors you, a shape-shifter, may wrap yourself. If this dialectic of yours, which does not build you up but puffs you up and makes you ridiculous (since it makes you a little boaster)—if this dialectic, I say, which asserts that each one cannot be good and evil at the same time, is admitted to the laws of Christian disputation, then one man cannot at the same time be both good by nature and evil by defect—which, however, the truth cries out can be, and you do not deny; and you are yourself produced even as a witness against your own dialectic, since, as the matter stands, of these two things which you do not doubt to be contrary, you attribute one to the Founder of man, the other to the will. Let your dialectic, then, blush, and, just as you withdrew from communion, so let it too withdraw from the disputation of catholics; but if—what we desire—you should be willing to return, let it remain outside.

**III. 33.** *Julian.* Let the examples, then, be referred to the case. That the sins of parents be imputed to children, and that the sins of parents not be imputed to children—both these contraries cannot alike be proved just; but if it is justice that the offspring of those who beget be held guilty, it is necessary that it be injustice if it is said not to be guilty for those same sins; and as it is good to command justly, so it is evil to command anything unjustly; and although a plain matter becomes lighter by arguing, nevertheless, since it helps to confirm even by the suffrages of the divine law a case set in the absolute, let us cling to this passage, in which whoever stands with a sound mind is not allowed to wander through the precipices of the present questions. Do you acquiesce, then, announcer of natural evil, that it is prescribed by the law of God that children not be punished for the sins of parents? Do you also acknowledge that this precept was understood by that people no otherwise than as we now defend that it must be observed? And therefore that King Amaziah, obedient to the precepts of God, bridled with praiseworthy moderation the indignation he had conceived over the murder of his father, and, the slayers of his father being killed, nevertheless spared their sons not through cowardice but through justice. Amaziah is indeed praised, and is reported to have done this according to the law of God, and is commended for having obeyed the will of the Lord; but yet, darkened with the remnants of vanity, it is not passed over in silence, and it is shown that he did not imitate the devotion of his father David[^34]. Yet here, degenerating from that holiness of his lineage, he held in judgment the justice commended by the divine law; so great was the weight of manifest equity. Weigh, then, what evil your faith embraces: you apply to God—whom we confess to be eternal and pious and just—that iniquity which neither the pride of the man in purple nor the grief of the bereaved committed.

*Augustine.* Amaziah was a man, to whom it was not permitted to judge concerning hidden things, which he could not know; therefore the precept that was given to man he held in his own judgment, that he should not kill the sons for the sin of the parents. But a sin so great that it turned into nature—which through one man entered into the world, without which no man is born—how could it be avenged by men, since it passed with death to all human beings in such a way that its own penalty is its companion even unto everlasting destruction, except where divine grace has healed generation by regeneration? This, then, pertains to the judgment of God, not of men, like many other things concerning which men cannot judge at all. And therefore God commanded man in one way to judge parents and children now leading their own lives separately, but he himself judged in another way, when, according to his unsearchable justice, he condemned the transgressing nature, which he knew in the root though it had not yet sprung up in the seed, along with its stock—being going to free from this condemnation whom he willed, by a grace likewise unsearchable; although even to children now living separately he repaid the sins of fathers living separately, which he was unwilling to be permitted to the man judging, because he himself knows why he justly does this when he does it, but human weakness does not know it.

**III. 34.** *Julian.* But to press the passage: it has been established that injustice cannot cleave to God; it has been established also that it was prescribed by him that the sins of those who beget should not harm the children; the very dignity of the one commanding asserted that what he prohibits is unjust. Furthermore, to deal more liberally with you, I grant you the faculty of answering concerning those two opinions which I set hand to hand above—that is, that the sins of parents be imputed to children, or not be imputed; which do you think ought to be believed justice? If you say that yours fits even the last judgment, I report back: do you think that the remaining one—ours—is just or unjust? Without doubt you will pronounce it iniquitous; but this is commanded to be kept by the authority of the law. You see, then, that one thing necessarily remains of three: that you either confess the law of God to be unjust—nay, through the law accuse God himself of iniquity—or certainly take refuge in the voice of your teachers and say that the law which was given through Moses was not commanded by your God, or, if you dare to bring forth neither of these, confess that a transmission of sin is asserted against the documents and precepts of the law. For it is not to be believed that you can be so unwise as to say that God holds justice indeed in his precepts, but iniquity in his judgments, or certainly, according to your teaching, that he keeps justice in his judgments but teaches iniquity in his precepts; which, although we dealt with above, we have nevertheless now necessarily repeated.

*Augustine.* Odiously you repeat what you say idly; for you have leisure to roll out again by garrulity the same things which you cannot assert by truth, and to say without measure what you are unable to build up in any way. For you want these two things to seem contrary to each other: that the sins of fathers are repaid upon children, and that children are not to be punished for the sins of fathers—as if I said one of these, and God the other. Deaf man, God said both; therefore both are just, because the just one said this. But that you may understand that God did not speak things contrary to each other, distinguish the persons of God the judge and of man the judge according to the diversity of cases; thus you will neither make God guilty, though repaying the sins of fathers upon children, nor compel a man to judge so; but you set these two before me as if contrary to each other, with so great a prolixity and perplexity of disputation, for no other reason than because you say much and savor little.

**III. 35.** *Julian.* But if you say that both are just—both what we say and what you say, that is, both what the law of God sanctioned and what Mani and the Traducianist feigned—here indeed, restraining the power of manifest reason, we meet you more kindly and more mildly. Why, then, if you believe both this to be good which we say and that to be good which you say, did you stir up all Italy with such factions? Why did you excite seditions at Rome with hired peoples? Why, from the expenses of the poor, did you fatten through almost all Africa droves of horses, which, with Alypius escorting, you dispatched to tribunes and centurions? Why, by offering the inheritances of matrons, did you corrupt the powers of this world, that the stubble of public fury might burn against us? Why did you scatter the quiet of the churches? Why did you stain the times of a religious prince with the impiety of persecutions, if nothing else is said by us than what you too are forced to confess good?

*Augustine.* Just as the charges you bring against us are false, so the teachings you feign for yourselves are false; but say, as much as you can, every evil against us, lying; we will only defend against you the Christian and catholic faith. And what need is there for us to return like curses, and not rather to believe the Gospel and rejoice that, from these most false curses of yours, our reward is increased in heaven[^35]? But how, in this matter of which we now treat, can we believe both that to be good which you say and that to be good which we say, when we say that God said: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^36], but you so praise what he commanded the man who judges—that he not repay the sins of fathers upon children—that what he himself said he repays, you charge as if it were ours, and disapprove of it as if it were false and unjust, and do not perceive that in this you are resisting and slandering not us, but God?

**III. 36.** *Julian.* But thus far I have used a milder eloquence; now, however, the splendor of flaming reason shows that there is no fellowship between evil and good, profane and sacred, pious and impious, just and iniquitous, and that therefore there are no battles between the precepts and the judgments of God, but that it is contrary to impute the sins of some to others and to command that those same sins not be imputed—since of these two it is necessary that, one being granted, the other be removed, that is, one being shown just, the other iniquitous—and that in the law of God it is prescribed that the sins of parents not be imputed to children, and on this account that, by the same authority, the contrary of it—that is, the opinion of transmission—has utterly collapsed along with the Manicheans.

*Augustine.* It irks me to say true things so often, when it does not shame you to say empty things so often. God says that he repays the sins of fathers upon children; that he not repay the sins of fathers upon children, God indeed says, but to man. Both are to be approved, because God says both.

**III. 37.** *Julian.* I have surely shown that we defend nothing other than what, first, reason marks as most equitable; then also God confirms by his law; thirdly, that what had been commanded was, no otherwise than we assert, fulfilled with the praise of the work. It has also been impressed that this is the true justice which God showed, even by commanding, to please him. And on this account it is established that the Manichean transmission finds no support either in reason or in the testimonies of the law.

*Augustine.* The Manicheans say that nature, without beginning, is always evil, whence they contend that all evil comes; but the catholics say—what you have been unwilling to say—that it was created good and corrupted by sin, and needs Christ the physician from the little ones to the old, *since for all he died, therefore all have died*[^37]. Whence the Manicheans think that evil is to be separated from good in such a way that it is outside; but we, although we separate evil from good by our understanding, and do not believe that what is called evil is any substance, nevertheless do not think that evil is to be separated from those who are freed in such a way that it is outside, but we know that it is to be healed in them, so that it may not be. For they say that evil is an evil substance, but we, a defect of a good substance and no substance. Notice how great the difference is, and cease to begrudge Christ the physician to the little ones who are to be healed, lest the wrath of God remain upon them, of him who says: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^38]. Look at him who said this: it is God, not Mani; look at him who said: *Through a man death, and through a man the resurrection of the dead; for as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^39]: he is the Apostle of Christ, not the disciple of Mani; look at him who said: *We are all born under sin*[^40]: he is a catholic bishop, not Mani or Pelagius or a Pelagian heretic. Wherefore, since man too punishes the sin of act, but only God the sin of origin, therefore God, although he says that he repays the sins of fathers upon children, nevertheless commands man not to condemn the children too for the sins of fathers. Distinguish divine and human judgments, and you will find that these two are not contrary to each other.

**III. 38.** *Julian.* But lest perhaps someone exist so dull as to want it proved to himself by clear sentences that God, namely, does not judge otherwise than he commanded judgment to be made—which indeed is the mark of a nefarious contentiousness—nevertheless, since arms supply the truth unto every fullness of documents, it will not irk me to show most keen witnesses to this very point. And so, filled with the Holy Spirit, the prophet Ezekiel speaks: *The word of the Lord came to me, saying: Son of man, what is this parable to you, of those saying in the land of Israel: The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the teeth of the children are set on edge? As I live, says Adonai the Lord, this parable shall no more be said in Israel; for all souls are mine, as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son; all souls are mine. The soul that sins, it shall die. But the man who is just, who shall do judgment and justice, who shall not eat upon the mountains and shall not lift up his eyes to the desires of the house of Israel, and shall not defile his neighbor's wife, and shall not approach a menstruous woman, and shall not oppress a man, and shall return the pledge to the debtor, and shall not seize plunder, who shall give his bread to the hungry and cover the naked with a garment, and shall not give his money for usury, and shall not take overabundance, and shall turn away his hand from injustice, and shall do just judgment between a man and his neighbor, and has walked in my precepts and kept my ordinances to do them: this man is just; he shall surely live, says Adonai the Lord. But if he begets a pestilent son, shedding blood and committing sins, who has not walked in the way of his just father, but who has eaten upon the mountains, and has defiled his neighbor's wife, and has oppressed the beggar and the poor, and has seized plunder, and has not returned the pledge, and has set his eyes upon idols, and has done iniquity, has given with usury and taken overabundance: this man shall not live; he has done all these iniquities; he shall surely die; his blood shall be upon himself. But if he begets a son, and the son sees all the sins of his father which he did, and fears, and does not do according to these things—who has not eaten upon the mountains, and has not lifted up his eyes to the desires of the house of Israel, and has not defiled his neighbor's wife, and has not oppressed a man, and has not taken a pledge, and has not seized plunder, who has given his bread to the hungry and covered the naked with a garment, and has turned away his hand from iniquity, has not taken usury and overabundance, has done justice and walked in my precepts: this man shall not die in his father's iniquities; he shall surely live; but his father, because he afflicted with affliction and seized plunder, did contrary things in the midst of my people, and he died in his iniquity. And you said: Why is it that the son has not borne the iniquity of his father? Because the son has done justice and judgment and mercy, has kept all my statutes and done them, he shall surely live; but the soul that sins, it shall die. The son shall not bear the injustice of his father, nor shall the father bear the injustice of his son; the justice of the just shall be upon himself, and the iniquity of the iniquitous shall be upon himself. And the iniquitous, if he turns himself away from all his iniquities which he did, and keeps all my commandments and does judgment and justice and mercy, he shall surely live and shall not die; all his offenses, whatever he did, shall not be in remembrance; in his justice which he did he shall surely live. Do I will with will the death of the unjust, says Adonai the Lord, rather than that he turn himself away from his evil way and live? But when the just turns himself away from his justice and does iniquities according to all the iniquities which the iniquitous did, if he does them, he shall not live; all his justices which he did shall not be in remembrance; in his offense by which he fell away, and in his sins by which he sinned, in these he shall die. And you said: The way of the Lord is not right. Hear now, O house of Israel: is my way not right? Now it is your way that is not equitable. When the just turns himself away from his justice and does an offense, he shall die in that offense which he did; in it he shall die; and when the iniquitous turns himself away from his iniquity which he did, and does judgment and justice, this man has guarded his soul and lives, in that he turned himself away from all his iniquities which he did; he shall surely live and shall not die. And the house of Israel says: The way of the Lord is not right. Is my way not right, O house of Israel? Is it not rather your way that is not right? Therefore I will judge each one of you according to his ways, O house of Israel, says Adonai the Lord*[^41].

*Augustine.* This, through the prophet Ezekiel, is the promise of the New Testament, which you do not understand—where God distinguishes the regenerated from the begotten, if they are now of greater age, according to their proper deeds; for those of whom it is said, *The soul of the father is mine and the soul of the son is mine*, without doubt lead their own lives separately. But if the son were still in the loins of his father—as it is written of Levi, that he was in the loins of Abraham when Abraham was tithed by Melchizedek[^42]—it could not then be said, *The soul of the father is mine and the soul of the son is mine*, when there was surely one soul. But the Prophet, veiling a mystery to be revealed in its own time, did not name regeneration, through which each son of man passes from Adam to Christ; but what he did not say at that time, he wanted understood at this time, in which the veil was to be taken away from those passing over to Christ. For since you profess to be a Christian—although you show that you are an antichrist, striving to bring it about that Christ died in vain—I ask you whether, if a man does all those works of justice which the prophet Ezekiel recalls by so often repeating them, he shall surely live even if not regenerated. If you say that he shall surely live, Christ contradicts the antichrist and says: *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^43]; which food and drink, whether you will or not, you are forced to confess pertain to the regenerated. But if, crushed by so great a mass of authority, you answer that he who has done all those good things, if he has not been regenerated, shall not live, answer what the reason is, and see that regeneration is opposed to generation, not imitation to imitation, in the Apostle's setting forth Adam on the side of sin and Christ on the side of justice. But I will show you more evidently that it pertains to the New Testament, in which is the inheritance of the regenerated, what you recall the prophet Ezekiel to have said—not indeed now, but when you have said all that, in the manner of your garrulity, you are going to say about his very words.

**III. 39.** *Julian.* Does not God appear a wealthy assertor of his own judgments, who in so many headings not only judged but even disputed concerning this question? Plainly, foreseeing the errors of our times, he worked two things justly and providently by so great a light or abundance of his words: one, that no one should be moved by any ambiguity of questions; the other, that among those who had plunged themselves of their own accord into shipwrecks no plank of excuse should remain. He speaks to the Jews, who—suffering captivity as their crimes demanded—boasted, to reveal the odium of their own transgression, that it was owed to their parents, not to their own conduct, and he addresses them with paternal authority: *Why*, he says, *this parable to you, that you should say: The parents have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge? As I live, says Adonai the Lord, this parable shall no more be said in Israel; for all souls are mine, as the soul of the father, so the soul of the son; all souls are mine. The soul that sins, it shall die*[^44].

*Augustine.* When he says, *If this parable shall be said any more in Israel*, he shows that it was wont to be said: *The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the teeth of the children are set on edge*; nor does he convict the fact that it was said, but promises a place where it shall not be said. But whence did they say this, except because they knew that God had said, *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^45]?

**III. 40.** *Julian.* To exemplify and confirm the equity of his judgment, God uses an oath, and heaps the authority of his decree with an attestation. The Apostle understood this kind of speaking, who discoursed thus to the Hebrews: *God*, he says, *willing to show to the heirs of the promise the immutability of his counsel, interposed an oath, that by two immutable things, by which it is impossible for God to lie, we may have the strongest consolation*[^46].

*Augustine.* And there too the New Testament was being promised.

**III. 41.** *Julian.* By these two things, then, he says God is shown not to be able to lie: by this very fact, that he promised, and that what he promised he testifies that he fulfills. Not that God, without such attestation, is wont to have a feeble and doubtful discourse, but that he might load his truth with grand documents, he made use of that kind of expression which makes even men accustomed to deceive be believed. By this weight, therefore, in the present case too, God admonishes and prescribes, both that no one of his people should suspect this which the Traducianists assert, and that he may know that God cannot judge in the way that he detests: *As I live, says Adonai the Lord, this parable shall no more be said in Israel*[^47].

*Augustine.* *It shall not be said in Israel*—you would say rightly, if you saw the true Israelites to be the regenerated, in whom this shall not be said. For in those who are not regenerated it is deservedly said, since they are not Israel, according to what the Apostle says to the Romans: *For not all who are of Israel, these are Israel*[^48]—where without doubt he wanted the sons of the New Testament, that is, the sons of the promise, understood. Finally it follows: *But in Isaac shall your seed be called; that is, not they who are the sons of the flesh, these are the sons of God, but the sons of the promise are reckoned as the seed*[^49].

**III. 42.** *Julian.* What is this: *It shall no more be said*, when this is asserted even today by such great efforts of the Manicheans? But this is what he indicates: that no one who is of the people of Israel, or who accepts the authority of this Scripture, will, after my definition, dare to credit any such thing. Furthermore, everyone who persists in that opinion and does not obey these letters will not be numbered among him who is the true Israel.

*Augustine.* If, then, God wants it understood that after his definition no one will believe this, it must be asked why before this it was not improperly believed that the sins of fathers were to be repaid even upon children. And if it be well asked, it will be found that on account of liable generation it was said: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^50], whence that proverb about sour grapes arose. But on account of free regeneration the New Testament was promised, where this shall no more be said, because the ruinous inheritance which comes from Adam is renounced through the grace of Christ, when this age is renounced—where it is necessary that the sons of Adam be weighed down by a heavy yoke, surely not unjustly, *from the day of their coming forth from their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all*[^51]; whence the sacred mysteries, with even the little ones renouncing, sufficiently show what is being done.

**III. 43.** *Julian.* After he had reproved the vanity of that parable, he sanctioned the sentence by divine promulgation and attestation, and even deigns to open the reason of his justice, why kinships are not burdened by mutual sins. *For all souls*, he says, *are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son; all souls are mine; the soul that sins, it shall die*[^52]. Why, then, this moderation of the sentence should be most equitable, he shows also by the propriety of souls.

*Augustine.* This propriety of souls pertains to separate lives; for no one can be reborn unless he is born. But why was Levi tithed when he was in the loins of Abraham, except because the propriety of their own souls was not yet in them?

**III. 44.** *Julian.* Since, he says, the soul of the father is mine and the soul of the son is mine—by which testimony, as by many others, it is taught that the soul, which God vindicates to his own right, owes nothing to the seeds—it is, he says, most iniquitous and stupid that my thing, my image, should be burdened with another's works.

*Augustine.* You separate, then, the flesh from the right of God, whom you think to vindicate the soul alone to his own right, and you have forgotten what is written: *For as the woman is from the man, so also the man is through the woman, but all things are from God*[^53]. Which surely was said either according to the flesh or according to both, yet not according to the soul alone. But as to what pleases you, that God should say, It is most iniquitous and stupid that my thing, my image, should be burdened with another's works—why do you not ask whence it is just that the soul should be burdened with the flesh drawn from the parents, and with the works of God himself? *For the corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^54]. And I think you acknowledge that even the corruptible body is the work of God. What, then, did the image of God merit, that it should be weighed down by a corruptible body, to the hindrance of the things to be known, if there is no original sin? But why do you not also make God say this: It is most iniquitous and stupid that my thing, my image, through the unbelief or negligence of parents, or of any among whom it lives, or through any necessity, should go out of the body without baptism and not be admitted to my kingdom nor live the life, because it has not eaten the holy flesh nor drunk the blood[^55]? Or will you dispute even against this sentence of Christ, and cry out, saying: Plainly, even if he has not eaten the flesh of Christ nor drunk his blood, he shall surely live? O voice of none but the antichrist! Go, say these things, teach these things; let Christian men and women hear you, let men of corrupted mind, reprobate concerning the faith, hear you; let them hear you, love you, honor you, feed, clothe, adorn you, and by following you, lost, lose themselves too. But *the Lord knows who are his*[^56], nor must we despair even of you, so long as his patience is bestowed upon you.

**III. 45.** *Julian.* To whom I gave this condition, that no external one should harm him against his will, but that he should of his own accord take hold either of sin or of justice, either of reward or of guilt.

*Augustine.* This can be said of the first nature of man, not of this corrupted and condemned nature. For neither in paradise, before sin, did the corruptible body weigh down the soul; or are you indeed so corrupted in mind that you dare to say even this? But if you do not dare, say why the image of God merited to be weighed down by a corruptible body, you who are unwilling to confess, with the catholic Church, the origin of sin.

**III. 46.** *Julian.* You are indignant with me, then, why I believe God swearing rather than Mani dreaming—who especially brings forth no testimonies of sound faith or arguments of a watchful talent, which, although they can never be found so great as to avail to overturn the foundations of truth, would nevertheless at present bring some consolation against the shame of stupidity, if they were aptly said. God persists in fortifying by swearing what he had made sacred by commanding. He proceeds to make plain even by examples what he sanctions, and says that if a man should be one keeping all the species of justice with unpolluted devotion, and should here beget a son who cleaves to pestilent morals and turns aside from his father's paths, the nobility acquired by the father's so great care of justice should profit him nothing; and again, on the opposite side, he sets the son of this sinner fleeing his father's ways by a better counsel, and shows that the iniquity of the parent harms him nothing. And he makes the condition of justice and of sin equal, asserting that the vices of those who beget so do not go through the seeds, just as the virtues cannot go, but that all souls pertain to his own right—through which that too is shown profane which you say, that both the souls and the bodies of the newborn are possessed by the right of the devil.

*Augustine.* It has already been answered to you: you are emptily wordy. The whole man—that is, both soul and body—pertains by his substance to the right of the Creator; but through defect, which is no substance, he has been made over to the devil—yet under the same power of the Creator, under which the devil himself too is set.

**III. 47.** *Julian.* Having asserted the balance of his examination, he then accuses your opinion in those who thought like things. *And you said: Why is it that the son has not borne the iniquity of his father? Because the soul*, he says, *that sins, it shall die; but the son does not bear the injustice of his father, nor does the father bear the injustice of his son; the justice of the just shall be upon himself, and the iniquity of the iniquitous shall be upon himself*[^57]. Which of us could ever have disputed on this point with as great diligence as God discoursed through the mouth of his Prophet, by dividing, comparing, and repeating?

*Augustine.* And yet, when God has done this, you mix your garrulity with so great a clarity of his words, knowing your case to be not good.

**III. 48.** *Julian.* But not content to have explained this alone, he applies also another argument, from the works of mercy, for the confirmation of this justice, and declares that even those very persons who sinned of their own accord, if they take refuge in penance and amendment, are not harmed by their past errors. *The iniquitous*, he says, *if he turns himself away from his iniquities which he did, and keeps my commandments, all his offenses, whatever he did, shall not be in remembrance; in his justice which he did he shall surely live*[^58]. That is: Since I am in this purpose of clemency, that I forgive even their proper sins to those who are corrected, how can it be that I impute another's to those who are born? Or do circumstances allow that innocence should be guilty before me when it is created, before whom it is effective even if it is taken up again after being interrupted?

*Augustine.* The case of those who repent is one thing, of those who are born another. For you utterly fail to find how you may show God just, if he both finds no sins in the newborn and yet weighs them down with a corruptible body and, furthermore, with so many and so great calamities. For the evils which infants suffer cannot be numbered: fever, cough, scab, pains of various members, flux of the bowels, worms, and other innumerable things arising from the flesh itself, and the torments of the very treatments, more numerous than those of the diseases, and from without the blows of wounds, the strokes of beatings, the assaults of demons. But you wise heretics, lest you confess original sin, are prepared to fill paradise with such flowers. For if you say these things would not have been there, I ask why they are in the little ones who have, as you contend, no sin at all. But if you are not ashamed to say that these things too would have been there, what kind of Christians you are, what need is there for us to say?

**III. 49.** *Julian.* This displeased the worshipers of idols; our faith too, which you see formed by this law, displeases you. The profane, then, said: *The way of the Lord is not right. Hear, then*, he says, *O house of Israel. Is my way not right? Is it not your way that is not equitable? Therefore I will judge each one of you according to your way, says Adonai the Lord*[^59]. Do you perceive by what testimonies we are fortified? Do we pursue anything ambiguous? Do we hunt for general words? Do we maintain our faith by weak or involved arguments? We execrate what God execrates, we understand what God expounds, we discourse what God disputed, we believe that on account of which God swore: *The son shall not bear the iniquity of his father, nor shall the father bear the injustice of his son; the justice of the just shall be upon himself, and the iniquity of the iniquitous shall be upon himself*[^60]. How God promises he will judge shines forth—that is, neither to impute the sins of parents to children, nor of children to parents; and on this account it is established, even by the testimonies of the Scriptures (concerning which reason did not allow doubt), that God holds in his judgments the same justice which he held in his precepts.

*Augustine.* From this at least understand that God spoke these things of those fathers and children who already lived separately, because, when he had said, *The son shall not bear the iniquity of his father, nor shall the father bear the injustice of his son*, he at once added: *The justice of the just shall be upon himself*. For can it be said of a little one in this age that his justice is upon him, since by his own life he cannot yet live either justly or iniquitously? Whatever penalties, then, he suffers in this age, by what merit does he suffer them, if he drew none from his parents? Since the most just God neither inflicts undeserved penalties on anyone nor allows them to be inflicted, nor can it be said that the little one suffers these evils for the exercise of a virtue which as yet is none in him. Furthermore, if you consider the age to come, which pertains to the inheritance of the New Testament, even of the little ones who die in that very age it is most correctly said: *The justice of the just shall be upon himself, and the iniquity of the iniquitous shall be upon himself*. For thus shall the begotten be distinguished from the regenerated, so that the latter shall surely live in that kingdom in which justice dwells, but the former shall surely die in that punishment where iniquity is tormented. But what is the justice of the latter, except that which passes into him from Christ, in whom all will be made alive? And what is the iniquity of the former, except that which passes into him from Adam, in whom all die?

**III. 50.** *Julian.* But lest perhaps you try to mock the ears of the simpler by such a cavil, so as to say that God here spoke of those who are of mature age—that is, that he said the crimes of parents do not harm those children who had emptied the natural evil by the holiness of their own works—it must be shown that this fraud accomplishes nothing. For although God prescribed absolutely that the iniquity of parents does not condemn innocent children, but each one is convicted by his own iniquities, and a faithful mind cannot doubt of this, nevertheless, that we may handle again the corpse of your opinion, pierced by so many thunderbolts of the law: in what sense will you think this disputation of God must be received—namely, that the offenses of parents harm the newborn children no more than they harm the adult? Or that they are only not allowed to harm those of greater age and working justice, but that the little ones, before they exhaust the natural venom by their own justice, are burdened by the crime of those who beget—and so that comes about which God denied through the Prophet would be?

*Augustine.* Rather, that comes about which God says in another place: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^61]. For since he himself says both—that is, both that the sins of fathers do not pertain to children, and that he repays the sins of fathers upon children—he cannot surely be contrary to himself; and therefore, to those who rightly ask how both are true, one of these is understood to fit generation, the other regeneration. You, who pervert your own heart, beware of him, and what you hear to be true, you will not call a cavil.

**III. 51.** *Julian.* But to this we refer. Profess, then, that that other thing also which is weighed in equal balance comes about—that is, that the justices of parents profit their children and sanctify them, and that they are only said not to profit those who, of greater age, have worn away the justices of their birth by their own crimes; so that, since both were denied by God, and the one discoursed and impressed by the example of the other, you—seeing that denial to be contrary to your reasonings—contend that it lied in the case of the little ones, and refute both its parts by the condition of the newborn. And when you have done this, you will perpetrate two evils alike: one, that it opposes the sentence of God; the other, that you assert a just man is born of a just man and a criminal of a criminal.

*Augustine.* I do not say that the Prophet's denial lied, but that it was not understood by you. For he prophesied regeneration, which alone makes the children foreign to the sins of parents that pertain to the judgment of God, not of men; but you, while you deny that generation draws the contagion of the ancient death from the fathers, try to extinguish the very cause of regeneration. For although the washing of regeneration washes away whatever sins it finds, nevertheless other sins could be healed even by repenting, as they can in those whom it is not lawful to regenerate again; but that which is contracted by generation is not loosed except by regeneration. The just man, then, is born of God, not of man, since by being reborn, not by being born, he becomes just; whence too such are called sons of God. Read the Gospel: *Not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God they were born*[^62]. Why do you seek to join kinds of things very diverse? Men are born of the flesh of men, are reborn of the Spirit of God. What wonder, then, if, just as a man takes justice from the Spirit of justice, so he draws original sin from the flesh of sin? For one man would not have come to free us in the likeness of the flesh of sin[^63], unless the flesh of us all were the flesh of sin. Since, then, your heresy is hostile to this Christian grace, do you still dare to wonder and complain that the Church of Christ detests you?

**III. 52.** *Julian.* But if, not bearing the din of the truth crying out against you, you try to escape elsewhere, you will fall into stronger snares. For if you say: The sentence of the Prophet is indeed true, but in the case of older persons, where he says that kinships are not burdened by mutual faults, yet in the case of the little ones it lies in one part of itself, where it says they are not guilty for the crimes of those who beget, but does not lie in the other, where it asserts that they are lifted up by none of the virtues of their parents—then you will plainly make public a most foul lust not of disputing but of feigning, not of speaking eloquently but of mere talking, not, finally, of a sound man, but of a Manichean falling away in his senses, if you think it permitted you, against perspicuous reason, against the honor of God, against the examples, against the testimonies of the precepts, against the exposition of the judgments, to reject what you please and assume what delights you.

*Augustine.* The sentence of the Prophet lies in no part. But you, not understanding what is prophesied through it—not to say you lie—certainly (which I may say without injury to you) do not know what you are saying. For even this, which you say the Prophet asserts, that *the children are lifted up by none of the virtues of the parents*, makes very much difference how and in what sense it is taken. For do you deny that children are offered to be regenerated to the Church their mother, and to be baptized by the ministers of God, by the faith of their parents? How, then, are the children lifted up by none of the virtues of their parents? Or will you dare to say that the Christian faith is no virtue? Or are they not lifted up, when they are sent into the kingdom of God in no other way than by that same regeneration? In temporal benefits too, why is it said to Isaac: *I will do it for you on account of Abraham your father*[^64]? Why is Lot too, the son of Abraham's brother, helped by the merits of his uncle, if children are lifted up by none of the virtues of their parents[^65]? Why, finally, both for the sins of Solomon is the kingdom diminished for his son, and for the good merits of David is not wholly taken away[^66], if children are burdened by no sins of parents or lifted up by no virtues? You glib man, not wise of heart, distinguish these things; here see, if you can, what was prophesied through Ezekiel. For it is plain both that an unregenerate father is no hindrance to a regenerate son for taking hold of the eternal life of which it is said, *he shall surely live*, and that a regenerate father is no help to this very thing for an unregenerate son, and in turn that a son is neither a help, as regenerate to an unregenerate father, nor a hindrance, as unregenerate to a regenerate father, so that the one *shall surely live* or the other *shall surely die*. But if you cannot see these things, why can you not be silent either?

**III. 53.** *Julian.* Who are you, who, goaded by the madness of Marcion, burst out to the destruction of equity? Who drag both the judgments and the precepts of God, while still slippery with the mysteries of the Manicheans, under the censure of your tongue? No one ever attempted to do this except one who first denied them. Will you dare, with Mani as your author, to break through the testament of God, which reason, equity, piety, truth—with the Prophets writing in faith, as a kind of order of sacred witnesses—sealed? You lost long ago both the holiness of understanding and the appearance of religion, if you think that by this sentence which is in Ezekiel either help is found for your transmission, or that destruction is not held.

*Augustine.* Garrulity, copious in poverty, ranges about where truth is not discerned, or even, if discerned, is spurned—where there is an abundance not most empty of words, but most certain of things. It is one thing to be straight and truth-telling, another to be eloquent and slanderous. The Prophet says true things to children and fathers leading their own lives separately; you, under the name of the Manicheans, slander with Pelagian rage the catholics who rightly understand the Prophet.

**III. 54.** *Julian.* *The iniquity of the iniquitous*, he says, *upon his own head; I will judge each one of you according to his ways*[^67]. *The fathers shall not die for the children, nor the children for the parents; the soul*, he says, *that sins, it shall die; for all souls are mine; I will judge each one for his own iniquities*[^68].

*Augustine.* God, who says these things, is the same God who says: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^69]. Unless you understand how both are true, by no means believe that you have understood the truth-telling Prophet, however much I bear with you as slanderous in your garrulity.

**III. 55.** *Julian.* Behold to what light, to what people of sentences, to what dignity of judgments your transmission both does not fear to do insult and tries to bring prejudice. Here there is no doubt that you are not ashamed to say what seems to remain—that is, that you answer that the sentence of the Prophet, nay of God through the Prophet, is true, the insuperable reason of the prudent, by which it is taught most iniquitous that the sins of parents be imputed to children.

*Augustine.* Blush; it is you who are most iniquitous; for he who said: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^70], is not iniquitous.

**III. 56.** *Julian.* Nor that you think this—that the seeds of all human beings are polluted by the guilt of the will, but yet that this justice does not hold in the crime of Adam alone, that his one sin is what is imputed to all. To which I do not know what I ought first to do: should I laugh, as singular absurdities demand? But the perdition of the many whom you deceive awaits compassion and tears. Should I, then, be consumed with serious grief? But the prodigies of your arguments strike out laughter, though from a mournful mind.

*Augustine.* Say what you are going to say, excellent corrector. On whose authority is Ambrose to be believed a deceiver—Ambrose, of whom Pelagius, your teacher, says that not even an enemy dared to reprove his faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures? Reprove him; for you are a stronger and more powerful enemy of the cross of Christ than if you were an enemy of Ambrose; you more audaciously begrudge the grace of God than if you begrudged that man of God. Reprove him, I say, mock him by attacking, lament him by grieving; for you can, by the great force of your eloquence, lead your vanity into urbanity and transform your madness into mercy. Say, then, that that man erred deformedly, that he was unhappily out of his senses in affirming that the discord of the concupiscence of the flesh and the spirit turned into nature from the transgression of the first man[^71]. For who is born without it, from the time men began to be born—being surely born in the flesh of sin? But you, most acute man, not believing that that sin was so great and such that it could then change nature itself into this evil and, by the merit of an ineffable apostasy, make it to be condemned along with its stock, assert—drunk with the Pelagian teaching—that this, which all the sober feel to be an evil and judge to be an evil, is a good. For by a wondrous eloquence you can adorn with praises the lust which the saints accuse with groans, and place such a lust as it even now is—namely, forcing itself on those unwilling and provoking to combat the hearts of the chaste—among the groves of paradise, as a beautiful and pleasant tree, even if no one had sinned there. Blush, you blessedness of the Pelagians, and see where you may flee, for the chaste flee from you.

**III. 57.** *Julian.* For who, if any age survive into this world, will be able to believe by the monuments of letters that there existed a man who believed and swore that what was natural was not natural, that what was of generation was not of generation, that what pertained to the condition of parents did not pertain to some for any other reason than because they had had parents? I think the following generation will reckon these things rather as feigned than as defended by any mortals. For these swimmings and these nauseas and these vomitings your nation and faith suffer, who say: The sins of parents cannot run down to children through nature, because a thing of choice is not bound to the seeds; but the sin of Adam, which was conceived of the will, passes into all human beings through nature, because a thing of choice is bound to the seeds; God does not condemn children too for the sins of parents, because it is of the highest iniquity, but he condemns the posterity of Adam on account of the sin of the parent, which can be avenged by no justice. Finally, it does not pertain to the condition of generation, because those who are spouses become parents—which pertained to Adam for no other reason than because he begot by the law of marriage. Is this to govern one's course, or to flounder? Is this to digest, or to be nauseated? Is this to take in something solid, or to keep vomiting? By the same words you affirm what you denied, by the same lines you deny what you affirmed, and you are angry that we do not acquiesce in a man who, ruptured by a grave disease, cannot hold down what he takes in.

*Augustine.* Far be it from us to say that God does not repay the sins of other fathers upon children, since divine Scripture so often and by name testifies for which sins of which fathers vengeance went forth upon which of their children—to the point that God, sparing King Ahab, deferred avenging a certain grave sin of his upon his son[^72]. But who can investigate what measure and what reason and limit there is of divine justice concerning the sins of other fathers, that they be repaid upon children? On account of which God reserved these judgments to himself, but forbade the man who judges so to avenge. But the apostasy of the first man—in whom the liberty of his own will was supreme and impeded by no defect—was so great a sin that by his ruin the whole of human nature collapsed, which the so great misery of the human race indicates—a misery which, from the first wailings of those crying to the last breaths of those expiring, is known to all, so that those who deny it claim for themselves the greater part of human misery by so horrible and incredible a blindness, as you do, who do not fear to say still that Adam was so made mortal that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he would die—even after the council of Palestine, where Pelagius himself condemned you who say these things[^73]. But go on, as it pleases you, and accuse even those so many Palestinian bishops too of being Manicheans; cry out that Pelagius yielded to the Manicheans for the hour, lest he be condemned by them. Fill paradise with the fruits of lusts, and spread through those happy meadows so many and so great evils, which we see infants suffer, as if they were not penal pains but vernal scents. And mock me as swimming, while you yourself, plunged into these depths, perish; mock me as nauseating and vomiting, while you yourself lie dead and, as if with a worm-breeding rottenness, so rot with restless garrulity; charge me as affirming what I denied and denying what I affirmed, when it is rather you who have done this, as I showed in your earlier volume, and those who read can notice that I have not done this, and find how falsely you said I had done it; say that I, ruptured by a grave disease, cannot hold down what I take in, when you, having utterly lost the spirit of life, cannot even take in what is brought to you.

**III. 58.** *Julian.* You yourself indeed see that the matter is not worthy of conflict; nevertheless, that the incautious minds may be helped, which take up, as a consolation of a wounded conscience, the grosser opinions and those flattering to evil morals, we ask whether you have found the invention of so great stupidity from any occasion of the Scriptures. If you say: *Through one man sin entered into the world*[^74], we admonish you to reread the earlier book of the present work, which, if you remember it, you will not be able to usurp the testimony of the Apostle.

*Augustine.* We rather admonish you to read the Apostle himself and to see that those very few words of his, of which we treat, are so manifest against you that not in a little book but plainly in a most long and most garrulous book you have labored in vain not to turn but to pervert them into your heretical sense, not to expound but to obscure them.

**III. 59.** *Julian.* But if you say that there is one form of baptism by which men of different age are consecrated, you will confess, even with me silent, that there is contained there no mention either of an infected generation or of a diabolical flesh or of any Adam; to which, however, in its own place, although it does not look to transmission, we shall fully answer.

*Augustine.* When you begin to answer, what will you there appear except, as is your wont, a heretic?

**III. 60.** *Julian.* Produce, then, against so great a dignity of the witnesses brought forward by us, against manifest justice, against the perspicuous reason of the law, at least one sentence by which you may show that you have been deceived; which I have added for this reason, because, even if there were one that might be thought to show some such thing by the ambiguity of words, it would nevertheless be compelled, by the splendor and mastery of the other sentences, to be expounded as accorded with justice.

*Augustine.* Certain, not ambiguous, divine testimonies are brought forward against you, as many have already been brought forward by us; but because they convict your darkness, they do not seem clear to you, and against whatever things are troublesome to you by their radiant light, you close your heart, lest from there the night of error be put to flight.

**III. 61.** *Julian.* But now, since not even a slight occasion for this opinion is found in the holy Scriptures, and, on the contrary, the faith we maintain is fortified no less by its reasonings than by examples and testimonies, you are utterly possessed by a deadly obstinacy, you who think that, with loss of the law, loss of reason, loss of prudence, and loss of justice, the dreams of the Manicheans must be agreed to.

*Augustine.* With loss of the law, you do not hear: *I will repay upon children the sins of fathers*; with loss of reason, you do not see that the evils which the little ones suffer—who made no proper sins in this life—have just causes before God only from the sins of origin; with loss of prudence, you do not beware of bringing forth or defending, against the most ancient foundation of the catholic faith, a novel teaching by which original sin is denied; with loss of justice, you are so iniquitous that, under my name, you do not fear to fling the contagion of the Manichean plague at so many holy men—sons and fathers, disciples and doctors of the Church of Christ, who were before us—and at the very mother of all, the catholic Church.

**III. 62.** *Julian.* Attend, however, to what we bring forward to this sense. Even if you could prove that Adam's sin is imputed to children, you would nevertheless assent that the crimes of other parents do not harm their offspring.

*Augustine.* Who would assent to this error, except one who does not believe God saying: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*?

**III. 63.** *Julian.* It was manifest that it was not of generation, but of some other cause whatever, that the children seemed liable—before an iniquitous judge, however—to the sin of the one who begot.

*Augustine.* You say most openly that God is iniquitous, who most openly says that he will repay the sins of fathers upon children.

**III. 64.** *Julian.* And it came about that, as the judgment of the judge was stained by the innocent being condemned, so the state of generation was vindicated by the examples of other parents. For if the cause of the path, if the cause of the crime had stood in the work of fruitfulness, this condition would pollute all fruitfulness.

*Augustine.* *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^75], God cries out; therefore, because he is truthful, you stray from the path of truth.

**III. 65.** *Julian.* But since, though the generation was not diverse, the state nevertheless appeared diverse, it shone forth plainly, even in that marriage whose guilt its offspring had shared, that generation had not been corrupted.

*Augustine.* Even if the generation is not diverse—since those who will die are born of those who will die—nevertheless what the Apostle says, *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^76], pertains not to other fathers, but to that one who sinned with so great an impiety as we cannot measure and estimate. But how great God judged it, we have learned by witnesses sufficiently fit—namely, both divine Scripture and the very misery of the human race—and we discern how great a misery was repaid for his sin to his offspring by a judgment of God surely not unjust, we who say—because we are Christians—that not, I say, the eternal death of both soul and body, but not even the temporal death of the body, nor so many and so great evils which we see the little ones suffer, would have existed even in paradise if no one had sinned. But other fathers, although they sin much, nevertheless, because they sin with a soul that is weak and in a corruptible body which weighs down the soul, nature is not thereby made to die by their sins, and their sins are repaid upon their children by a punishment far diverse and far less, by the hidden and just judgment of him who arranges all things in measure and number and weight[^77], and who does not say falsely: *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*.

**III. 66.** *Julian.* What, then, was accomplished? Namely, that it is so certain that there is no transmission of sin that, even if you taught that children are punished on account of Adam's sin, it would nevertheless be established that sins cannot be inborn nor crime go with the seed, since you would confess that this is not in all fruitfulness; and on this account, even if the vices were handed on to the persons, they would nevertheless not be mingled with the conceptions. But now, since it is agreed between me and you that the sin of parents could not pass to children except by a defect and guilt of generation, while it accorded with reason, with examples, and with the law that the state of generation could by no means be corrupted—which is confirmed even by your own concession, you who say that the sins of no parents except two pass over to their offspring—it has been most invincibly accomplished that neither was the fruitfulness of the first human beings corrupted by the devil, nor can any sin be born.

*Augustine.* This is not said to you; you yourself say to yourself what we do not say. The sins of other fathers too are repaid upon children by divine justice, not human; for God knows when and how he most justly does this, but man does not know, and according to his own knowledge he ought to bring judgment. For he can know, when he judges—though not even this always—the deed of each person; but whence does he know with what and what kind of bonds the nature from which it arose is connected to the nature? But that one thing by which human nature was changed into the necessity of death—even if there are no others—suffices for condemnation, unless the bond of generation be loosed by regeneration; this we say, even if you are unwilling to hear; this you do not overcome by truth, even if you persist in attacking it by garrulity.

**III. 67.** *Julian.* It is asked of us, then, why we do not assent that sin is natural. We answer: Because it has no color of verisimilitude, much less of truth, none of justice, none of piety, because it makes the devil seem the founder of human beings.

*Augustine.* It plainly makes him seem so—but to you, not to those who know how to distinguish defect from nature, although the defect is in the nature. Read the Epistle to the Hebrews and see that solid food is theirs who have their senses exercised to separate good from evil[^78], which you do not have. Therefore, when we say: Man is born with a defect, you think the devil is being called his founder—being so blind or contentious that you can neither attend to, nor are willing to attend to, even the defects of bodies with which some are born; the merits of which defects, if they be sought of you, you find nowhere to take refuge except a precipice, so long as you are unwilling to return to the solid rock of the catholic faith.

**III. 68.** *Julian.* Because it fastens the crime of iniquity upon God the judge.

*Augustine.* It is you who do this, because the heavy yoke upon the little ones is iniquitous, if there is no original sin.

**III. 69.** *Julian.* Because it breaks and destroys free choice, by which safeguard especially the Church of Christ is fortified against the various errors.

*Augustine.* It is you who oppress free choice, to whom—for restoring or helping it—you deny the grace of God.

**III. 70.** *Julian.* Saying that all human beings are so incapable of any virtue that in their very mothers' wombs they are filled with ancient crimes.

*Augustine.* How would we say that men are justified by grace—that is, made just—if we denied that men are capable of virtue?

**III. 71.** *Julian.* Yet you contrive the force of these crimes to be not only the expeller of natural innocence, but also thereafter, through the whole of life, the compeller into all vices.

*Augustine.* By the grace of God our necessity is diminished, by the grace of God it is ended; here your garrulity has nothing to do.

**III. 72.** *Julian.* Which law of sin you swear remained and flourished even in the Prophets and Apostles, who were already conspicuous by the great splendor of their morals and signs, and even after that grace of the mysteries of Christ, which you think aids all these disgraces of your teaching.

*Augustine.* The Prophets and Apostles faithfully fought down that received lust of yours—that is, the concupiscence of the flesh fighting against the concupiscence of the spirit—because they did not praise it.

**III. 73.** *Julian.* Since it extinguishes the effort of every kind of honor, since it consoles and increases with infamy the obscenity of the morals of the works of God—that is, of human nature—since it condemns all the commandments of the law to the guilt of impossibility, that is, of iniquity.

*Augustine.* He who says, *Not what I will do I do, but what I hate, that I do*[^79], places the possibility of his own perfection in the grace of God against you; he does not trust in his own power, lest he be empty as you are.

**III. 74.** *Julian.* Since it has no less of foulness than of profanity, when, for the highest testimonies of its assertion, it embraces the shame of the genitals.

*Augustine.* We do not embrace the shame of the genitals, but rather acknowledge whence this shame comes—which you are unwilling to do. For we hear the most manifest testimony of Scripture, against which you grow deaf when you ought to grow dumb. For who, hearing that those first human beings were naked and were not ashamed, does not see whence it came about that after sin they were ashamed to be naked, and hastened to veil with girdles of whatever kind those members of which they were ashamed[^80]? But a man has been found who would set even such a lust—which must be resisted lest one sin, and of which even the shameless are ashamed—in paradise, even if no one had sinned there, and is not ashamed to say these things of so great and so sacrilegious a deformity. I ask you, if this lust, as we believe, brings you only the trouble of resisting, but exhibits no good will, why do you think so great a wage of praise must be paid to it, false though it be?

**III. 75.** *Julian.* And, in place of the sacred authorities, it swells up with accusations of God.

*Augustine.* Is it not, then, a sacred authority: *The body is dead because of sin*? And is it an accusation of God, not a praise: *He will give life also to your mortal bodies*[^81]? I think that not an accuser, but one full of God himself, a preacher of God, said these things, where he shows that Adam was not so made, as you say, that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he would die.

**III. 76.** *Julian.* In his precepts a tyrannical immoderation.

*Augustine.* The precept of God is not tyrannical, but, that it may be fulfilled, he himself must be asked; this you are unwilling to do, because you trust in your own power.

**III. 77.** *Julian.* In his judgments a barbarous iniquity.

*Augustine.* Since the judgment of God is not iniquitous, therefore in the misery of the human race, which begins from the weeping of the little ones, original sin must be acknowledged.

**III. 78.** *Julian.* In his oaths it affirms that there is a Punic falsity.

*Augustine.* When God says, *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^82], does he speak in Punic?

**III. 79.** *Julian.* Since, in place of disputations and all syllogisms, it leans on the dreams and fury of Mani.

*Augustine.* He was neither a Manichean nor dreaming nor raving—whether he who said, *We too were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest*[^83], or he who said, *We are all born under sin, whose very origin is in fault*[^84].

**III. 80.** *Julian.* These, then, are the causes which kindle us to the assault on natural evil, which make us disdain and despise the fellowships of the lost.

*Augustine.* The pious consensus of so many and so great catholic Doctors you would in no way, except as one lost, call a fellowship of the lost.

**III. 81.** *Julian.* Which causes grant that we are no more terrified by the crash of the whole world than if we saw a wood of bitter lupine resounding, stirred either by unclean swine or by rushing winds.

*Augustine.* The catholic Church, spread through the whole world, which prudently shuddered at your mouths, is not a wood of lupine, but willed to be safe from lupine bites.

**III. 82.** *Julian.* Therefore we believe God to be both just and pious and truthful, and on this account we hold that his law commanded nothing impossible, that his testimonies affirm nothing false, that his judgments pronounce nothing iniquitous, but that he himself is the founder of human beings, whom he creates liable to no crime, but full indeed of natural innocence, and capable of voluntary virtues.

*Augustine.* Whence, then, does he not admit his own image, unbreathed-upon, unexorcized, unbaptized, to his own life? Is this how the wage of innocence is repaid? Or rather, having drawn guilt by generation and not having it remitted by regeneration, is it justly, with life denied, punished by death following? For the Apostle would not have detested those estranged from the life of God[^85], if there were no penalty.

**III. 83.** *Julian.* From which gathered points it is necessary that one of two be held: namely, that either God be believed such as the Traducianist Manichean feigns, or that you, with all who concede to the decrees of transmission, be understood such as God, honored, battles against. But God cannot be such as Mani dreams, but he is the pious and just and true God, such as all holy Scripture, all law, and our faith venerates. And on this account, this is your teaching, which, as it is taken up with injury to God, so it is destroyed with honor to him.

*Augustine.* If you hold God to be pious, why do you impiously prohibit the Savior—that is, Jesus—from the little ones? If just, why do you believe in a heavy yoke upon the little ones without the merits of any sin? If true, why do you not believe him who says: *I will repay upon children the sins of fathers*[^86]?

**III. 84.** *Julian.* It is time for us to pass on to other things; but the dignity of the case demands of us that we render even those things which we think were omitted in the earlier book. Which indeed a prudent reader will perhaps judge superfluous; yet, since it has been found that a case laid down to the perils of affairs clings even to slight supports, it is worth the effort to take away the consolations of one from whom you have removed the safeguards.

*Augustine.* Since you have begun to pass on to other things, we now ought to show what we promised above—namely, that the prophecy of Ezekiel, by which it is said that the sins of fathers are not repaid upon children, as the sins of children are not repaid upon fathers, pertains to the foretelling of the revelation of the New Testament. For the prophet Jeremiah too says something of the kind, and there he makes clear whence this is said. For among other things: *Turn back*, he says, *O virgin Israel, return to your cities, mourning; how long will you turn away, O dishonored daughter, since the Lord has created salvation for a new planting, in which salvation men shall go about? Thus said the Lord: They shall yet say this word in the land of Judah and in its cities, when I shall recall its captivity: Blessed be the Lord upon his just holy mountain, and those who dwell in Judea and in every city of it, together with the husbandman, and he shall be lifted up in the flock, because I have made drunk every thirsty soul and have satisfied every hungry soul. On account of this I rose up and saw, and my sleep was made sweet to me. Therefore behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will sow Israel and Judah with the seed of man and the seed of beast. And it shall be: as I watched over them to pull down and to afflict, so will I watch over them to build and to plant, says the Lord. In those days they shall not say: The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the children's teeth are set on edge; but each one shall die in his own sin, and the teeth of him who eats the sour grape shall be set on edge*[^87]. Surely it is manifest that this pertains to the days of the new planting, of which he was speaking that he might say this. Where both that seed of man and seed of beast, which God promised he would sow, understood spiritually, make those who preside and those who are ruled to be recognized. But because it was ingrained in the hearts of the people that it is written in the Old Testament, *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*[^88], lest anyone reckon the Scripture of God to be contrary to itself, that he might show more openly that that fits the Old, this the New Testament, he at once subjoined: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a New Testament, not according to the Testament which I disposed for their fathers in the day in which I took their hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt*[^89], and the rest. To that Testament, then, generation pertains, but to this regeneration; whence there the sins of fathers are repaid upon children, but here, with the bond of generation loosed through regeneration, it is not said: *The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the children's teeth are set on edge*, but: *the teeth of him who eats the sour grape shall be set on edge*, because each one shall die not in his father's but in his own sin, if he commits it. But you, where the prophecy says, *The son shall not bear the sin of his father*, did not show how the Scripture agrees, where it is said, *I will repay the sins of fathers upon children*. For these will remain contrary to each other, unless each of these two be referred to its own Testament, which the prophet Jeremiah most evidently showed.

**III. 85.** *Julian.* Therefore, when we had come to the sentence of the apostle Paul which the Traducianist was wont to peddle to those inexperienced in the Scriptures—that is, *Through one man sin entered into the world*[^90]—I first showed, by the testimony of his own discourse, that the Traducianists had been removed from the inner sanctuaries of that passage, since the Teacher of the Gentiles, recalling the antiquity of sin, had set a fixed number, as the strongest bodyguard, for the keeping of generation, so that, saying that through one man sin entered into the world, he prescribed that he thought nothing of generation, which could not exist except through two. And I assigned that a distinction was made between the intercourse and the sin of parents, since it was said that sin indeed entered into the world, but through that number which could not fit offspring. And it was dealt with sufficiently throughout the whole book that there not the nature of sin was indicated, but the pattern, since it was established to have been taken up by the transgressors who followed afterward by imitation, not by generation. But because we read it written in the Epistle to the Hebrews concerning the Jews: *They arose from one, and that one as good as dead*[^91]—that is, from Abraham—and higher up in the same epistle it is read concerning Christ: *He who sanctifies, and they who are sanctified, are all of one*[^92]—lest the Traducianist seize any of these or like things, if it can be found, and argue that something of the weight of our reply was shaken, by which we affirmed that 'one' was said, through whom sin passed, for this reason, lest generation be allowed to be understood, since in this epistle generation is found indicated by the name of 'one'—therefore, I say, I thought this must be handled again. That the reader, then, may be attentive, I give warning; for this objection will be destroyed by a manifold kind. There, then, where mention of Abraham is made, his wife Sarah is also inserted; for such is the order of the words: *By faith he who is called Abraham obeyed to go out into a place which he was to receive as an inheritance, and he went out not knowing where he was going. By faith he sojourned in the land of promise as in a foreign land, dwelling in little huts with Isaac and Jacob, coheirs of the same promise; for he awaited the city having foundations, whose maker and founder is God. By faith Sarah too, being barren, received power for the conception of seed even beyond the time of her age, since she believed him faithful who had promised. On account of which from one arose, and that one as good as dead, as the stars of heaven in multitude and as the sand which is on the seashore innumerable*[^93]. When, therefore, he had recalled both—that is, both Abraham and Sarah—and had said that she was made unfruitful by the law of old age, but by faith received the power of conceiving seed, he securely subjoined that from one arose so many peoples, who might be compared to the multitude of the stars. That the joining of the parents, then, might be set forth, both the proclamation of such faithful ones and the truth of the deed required; but that, of those two themselves, in commending the propagated multitude, he should say not both but one, the art of one praising admonished. For he wished to show how much sure faith had merited; reckoning that, in saying that a multitude exceeding numbers was propagated by the power of God, it was more fittingly suited to the praise of his work if he said they were procreated from one rather than from two—especially since, in the increase of praise which the last clause heaped up, there was no loss of history, which above he had supplied by the mention of each person.

*Augustine.* He who does not understand that you say nothing, I do not know what he understands. You had said that the Apostle said, *Through one man sin entered into the world*, for this reason: because that man gave the rest an example of sinning. *For if*, you say, *he wished generation understood in that passage, he would say "through two," not "through one"*—when rather, if he wished an example understood, he would say 'through one woman,' not *through one man*, since it is established that she gave even her own husband an example of sinning. But because by generation sin entered into the world, to be healed only by regeneration, therefore he said: *through one*, because, as the example of human sin began from the woman, so generation began from the man; for the sowing, which pertains to the man, without anyone doubting, precedes the conception, which pertains to the woman. But behold, a most evident testimony has been found—whether by yourself or by another who perhaps objected this to you—where without any ambiguity innumerable men are said to have arisen from one, although they had a man and a woman, surely two parents, in order to arise; but it was said most correctly on account of the beginning of generation, which is from the seed of the man. Finally, when he set forth praiseworthy examples of faith, he began from Abel himself, and, having recalled Abraham, he came to Sarah. By now he had surely passed from him and was speaking of his wife, and yet, when it came to recalling the generation of the immense people, he returned to him, because he begot what she bore. And if you considered this, as you ought, you would not blaspheme the faithful preacher of faith who wrote the epistle, nor say that he was *admonished by the art of praise*. Admonished to what, I ask? That he should lie, asserting that those arose from one who arose from two? *More fittingly*, namely, as you reckon, *weighing this to be suited to the praise of the work of God*. You err greatly; a false praise does not delight God. You indeed are wont to offer it gladly to lust; but falsity greatly displeases truth. For why you should offer yourself to lust itself as not a praiser but a flatterer, I do not know. Will it for that reason love you more? You are utterly deceived; it does not love the man, but plainly goads him to love what he ought not to love. But if it can be said in any manner of speech, not falsely but truly, that those arose from one who arose from two, why do you think generation could not be intimated where it was said, *Through one man sin entered into the world*, because generation is done through two, not through one? Since it is known to all that either he rather, or first, generates who sows, but the woman either does not generate but bears, or, if even the bearing itself can rightly be called generation, first conceives from the generating man and afterward generates the offspring she had conceived. Accordingly the Apostle, since he wished it understood that sin entered into the world by generation—which Christ would wash away by regeneration—said *through one man*, who then either first or rather generated; especially because, as we have already said and must be impressed on you, if he said this on account of the primacy of the example, he would say 'through one woman,' from whom first in the human race the example of sinning proceeded, and would rather pass over the man, whom he knew to have followed the woman's example, so as to sin himself by imitating.

**III. 86.** *Julian.* To the Hebrews, then, where the discourse is of generation, many are said to have come *from one*; but to the Romans, where the Apostle speaks of sin, he says that *through one man* sin entered—by which number he most absolutely taught that he thought nothing of generation.

*Augustine.* To speak thus against open facts is not, as you think, praiseworthy eloquence, but incredible shamelessness.

**III. 87.** *Julian.* But where it is said of Christ: *He who sanctifies, and they who are sanctified, are all of one*[^94], it can be taken not of Adam, but of God, by whose power Christ was made according to the flesh, and the whole human race; and on this account, to undermine the understanding of the Apostle, by which he said that through one man sin entered into the world, the likeness of other words could bring no weight. Yet let this, which follows, cleave above all to the mind of the prudent reader, that he may understand that I have thus far acted much more remissly than the matter demanded. For let us allow that by very many testimonies it is taught that generation, which cannot be done except through two, is nevertheless wont to be said to be done even through one. But the opinion of transmission will accomplish nothing: why? Namely, because some things we say abusively, others properly. Therefore those which are proper bestow their appellations without loss; but those which are said by usurpation are not allowed to bring prejudices of their definitions upon the principal, properly so-called things; and this is all done blamelessly, when to things about which there is no doubt we impart, by abuse, alien terms.

*Augustine.* But you are about to bring doubt even to open facts which are against you.

**III. 88.** *Julian.* Furthermore, when some matter is brought in against the opinion of all by that word in whose sound it has all its support, it is gravely sinned against if, with its proper name ready at hand, it is marked by the begged appellation of an abusive term. When, therefore, the discourse is of offspring, since no one doubts that each one born has two parents, nor does this need confirmation, it will not inconvenience the understanding if I say that he was generated from one; for my speech will move no one so far as to think that he could be born either without a father or without a mother.

*Augustine.* One born has indeed two parents; but that he be born, one begets him by sowing, the other bears him by bringing forth. Whence it sufficiently appears to whom generation is chiefly, or to whom first, to be attributed—so that you may cease to spread the clouds of garrulity over things set in the light. But who speaks so as to say that anyone is generated from one? Since everyone who hears this thinks of nothing but the father, nor does anyone but one father generate a man by sowing; but two or more generated from one are rightly wont to be said who might be thought to have one father. But where a father and mother are to be thought of, who is said to be born from one except falsely? For if it be manifest that two walked together or did something together, do you for that reason say without lying that one walked or that one did this, because it is manifest that there were two? Will not your lie be the more shameless the more manifest that is? The singular number is indeed put for the plural by a tropical manner of speech, as in the plagues with which Egypt was struck, where 'frog' or 'locust' is said in the singular number, when there were many[^95]; but if one said 'one frog' or 'one locust,' who would doubt that this lie would be the more empty the more clear that truth is? Cease, then, to sell these vapors to men most inexperienced in these matters, and understand that through one man sin entered into the world—not as you, but as the Apostle says. *Through one man*—not preceding by example, for this would be said of the woman, but generating by the beginning, because he first sowed what she conceived, and he himself begot what she bore—*sin entered into the world*[^96], as it is written: *Abraham begot Isaac, Isaac begot Jacob*[^97], and through the other following generations this manner of speech persists, nor was it said: Abraham and Sarah begot Isaac, or Isaac and Rebecca begot Jacob; and where it was necessary to recall the mothers, the Evangelist did not say: Judah and Tamar begot Perez and Zerah, but said: *Judah begot of Tamar*[^98], and wherever he added the mothers too, he nevertheless gave the generation to the fathers, not saying: He and she begot him, but: He begot him of her. Whence you may understand that innumerable men were said to be generated from the one Abraham in this way, because he, one, begot them of Sarah. And therefore it was said, *Through one man sin entered into the world*, that there might be understood the beginning of generation, which is from the man, not the example of imitation, which in the human race entered into the world through one woman rather than through one man.

**III. 89.** *Julian.* But when sin is treated of, which against the opinion of all and all reason is feigned to be inborn.

*Augustine.* It is not feigned against the opinion of all and all reason, but against your error original sin is shown, with Scripture and the very misery of the human race as witness.

**III. 90.** *Julian.* And this sin is properly said to have passed through one, if he gave an example of offending, but most unfittingly if he engendered this sin, which could not pass to posterity except through two.

*Augustine.* Rather, it entered through one, by whom was sown what she bore; but the example went before in her, which he followed.

**III. 91.** *Julian.* And the Apostle commended that sin passed, but through one, and the truth showed that this pertains properly to the example, the Traducianist acts with intolerable shamelessness, so as to say that that number too reaches him, bent by abuse.

*Augustine.* What is it that, so often, by a new and as it were insulting name, you think you accomplish—that the truth of the most ancient catholic teaching may be fled while the novelty of the name is dreaded? What cannot be derided in this way? But by vanity, not by urbanity. For the Apostle says: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all human beings*[^99]; these words we both accept. If, then, we are Traducianists on account of the sin which was transmitted by generation, so that it passed to all human beings, you are Traducianists on account of the sin which you feign to have been transmitted by imitation, so that it passed to all human beings; and the Apostle appeared first a Traducianist, who—whether he thought this, which it is clear he thought, or that, which by erring you say—nevertheless, by saying that sin entered into the world through one man and passed to all human beings, gave the occasion of this name. But if the name of transmission does not fit these words, where it is said that sin entered through one man and passed to all human beings, then this name fits neither us nor you nor the Apostle; but plainly to say this, to object this, to repeat this constantly and odiously, fits your ineptitude well enough.

**III. 92.** *Julian.* For while it is an unheard-of monstrosity if anyone, leaving the proper words, should try to build up even a tolerable teaching—a new one, however—by abusive and ambiguous words, then this is infinitely more deformed: that Augustine strives to confirm matters obscene and accusing the justice of God by such doubtful sentences that he confesses they sound, against himself, properly, but for himself, only by sufferance. Would any learned man trust that argument which, when it comes into court, is weighed by his enemy as a home-born slave, but by him as a fugitive and a kidnapped one?

*Augustine.* Of abusive and proper words you speak in such a way that, despising the fewness of the men who understand you and find you raving, you choose to seem to the many who do not understand you to say something, when you say nothing. Better, then, do I leave you to the few learned, who, even without my showing it, most easily understand that you say nothing, than that, in refuting you, although I say true things, I say things which the many do not understand. Nevertheless, where it was said, *Through one man sin entered into the world*, on account of the author of generation, and therefore *the pattern of him who was to come*, on account of the author of regeneration, the words are not abusive, but proper.

**III. 93.** *Julian.* What, then, is set down to the Hebrews, *From one all*, the praise of God demands—after the mention of the parents, however; but what is said of Christ, *He who sanctifies, and they who are sanctified, are all of one*, is referred to God.

*Augustine.* Rather, after the mention of the parents—especially since he had already passed from the father and was speaking of the mother—the mother herself ought not to have been passed over, but it ought to have been said: They arose from two, who arose not from one but from two, that the praise of God might be true, not false—unless because this too is true, that they arose from one, nor was it said abusively, but properly, on account of the father, the author of the seed, not to increase the praise of God by a lie, as you reckon. For to say: A man did what two or more men did, can be a tropical manner of speech; but if anyone says that one man did what two did, unless one be the author of the deed of those two, he either lies or is deceived, as we already said a little before about the locust and the frog.

**III. 94.** *Julian.* For so the Apostle to the Corinthians: *For as the woman*, he says, *is from the man, so also the man is through the woman, but all things are from God*[^100]. Thirdly, reason has shown that, even if neither of these things which support us had existed, nevertheless that sentence by which Paul argues that through one man sin entered into this world entered into no covenant with the Manicheans.

*Augustine.* It is you rather who, by saying these things, are shown to be in covenant. For what covenant has the Apostle with the Manichean, when the Apostle says: *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^101]—which overturns your heresy—but the Manichean says: The body is immutably evil on account of a nature of evil coeternal with the good? And likewise in that passage where it was said, *The body is dead because of sin*, the Apostle says: *He who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies*[^102], but the Manichean says that the bodies of the flesh do not pertain to the creation of the good God but to the nature of evil, and that Christ was not raised from the dead, but did not die? You, then, who are not Manicheans, but, by a different plague, are not sound either, say how the body is dead because of sin, you who assert that the death of the body entered into the world not through the sin of that man, but by the law of nature.

**III. 95.** *Julian.* And on this account our reply remains unshaken, which shows that, through one man, nature was not corrupted and vitiated by generation, but, nature remaining whole, the Apostle alleges only the vitiated will of those who sin.

*Augustine.* Are you so blind, or do you so blind men by your dark disputations, that you dare to deny that bodies are born defective? Do bodies not pertain to the nature of man? Or—what the Manicheans say, whose madness, though you do not know it, you are unwilling to notice and consider how much you aid—is a good soul held mixed with the bodies of the nation of darkness? Say, then, the merits of defective bodies, you who deny that the little ones draw any sins from their parents. Behold, the Manicheans say: This mortal flesh so pertains not to the workmanship of God but to the nation of darkness, that even the bodies of men, whom you hold to have been made in the image of God, are often born not only corruptible and liable to the condition of death, but even defective. What will your heresy answer them, except that human nature—though under God the creator and maker—is nevertheless such that, even if no one had sinned, even in paradise such bodies of men would be born? O abominable and damnable utterance! But we, since we do not say that the bodies of men—not only corruptible and bound to the necessity of death, but many of them languid, distorted, deformed in them by innumerable defectiveness—would have arisen in paradise with no preceding sin of parents, and judge those who say so most worthy of anathema, attribute these things not to a nature so instituted from the beginning, but to one afterward vitiated by the merit of iniquity, so that we destroy both you and the Manicheans by the unshaken and ancient stability of the catholic faith.

**III. 96.** *Julian.* But let us return to that book which he addressed to Valerius, in which he set before himself, to be discussed and refuted, sentences plucked from one book of mine only. And indeed, in the first book of the present work I had come as far as those sayings of Augustine by which, his shamelessness uncovered—by which he fled the odium of that opinion, lest he seem to assert the devil to be the founder of human beings—he declared God to be the author of evils, and to create a substance of such desert as receives guilt before the use of reason, and as is placed in the kingdom of the devil by the hands of its own founder.

*Augustine.* He who distinguishes nature from defect does not say what you say; he who reads intelligently what I say does not think that I say what I do not say.

**III. 97.** *Julian.* And that he is a potter of wrath, fashioning vessels of perdition[^103].

*Augustine.* However much you do not understand how God makes from the same mass one vessel for honor, another for dishonor[^104], he nevertheless so makes them that he does not make a third kind of vessel, neither for honor nor for dishonor—such as you want the little ones to be; and that the image of God should not enter into the kingdom of God you reckon to be no dishonor. For you so love the kingdom itself that you believe it to be not even a slight penalty for a man not to be there, but none at all.

**III. 98.** *Julian.* But which are forced into perdition not by the advance of free will, but by the power of their own framer.

*Augustine.* You can say that God forces into perdition not any men whatever, but already his regenerated sons, by making them live whom he foresees will fall away from the faith, when he could have snatched them from here before they were changed by malice.

**III. 99.** *Julian.* And so great a wickedness of this prodigious teaching he labored to strengthen by the sentences of the apostle Paul, which I expounded by the context of the whole reading; and I showed that the prophet Isaiah, from whom it is established that this comparison of the potter was taken, most fully defends the cause of divine justice.

*Augustine.* What did you show to those who read and understand, except that you tried, by excessive garrulity, yet were unable, to pervert the apostolic words?

**III. 100.** *Julian.* And I completed the second book—with the Apostle's exposition, with Augustine's arguments set over against it, by the faculty which the truth supplied for that exposition; and therefore let us now return to the order of his book.

*Augustine.* You completed the second book not with an exposition of the Apostle, but under the profession of an exposition, by an empty assault, with vanity supplying you, not truth, what you might say.

**III. 101.** *Julian.* Therefore, after he had objected to himself one heading from the little preface of my earlier work, as if to assail it, and had subjoined that point about the potter of sinners as his God's, he charges me—with what constancy and purity let it be made public by his own words: *It is not, then, as you, deceiving yourself and others, say, that if anyone says there is free choice in men, or that God is the founder of the newborn, he is called a Caelestian and a Pelagian; for the catholic faith too says these things. But if anyone says that there is in men free choice for rightly worshiping God without his help, and whoever so says that God is the founder of the newborn that he denies him to be the redeemer of the little ones from the power of the devil, this man is called a Caelestian and a Pelagian. That there is free choice in men, and that God is the founder of the newborn, we both say; you are not Caelestians and Pelagians from this. But that anyone is free to do good without the help of God, and that the little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness and so transferred into the kingdom of God—this you say; from this you are Caelestians and Pelagians*[^105]. That you swim in the swamp of your impiety and dread, I have frequently shown, and it is established that a prudent reader will not doubt of this.

*Augustine.* That you cannot swim, but are sunk, others know who recognize you to be a heretic; for by that same submersion you have lost even your sense.

**III. 102.** *Julian.* And therefore, in the first book, that I had not lied who had written that all who fled the odium of our society rush down into the precipices of the Manicheans, who deny free choice and that God is the founder of men, and that you—what you had tried to fend off—at once, in the words now made plain, seized upon it, I disclosed by the insertion of even those writings which you had sent to Boniface[^106]. Nevertheless this your reply too, which I have now set down, confesses this; for you said that it is the catholic faith which believes both free choice and that God is the founder of the newborn—both of which it is certain that the Manicheans, of all heretics, deny along with you.

*Augustine.* The Manicheans—what you are unwilling to see—are rather helped by you, who attribute the so many and so great evils which we discern the little ones to suffer not to the merits of sins, but to the nation of darkness; for you have no way to flee, when you are asked by them whence these evils come. But when we refer all these things to the free choice of man, by which human nature, though it was instituted good, was corrupted, they are conquered, along with you, by the catholic truth.

**III. 103.** *Julian.* But because you assume the name alone of the catholic faith, of whose substance you are stripped, as a thin little cloak, you want us to believe that this too is held by you which the catholics confess—that is, both that there is free choice in men and that God is the founder of the newborn. But if this was brought forth by you simply and faithfully, put an end to the conflict by silence; let the report of those who slander run back to us, who objected to you what you have wiped out by a secure denial. Add only this to this sentence: that if any sect be found, any disputation, which tries by any argument to tear down this double confession which you professed to be catholic, it is either not yours or no longer defended by you. But if it helps to defend by a rich disputation those things which you are going to say you denied, unfold the definitions of free choice, and gird its bounds, by absolute distinctions, with certain limits, as it were.

*Augustine.* Enemies and defenders, by defending free choice you oppress it, which you are unwilling to be recalled to its own bounds by the goodness of the almighty and true defender.

**III. 104.** *Julian.* Assert also that God is the founder of such men as befit his hands and his justice.

*Augustine.* O foolish new heretics! If defective works do not befit the hands of God, will you dare to withdraw from the hands of God any bodies of men which you see often born somewhat defective? Why, then, do you not confess, with the catholic truth, that nature was corrupted by the choice of the man who first sinned—a nature from which God makes what befits not only a good maker but also a just judge—lest the Manicheans force you to attribute human bodies to a malignant and unjust maker?

**III. 105.** *Julian.* Of which surely none was done by you; but after you answered that the catholics confess free choice, of whose number you feign yourself to be, you at once applied a definition which would snatch away what you seemed to have granted. For you say: *But if anyone says that there is in men free choice for rightly worshiping God without his help, this man is called a Pelagian*; and likewise: *That there is free choice in men, we say; but that anyone is free to do good, you say*.

*Augustine.* If you had there added: *the heretics say*—although I did not say those words—you would nevertheless not have departed from my sentence. For it is true that the heretics say, that is, you yourselves, that anyone is free to do good without the help of God. But why I did not read there *without the help of God*—words of mine which you yourself set down a little before in this sentence—as long as I can, I attribute rather to a faulty manuscript than to you. Say, then, the rest.

**III. 106.** *Julian.* For as to what you say—that we say the liberty of choice suffices for each one to worship God rightly without his help—you lie altogether. For since the worship of God is understood in many ways—both in the keeping of the commandments and in the execration of vices and in the simplicity of conduct and in the order of the mysteries and in the profundity of the teachings which the Christian faith attains concerning the Trinity or the resurrection and many other like things—how can it be that we say, unconfused, that free choice suffices without the help of God for his worship, when we read the Lord saying in the Gospel: *I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, who have hidden these things from the wise and prudent and revealed them to little ones; yes, Father, since so it was well-pleasing before you*[^107]? Since surely all these things, both those contained in the teachings and those in the mysteries, the liberty of choice could not by itself have found out—although, that idols should not be worshiped, nor God, who was becoming known as the Founder of the world itself, despised, natural reason could, as the Teacher of the Gentiles is witness, teach[^108]. Not, then, this which you feign do we or any of the prudent say, but we affirm that a man of free choice is made by God, and helped by innumerable species of divine grace, to whom it is possible either to keep the commands of God or to transgress them. And this is where we defend that there is free choice: that, since God asserts his kindness in so many ways—that is, by commanding, blessing, sanctifying, restraining, provoking, illuminating—each one of those who already use reason may have a free will either to keep or to despise the will of God.

*Augustine.* You say so many things by which God helps us—that is, *by commanding, blessing, sanctifying, restraining, provoking, illuminating*—and you do not say, by giving love, although the apostle John says: *Love is from God*[^109]. Whence he likewise says: *Behold, what manner of love the Father has given us, that we should be called and be sons of God*[^110]. In this love, which is given to the human heart by the spirit, not the letter, is also understood that power of which the same John, in his Gospel: *He gave them*, he says, *the power to become sons of God*[^111]. This you say is from man to man through free choice, having the spirit of this world, not him who is from God, on account of which you do not know what things have been given us by God[^112]. Therefore you have neither peace with the Church from which you went out, nor love, which you deny to be the gift of God, nor faith, because you are heretics, since *peace to the brethren and love with faith* is not from human free choice, but *from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ*[^113]. If you acknowledge in these words the apostolic teaching, acknowledge also in your own words that you are a heretic.

**III. 107.** *Julian.* We do not, then, believe free choice fit for his worship—such as those initiated in the mysteries exhibit—without the help of God; but we confess the liberty of choice to be a rich witness of divine equity, so that, at that time when we must be manifested before the tribunal of Christ and each one receive his own according to what he did through the body, whether good or evil, God may be taught to judge nothing unjustly—he who imputes to no one a sin except what the one set for it could also have avoided.

*Augustine.* You admonish me well what to say against you. *We all*, surely, as the Apostle says, *must be manifested before the tribunal of Christ, that each one may bear according to the things which he did through the body, whether good or evil*[^114]. Will you separate the little ones from this universality? Say, then, what good they did through the body by their own free choice, that they should receive the kingdom of God, so great a good; or what evil they did by their own will, the little ones who shall be estranged from this life of God. But if it is necessary that you confess that, without any works of free choice which each does through the body, they are made alive in Christ, why do you not confess that those die in Adam, since you know that Adam is, by contrast, the pattern of him who was to come? Or will you, with closed eyes, open your mouth and say that to the former, in whom they were reborn, the spirit of justice profited, but to the latter, in which they were born, the flesh of sin did not harm? Who but you will dare to say this? But men of greater age, when they hear or read that each one will receive according to the things which he did through the body, ought not to trust in the power of their own will, but rather to pray that such a will be prepared for them by the Lord, that they may not enter into temptation. For *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^115]; and: *Pray*, the Lord himself says, *that you enter not into temptation*[^116]; and: *We pray to God*, says the Apostle, *that you may do no evil*[^117].

**III. 108.** *Julian.* Your former sentence, then, will accomplish nothing by obscurity; but the second, in which you repeat that you confess free choice indeed, yet not so that any man is believed free to do good, has laid bare your whole inward parts.

*Augustine.* You now force me to attribute to you what a little before I attributed to the manuscript. For behold, you so repeat my sentence that you do not complete it with my words, which are very necessary and contrary to you. For I said that no one is free to do good without the help of God, but you say that I confess free choice indeed, yet not so that any man is believed free to do good—and you do not add what I added: without the help of God. I do not accuse the thief; I civilly reclaim what was taken away: render back my words, and yours will be of no force.

**III. 109.** *Julian.* For behold, as we did in the first book, so now we closely press you to show how you did not deny free choice. Let this sink into our reader: that you confessed that the catholics proclaim free choice and that God is the founder of the newborn—the two things which none but a Manichean ever denied; but since we have both granted these in common, while neither to you nor to your teaching does the liberty of choice stand firm, it is altogether accomplished that you and your teaching hold nothing of the catholic faith. I ask, then, what is the force or what the definition of free choice. Surely no change of natural things is in its power. For no one ever changed in himself the offices of the senses—for instance, so as to receive voices by the nostrils or odors by the ears; no one changed the propriety of his own sex; no one was able, by free choice, to pass into the form of another animal; no one was able to change the hairs of his body into inborn fleeces; no one vindicated to himself, according to his talent, either the quality or the quantity of his body. By these examples one may range over all things which look to this condition. Let us, then, pass from natural things to the discussion of external things. To whom did the fruitfulness of fields, to whom the prosperity of voyages, to whom nobility and wealth, to whom the constancy of nobility itself stand in the right of his will, so that he might confess himself to have been made by God, by his free will, for the acquisition of these or like things? Natural things, then, are always borne by unshaken orders, but external things by uncertain chances. In what things, then, does free choice consist, on account of which men excel the beasts, on account of which they were made in the image of God, and by which alone the justice of the divine examination is upheld? In what, I say, is this free choice, which it is certain the Manicheans deny, just as you too confess the catholics assert it? Without doubt in this: that it is possible for a man, without any inevitable compulsion of natural things, either to send his will into crime or to restrain it from crime.

*Augustine.* To restrain the will from crime—this very thing is nothing other than not to enter into temptation. But if we had this in the power of our own will, we would not be admonished to ask it of the Lord by praying. To whom, then, it is said, *Turn away from evil*[^118], this surely is said, that he restrain his will from crime. And yet the Apostle, when he could rightly say even this: We command you to do no evil, says: *We pray to God that you may do no evil*[^119]. Behold why I said—not as you say I said—that no one is free to do good without the help of God; for this help the Apostle prayed for the faithful, he did not take away free choice from the nature of man. Lifted-up and inflated men, do not trust in your own power; subject yourselves to God, and, that you may restrain your will from crime and not enter into temptation, pray; and do not reckon that you do not enter into temptation when, by a strong will, you restrain the concupiscence of the flesh from some evil work. You do not know the wiles of the tempter; you enter into a greater temptation when you attribute these things to your own will without the help of God. I would indeed wish you would teach us how you said that those things which are called external among the goods or evils of men—such as riches or poverty and the rest—are borne by uncertain chances. For the catholic faith too so takes these away from human power that it attributes them to divine power. But I say this because I fear for you, lest perhaps you have added this too to your error—that whatever men suffer or attain, whether in their own bodies or in external things, you deny to pertain to divine providence, and on this account that whatever evils even the little ones suffer, you so assign to uncertain chances that you try to estrange these from the judgment of him without whose will, as the Lord himself says, not even a sparrow falls to the ground[^120]—since you see your heresy shipwrecked in this misery of the little ones, which under a just God would be none, unless human nature, corrupted and condemned by the magnitude of the first sin, had merited it.

**III. 110.** *Julian.* And, that the matter may be illuminated in few by examples: so that it may be as free for a man to want to commit sacrilege as not to want it, as free to want to perpetrate parricide as not to want it, as free to want to commit adultery as not to want it, as possible to bear true testimony as false, as free to obey God commanding as the devil persuading.

*Augustine.* You say truly; this is free choice, and just such Adam received; but what was given by the founder and vitiated by the deceiver must surely be healed by the Savior. This you are unwilling to confess with the Church; from this you are heretics. Man, who do not consider where you are, and in evil days, as though in good ones, are lifted up, blind—when free choice was such as you describe, man was not yet made like unto vanity, that his days should pass away like a shadow[^121]; for God is not vanity, in whose likeness he had been made—which is renewed by his grace from day to day. It was not yet said: *I was conceived in iniquities*[^122]; it was not yet said: *For who is clean from filth? Not even the infant whose life upon the earth is of one day*[^123]; finally, it was not said: *Not what I will, but what I hate, that I do*[^124]; and: *I know that there does not dwell in me—that is, in my flesh—good; for to will lies near me, but to accomplish the good I do not find*[^125], and: *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind*[^126]. This evil was not in Adam when he was created upright, because human nature had not yet been depraved; he had a ruler, whom he left by free choice; he was not seeking a liberator, through whom he might be made free from defect. For even if those words, *Not what I will do I do*[^127], and other such, are the words of a man—as you say—not yet set under the grace of Christ, then even from this you are convicted, both that Christ found men of so weak a will to do good, and that human nature cannot repair the weakness of free choice to do good except through the grace of Christ. And on this account it is true, what I said, that no one is free to do good without the help of God; and therefore you took away *without the help of God*, that a field might be opened for you in which you might display yourself more garrulously than eloquently through many things, by which you would not delight the reader, but rather, as far as you could, hinder one who wished to understand. Subject yourselves to God, that you may be corrected. No one is free to do good without the help of God. Why, in order to cast yourselves down, do you lift up the human will? Pray rather that you enter not into temptation.

**III. 111.** *Julian.* But for this reason in the foregoing I placed the perfected will rather than the effect of things, since both parricide and sacrilege and adultery or things like these are easier to avoid than to do; for the faculty of perpetrating what it wills is not always at hand for an evil will, but, on the contrary, abstinence from these consists in the greatest quiet—unless perhaps this very thing be called labor by you, to be unwilling to labor. I pass over the things which both the holy Scriptures—which the Prophets, the Evangelists, the Apostles brought forth—and the disputants shining with catholic soundness, John, Basil, Theodore, and those like them, commended: that it is much greater labor to commit crimes than to avoid them.

*Augustine.* Would that you held their faith, you would not deny original sin in the little ones.

**III. 112.** *Julian.* Meanwhile, as far as the present business looks, I impress that free choice was given for no other reason, nor can be understood in any other thing, than that no one be carried off, with a captive will, either to justice or to iniquity.

*Augustine.* He who, through the law which he sees in his members fighting against the law of his mind and taking him captive under the law of sin, cries out: *Not the good which I will do I do, but the evil which I hate, that I do*[^128]—you surely ought to say how he is not carried off to evil by a captive will. For, to speak according to you for the meantime, if this man groans under an evil habit, not yet, as you say, set under the grace of Christ, does this man have or not have free choice of will? If he has it, why does he not do the good which he wills, but does the evil which he hates? But if he does not have it for this reason, because he is not yet under the grace of Christ, behold, what I have already said, I say again and see must often be said to you: No one, except through the grace of Christ, can have free choice of will to do the good which he wills and not to do the evil which he hates—not so that his will be carried off to good as to evil, but so that, freed from captivity, it be drawn to its liberator by the liberal sweetness of love, not by the servile bitterness of fear.

**III. 113.** *Julian.* There are indeed sweet incitements of vices, and often bitter torments of penalties, which are stirred up by persecutors; but both the censure of honor beats back the former, and the greatness of patience consumes the latter.

*Augustine.* You declaim among those *who trust in their own power*[^129]; attend to yourself, lest you cry out among those who will be tormented for their own pride.

**III. 114.** *Julian.* Nevertheless the possession of the virtues is not toilsome, which enjoys, outside a certain kingdom of a good conscience, the promised sublimity of eternal blessedness; yet the helps of the grace of God are present, which never abandon the will in the part of virtue—of which grace, though there are innumerable species, they are nevertheless always applied with such moderation that they never drive free choice from its place, but offer supports as long as one wishes to lean on them, while yet they do not oppress a reluctant soul. From this indeed it is that, as some ascend from vices to virtues, so others also relapse from virtues to vices.

*Augustine.* How can it be that the helps of the grace of God drive free choice from its place, which rather, when it has been driven by vices and subjugated to wickedness, they free, that it may return to its own place? But when it is asked of you what these helps of the grace of God are, you proclaim those which you recalled above—that God helps by commanding, blessing, sanctifying, restraining, provoking, illuminating—all of which are done even through men according to the Scriptures. For men too command, and bless, and through the divine sacraments sanctify, and by rebuking restrain, and by exhorting provoke, and by teaching illuminate; *nevertheless neither he who plants is anything nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^130]. But this is the increase, that each one obey the precepts of God—which is not done, when it is truly done, except by love. Whence the Church *makes the increase of the body unto its own building up in love*[^131]. This love none but God gives; for love is from God[^132]. This you are unwilling to name among the helps of grace which you recall, lest you grant that this very thing, that we obey God, is of his grace. For you think that in this way free choice is taken away, since no one can do this except by will; but—what you are unwilling to admit—*the will is prepared by the Lord*[^133], not by words sounding from without, but as, with the queen praying and being heard, God turned and transferred the indignation of the king into mildness[^134]; for as he wrought this in the man's heart by this divine and hidden manner, so *he works in us both to will and to work for the good will*[^135].

**III. 115.** *Julian.* How, then, do you confess free choice, to which you say only one thing is possible—that is, to do evil—but it is not possible to turn aside from evil and do good?

*Augustine.* I say it is possible for the will of man to turn aside from evil and do good—but for that will which God helps freely, not which Julian, ungrateful, puffs up.

**III. 116.** *Julian.* I pass over for the meantime with what rage you rave against the whole law, which you believe commanded mortals those things of which it saw no faculty in them.

*Augustine.* What you say is not true. God commands these things which can be done, but he himself gave the ability to do them to those who can do them and do them, and those who cannot, by commanding he admonishes to ask of him that they may be able. And that not all things are done by each of the saints, God knows how he provides for their humility, to whom, daily saying, *Forgive us our debts*[^136], he so gives aid for having obedience that there is something whence he may also grant pardon.

**III. 117.** *Julian.* But I ask, with what poets you ran into such a Hippocrene, that you feigned a two-membered beast—not indeed by versifying, but by blaspheming—whose body you would form of an evil necessity and whose face alone you would overlay with the name of liberty.

*Augustine.* You paint for yourself what pleases you, you who have leisure, with an empty heart, to revolve empty phantasms. For why do you set the supports of grace under the good will, when the evil will leans on no support, that it may be evil or persist in being evil? Or here does your balance—which you try to suspend on both sides by equal weights, so that the will may be as free to good as to evil—by inclining to one side, betray you as raving?

**III. 118.** *Julian.* For so you argue even in that work which you sent to Rome: *The will, which is free in evils, is not free in goods*.

*Augustine.* Why do you not add what you read there: *if it has not been freed*? Or what is it that the Lord says, when he was speaking of the fruits of the branches—that is, of good acts: *Without me you can do nothing*[^137]—except because no one is free to do good whom he himself does not free?

**III. 119.** *Julian.* And here you call him a Caelestian who thinks that anyone is free to do good; you call free, then, that which you contend cannot do anything but evil. Find, if you can, another definition of a thing not free, and claim this as free—if you had so fallen out of your senses that you did not see the definition of the free in its own body, or you ought at least to have understood from its contrary what the essence of liberty was. For suppose you could likewise have doubted what that was which is called the sight of the eyes, and you defined it in this manner: sight is either to have one's eyes gouged out, or, at the time of seeing, to be able to perceive nothing because of any hindrances—and you thought this definition fit sight—but you betook yourself to assert what would be the explanation of its contrary, that is, of blindness, and when you found no other than that, in the animal to whose nature the sight of the eyes belonged, either the gouging out of the lights or the obstruction of a thick humor depriving it of the faculty of seeing, you would without doubt come to your senses and see that one definition cannot suit contrary things. And so it would come about that, if blindness could be called nothing other than, in an animal's eye at the time when it ought to see, the privation of discerning, the definition of sight too would be rendered by the negation of those things by which you had explained blindness—namely, that sight is nothing other than the fitting faculty of discerning, at a suitable time, with eyes neither gouged out nor obstructed. Which things being shown to you, if you resisted obstinately, you would obtain nothing else than that your hearers would either believe you to strive, by a shameful pertinacity, against your own conscience, or, if you made them believe that it utterly seemed so to you, would judge you to have the eyes of your mind no less gouged out than him who had been defined by you as having sight.

*Augustine.* I do not want you to define, but to make an end of blindness and see that it could not rightly be said by Christ, *Without me you can do nothing*[^138], if men could have been free to do good without the grace of Christ.

**III. 120.** *Julian.* So, then, to refer the example to the case, you could have understood, even from the definition of the not-free, what you ought to call free. For even if your understanding had been darkened in the definition of free choice, so that you reckoned that could be called free which was taught to cleave to one of two contraries, you ought to have considered that the captive—that is, the not-free—can be explained no otherwise than as one vindicated as bound to one of two contraries, and therefore that the name must be given to liberty by the negation of captivity; so that, since that which was not free was compelled to cleave to one of the contraries, its opposite—that is, the free—should be allowed to be bound to neither.

*Augustine.* Why do you entangle plain things with perplexed garrulity? To evil he is free who does evil things by will, either by act or by word or certainly by thought alone; and what man of greater age cannot do this? But to good he is free who, by a good will, does good things himself too, either by act or by word or certainly by thought alone; but this no man can do without the grace of God. And if you say anyone can, you contradict him who said, *Without me you can do nothing*[^139]; you contradict also him who said, *Not that we are sufficient to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God*[^140]. I think he did not say that he was sufficient to think of himself something good, not evil, but from God; and of a good thought there is both a good word and a good work. And on this account he who is not sufficient of himself to think anything good is surely neither sufficient to speak nor to do anything good of himself, but, if he is under grace, his sufficiency is from God; whence it was said: *For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you*[^141], and: *As many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God*[^142]. Considering these things, I said that no one is free to do good without the help of God; fearing these things, you said that I said no one is free to do good, and you took away *without the help of God*, which I added. Whence I do not doubt that you already know you have been conquered, but by prolix empty talk you act lest you appear conquered, you who so define free will that, unless it can do both—that is, both act well and act ill—it could not be free. And on this account it is necessary for you to take away liberty from God, who can have only a good will, but cannot have an evil one.

**III. 121.** *Julian.* Since this is so, and you struggle most wretchedly against this passage, it is uncertain what ought rather to be reckoned of you: whether you defend false things against your own conscience, or whether you think that which is false to be true; and on this account it is uncertain whether you have lost the eyes of reason, while it is certain that you have lost the eyes of faith.

*Augustine.* Do you really console yourself for being defeated by hurling insults?

**III. 122.** *Julian.* But, to recapitulate briefly what has been argued, free choice—which, on the side of evil, is helped by the pleasures of the vices or the persuasions of the devil, and, on the side of good, by the teachings of the virtues and the various kinds of divine grace—cannot stand at all unless the necessity both of righteousness and of sin is taken away from it.

*Augustine.* If you placed among the kinds of divine grace that love which—as you read most plainly—is not from ourselves but from God, and which God gives to his children, without which no one lives devoutly and with which no one lives otherwise than devoutly, without which no one has a good will and with which no one has anything but a good will, then you would truly be defending free choice and not puffing it up. As for necessity, if you mean that by which one is crushed against one's will, there is no such necessity of righteousness, because no one is righteous against his will; rather, the grace of God makes a willing person out of an unwilling one. But if no one sinned against his will, it would not have been written, *You have sealed up my sins in a bag, and you have noted whatever I have admitted against my will*[^143].

**III. 123.** *Julian.* The Catholics confess the truth of this, but the Traducianists, along with their masters the Manicheans, deny it.

*Augustine.* These are insults, not judgments. I wish you could judge; but who, however worthless, cannot hurl insults?

**III. 124.** *Julian.* We, then, have spoken the truth, since those who are deceived by you become Manicheans in order not to be called heretics, and, while they fear a false disgrace, they incur a true crime, like wild beasts that are hemmed in by feathers so that they may be driven into the nets and forced, by an empty terror, into real destruction. But we say that God is the maker of those who are born in this way: since it is certain that the God of the Catholics, who is the true God, can do nothing that is evil, by the dignity of the maker himself men who are made by him are in no way to be believed to come forth from his hands, before any use of the will, as wicked and guilty. When you deny the one of these, you undermine both. For you say that you indeed believe God to be the maker, but the maker of evil men, and by this you contradict your own teaching, since you deny that you assert the devil to be the maker of men.

*Augustine.* Whatever in human beings, who are born with a defect, pertains to God the creator is good, because even what is just is good; but God is the author of natures, not of defects. Now I come to that point: let us see what you are going to say about it, how it is that little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness when they are transferred into the kingdom of Christ through the sacraments of the Church. For however many wrappings of manifold loquacity you envelop yourself in, when you come to this, you will appear a naked heretic.

**III. 125.** *Julian.* For when you ascribe to the work of God such people as he cannot make, you show that what you had said was made by him pertains in no way to God.

*Augustine.* None but God can make human beings. Say rather how it is that little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness when they are reborn through the divine sacraments.

**III. 126.** *Julian.* Although this has been dealt with more fully in the first book, let us nonetheless express here, even if briefly, what you hold. You are afraid to assign any substance to the work of the devil, but you are not afraid to fasten upon God a charge that is not slight but grave. With you the nature of human flesh commands more reverence than the righteousness of God; for it is as though it were something great that you were afraid to assign the substance of man to the devil, and as though it were something of no great importance—namely, the righteousness and holiness of God—that you brought a charge of wickedness against. As if it would not have been more bearable, between two opinions, both of them false, to assign the flesh to the works of the devil than to assign iniquity to the works of God.

*Augustine.* You rather are the ones who blaspheme the righteousness of God, when you say that under his omnipotence little ones, without any evil merits, suffer such great evils. But now tell me how you separate them from those whom God *has rescued from the power of darkness, that he might transfer them into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^144].

**III. 127.** *Julian.* For just as here you said that, in the power of the devil, little ones are formed by God as guilty, so in the later parts of your book you vomited up something fouler than the rites of the Manicheans, saying of God, *He creates the evil in the same way that he feeds and nourishes the evil*.

*Augustine.* When you come to those same parts of my book, it will be clear there in what sense the words you here object to were said. Now then, tell me, I beg you, how, when little ones are reborn and transferred to the kingdom of Christ, they are not rescued from the power of darkness.

**III. 128.** *Julian.* Therefore God creates evil.

*Augustine.* You do not understand in what sense he says through the Prophet, *I create evils*[^145].

**III. 129.** *Julian.* And the innocent are punished on account of what God does.

*Augustine.* They are neither innocent in their origin nor punished on account of what God does.

**III. 130.** *Julian.* And they are possessed by the devil, because God does this.

*Augustine.* The Apostle too handed a man over to Satan, but by righteousness, not by malice[^146], and God *handed certain people over to a reprobate mind*[^147]—and would that you too were not among them!

**III. 131.** *Julian.* And God imputes to human beings the crime of his own hands.

*Augustine.* What little ones draw from their corrupted origin is not the crime of the hands of God.

**III. 132.** *Julian.* And what the devil has subtly suggested, God skillfully and persistently forms and protects and guards and shapes.

*Augustine.* God does not form what the devil has suggested; rather, out of the nature that the devil has corrupted, God well forms what he forms.

**III. 133.** *Julian.* And God demands from man the fruit of goodness, though it was God who engendered evil in him.

*Augustine.* God did not engender evil; rather, the evil that corrupted generation engendered, God purifies through regeneration.

**III. 134.** *Julian.* And then he lies throughout the whole law, since the Lord is righteous.

*Augustine.* You lie—you who deny that the children of Adam have a sin worthy of the heavy yoke[^148]. What are you trying to show, except that God is unrighteous?

**III. 135.** *Julian.* And one who takes on so many crimes is still called God?

*Augustine.* Since God takes on no crimes, he does not take on this one either that you fasten upon him—namely, that, without the merit of any original sin, little ones suffer so many and such great evils, with him either causing or permitting it.

**III. 136.** *Julian.* *The memory of* the Manicheans *will perish with a crash, for the Lord remains forever*[^149], who *has prepared his throne for judgment, and he himself will judge the world in equity*[^150]. There is no crime in God. Therefore he does not create the evil, since, if they were evil by nature, God could not create them; and so, just as it belongs to the Catholics to confess that God is the maker of the good, so it belongs properly to the Manicheans to believe that God is the maker of the evil.

*Augustine.* If you were not deaf to the divine words, where it is said, *He will judge the world in equity*[^151], you would learn the equity of God even in the punishments of infants; for by nature they are good, in that God creates them, but by their defect they are evil, on account of which God heals them. By this Catholic sentence not only does the memory of the Manicheans perish, but so too does that of the Pelagians, along with the crash of their loquacity.

**III. 137.** *Julian.* But let us look at the rest as well.

*Augustine.* See how you proceed, so that the reader may withdraw far from what I have said and think you have answered, while he forgets the things you had set out as though to be refuted. For I, in order to press you with apostolic authority, said that you say that little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness and so transferred into the kingdom of God; and since you say nothing against this, you have indeed appeared, as I said above, a naked heretic—but I did not labor to strip you, because against the apostolic faith of the most ancient mother, the Church, you have dared to cover yourself with no wrapping of your empty verbiage.

**III. 138.** *Julian.* *Hear, then, briefly what this question turns on. The Catholics say that human nature was founded good by God the good creator, but that, corrupted by sin, it needs Christ the physician. The Manicheans say that human nature was not founded good by God and corrupted by sin, but that man was created by the prince of the eternal darkness out of the mingling of two natures that have always existed, one good and one evil. The Caelestians and Pelagians say that human nature was founded good by God who is good, but that it is so healthy in the little ones who are born that at that age they do not need the medicine of Christ. Recognize, then, your own name in your teaching, and stop fastening upon the Catholics—by whom you are refuted—a teaching and a name that are not theirs; for the truth refutes both, both the Manicheans and you. For to the Manicheans it says: "Have you not read that he who made man from the beginning made them male and female? For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother and shall cling to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh; and so they are no longer two, but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let no man put asunder"*[^152]. *For in this way it shows, against the Manicheans, who deny both of these, that God is both the maker of human beings and the joiner of spouses. But to you it says: "The Son of man came to seek and to save what was lost"*[^153]. *But you, you excellent Christians, answer Christ: If you came to seek and to save what was lost, you did not come for the little ones; they were not lost and they were born saved; go to the older ones. From your own words we set down our case against you: "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick"*[^154]. *And so it comes about that the Manichean, who says that an evil nature has been mingled into man, at least wants the good soul to be saved from there by Christ; but you, in the little ones, when they are saved in body, contend that there is nothing for Christ to save. And by this the Manichean indeed reviles human nature detestably, but you praise it cruelly. For whoever believes you, the praiser, will not offer his infants to the Savior*. I noted indeed, even in the first work, that you do nothing more than try to be little understood.

*Augustine.* I am thoroughly understood, like it or not; but you, who are going to say nothing against these things, do not wish to be understood as having said what I said, which is most true and most firm; and finally the matter itself has shown that these things could not be refuted by you.

**III. 139.** *Julian.* And I have shown that almost the greater part of your fictions is understood with more labor than it is refuted.

*Augustine.* You both labor—which is worse for you—and are defeated; yet you do not labor to understand me, which you could do most easily, but you labor to refute me, which you cannot do.

**III. 140.** *Julian.* But if I wished to do this point by point, I would seem both superfluous, by repeating the same things, and long-winded, by pursuing everything.

*Augustine.* See how you act, so that the reader may withdraw far from what I said and think that you have answered, while he forgets those things that you had set out as though to be refuted.

**III. 141.** *Julian.* Hence, although all the writings you have published against us hasten toward one end—namely, to persuade us that evils are natural and that the devil is the maker of human beings or God the maker of crimes—nonetheless I think it serves the aim of brevity if I gather into one, from the various parts, those arguments above all by which you think you defend your whole opinion when you take cover, and if I help them first by setting forth an explanation, so that what they attempt may appear, and then bring them forth not scattered but collected, not tangled but unfolded.

*Augustine.* With a wondrous zeal for brevity you give eight books in return for my one, which you do not overturn even with such a length of much talking. Now say, if you can, how you can avoid being forced to say to Christ—if not in words, at least by your very perverse meanings: *If you came to seek and to save what was lost, you did not come for the little ones; they were not lost and they were born saved; go to the older ones. From your own words we set down our case against you: "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick"*[^155]. Answer that. Why do you try to wrap up and hide truths in empty things?

**III. 142.** *Julian.* And so, in the later parts of your book, after you had said, *God creates the evil in the same way that he feeds and nourishes the evil*, you add: *Because what he grants them in creating pertains to the goodness of nature, and the increase he gives them in feeding and nourishing he gives as a good help, not indeed to their malice, but to that same good nature which he, who is good, created. For insofar as they are human beings, it is a good of nature, of which God is the author; but insofar as they are born with sin, to perish if they are not reborn, they pertain to the seed cursed from the beginning by the defect of that ancient disobedience*. *Yet the maker uses even the vessels of wrath well, so as to make known the riches of his glory in the vessels of mercy, so that whoever, belonging to the same mass, is set free by grace may not ascribe it to his own merits, but "let him who glories, glory in the Lord"*[^156]. *Departing from this faith, that fellow does not want those who are born to be under the devil, lest the little ones be carried to Christ to be rescued from the power of darkness and transferred into his kingdom*. *And thus he accuses the Church spread through the whole world, in which everywhere all the infants to be baptized are exsufflated for no other reason than that the prince of this world may be cast out of them*. Likewise, lower down, speaking of the disordered desire without which there can be no mingling of spouses—a desire which we defend as natural and as pertaining to the work of God, not as some great good, but as a sensation of bodies that were made by God, while you, locating your teaching in its shame, try in so many writings to assert that it was placed in human inmost parts by the devil—speaking, then, of this disordered desire, you say, as you frequently do, so too in the later part of your book: *And so*—you say—*we do not censure the honorable mingling of spouses on account of the shameful disordered desire of bodies. For that mingling could be without any preceding commission of sin, and the married would not blush for it; but this disordered desire arose after sin, and in confusion they were compelled to veil it*. *Hence it has remained for later married people, even though they use this evil well and lawfully, to avoid the human gaze in this kind of work, and so to confess that what is shameful, when no one ought to be ashamed of what is good. Accordingly, whoever lies with his wife lawfully uses an evil well by the shameful disordered desire; but whoever does so unlawfully uses an evil badly*.

*Augustine.* Why has the sentence been interrupted and, with certain things omitted, this added as though it followed: *Accordingly, by the shameful disordered desire*, and the rest? But there have been omitted my words where it was said: *Thus these two are bound together, both the good of the praiseworthy union, from which children are begotten*, *and the evil of the shameful disordered desire, from which those who are begotten must be regenerated, lest they be condemned*. Why do you remove these words of mine from the middle of my sentence and, with them removed, join other words of mine as though they followed? What is this you are doing? Why do you do this? It is not enough that you let go of those things you had set out in order from this very book of mine to be refuted, and pass on to others, so that the order is disturbed and what you had proposed slips from the reader's memory. On top of that, even the things that you insert out of order, as it pleased you, you do not set down faithfully whole and entire, but you cut them where you wish, take away what you wish, join them how you wish. But do whatever you wish; you will appear convicted and defeated, as you do not wish.

**III. 143.** *Julian.* *For it more rightly takes the name of an evil than of a good, since both the evil and the good blush at it, and we more rightly believe the Apostle, who says, "For good does not dwell in my flesh"*[^157], *than this fellow, who says that it is a good*.

*Augustine.* It would have been no great matter to fill this sentence out from my book as well. For I said: *than this fellow, who says that it is a good—at which, if he is confounded, he confesses it an evil; but if he is not confounded, he adds the worse evil of shamelessness*. But I do not know why you have not set this down, since you could have left it unanswered, as you did the others, which you set out as though to be refuted and yet were unwilling even to touch.

**III. 144.** *Julian.* And a little later you add: *Since human nature, which is born either of marriage or of adultery, is the work of God. If it were evil, it would not be something to be generated; if it had no evil, it would not be something to be regenerated; and, to conclude both in a single word, human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved; if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved. Therefore whoever says that it is not good denies the Creator of what was founded good; but whoever denies that there is evil in it begrudges the merciful Savior of what was corrupted. Wherefore, in human beings who are born, adulteries are not to be excused on account of the good which is created there* *by the good Founder, nor are marriages to be accused on account of the evil which is there to be healed by the merciful Savior*. These many things, which I have set down from your writings, since you bore a great toil of thought in them, so as to seem to say something acute—both from our custom, by which we do nothing cunningly, and from the security of truth, since they can scarcely be understood from your discourses—must be distinguished by our restatement. You said, then, that the Manicheans disparage the nature of the flesh and assert that man was made from the mingling of two natures, one good, the other evil; while we, whom you call heretics, say that human nature was founded by the good God—on the testimony of its author—good, and that in infants it is so healthy that they do not need the medicine of Christ; and that you say that the nature of Adam was indeed once founded good by God who is good, but nonetheless was corrupted by sin and therefore needs the medicine of Christ. In the first book of the present work I have shown, by joining the opinions of both, how your faith differs in no way from the profanity of the Manicheans—from which it is manifest that it was born—where it appeared that you are joined to Jovinian through fear, but to the Manichean wholly through love. This, then, must be discussed by us in the present manner of treatment as well; but I distinguish our position first, then yours. You said truly, then, that we say that human nature was founded good by the good God, but only up to this point is it ours; for you either did not see or suppressed the one part by which our position is completed, while you set down another part as from your own, since it is not ours. For we say not only that the nature of man was founded good by God in Adam, but that in all the little ones it is founded good by that same God who was the author of that first man, on account of which we assert that he is the founder of all human beings.

*Augustine.* For what else do we too say about the Lord God, the founder of all human beings? But—far be it from us!—you deny that God the Savior is necessary to the little ones, by asserting their nature to be so good that you say there is no evil in it on account of which it would need Christ the physician. Answer this; refute first what you had set out to be refuted; show why the little ones to be baptized are exsufflated, or, by declaring open war on the whole most ancient Church, contend that they ought not to be exsufflated. Do this, press on here; against this firm foundation, if you can, drive something by which it may be shaken. Why do you flee beneath the hides of your loquacity? Why do you raise up your most empty smoke, by which, with these things covered over and obscured, the reader may so forget them that he thinks you, who can say nothing, are saying something?

**III. 145.** *Julian.* Therefore you not only removed this part of the statement from our definition, but in its place you also brought in your own, which at first glance might carry some hatefulness, but which, once examined, would vanish. For you set down that we say that nature was founded good by the good God, but that it is so healthy that it has no need of the medicine of Christ. See, then, what great poverty of truth you labor under, you who, even in the very words that are said—good—do not fear to commit so plain a theft; for when you had set out above "good nature," you added: "it is so healthy." Is the opposite of good "healthy"? Surely, when we say good, we properly set nothing against it but evil; but if the case demands that we say healthy, we place on the opposite side "weak" or "sick," so that if it is healthy, it is by no means weak, and if weak, it is said not to be healthy; but when we say, "This is good," we set against it not the term "unhealthy" but the word "evil." It was therefore for you to say that we define human nature to have been founded good by God, but to be so good in the little ones that it would seek no corrector of its constitution—or, if you preferred to indicate by another word, that is, by "health," the things that were good, you should have marked the whole with a consonant phrasing. But as it is, you slip between the rocks of definitions like a little eel; for when truth had compelled you to confess that we say the nature of human beings is good, founded by the good God, and you saw in that part of the statement much reason but no hatefulness, you immediately migrated to other words, adding: but that we so defend it as healthy that we believe the medicine of Christ to be unnecessary to it. But if you fall into all this through inexperience, you are found most dull; but if you do it by cunning, most crafty.

*Augustine.* When would you say such things, except by having nothing to say? For we ought not, as you suppose, to have said "so healthy" of what we called good, lest false antitheses be set against it—and how great the inexperience is with which you say this, if I wished to show it, I would dwell, like you, on matters not necessary. Take away the superfluous things from the midst—things which, even if they were true, would profit you not at all—and say, if you can, how you do not deny Christ the physician to the little ones; for when you begin to say it, then it will appear why you thought these empty things, which do not pertain to the matter at issue, should nonetheless be inserted.

**III. 146.** *Julian.* For see, I answer briefly that we so far from denying the medicine of Christ to the little ones, whom we know to be innocent, that we confess that they above all have need of it in greater abundance. For they are born tiny and weak, who not only cannot be fed by their own labor, but cannot even implore the help of their parents—who are subject to so many mishaps that even thicker milk, and very often the sleep of nursing mothers, is fatal to them.

*Augustine.* See why you drown out the reader's memory by inserting superfluous matters. You do not deny that Christ the physician is necessary to the little ones on account of the evils of the body, by whose weakness the feeble are harmed or killed, and you deny that Christ the physician is necessary to them on account of the fact that they are under the power of the devil, from which they are exsufflated to be baptized so that they may be set free; so that you may not be understood to have set out this objection to yourself as though to be refuted for any other reason than that, lest you be caught having feared it, and to have not attempted to refute it precisely because your darts, against a most firm and as it were adamantine foundation, would rebound back upon yourself, to your own ruin—and that for this reason you inserted a superfluous disputation about antitheses, so that the reader, forgetting that unconquered point, might let you, in whatever most empty things you brought in, catch your breath as though answering something while saying nothing. For you brought in the weakness of the infant body, subject to many mishaps, as though they were tortured by it, or were oppressed by its great infirmity, or as though any evil could befall them at that age, if human nature remained as it had been established. Show, if you can, why the Church of Christ exsufflates the little ones to be baptized, or contend, if you can, that they ought not be exsufflated, or be silent, if you cannot; rather, be silent, because you cannot.

**III. 147.** *Julian.* By the condition of their mortal body they incur both the hardships of illnesses and the pains of sufferings and the dangers of diseases. We confess, then, that the medicine of Christ, by whom they were also made, is necessary not only to the little ones, but to the nature of all mortals.

*Augustine.* Therefore you believe that all these evils would have existed even in paradise, if no one had sinned, and you imagine that human beings would have died there like cattle, because you believe that a mortality of bodies common to all would have been there. O wretched men—if only you thought of the blessedness of that place with a Christian feeling, and did not believe that even the beasts would have died there, just as they would not have raged, but would have been subject to human beings with a wondrous gentleness, and would not have sought their food from one another's deaths, but, as it is written, would have taken food in common with human beings[^158]! Or if extreme old age dissolved these, so that only human nature there possessed eternal life, why should we not believe that they would be taken away from paradise to die, or would go forth from there at the perception of imminent death, lest death should befall anyone living in that place of life? For not even those very human beings who had sinned could have died, had they not gone forth, by the merit of their iniquity, from the dwelling of such great happiness.

**III. 148.** *Julian.* Whose infirmities, just as in the present life they are softened by various kinds of remedies, so too will be fully emptied out—yet only in the bodies of the righteous—at the coming of the resurrection.

*Augustine.* Among these righteous do you place also the little ones, who have done nothing by their own will, either good or evil? Or do you separate them from the righteous, whom nonetheless you do not separate from the happy resurrection of the flesh? Why then did you make an exception of the bodies of the righteous, in which these evils are emptied out at the coming of the resurrection? But if the little ones are counted righteous on account of the righteousness of the second man, who is the author of regeneration, why are they not counted unrighteous on account of the sin of the first man, who is the author of generation?

**III. 149.** *Julian.* See, then, in how many ways we confess that the medicine of Christ is necessary to human nature. But I know you will cry out that you are being dealt with by a quibble; for it was not this medicine, by which bodies are aided, but the grace of Christ that you signified by the name of medicine, which you affirmed is denied by us. To this it can be answered that this is to be ascribed to you, who were unwilling to mark by their proper names the thing you wanted understood. But because it has been understood, even if belatedly, what medicine you were speaking of, which you falsely claimed we deny in a confused way, let us answer this too which you brought in, in the second place: just as in the first work we protested that we confess the grace of Christ, that is, baptism, from which Christ instituted his rite, to be so necessary to all ages in common, that whoever denies it to be useful even to the little ones we strike with an eternal anathema.

*Augustine.* We are dealing with medicine, by which name Christ wished his grace to be called, when he said of himself, *Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^159]. But you say that the grace of Christ is necessary to the little ones not on account of their being healed, but only on account of their being adopted into the kingdom of God; do not, then, make yourself out to be answering, where you see that you cannot answer.

**III. 150.** *Julian.* And since we drive this home with a clear profession, you are convicted of public falsehood, you who write that human nature is so defended as good by us that we deny that the medicine of Christ is necessary for the infants, who are healthy.

*Augustine.* I spoke the truth; for you without doubt deny to the little ones the medicine of Christian grace—which is not conferred except on Christians, and not on all human beings, even unbelievers, and moreover not on puppies and piglets, little fish and little worms, and every kind of living things whatever—those little ones whom you contend were generated without any guilt of origin, which is healed by regeneration. Hence you just now, having suffered great straits, withdrew the name of medicine and substituted that of grace, because you can say that grace is necessary to the little ones on account of adoption, but you cannot say that medicine is necessary, through the sacraments of Christ, to those to whom you dare to promise eternal salvation even if they do not become Christians. For you do not want Christ to be Jesus to them—and he is called this, on the testimony of the angel, on the testimony of the Gospel, because *he saves his people*[^160] not from the diseases and wounds of the flesh, from which he heals any human beings whatever and any winged creatures and creeping things, but from their sins.

**III. 151.** *Julian.* Yet this grace, since it is also called medicine, makes—the law of righteousness being preserved—others good out of evil, but it makes the little ones, whom it creates good by founding, better by renewing and adopting them.

*Augustine.* Therefore what Jesus himself says, *Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick*[^161]—as far as it pertains to the medicine that is offered by Christ to Christians alone—is false in the little ones, whom you say to be both healthy and to need the medicine of Christ, so that you may avoid the hatefulness. But how does Christ renew those whom he finds new, most recent from their birth, if they draw nothing from the oldness of sin? Or will you say that even something not old is renewed, when you read in Hebrews, *When he says "new," he has made the first old*[^162]? Say, then, whence the newly born are old, whom you say are healthy from the oldness of sin; and yet, to avoid the indignation of true Christians, you pretend that they are renewed by Christ. Finally, it is one thing to be healed, another to be renewed; for healing, a cure is necessary; for renewal, a restoration. It is manifest, then, that your heresy utterly denies Christian medicine to the little ones.

**III. 152.** *Julian.* See, then, it is plain that we do not deny that the grace of Christ is useful to the little ones. What controversy, then, remains, on account of which the Traducianist charges us with error—namely, because we do not grant that the nature of Adam was made good, but the nature of all human beings made evil? This indeed I profess not only not to grant, but even to attack with all my might. Therefore, with those nets of your fictions—childishly distorted—and those vulgar madnesses removed, by which you whispered that we deny the grace of Christ to cradles, let us join battle on this, in which the sum of the contest lies. In the order, then, that I promised, since our own position has now been defended, let us weigh both the views of the Manichean, whom you pretended to oppose, and your own.

*Augustine.* You have not defended your views, but have shown that they cannot be defended.

**III. 153.** *Julian.* He, then, says that man was created by the prince of darkness, that is, the author of evil, out of the mingling of two natures, good and evil. What do you say? That man is created by the good God indeed, but that all human beings are created evil.

*Augustine.* In a human being who is born there is both the nature, which you do not deny is good, for which we praise God the creator, and the defect, which you do not deny is evil—if at least, under pressure, you confess that Christ the physician is necessary to the little ones, since you cannot deny that Christ said of himself, *the healthy have no need of a physician*[^163].

**III. 154.** *Julian.* And so between you and the Manichean there is no disagreement about the quality of the nature, but only about its author; for you reckon this evil to God, whom you confess to be the creator of the little ones, which the Manichean reckoned to the prince of darkness, whom he supposed to be the founder of human nature. To restore the pact, then, no great obstacle remained for you. Yet I will soon teach that, although in each of you no traces of the truth are to be seen, his statements nonetheless agree with one another more than yours do. What, then, do we say? Without doubt, what is at odds with each of you: namely, that nature was neither made evil by the good God nor made or mingled otherwise by the prince of darkness, but that one God, the author of all things, not only made the nature of human beings good in the first man, but also makes it in each one who is born; to which nature, nonetheless, we profess that the help of its creator is, as useful in many ways, so also necessary. Although the account of the conditions is one thing, of the gifts another; nor is the maker here esteemed more highly than the work. Both of you, then, you and the Manichean, equally affirm a natural evil, that is, both of you alike say that the nature of human beings is evil—but he more faithfully, you more fraudulently. For from this evil, which you both judge to be breathed by the devil into the human inmost parts alike, he withdraws the origin of no one, whereas you, in order to seem to differ in something which is nothing, try to except from it two men alone, whose persons you nonetheless do not free from sin, but say that by the advance of a more educated mind there was not in them the natural thing which, nonetheless, you persuade us was made natural through them. Thus, lest fraud should remain unpunished even here, the theft you had committed against your master was chastised by the addition of folly; for to believe natural what you confess was taken on from the will is the invention not, I say, of an unlearned, but of a drunken mind. But of this elsewhere; now, for the moment, let us press the matter. The Manichean, then, affirms that evil is natural; you assent. He says that sins are born; you agree that it is so. He says that the nature of human beings is evil; this too you confirm. He says, of absolutely all; here you resist and ask that that first pairing of two men be set apart—though indeed you do not vindicate them from guilt, but rather affirm them to be the authors of the natural evil. Even if we could grant you this, your master will not allow it, but perhaps will even punish your slow wit with the rod, so that it is necessary for you either to acquiesce in his authority or to desert his schools altogether. But finally he gathers and says that the good cannot be the author of an evil nature, and that therefore man, whom you both confess to be naturally evil, is the work of the prince of darkness, that is, of the devil.

*Augustine.* That human nature, founded good by the good God, was so corrupted by the great sin of disobedience that even its posterity drew thence the deserving of death and punishment—to which posterity, nonetheless, the good God does not deny his good handiwork—this the Catholic faith says, both against you and against the Manicheans. But you, who deny this, I beg you, consider paradise for a little while. Does it please you that we set there chaste men and women struggling against disordered desire, pregnant women nauseating, loathing food, growing pale, some pouring forth premature births in miscarriage, others groaning and howling in childbirth, and all the children themselves weeping, laughing late, speaking later and that with a stammer; then being led to schools to learn their letters, weeping under thongs, rods, and switches, with a variety of punishments distributed according to the variety of their abilities; and on top of this, innumerable diseases and assaults of demons and bites of beasts, by which some would be tortured, some even consumed; while those who were healthy would be reared, amid uncertain mishaps, with the wretched anxiety of their parents; there would be there too bereavements and griefs and the longings, with sorrows of heart, for dearest ones lost? It is long to pursue all the evils with which this life abounds—and yet these are no sins at all. If, then, these things were going to be in paradise, with no sin preceding by whose merit they would arise, look for those, not faithful at all but mockers, to whom you may preach them. Surely, if such a paradise were painted, no one would say that it was paradise—not even if he had read this name inscribed above it; nor would he say the painter had erred, but he would plainly recognize a mocker. Nonetheless, of those who know you, no one would be surprised if your name were added to the title and it were written: The Paradise of the Pelagians. But if you blush at this (for truly there must be thought to remain in you no vestige of shame, if you do not blush at this), then change, I beg you, at last your perverse opinion and believe that human nature was changed into these miseries by that great sin, and that these things could in no way have been in paradise—and that for this reason those went forth from there whose very offspring deserved to bear such things, as the contagion of sin, together with the condition of punishment, passed into all. This Catholic teaching both defends the righteousness of God, since not without merit would he have willed the life of mortals to be penal, and overthrows both you and the Manicheans: you indeed, because by such evils you attribute a horrible unhappiness to paradise, but the Manicheans, because by such evils they assert the nature of their God—and through this, what else than their God himself—to be unhappy. Accordingly, it ought not move me that you set against me a Manichean master, to chastise my slow wit with the rod; but I beseech you, let it move you that, according to the unspeakable and dreadful monstrosity of your error, you would be educated with the rod even if you were born among the peoples of paradise. If you shudder at this most deformed absurdity together with us, as you ought, whence comes, I ask you, even this misery of children—which without doubt is not from a nature of evil, of which the Manicheans rave—unless because by that great sin, inestimable also to us, human nature was so corrupted and entangled in most just punishments, that from it arose not only the corruptibility of bodies subject to so many wretched mishaps, but also a slowness of abilities liable to rods and other beatings; and so this malignant world runs, through evil days, even to its end, in such a way that even the saints, rescued from eternal punishment through divine indulgence and having received the pledge of incorruptible salvation, are nonetheless commanded to bear the punishments of this life, using them well, with the reward of patience, rather than to deserve to be free of them even after the remission of sins?

**III. 155.** *Julian.* But against this last point you rise up entirely, and, when you have boarded the same ship with the Manichean, and the same spirit has directed your course—the crossing made—you have now thought, in mid-sea, that you must nonetheless go ashore on another coast. But perhaps, through choice of nearby advantage, even a slow lingering may become more bearable. Into what harbor, then, do you steer your ship? *I say*—you say—*that God is indeed good, but that the same is the founder of the evil*. O flight from danger driven onto the rocks—to have wished to pour the whole bilge of the Manichean over him whom you supposed to be God!

*Augustine.* Do you really deny that even in evil human beings there is a good nature, both of mind and of body? Of this good God is the founder; and the Manichean calls it evil, and to this good which he calls evil he assigns an evil author. For he does not spare even the soul; but just as he says there is a certain soul of the flesh that is his own, so he says there is an evil nature coeternal with the good God, so that it cannot be good at all; but the other soul, the good one in the same man, was not made by God but is the substance and nature of God, thrust into the miseries of this mingling by no iniquity of its own, but by an evil necessity of God. But this whole handiwork, which is man, he says is both evil and belongs to an evil author. Do you see how different are his views, and how, on account of this, he is most insanely and wickedly out of his senses? But you, who therefore think that the evil cannot be born, because the good God creates them, contend, if you can, that bodies cannot be born defective either, because God whole and entire creates them as well; contend, finally, that just as the evil cannot be born, because he who is good creates them, so neither can men slow of ability or foolish be born, because he who is wise creates them. Or is foolishness not an evil, when Scripture says that the foolish man is incomparably more to be lamented than the dead[^164]? As, then, you do not, with us, call God the author of foolishness, though you confess that foolish men are born with God creating them, so we do not call God the author of malice, and yet we can rightly say that, by the obligation of original sin, evil human beings are born only with him creating them—because none but he creates human beings.

**III. 156.** *Julian.* It is therefore absolutely clear that the opinion of that man has more of consistency: that if anything evil were founded naturally, it would point to an author like itself.

*Augustine.* Who would hold such views but the Pelagian heretics? Therefore, since according to you mortal man is founded not penally but naturally, he points to an author like himself, mortal; and—so that your foolishness may at least blush before the foolish—since a foolish man is founded naturally, he points to an author like himself, foolish.

**III. 157.** *Julian.* But truth prescribes, first, that it cannot be called evil, that is, sin, which is such because its nature has compelled it; and that sin is nothing other than a free will deviating from the path of righteousness.

*Augustine.* But this original sin too draws its origin from the will of the one who sinned, and so there is no sin except by the will.

**III. 158.** *Julian.* By which defenses the nature of all things is proved innocent, which, remaining as it was founded, is proved liable to no crime.

*Augustine.* But it did not remain as it was founded. Therefore it is proved liable to a crime, and it made its own stock liable to its own crime as though by inheritance; which nonetheless, insofar as it is founded by God, is itself also good.

**III. 159.** *Julian.* Evil, therefore, cannot be natural, and through this no creature is guilty naturally, nor is the author evil. For evil is found not of an existing nature, but, just as every creature, insofar as it is founded, is good, so too God, the author of good natures, stained by no crime of his work, is proved good through all things. Therefore the whole of whatever the Manichean had brought forward has been overthrown by this single reasoning of the Catholics.

*Augustine.* This you say truly. Absolutely *every creature, insofar as it is founded, is good*; and therefore, as we say this, so we both say also what follows, that *God, the author of good natures, stained by no crime of his work, is proved good through all things*. For this whole thing is bound up there, that every creature, insofar as it is founded, is good. And through this, man too, insofar as he is founded, is good; but insofar as he is generated from a corrupted origin, he is not good, and therefore he must be regenerated.

**III. 160.** *Julian.* But nonetheless that man, who perished by the thunderbolt of so manifest a truth, seems in some part to breathe, when he is compared with you; for in him the structure of his whole teaching collapses when only that single foundation totters, whereas in you three things likewise totter, as one in him. And through this, see whether anything of your building can rise up. Mani, then, supposing that to be natural which can only be voluntary—namely, sin—laid his foundations on emptiness; the rest he placed consequently beside it, saying that, since sin is natural, the nature is evil, and the author of an evil thing is not good, and through this the whole human race must be reckoned to the prince of darkness. All of which could certainly have stood, had not truth dug away that first point of his, namely, that sin, the work of free will, cannot be taught to be natural, and that whatever was natural cannot be sin.

*Augustine.* A similar reasoning can be woven against you; for since you are not so foolish as to deny that foolish abilities—that is, foolish men—are born, hear, then, how much by your folly you aid the madness of the Manichean. For one taught by you himself says that, since foolishness is natural, the same nature is foolish—just as you said, *Since sin is natural, the nature is evil*; then he adds that the author of a foolish thing is not wise, just as you said, *But the author of an evil thing is not good*; finally he concludes: and through this the race of foolish men must be reckoned to the prince of darkness—for so you too concluded, saying, *And through this the whole human race must be reckoned to the prince of darkness*. See, the Manichean has conquered you with your own mouth, has cut your throat with your own sword. What will he do? For nothing can aid you against him without pressing and stabbing you still more—namely, what you add and say: *All this could certainly have stood, had not truth dug away that first point of his, namely, that sin, the work of free will, cannot be taught to be natural, and that whatever was natural cannot be sin*. How does this aid you, and how does it not rather choke you more and more? For can you say that foolishness cannot be natural? It stands against you, then, what the Manichean placed first in his similar reasoning—namely, that foolishness is natural; to which, as a foundation, you thought the rest of his points were consequently built up to the very summit, where he says that such a race of men must be reckoned to the prince of darkness. But we overturn that first foundation of his, we who say that foolishness is natural for this reason, that men are born foolish from an incidental defect, from which an origin of this kind would be deservedly drawn—not because human nature was depraved when first established, as the Manichean raves. And through this also, what he consequently adds, that the same nature is foolish, we concede to be true on account of the defect with which a foolish man is born, not on account of what is in him the handiwork of the good founder; for he was born foolish by an incidental defect, but man was created with God operating. Then, what he adds, that the author of a foolish thing is not wise—if the thing itself, man, is understood—we say is not consequent; for we say that God is the author of that man who was born foolish, although we do not say he is the author of foolishness itself. This foolishness is not a nature and substance, which is not born except with God creating, but a defect of that same nature, which befell with God permitting; and that God permits these things by a just judgment we certainly do not doubt. Thus we destroy both the Manicheans, the evil builders of their own ruin, and the Pelagians, the foolish helpers of the Manicheans.

**III. 161.** *Julian.* By the overthrow, then, of his first definition, the wedge of reason poured his summits to the ground. What, then, do you hope for yourself, whose three points are as much on the slippery slope as his one? For you say, first, that sin is natural; second, that God is good, who founds, forms, and extends the evil, that is, sins; and third, that a matter of the will has been mingled into the seeds. These three heads of your definitions, then, each totter by themselves—they totter, I say; nay, like ropes they slip apart from sand before they can come together. The one, then—that is, natural sin—already lies destroyed straightway in the Manichean; but the other two, which are proper to your opinion, fall together with the Manichean, who likewise perishes, but in their accustomed manner. For if he could not vindicate human beings born guilty, neither through an evil nature nor through the prince of darkness, how much more cannot you fools, who, to display the crimes of those who are born, have joined to it an accusation of God? And through this, just as sin cannot be a nature—by which thunderbolt the teaching of the Manichean burst apart—so it recurs that what is sin cannot be natural; for a matter of the will does not pass over into the condition of a substance, which you think has happened. But much more does it stand firm that the good God never creates the evil; and so it appeared that there cannot be sin in one who is born, whom God creates.

*Augustine.* It stands firm that the good God does not create the evil, in just the way that it stands firm that the wise God does not create the foolish. For if you say, "The wise creates the foolish," it will be answered you: "Why, then, does not the good also create the evil?" Hence, having become intent, when you ask whence the foolish are born—whom nonetheless God creates as men—there perhaps you will find the defect of origin, you who do not wish to confess original sin. Or are you prepared to say that, with no one's sin preceding, even foolish men could have been born in that happiness of paradise—men who could be educated not, I say, by rods, but not even by cudgels? But if you do not say this, lest this absurdity transcend all foolishness, say by what merit the image of God is born with such great deformity of mind, that by no strength of age, no length of time, no labor of studies, no diligence of teachers, no punishments of beatings, he can attain—I do not say to wisdom, but to any useful learning whatever—you who do not wish to believe that the just God, therefore, dismissed from paradise, that is, from the place of happiness, human nature corrupted and condemned, lest any death befall there—that is, neither the temporal death of the body nor the eternal death of the whole man—nor those evils, so many and so great, of abilities and bodies which we discern in the human race, which had to be born from a depraved and punished root and a lost mass, should be born in that region of happiness, but rather in these lands of the misery of mortals, which was most justly inflicted on those assigned to it, with guilt pursuing those who are born and toilsome hardship not departing from those who are reborn, even to the death of the body.

**III. 162.** *Julian.* The matter is clear and already sufficiently treated in the first work; but since you wished to be so deceitful as to strive to make some distinction between yourself and the Manichean, I am now obliged to press the case, so that it may be clear that not only your past statements but also anything you may henceforth attempt to bring in have been countered. We say, then, that the work of God in one who is born is so good that the things which are natural to his substance need no corrector; since whoever judges that what he confesses was made by God ought to have been made otherwise, without doubt censures the one whom he confesses to be the craftsman of a creature to be corrected into another form.

*Augustine.* Be silent, I beg you; you do not know what you are saying. Some are born with closed mouths, and these are opened by physicians. There was among us a certain Acatius, born in an honorable place among his own people; he used to say that he was born with closed eyes, but, because they were healthy within, though they were not open with the eyelids stuck together, the physician wished to open them with a knife, and his devout mother did not permit this, but accomplished it by applying a poultice from the Eucharist, when the boy was already nearly five years old or more—whence he used to relate that he remembered this well enough. I pass over that man blind from birth in the Gospel, to whom the craftsman himself restored the eyes, which, for the sake of commending his marvels, he had made less than whole; for there the cause why he was born blind is not passed over in silence: namely, not on account of his own sin or his parents', but that the works of God might be manifested in him[^165]. Yet ask the physicians, and let them tell you to how many people, where they can, they bring help, lest defects inborn in their bodies should remain or even kill those who are born; for some are born—just as others with their mouths, so these with their lower passages closed—which defects, if they remain, do not let them live. For when help is brought to such people by medical skill, the works of God are not corrected as faulty. For what true worshiper of God does not know that such people had to be born in the way they were born? But this too pertains to the hardships of the human race, in which these evil days are passed, full, by the just judgment of God, of labors, sorrows, fears, and dangers; all of which evils—far be it that they should have existed in that happiness of paradise—and through this they have not sprouted except from the root of sin. What of this? The very abilities, if they are left as they were born and not cultivated by skillful teaching, with great labor of teachers and learners—is it not plain what they will remain? But you, fill your paradise with human beings born defective in both body and mind, so that, with your eyes unhappily closed and your mouth shamelessly open, you may deny original sin.

**III. 163.** *Julian.* And so, with probability and that moderation of praise which is owed to natures preserved even in any who are born, we say that the increases of divine benefits are useful and necessary to all ages in common, yet in such a way that neither virtue nor sin is ascribed to anyone without his own will.

*Augustine.* Virtue is not ascribed to anyone without his own will, but *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^166], as that of the king for which Esther prayed[^167].

**III. 164.** *Julian.* Although in this too the more abundant clemency of God is proved, since he sanctifies the little ones who do not know it; for it pertains to the praise of his mercy, since guilt does not stain them while they do not know it; this indeed belongs to the laws of equity.

*Augustine.* If no guilt stains them, why are not all sanctified? And why are all who are sanctified exsufflated?

**III. 165.** *Julian.* But I am speaking of those virtues which we acquire by the use of reason now perfected. And through this, because it was necessary that this too be driven home here, as so often, I admonish the reader to be attentive to the things we bring in; for he will see that the Traducianists differ in nothing from the Manicheans, so that they object to us nothing in the way of arguments other than what is contained in their books. I did this indeed in the fourth book of my first work too.

*Augustine.* And I answered you in my sixth book.

**III. 166.** *Julian.* But because, after the publication of those books, by your prayer, most blessed father Florus, a letter of the Manichean was found at Constantinople and sent to these parts, it is worthwhile to insert some of it, so that all may understand whence these arguments for traduction descend.

*Augustine.* How do you say that the letter was found and sent by someone's prayer, if God does not work the wills in the hearts of human beings? Surely the man who found it sought the letter by his will, or by his will was seeking something in the place where he could find it, or, when human beings were speaking by their will about such matters, he indicated by his will to the one with whom it had been that he had it, which he could show and hand over to one willing, which the other too, willing, would direct to these parts; and in whatever other way it may have been, it was absolutely by the will of a human being or human beings that that letter was found and directed. And yet you say this was done with a human being praying. Why, then, do you not confess that, without any command sounding from without, God, by a hidden prompting, prepares and rouses the wills of human beings most effectively to fulfill what he has willed—you who do not defend free choice to be understood, but exalt it to be precipitated?

**III. 167.** *Julian.* And although I have already disputed, in that first little work, concerning the concupiscence or pleasure of the flesh, which is also called disordered desire, which has been implanted in the sexes for the sake of manly propagation—disputed to the point of teaching that it is nothing other than one of the instruments of the body, implanted alike in rational and irrational creatures, pertaining to the work of God, who affected our flesh in its senses—nonetheless, because Augustine presses upon it vehemently and says that it is evil, that is, natural sin and the parent of all sins, which he tries to prove above all by its shame, and mocks me because I blush to name it outright—

*Augustine.* I say that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit and which is also called disordered desire, is evil—now in this flesh to be reined in and to be lessened by good habit, but in eternal life to be healed in every part—not, as the Manicheans rave, to be separated as though some evil substance had been added or mingled into us; but, whatever you may think about it, that it is such as it now is, so that it solicits to the perpetration of unlawful things even the hearts of the chaste, whether married or continent, and, unless it is resisted by a stronger will, overturns them—such a thing I would not believe could be set up in paradise, had I not found it in these books of yours, most full of empty and insane loquacity.

**III. 168.** *Julian.* Therefore I too will now also briefly vindicate, by exposing it, the quality of the thing which I defended from the mouth of the Manichean by distinguishing it in my earlier writings—against the accusers of it, who nonetheless profess that they serve it against their will.

*Augustine.* You can fittingly serve the praised disordered desire, but we, with the help of the Lord, attack and conquer the accused one.

**III. 169.** *Julian.* What, then, is retained in your books, old man Augustine: *Disordered desire arose after sin, and in confusion they were compelled to veil it*.

*Augustine.* What, then, is retained in your books, young man Julian, makes it appear that you can find nothing to say against my books, and that you seek out slanders to heap upon us loquaciously and emptily.

**III. 170.** *Julian.* And again: *Whoever lies lawfully uses an evil well by the shameful disordered desire; but whoever does so unlawfully uses an evil badly; for it more rightly takes the name of an evil than of a good, at which both the evil and the good blush, and we more rightly believe the Apostle, who says, "For good does not dwell in my flesh"*[^168]; and elsewhere: *The disordered desire of marriage is not a good, but the obscenity of those who sin, the necessity of those who generate, the burning of wantonness, the shame of marriage*; and that: *What they afterward did by propagation, this is the good of marriage; but what they first covered in confusion, this is the evil of concupiscence, which everywhere avoids the gaze and seeks shameful secrecy*—or other things you are accustomed to use for this argument, brought forth more by your memory than by your wit. The Manichean, then, was hard pressed, so that he composed what he thought acute, but you were frustrated, you who thought you could hide what you had read and held.

*Augustine.* For who is there who has even slightly come to know the teaching of the Manicheans, and to whom it is hidden that the Manicheans say the concupiscence of the flesh is evil? But this is not proper to their teaching; for what else does even he say who says, *The flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, and the spirit opposed to the flesh; for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the things you want*[^169]? What else does even he say who says, *Whoever loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him, because all that is in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh and the concupiscence of the eyes and the ambition of this age, which is not from the Father, but is from the world*[^170]? Therefore that the concupiscence of the flesh is evil the Manicheans do not say by their own teaching—which those who are not blind see that the Apostles too say—but it is your error that aids the proper and venomous opinion of the Manicheans, you who deny that the concupiscence of the flesh, which, like it or not, solicits to the perpetration of unlawful things and to which chastity is opposed, befell nature from sin, the nature which God founded good. And so you act in such a way that the Manicheans conclude that what they convict—by the struggle of the chaste and the testimony of the Apostles—to be an evil from the race of darkness and from an evil substance coeternal with God, is not an evil quality to be healed, but an evil substance to be separated, and that it did not befall a good nature, but was mingled into a good nature. But go on and contrive slanders against us out of the plague of the Manicheans, whom you so aid as to make them unconquered—unless both you with them are conquered by us, by that Catholic truth which is truly unconquered.

**III. 171.** *Julian.* Hear, then, now, and learn what your parent writes to a certain daughter of his, your sister.

*Augustine.* These are insults, and not even witty ones, but empty.

**III. 172.** *Julian.* *Mani, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to his daughter Menoch. May grace and salvation be granted you from our God, who is truly the true God, and may he enlighten your mind and reveal his righteousness to you, since you are a fruit of the divine stock.* And after a little: *Through whom you too*—he says—*have been made splendid by recognizing what you formerly were, from what kind of souls you flowed, which is confounded with all bodies and clings to various flavors and appearances. For just as souls are begotten from souls, so the fashioning of the body is digested from the nature of the body. "What, therefore, is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the spirit is spirit"*[^171]; *but understand the spirit as the soul: soul from soul, flesh from flesh*.

*Augustine.* If I should tell you that I am entirely ignorant of this letter of the Manichean—though I would be telling the truth—you would not believe me at all and would contend with me, as you are accustomed, with empty loquacity; but if the Manichean said this, what wonder is it that he destroyed himself? For if the soul of a man is born just as the flesh of a man, whether evil or good—for they rave that there are two souls at once in one man, one evil, the other good, flowing from their different principles—if, then, the soul is born when the flesh is born, surely that evil one is not coeternal with God, nor was the good one, against the princes of darkness, brought forth from the eternal Father, as his sect raves. But in whatever way the Manichean says souls are born, what does it pertain to us, who know and hold that those words of the Lord, *What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the spirit is spirit*[^172], come about not when a man is born of a man, but when he is reborn of the Spirit of God? For the whole Gospel passage itself allows no other understanding. Seek there, then, those to whom you can sell these fables of the Manichean, and rather, on account of those things I said above, show how you are not a helper of the Manicheans.

**III. 173.** *Julian.* You recognize, surely, how most expressly the Manichean confirms the traduction of souls, and by what testimony he uses for the disparagement of the flesh—namely, that which is bandied about in your mouth, that is: *What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the spirit is spirit*[^173].

*Augustine.* I have already said how we take these words of the Gospel, since they teach not generation but regeneration. You say, if you can, how you do not aid the sacrilegious words of the Manichean about the concupiscence of the flesh, which for this reason you deny descends into our nature by propagation from the nature of the first man corrupted by his transgression: namely, so that he may be supposed most rightly to reckon it to the race of darkness coeternal with God. For most foolishly and most shamelessly you deny the evil by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, and against which the inner wars of the chaste are waged.

**III. 174.** *Julian.* Therefore not only by stating it but also by repeating it he drives home that it is proper to his teaching to suppose a traduction of souls, which he tries to prove also by the likeness of generating bodies. *Just as souls*—he says—*are begotten from souls, so the fashioning of the body is digested from the nature of the body, and just as flesh from flesh, so soul from soul*. But let us proceed to the rest: *Just as, then, God is the author of souls, so the devil is the author of bodies through concupiscence, so that in the lime-twig of the devil, through the concupiscence of the woman, the devil hunts not souls but bodies*.

*Augustine.* The Manichean could not say that the devil hunts bodies but souls; for he says that bodies pertain to the nature of the race of darkness, from which the devil too comes. Accordingly, according to the Manichean, the devil is said to hunt not bodies, which are his own, but the good souls, which are not his own; but our faith knows the good God to be the creator both of souls and of bodies.

**III. 175.** *Julian.* *Whether through sight or through touch or through hearing or through smell or through taste. Take away, then, the root of this malignant stock, and at once you contemplate yourself as spiritual. "For the root"—Scripture says—"of all evils is concupiscence"*[^174]. You see by what spirit and on account of what teaching the Manichean attacks the concupiscence of the flesh, calling it the law of sin, which, if it were taken away from bodies, his daughter, to whom he writes, would see herself made spiritual. By what sentences of the Apostle he strives to confirm this opinion, let us hear: *"For the flesh is opposed to the spirit," because it is the daughter of concupiscence, "and the spirit to the flesh"*[^175], *because it is the son of the soul*.

*Augustine.* The Manichean supposes two substances in the words of the Apostle, one good, the other evil—not one good substance and another a defect of a good substance, contracted by generation from the sin of the first man, to be healed by regeneration through the righteousness of the second man—which, both against the Manicheans and against you, the Catholic faith hurls as a most invincible weapon and lays both of you low.

**III. 176.** *Julian.* You understand that the marrow of the Manichean's teaching, with which your faith grows together, has been laid bare. And now he proceeds to accuse us, that is, the Catholics: *Therefore see how foolish are those who say that this fashioning was founded by the good God, which they are sure is begotten by the spirit of concupiscence*.

*Augustine.* In this we are accused by the Manicheans, both we and you alike, since we both say that the fashioning of the flesh was founded by the good God, but the Manicheans say that the spirit of concupiscence is an evil substance, not a defect of a good substance, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit—which we say in order to refute them and which you deny in order to aid them. For because they convict, even with you resisting, that the concupiscence by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit is an evil, then, if it is not, as you suppose, a defect of a good substance, it will be reckoned an evil substance; which is the teaching of the Manicheans, hostile to the Catholic faith with you as their helpers.

**III. 177.** *Julian.* *They come together with the mind unwilling and act in secret shames, at which time they hate the light, that their works may not be made manifest; for which reason the Apostle says: "It is not of one who wills"*[^176], *so that it may be understood: this work. For whether we do good*, *it is not of the flesh, because "the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornication"*[^177] *and the rest; or whether we do evil, it is not of the soul, because "the fruit of the spirit is peace, joy"*[^178]. *Finally the Apostle cries out also to the Romans: "I do not the good that I will, but I work the evil that I abhor"*[^179]. *You see the voice of the rebellious soul defending the liberty of the soul against concupiscence. For it grieved that sin, that is, the devil, was working in it all concupiscence. The authority of the law indicates its evil, when it censures all its uses, which the flesh admires and praises; for all the bitterness of concupiscence is sweet to the soul, by which the soul is nourished and roused to vigor. Finally, the mind that restrains itself from all use of concupiscence keeps vigil, grows rich, and increases*, *but by the use of concupiscence it is accustomed to decrease*. Do you understand that we have caught you out at last as to whence you not only get your wisdom but even your words? For you embrace your master with so great a love that you stand not only in his paths but even in his footprints—as you assert in all your writings, just as both those books which you published addressed to the name of Marcellinus and these which you have destined for Valerius bear witness: namely, that this concupiscence of the flesh, which you also call shameful, was mingled by the devil into human bodies.

*Augustine.* For with what shamelessness do you deny it shameful, against which—seeing that you gladly praise it—I wonder whether you fight faithfully? But when you used to lie with your wife, although, fearing nothing, you panted for the use of the pleasure granted you, you nonetheless, blushing, sought secrecy. Accordingly this thing you have taken up to defend, if the happiness of human beings in paradise had remained, would either be none at all there, or would exert no movements of its own against the bidding of the will, against which it would be necessary to resist, so that an honor fitting to that happiness might be rendered to it. But since you have advanced so far in its praises that you contend that disordered desire, such as we now perceive it to be, was going to be such in paradise, soliciting the hearts of the chaste who resist it, who does not see that it is not by wisdom, not by eloquence, but by shamelessness that you resist me, because by a perverse modesty you are prevented from confessing yourself defeated? We, then, say that the concupiscence of the flesh is neither a substance of an evil nature, which the Manicheans say, nor is its every use condemned by us as by the Manicheans; nor, on the other hand, do we call it good, which the Pelagians say, nor is its movement against the spirit praised by us as by the Pelagians; but we say that it is a defect of a good substance, which was turned into our nature through the transgression of the first man—which the Catholic Christians say; and the lawful and honorable use of this evil, for the sake of procreating children, is approved by us as by the Catholic Christians. Thus we overcome and avoid both the Manicheans and the Pelagians, whose errors are diverse from each other in such a way that the one who seems lesser is shown to be the helper of the one who seems greater. For what evidently enough appears to be evil, while the Pelagians deny it to be a defect of a good substance, they aid the Manicheans, who say that this defect is an evil substance coeternal with the substance of the good God.

**III. 178.** *Julian.* That this is the daughter of sin and the mother of other sins, and that the Apostle Paul complains of it, when he says, *I know that good does not dwell in my flesh*[^180]; and: *I do not the good I will, but I do the evil I hate*[^181]—which has always been so expounded by the Catholics that it referred not to the disgrace of nature, but to the odium of habit.

*Augustine.* So you are a Catholic, and Ambrose is not a Catholic? Human beings, whomever you have been able to deceive, have not so far vanished away by the contagion of your vanity that they would dare to take this into their minds. Accordingly, attend a little. The discord of flesh and spirit, of which it is written, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh; for to will is present to me, but to accomplish the good is not*[^182], and the rest of this kind, and that much more manifest one, *The flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, and the spirit opposed to the flesh; for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the things you want*[^183]—that this is in human beings neither we doubt, nor you, nor the Manicheans; but whence this discord is in one human being, thence comes the disagreement, and three opinions are put forth in this disagreement: one ours, the second yours, the third the Manicheans'. But lest we seem to say arrogantly or deceitfully that ours is the Catholic one, let him put it forth whose faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures even an enemy did not dare to censure—as your own Pelagius proclaims of him. Ambrose, then, says that this discord of flesh and spirit befell our nature through the transgression of the first man; but you say it is from the force of habit; the Manicheans say it is from the mingling of two coeternal natures, namely, of good and of evil. I could already say, let him who wishes to remain a Catholic choose which of these three he wishes; for I need not fear that anyone fleeing the Manicheans would prefer you to Ambrose. But your opinion, by which you say that this evil grows up by the force of habit, he judges most empty who sees that no one is born without this evil. For as soon as anyone begins to use reason, if he desires chastity, he now perceives the concupiscence of the flesh—which had been lulled to sleep by age—as if waking and resisting; and either, conquered, he is dragged off, or, lest he slip into consenting to it, he, if he is devout, fights against it with the Lord's help. But if you are unwilling to accept this, why do you rage against me? Pit the Manicheans and Ambrose against each other, and in the spectacle of this contest choose, if you are Catholics, which side you favor. I think that Ambrose is judged the victor by you; but because you are not Catholics, even so we look on secure; for without doubt Ambrose conquers both you and the Manicheans.

**III. 179.** *Julian.* You, however, by arguing and by usurping the testimonies of the Scriptures, affirm that all the saints were as defiled by this concupiscence, which you call the law of sin, as they complained of it.

*Augustine.* The saints are not defiled by this evil, who are known to be or to have been the strongest vanquishers of this evil; but lest they be inexpiably defiled by this evil, let its most shameless praisers rather be afraid.

**III. 180.** *Julian.* What does the Manichean say? *Through concupiscence the devil is the author of bodies; through this the devil hunts bodies, not souls; take away*—he says—*the root of the malignant stock, and you become spiritual; of this the Apostle cries out to the Romans: "I do not the good I will, but I work the evil I abhor"*[^184]. He also calls us fools, who say that this fashioning of the body pertains to God, while we confess that it was generated through concupiscence. You see, then, how great a concord there is between you and the Manichean in attacking us; you fight with his words, you rely on his arguments, and you say that we lie, who say that you not only were, as you yourself write, but even are his disciple. And yet in this he is the more prudent, who, since he had believed that this concupiscence of the flesh was sent in by the devil, gathered that one must confess that whatever appeared to be brought forth from a diabolical concupiscence does not pertain to the work of God.

*Augustine.* Against all this our earlier answer holds good, and along with it the others above, which whoever wishes may read.

**III. 181.** *Julian.* But you are duller, who, saying that man is the fruit of diabolical concupiscence, nonetheless ascribe him to the works of God—not as though good were made from evil, but evil, and brought forth from an evil root, yet having a good author, which fruit of his own root the devil claims for himself.

*Augustine.* Man is some good, even any man who, at a greater age, is an evil man. For the fact that he is an evil man does not take from him the fact that he is some good, because he is a man, of which nature—that is, of this good—God is the author, whatever evils he may either draw from his origin or add from his will; for the defects are not of the substance and nature, to be healed by that Savior by whose work as creator the nature was founded which was corrupted by those same defects. This is what overthrows both the Manicheans and you, who contend not with me but with Ambrose, whom I have set against the Manicheans in this contest, in which he conquers both them and you.

**III. 182.** *Julian.* For by your discourses the devil rightly plucks all who are born of concupiscence as though they were the fruits of his own tree; and so you blaspheme equally with the Manichean, we who vindicate the concupiscence of the flesh as founded by God.

*Augustine.* If you had been willing to come to the case—why infants to be baptized are both exsufflated and cleansed by exorcism—there you would appear most plainly, not only to learned Catholic Christians but even to unlearned ones, a new heretic. But because you set this out to yourself from my book as though to be refuted, yet were so afraid to treat it that you avoided it with deceitful loquacity, and by saying many things covered over what you were silent about, I repeat to you the Apostle, like it or not, and drive him home, saying of God the Father, *Who has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^185]. From here make an exception of the little ones, if you can, and dare to say that they are indeed transferred into the kingdom of Christ by regeneration, but not rescued from the power of darkness. Yet prepare yourselves to receive most worthily upon your faces those same exsufflations which in the Church of Christ are applied both to the older ones and to the little ones; for you must be exsufflated by such exsufflations, by which it is most plainly shown to be true what you deny.

**III. 183.** *Julian.* But now that statement of yours, which I sifted in my earlier work, where you say, *Sometimes disordered desire does not act when the mind wills, while sometimes it acts even when the mind is unwilling*, and you accuse this pride of it, by which it is moved when the mind is unwilling—this was set forth by the Manichean not only in his meanings but even in his words. For when he had accused us, because we said that human beings are made by God whom we confessed are sown through the pleasure of those coming together: *Fools*—he says—*say that what they are sure is begotten by concupiscence was founded by God, when they come together with the mind unwilling*.

*Augustine.* But the Manichean does not understand that even from the evil of a human being God can create what is good, and that the human being himself can use the evil well through conjugal modesty, an evil which entices him resisting to a damnable shamefulness. But you, who deny that this evil is an evil, why do you fight against it, lest you live shamefully, and why, if you consent to it, is it necessary that you live shamefully?

**III. 184.** *Julian.* But what you set down: *It is the evil of concupiscence that everywhere avoids the gaze and seeks shameful secrecy*; so too the Manichean: *In secret shames they act*—he says—*at which time they hate the light, that their works may not be made manifest*.

*Augustine.* Just as the Manichean does not know what good is done through the evil of carnal concupiscence, so you too do not know for what evil shame seeks secrecy even in the good work of marriage.

**III. 185.** *Julian.* But what you say: *Because whoever lawfully comes together with concupiscence uses an evil well*, so that you recall the more that the Apostle is to be believed, who said that good did not dwell in his flesh, and you want this "not good," that is, this evil dwelling in the flesh of the Apostle, to be seen as the concupiscence of the flesh—the Manichean set it down in no other way than you. For after he said: *That their works may not be made manifest; for which reason the Apostle*—he says—*cries out to the Romans: "I do not the good I will, but I work the evil I abhor"*[^186]. *For he grieved*—he says—*that sin, that is, the devil, was working in him all concupiscence; the authority of the law indicates the evil of concupiscence, when it censures all its use, which the flesh admires and praises*.

*Augustine.* Does the Apostle censure all use of carnal concupiscence, he who says, *And if you take a wife, you have not sinned, and if a virgin marries, she does not sin*[^187]? Therefore the Manichean does not know what he is saying. But neither do you understand what you are saying, you who think the Apostle signified something other than the concupiscence of the flesh, of which another Apostle says that it is not from the Father but from the world[^188]; and that this descends, so that the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, from the transgression of the first man, a learned and teaching Catholic both learned in the Catholic Church and taught.

**III. 186.** *Julian.* But what you thought availed much to hold the distinction between you and the Manichean—that you said nature was indeed made good, but made so only in the first human beings, while afterward the whole was overthrown through concupiscence—even the Manichean disputed thus: *It is worthwhile*—he says—*to note that the first soul, which flowed from the God of light, received this fabric of the body to rule it with its own rein. The commandment came, sin revived; what had seemed captive, the devil found its joints, seduced into it the matter of concupiscence, and through it killed it. The law indeed is holy, but holy for the holy; and the commandment both just and good, but for the just and the good*[^189]. So too in that letter to Patricius: *As though made from the flower of the first substance*—he says—*better than those that followed*. It is therefore no great matter, nor does it advance anything for your defense, that you thought Adam should be set apart from the disparagement of human nature—concerning whose state we shall presently dispute more fully. Here let it suffice to have shown that nothing so crude is found in your views that it has not already been worn smooth by the Manichean's rolling of it.

*Augustine.* The Manichean says that not only man but the whole world consists of the mingling of two coeternal natures, namely, of good and of evil, and everything pertaining to it—in such a way, indeed, that he attributes the very fabric of the world, though from the mingling of good and evil, to the good God as craftsman, but says that the animals and all things born from the earth, and man himself, are the works of a malignant mind, which he attributes to the race of darkness. Hence it is that he says the first soul "flowed from the God of light and received this fabric of the body to rule it with its own rein." For he says this not of man but of the good soul, which he supposes to be a part and nature of God mingled into the whole world and all things that are in it, but in man to be deceived through concupiscence. This concupiscence—which must often be driven home—he wants to be not a defect of a good substance, but an evil substance. He says that Adam was not empty of evil, but had less of it and much more of light. Do you see how opposed the Catholic faith is to this madness, which says that the nature of God is corruptible and corrupts it by mingling with an evil nature—the Catholic faith, which says that all the evils of the human race (no small part of which we see even the little ones suffering), and concupiscence itself, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit—all of which evils your paradise, but yours, is filled with—are not except from a good nature, well established by the good God, but corrupted by its own will and the transgression of the first man? And in denying this, what do you do but cause all these evils—which you do not want to come into the little ones from the sin of the good nature—to be attributed to the mingling of the evil nature which the furious error of the Manicheans introduces? You see, then, even if you do not wish to see, that the insane and lost Manicheans, lest they conquer with you aiding them, are to be conquered together with you—nay, with the Lord's help, are already conquered together with you.

**III. 187.** *Julian.* He persists, indeed, in inveighing against us and adds: *But these, who against the evangelical and apostolic books, which they read in vain, have dared to call this concupiscence a good—you may see*—he says—*that their saints sometimes slept with their daughters, sometimes did business mingling with many concubines and wives, nor do they see this of the Apostle: "What fellowship has light with darkness, the faithful with the unfaithful, Christ with Belial*[^190]?" *They wander, massed in the cloud of concupiscence, whose poison they so enjoy that, seized with madness, when they do this, they suppose it granted by God, as if they did not know that the Apostle said: "What is done by them in darkness is shameful even to speak of"*[^191]. You see, surely, how he troubles the business of shame and thinks it avails very much against us, who do not dare to call evil what we nonetheless confess must be veiled by shame. You have therefore woven no garment with which to cover the deformity of your teaching, except the rags in which you grew up, in the inheritance of your parent and master. He persists, then, in raising himself up against us, and, making an apostrophe: *Come now, you*—he says—*defender of concupiscence, narrate openly its fruits and works. Behold, against it I do not fear the light, which it dreads, which it hates. "For everyone who does evil hates the light and does not come to the light, that his works may not be made manifest"*[^192]. *Do you see that concupiscence is the origin of evil, through which wretched souls serve disordered desire, not of their own accord, since this is the only thing we do with the mind unwilling*? Hence it is that you too: *For what is it*—you say—*that the lips, the tongue, the hands, the bendings of the back, the neck, and the sides are placed in our power to be moved to their proper works, but when it comes to the sowing of children, the members created for this work do not serve, but it is waited for that disordered desire, as though of its own right, should move them, and sometimes it does not act when the mind wills, while sometimes it acts even when the mind is unwilling*? You enumerated, surely, all the offices of the members, and, commending them as serving the command of the will, you call the business of disordered desire that which alone we do with the mind unwilling. What does the Manichean say? *Do you see*—he says—*that concupiscence is the origin of evil, through which wretched souls serve disordered desire, not of their own accord, since this is the only thing we do with the mind unwilling?* But let us see what else he adds: *Finally, every sin is outside the body, because it is actual; but he who fornicates sins against his own body*[^193]; *for every sin, before it is done, is not, and after it is done only the memory of that work, not the appearance itself, remains. But the evil of concupiscence, because it is natural, is before it is done, when it is done is increased, and after it is done both is seen and remains*. Why, then, do you quarrel with us, because we call you a Manichean, when neither in his writings is anything held other than what you say, nor in yours anything other than what he wishes to persuade? But now that which runs about through the common mouths, instilled nonetheless by you—that is, *If sin is not natural, why are infants baptized, who it is established have done nothing evil of themselves?*—is contained in that same letter of the Manichean. But I said that this flits about on the tongues of many, because it is a vulgar argument grasped even by the slowest. For the rest, in your books you place your whole hope in it; and this very thing, then, your preceptor pursues in this way: *These are to be questioned by me in these words: If every evil is actual, before anyone does evil, why does he receive purification by water, since he has done no evil of himself? Or if he has not yet done it and is to be purified, they may indeed show a sprouting of an evil stock naturally—those very people whom madness does not allow to understand either what they say or about what they affirm*. You hear how he reviles us? He calls us mad and not understanding either what we say or what we affirm, who deny the sprouting of an evil stock, since we baptize even those, with the water purifying, who have done no evil—that is, the little ones. Many things are set down from his opinions; but unless the title indicated his daughter Menoch and the Manichean who calls himself an apostle of Christ, they would promise you outright as their author. Since, then, you say nothing other than what you learned with Mani teaching, do you suppose that, in the estimation of the Catholics, you are to be held otherwise than he, through whom you drank the sacraments of such teachings?

*Augustine.* You have at last finished what you thought should be said against us from the letter of the Manichean—which, aided by the prayers of your colleague Florus, you rejoice to have found—where indeed the Manichean accuses the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, but supposes that it must be objected to the Catholics in such a way as though they said it is good, because they commend, with the soundness of the Lord's and the apostolic teaching, the good of marriage. For when could the Manichean discern the evil of carnal concupiscence from the good of marriage, when the Letter to the Hebrews says that solid food is only for those who have their senses exercised to separate good from evil[^194]? But plainly you have fallen inescapably into the bite of this Manichean—you, so great a praiser of the concupiscence of the flesh, that you would place it, not such as it could have been there, if it had been right for it to be, but altogether such as it now is, even in paradise, that is, in a place of so great blessedness, that its inhabitants would so enjoy a blessed peace that within themselves, by an internal war, they would fight against the movements of concupiscence lest they slip into consent to that unlawful or dishonorable lying together. This evil could not be in paradise, except perhaps in that painted one of which we spoke above, which would have the title inscribed above it: The Paradise of the Pelagians, where, even if concupiscence itself, soliciting chaste hearts with its hidden movements, could not be expressed by any art of the painter, there could nonetheless be painted pregnant women repelling useful foods with loathing, craving harmful ones with foul delight, nauseating, vomiting, growing pale, sometimes pouring forth immature offspring in miscarriage, even in childbirth itself protesting by their misery the punishment which mother Eve received, and—though by no sounding picture, yet by imitating, as it could, the faces of the sad, the groaning, the weeping—all the children themselves weeping by the common lot of those born, and by the variety of various punishments, and many afterward even under the beatings of teachers. Whoever, not knowing, looked at this picture and read the title and sought the cause, this excellent reasoning—but yours—would be rendered to him: in which you would say: Such would altogether be the condition of the human race even there, because such it is here, where likewise from the offspring of human beings no origin of sin is drawn. If he acquiesced, he would become a Pelagian; but if he were unwilling to acquiesce in this mocked folly, he would be accused by you as a Manichean. But to this contention, O holy bishop of God, Ambrose—learned in the Church and a Teacher of the Church—say to these people that the concupiscence by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, and concerning which the Manichean, not knowing what he is saying, stretches out his mousetraps for the unskilled, befell not from the mingling of another nature, as he raves, but from the transgression of the first man, our good nature founded good by the good God. But you perhaps, with a most shameless obstinacy, choose to lend aids to the most impure Manichean rather than to acquiesce in the holy Ambrose. Do as you please; but the Manichean will not rejoice even with you as his helpers, since by the Catholic faith, in the name and power of Christ, Ambrose conquers both. For if this concupiscence were such that by its carnal movement it neither preceded nor exceeded the will of man, but always followed his choice, surely neither would the Manichean find what to censure justly in it, nor would any of us contend that the married could not have had it in paradise, nor would Ambrose say that we drew it from the transgression of the first man, because he would not see it have desires opposed to the spirit. But now, because it is such that it has desires opposed to the spirit, even if, with the spirit resisting, it does not conquer—and therefore could not be in that great peace of the blessed enjoying paradise, nor is it right to believe God corruptible by the mingling of an evil nature—it remains that the faith of Ambrose, concerning the contagion of the first sin, conquers both you and the Manicheans.

**III. 188.** *Julian.* It remains that we examine that statement of yours, which you, having promised to reduce it to a single sentence, gathered briefly—as is not to be denied—and rather cleverly: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be generated; if it had no evil, it would not be something to be regenerated; and, to conclude both in a single word, human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved; if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved*. In this place you are not to be defrauded by us of the praise of your wit; for it could not, in fact, have been said better according to your reasoning—but nonetheless the nature of things does not allow that exact cleverness should advance anything. It is hard for you altogether to kick against the goad; for whatever you assemble is dissolved no less than ice by the fire of truth applied to it. In short, attend now to what is brought forward by us. You concluded in this way: *Surely human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved*. We assent; you said it altogether truly: *If it were evil, it would not be something to be saved*, because a thing evil and naturally evil would neither deserve nor receive salvation. Why would it not receive it? Because it could not be made other than it was made; but it would not deserve it, because there would be nothing in it that the clemency of God would deign to set free. But when we say, *if it were*, which surely it cannot be, we do not grant it hope of the denied effect, but, for the destruction of another opinion which arises, we are accustomed to speak about impossible things. If this or that, for example, were so, that without doubt would follow, so that, since that cannot be, this too, for the removing of which it had been brought in, is much more denied. Already, then, we have often shown that something cannot be evil by nature; but nonetheless now, without prejudice to this established point, we approve that part of your statement in which you said: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved*. You located salvation in baptism and argued consequently that, if the Manicheans, who say that nature is evil, spoke the truth, the Christians would be out of their senses who believed that the remedy of baptism must be applied to an evil nature; and therefore one who affirms that the nature of human beings is evil is compelled to deny grace—which recurs thus, that one who affirms grace praises the nature of human beings, for whose salvation he understands it to have been provided.

*Augustine.* What you say is not true; you deceive or are deceived. For it is not one who affirms that the nature of human beings is evil, but one who affirms that it is an evil, that is, not one who affirms that it is evil, but one who affirms that it is an evil, that is compelled to deny grace; for where it is evil, there it the more needs grace. For an evil man is indeed an evil nature, since a man without doubt is a nature; so an evil woman is an evil nature, since a woman certainly is a nature. How, then, is one who says this compelled to deny grace, when grace brings help to evil natures, that is, to evil men, that they may cease to be evil? But we say one thing when we say, "That man is evil," another when we say, "That man is an evil"; the former can be true, the latter cannot. Just as if we say, "That man is defective," it can be true; but if we say, "That man is a defect," it cannot be true. Accordingly, do not err or send human beings into error, and understand me to have said it thus: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be generated; if it had no evil, it would not be something to be regenerated*, as if I had said: Human nature, if it were a defect, would not be something to be generated; if it had no defect, it would not be something to be regenerated. Likewise what follows, where I said I conclude both in a single word, I said thus: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved; if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved*, as if I were saying: Human nature, if it were a defect, would not be something to be saved; if there were no defect in it, it would not be something to be saved. Behold, I have made my words plainer—not so that you might find what to say against them, but so that you might be understood to have been unable to find what to say.

**III. 189.** *Julian.* Mark, then, what is gathered from this: that the denial of grace does not cling to the praise of human nature; rather, that these four things are so joined that one cannot be held without the other—but the disgrace of nature begets the denial of grace, and the praise of grace begets the proclamation of nature; for these can be turned about in whichever direction you please. You said well, then: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved*; for the Manicheans say both of these, namely, that the nature of the flesh is evil and that it can neither be saved by grace nor ought to be.

*Augustine.* But they say that the nature of the flesh is evil in such a way that they say it is an evil, not that it has an evil, because they suppose the defect itself to be not an accident of a substance, but a substance.

**III. 190.** *Julian.* But after this you bring in a sentence dear to you and say: *But if there were no evil in human nature, it would not be something to be saved. Whoever, then, says that it is not good denies the Creator of what was founded good; but whoever denies that there is evil in it begrudges the merciful Savior of what was corrupted*. Let the reader, then, be attentive; he will see that you said nothing other than what you had denied must be said; for you declared that there is evil naturally in the thing which you had said was not evil naturally.

*Augustine.* I had not said that it is not evil, but that it is not an evil—that is, to speak more plainly, I had not said that it is not corrupted, but that it is not a corruption; reread and understand.

**III. 191.** *Julian.* But an evil nature cannot be understood as anything other than to have something evil congenital with it.

*Augustine.* Whether the evil is congenital, like foolishness of mind, or taken on by the will, like homicide, a man can be called an evil man, even an evil nature, because a man too is a nature, just as an evil horse can also be called an evil animal, because a horse too is an animal.

**III. 192.** *Julian.* And, to make a brief and clean conclusion: if there is in a nature an evil such that an evil is born from it together with the very seeds, it is without doubt convicted of being evil.

*Augustine.* Even if it is convicted of being evil, it is nonetheless not an evil, because, even if it is convicted of being corrupted, it is nonetheless not a corruption.

**III. 193.** *Julian.* But if it is vindicated from evils and defended as good, neither can an evil be born from it nor be in it naturally. Your conclusion, then, has vanished, because the sentence you brought in second did not distinguish the first, but doubled it.

*Augustine.* It does not at once follow, if it is defended as good, that it is vindicated from evils. For the same nature is both good, insofar as it is a nature, and evil, if it is corrupted; yet no nature at all is an evil. My conclusion, then, has not vanished, by which I said: *Human nature, if it were evil, would not be something to be saved; if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved*. But if you wish to speak the truth, say rather that your argumentation has vanished, by which you wished to refute my conclusion.

**III. 194.** *Julian.* *Human nature*—you say—*if it were evil, would not be something to be saved; if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved*. What else have you shown than that one and the same thing must be saved on account of that on account of which you had denied it must be saved?

*Augustine.* It must be saved because it is evil, not because it is an evil; but if it were an evil, it would not be something to be saved. For not because it is an evil, but because it has an evil, is it evil—just as not because it is a defect, but because it has a defect, is it defective. Because it is defective, then, it must be saved; but if the nature itself, that is, the substance itself, were a defect, it would not be something to be saved. How, then, did I say that one thing must be saved on account of that on account of which I had denied it must be saved, when it is one thing to have a defect, another to be a defect? It must be saved, then, because it has a defect, not because it is a defect; but if it were a defect, it would not be something to be saved. You see how this is not to answer, which is not to be silent—since you see that you have answered nothing, and yet have been unwilling to be silent.

**III. 195.** *Julian.* And through this you have turned back to repairing, by your argumentation, the Manichean, whom by your profession you had despised. For by saying, *If it were evil, it would not be something to be saved*, you had put on the appearance of fighting; but by adding, *but if there were no evil in it, it would not be something to be saved*, you taught that you serve him faithfully. Since, then, it has appeared that this opinion of yours is contained in that of the Manichean—namely, that the nature of human beings is evil, in which both he and you alike say there is an evil, and you have declared that an evil nature is not to be saved—it has been most absolutely established that your faith, with its forces wholly joined, attacks both nature and grace.

*Augustine.* Just because you change my words so as to seem to have answered something, do you therefore make it difficult or impossible for readers either to recall to mind, or at least, in this very book of yours a little above, to reread and see what I said, where you yourself set down those words as they were said by me? For I did not say, "If it were evil, it would not be something to be saved"—since for this reason it must be saved, that it may not be evil; but I said, *If it were an evil, it would not be something to be saved*. For by having a defect it is evil, and when this is consumed it without doubt becomes saved; it is not itself a defect, otherwise it would itself be consumed when the defect is consumed. But when the defect is consumed together with the salvation, what in this way is saved but the nature? When, therefore, the defect is said to be healed, not the defect itself but that nature in which it was inhering is saved—which, if it were a defect, would not be saved, but would rather be ended by the healing; nay, no health could come about in this way, since, if it did come about, the saved nature would remain when the defect was consumed, by which it was not healthy; but if the nature were a defect, it would surely not be healed when the defect was consumed, but would itself be consumed. And through this human nature would not be something to be saved—not if it were evil, but if it were an evil, that is, if it were a defect; if there were no evil in it, that is, if it were not evil by a defect, it would not be something to be saved. Behold what I said; do not, by changing my words, wish to make room not for your answers but for your contentions.

**III. 196.** *Julian.* And it follows that no one can praise the grace of Christ unless he has praised in nature the good work of the Founder.

*Augustine.* This you said truly, and therefore even in an evil nature there is the praiseworthy work of the Founder, because an evil nature too, insofar as it is a nature, is a good, and, so that this good may be set free from the evil, grace is necessary.

**III. 197.** *Julian.* Surely here there is no question about the pursuits of grown men, but the matter turns solely on the quality of those who are born, in whom the status of nature is examined. This nature, then, you assert—together with the Manichean—to be, without any admixture of its own will, guilty, condemned, full of evils, that is, of crimes, which we maintain to be, without crime, without the evil of sin, harmless and capable of virtue.

*Augustine.* We recognize you, we recognize the great guardian, who do not allow the Savior to come to the rescue of the little ones whom you guard. But if you do allow it, tell me, I beg, when they are baptized healthy in body, from what evil, from what defect, from what part they are saved. But what will you say, you who make for yourself your paradise, which you do not blush to fill with the undeserved punishments of little ones?

**III. 198.** *Julian.* Do you then accuse this nature in the little ones as criminal, defective, and condemned by its own will or by the condition of being born? If by its own will—so that those little ones would be said to have sinned by their own pursuits—a monstrous opinion indeed arises, yet the traduction of sin is denied, for one did not receive from another what he himself was able to commit; but if it obtains so many evils not by its own action but by its condition, it is convicted of being unjustly iniquitous.

*Augustine.* I accuse the nature of the little ones neither by their own will—for no one is born because he wills—nor by the condition of being born, except that they are born wretched, not that they are born. For human nature, fruitful by the blessing of God, would be born even in paradise, even if no one had sinned, until the number of saints foreknown by God should be completed. But those little ones would neither weep in paradise nor be mute, nor would they ever be unable to use reason, nor would they lie feeble and helpless without the use of their members, nor be afflicted with diseases, nor harmed by beasts, nor killed by poisons, nor wounded by any mishap, nor deprived of any sense or any part of the body, nor be vexed by demons, nor, rising into boyhood, be tamed by beatings or trained by labors; nor would any of them be born with so empty and dull an ability that he could be corrected by no labor or pain; but, except for the size of their own body on account of the incapable wombs of mothers, they would be begotten altogether such as Adam was made. But now they would neither be such as we see, nor suffer such things, had not human nature, by that great sin, been changed and condemned into these miseries. They are not, then, in this state by the condition of being born, but by the contagion of sin and the condition of punishment.

**III. 199.** *Julian.* In what, then, concerning the judgment of nature, do you and the Manichean differ? Because he, you say, confesses it evil. If, then, you deny this, declare it to be good, and the contention is removed; you will at once be caught in the nets of truth unto salvation. But you cry out against this. Let us hear, then, what you bring forth, Aristotle of the Carthaginians. *So great an evil*—you say—*is in it, that it is possessed by the devil and deserves eternal fire*.

*Augustine.* But you, who deny that it is possessed by the devil, without doubt deny that it is rescued from the power of darkness when it is transferred into the kingdom of Christ by regeneration, and you accuse the whole Catholic Church of the great crime of treason. For by no other crime is a person held guilty under the laws of this world than whoever exsufflates the image—though not a living one—of the emperor. But the little ones are exsufflated in the exorcism before they are baptized; therefore living images are exsufflated—not of any king, but of God; nay rather, what is exsufflated is the devil, who, by the contagion of sin, holds the little one guilty, so that, when he has been cast out, the little one may be transferred to Christ. Let the madness of Julian, then, be exsufflated, lest the Church be said to be guilty of treason in the cleansing and exsufflation of the little ones. But if it is not rescued from the power of darkness, and the little one remains there, why do you wonder that he who is not allowed to enter into the kingdom of God will be in the eternal fire with the devil? Or because the Pelagians prepare for unbaptized little ones some I-know-not-what place of rest and eternal life apart from the kingdom of God, will the sentence of Christ therefore be false: *Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved; but whoever does not believe will be condemned*[^195]? But whoever denies that the little ones believe through the mouths of those who carry them, let him deny that they receive baptism too, because they struggle in the hands of those who carry them.

**III. 200.** *Julian.* But it is so far from evil that none but a Manichean could accuse it.

*Augustine.* Did the Manichean, then, say, *We are all born under sin, whose very origin is in defect*? But since a learned man and a Catholic Teacher said this, what are you but a deceived and deceiving heretic?

**III. 201.** *Julian.* I, however—he says—am not a Manichean, since I absolve it in words and condemn it in my judgments.

*Augustine.* You condemn it by a judgment—and that an unjust one—which you want to suffer such great evils without any evil merit.

**III. 202.** *Julian.* What, then, shall I here first accuse—the shamelessness of the liar, the licentiousness of the disputant, or the profanity of one who believes such depraved things? Yet I understand that we must use the words of the Prophet: *Blessed are we, O Israel, because the things that please God are known to us*[^196].

*Augustine.* So then, the things that please God are known to you? Foolish man—that little ones should be oppressed by a heavy yoke without any contagion of sin does not please God. They therefore draw a contagion from that first and great sin, which you, unwilling to confess, what do you give to God but an unjust judgment?

**III. 203.** *Julian.* But the office of invectives the censure of the readers will supply; let us proceed to the rest. Surely there is in things no other evil that is truly evil except what we call sin.

*Augustine.* If the punishments of sins are not also evils, there is no way for you to defend as true what God said, who said that he creates evils[^197]; for he is not to be said to create sins.

**III. 204.** *Julian.* From which sin is begotten in each one, as you say, a natural evil; there is therefore nothing else truly evil than a deserved evil.

*Augustine.* Not only a deserved evil, but also the punishment which is rendered for an evil merit is an evil; for it is not that sin is truly evil and the punishment of sin not truly evil. If, then, there were no deserved evil in the little ones from their origin, whatever evil they suffer would be unjust. And therefore these evils could not be in paradise, because there would be there no deserved evil, if a happy obedience remained. And through this, because here there are in the little ones evils that would not be there, they have also a deserved evil, which they would not have except originally.

**III. 205.** *Julian.* If, then, the Manichean too calls nature evil for no other reason than that he judges it to be of an evil merit, on which he thinks punishments must be inflicted, and you affirm that there is an evil in human nature, and no other evil than what the Manichean says, that is, the concupiscence of the flesh, which you preach was breathed in by the devil; and you say that it is so of an evil merit that you persist in adjudging it to eternal torments, then without doubt you, no otherwise than the Manichean, pronounce it most wicked and condemned.

*Augustine.* Blush now at last. The concupiscence of the flesh is not from the Father, but is from the world[^198], that is, from human beings, with whose offspring the world is filled; but this the Manicheans attribute to the race of darkness, with you helping, who do not want this evil to have turned into nature through the transgression of the first man, according to the Ambrosian—that is, the Catholic—faith.

**III. 206.** *Julian.* Let us see, then, how the second part of your statement comes back here too. For you said: *Whoever says that human nature is not good denies the Creator of what was founded good; but whoever denies that there is evil in it begrudges the merciful Savior of what was corrupted*. If, then, as you were compelled to profess, whoever denies that it is good denies also its Founder, that is, God; and to be an evil nature is nothing other than to have an evil merit naturally—then your own confession too lends support to our truth: namely, that you deny that the good God is the founder of the human beings whose nature you swear to be evil, both by its defects and by its punishments.

*Augustine.* Human nature, even if it is evil because it is corrupted, is nonetheless not an evil, because it is a nature. For no nature, insofar as it is a nature, is an evil, but altogether a good, without which good no evil can be, since no defect can be except in some nature, although there can be without defect either a never-corrupted or a healed nature; which, if the Manicheans understood, they would not be Manicheans at all, introducing two natures from contrary parts of good and evil. If, then, you see what is between us and the Manicheans, be silent; if you do not see, be silent.

**III. 207.** *Julian.* We do not, then, begrudge the Savior to that nature which we defend by the dignity of its Founder; but we deny that it is an evil, lest we become Manicheans, and we deny that there is an evil in it, lest, with the names changed, we be just as many Manicheans.

*Augustine.* You are altogether against the Manicheans when you say that nature is not an evil; but you are Pelagians when you say there is no evil in it, and unknowingly you give support to the Manicheans by saying that the evil which manifestly is in it is not from the transgression of the first man, so that they may conclude that there is another substance and nature of evil.

**III. 208.** *Julian.* But that it is corrupted in each one, by his own choice, whether doing good or evil, we say is a merit of the person, not of nature. And therefore we assert that through the grace of Christ that is healed which can be corrupted, since nothing else is shown to be wounded than what is taught to be healed.

*Augustine.* Surely you begrudge the Savior to the little ones—they too to be exsufflated like the others, if it were believed to profit you that you should be rescued from the power of darkness and transferred into the kingdom of Christ.

**III. 209.** *Julian.* But in the baptized it is not the concupiscence of the flesh, which is natural, but the guilt of evil concupiscence that is taken away.

*Augustine.* The apostle John said that the concupiscence of the flesh is not from the Father, but from the world[^199], making it in this way to be understood as evil; but you say that not the concupiscence of the flesh, which is natural, but evil concupiscence has guilt. For with you, not even when fornication is desired is the concupiscence of the flesh evil, because, as you say, one who uses it thus uses a good badly. It is therefore always itself a good, as you would have it, whether one desires marriage or adultery by it, since, if marriage, one uses a good well, if adultery, one uses a good badly. Fight, then, with the apostle John, not with me; for you do not agree with him when he says that it is evil—the concupiscence of the flesh, which he says is not from the Father, but from the world—as long as you say that the concupiscence of the flesh, even when an evil is desired by it, is good, and through this is never evil. But, believe me, no one is a Christian who would not rather agree with the apostle John than with you.

**III. 210.** *Julian.* And through this, that there is no sin [in] nature is proved by the testimony both of the Founder and of the Redeemer of the human race.

*Augustine.* What is this you say, too inconsiderately: *That there is no sin in nature*? As though sin could be at all except in a nature, even if it is not drawn from origin, but committed by the will. For whether it be the sin of an angel or of a man, it is manifest that it is either in an angel or in a man; but who would so deviate from the nature of things as to deny that a man or an angel is a nature? What is it, again, that you speak with closed eyes? What is it, I ask, that you say: *That concupiscence is not sin*? Do you not see that you dispute against the Apostle? For he showed plainly enough that concupiscence is sin, where he says, *I did not know sin except through the law; for I did not know concupiscence unless the law said: You shall not covet*[^200]. What can be said clearer than this testimony, what emptier than your opinion?

**III. 211.** *Julian.* And, to recapitulate what has been argued: that the opinion of traduction is hostile to the righteousness of God, I have shown by the attestation both of the commandments of God and of his judgments.

*Augustine.* That you rather are hostile to the righteousness of God—whom you make to have unjustly placed a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb[^201], while you deny original sin—has been shown both by the testimony of the Scriptures and by the very calamities of the little ones, which could not be in paradise except in yours.

**III. 212.** *Julian.* But that the concupiscence of the flesh, implanted by God, the creator of bodies, for accomplishing the business of fruitfulness in the sexes, is accused by none but the Manichean, I have thoroughly taught by the insertion of his writings.

*Augustine.* That the concupiscence of the flesh, which the divine Scripture teaches to be evil—for through it *the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit*[^202]—and which you, helping the Manicheans to attribute it to the race of darkness coeternal with God (since you do not want it to pertain to original sin and dare moreover to place it in the paradise of God), we have shown by the most evident documents both of divine authority and of sober reason.

**III. 213.** *Julian.* And that there are no other arguments with you for traduction than those the Manichean had furnished, I have shown.

*Augustine.* That Ambrose is not a Manichean, who as a most invincible champion of the Catholic faith laid low both the Manicheans and you—he who said that there is a discord of flesh and spirit, where, through this thing you have taken up, the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit—and that through the transgression of the first man it turned into our nature, you are compelled to confess, convicted and confounded even by the testimony of your own prince, who praises that same bishop.

**III. 214.** *Julian.* But that you are a denier of free choice and of God, the founder of those who are born, I have laid open by the examination of your own statement.

*Augustine.* That I am not a denier of free choice—which you, by exalting it, precipitate—but rather a defender of it, by confessing the grace of God (which choice you deceive by trusting in your own power), innumerable holy testimonies proclaim; and that we do not deny that God is the founder of those who are born, but that you deny that God is the Savior of those who are born, the things we have both said will show to those who read.

**III. 215.** *Julian.* But that an evil nature is pronounced by you—the statement which you had promised to conclude in a single word—I have convicted by its unfolding.

*Augustine.* That human nature is not an evil, but that there is an evil in it, I have shown—teaching the one through the substance of that same nature and God the founder of that substance, the other through the misery of that same nature and God the Savior from that same misery. But that you could not dissolve my reasoning, even if I had not answered you, a diligent and understanding reader could find out.

**III. 216.** *Julian.* And therefore I trust that it is clear, even to those slowest to understand or deceived by their favor toward you, that you shun only the name of the Manichean, but for the rest, in all faith, you cling to his teachings, together with all the followers of traduction, no less foul than dull.

*Augustine.* The matter is altogether otherwise and the contrary of what you suppose, which you say is clear even to the slowest. For this rather is clear even to the slowest, if they will not refuse to spend attentive care on reading the writings of both of us: that I have shown not only that I am the enemy of the error of the Manicheans and have overthrown their nefarious teachings with the God of truth helping, but also that you so aid the madness of the Manicheans that they would altogether glory in being unconquered, unless the Catholic faith, which we defend with God's mercy, conquered them—not through you or by you, but rather together with you. But that you chose me alone, against whom—the more frequently, the more hatefully—you might hurl the name and crime of the Manichean, was because in that way you thought you could destroy, without odium, the most firmly founded Catholic faith which accuses you, and the most well-known multitude of its illustrious and holy defenders, who learned and taught what we have learned and teach—destroy them, I say, by the horror of an abominable name, not by the truth of the charge objected. *But the firm foundation of God stands: for the Lord knows who are his*[^203]. But how, with a few of the words of my book, which you had set out as though to be refuted, attempted as though for the appearance of some sort of fight, you have avoided much else, far more diffuse and interspersed, by your cloudy loquacity—because it is long to show this here, I leave it for the reader to notice and find out for himself.
