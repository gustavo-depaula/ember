# Book Five

**V. 1.** *Julian.* It has been ascertained that in the perils of every age the reverence of uncorrupted judgment flourishes among few, who, both given to the pursuits of knowledge and desirous of the virtues, either can track down the truth, or, when it is found, dare to defend it; or, as the Apostle says: *They have their senses exercised to the discernment of good and evil*[^1]; nor are they broken by any storms of adversities, who hear from the same preceptor that one must resist sins even unto blood[^2]. These prudent ones, then, whom the peoples of the dull make seem few, study knowledge and fortitude in equal measure. For neither, without the other, either attains its fruit or its honor; since both fortitude, unless applied through knowledge to the best things, breaks out into despicable madness; and, on the other side, the examined laws of justice, unless the wall of magnanimity enclose them, will lie open at once to plunder, and be led away into bondage to crimes. These two horses, then, without which one cannot triumph over the errors of the world, in diverse ages all the rarest sort have existed, who would cultivate them and yoke them and rule them; since both the flight of labor and the diversity of secular cares impede the pursuit of knowledge, and dread strikes down the constancy under hardships which are stirred up by the wicked. Which kinds of assaults faithful and wise minds indeed conquer; but they are so rare that, among the peoples of the insane, because they do not rage, they seem to be insane.

*Augustine.* Does not this rarity of human beings—which you yourself record—in whom there is both knowledge and fortitude, admonish you what you ought to feel about the human race, and about this whole mass of rational and mortal living beings? For why does not the race of mortals, by a natural appetite, rise up—whether the whole, or at least for the greater part—to the pursuit of knowledge and the strength of fortitude, so that we should rather wonder at the rarest individuals for deviating and falling away from what the institution of nature seeks after? Why does it slip down into the depth of unskillfulness and the softness of cowardice, as down a slope, with I-know-not-what weight, as it were, pressing it? You say, surely, that the flight of labor is the cause, by which it comes about that human beings do not know the things they ought to have known; but I would wish you to say what cause there is, that for a man so well naturally established, it is most laborious to learn the things useful and wholesome to nature, and so, fleeing labor, he rests more familiarly and more gladly in the darkness of ignorance. Surely so great a rarity of the talented and studious, through which two things one comes to the knowledge of human and divine things, and so great a multitude of the slow and slothful, indicates sufficiently in which direction nature itself is carried by its own weight, as it were—a nature which you deny to be vitiated. Nor do you think, according to the Christian faith, what Adam was made, who imposed names on all kinds of living souls[^3]; which we read, even in secular letters, to have been the indication of a most excellent wisdom. For Pythagoras himself, from whom the name of philosophy arose, is reported to have said that he was the wisest of all who first imposed words on things. But even if we had learned nothing such of Adam, it was surely ours to conjecture by a true reasoning what nature was founded in that man, in whom there was no vice at all. But who would be so slow of wit as to deny that dull or sharp wits pertain to nature, or to think that slowness of memory or of intelligence is not a vice of the mind? And what Christian would doubt that those who in this age, full of errors and hardships, appear most talented—whose corruptible bodies nonetheless weigh down their souls—if they be compared to his wit, differ far more from him than tortoises differ from birds in swiftness? With such excellent wits, then, the happiness of paradise would be filled, if no one had sinned; for God was going to create such ones from the parents as he had created that one without parents, namely, to his own image. For not yet had man been made like to vanity, that his days should pass like a shadow in this hardship-filled age[^4]. But if this were so, would this complaint of yours have any place? Would the acquisition of knowledge be laborious, so that, through flight of labor, human beings preferred to be unskilled? Would we have need of that very fortitude, which you say truly is found scarcely in the fewest, where there would be no hardship which we should have to sustain bravely for the truth? Since, then, these things are all turned to the contrary, you deny our nature to be vitiated for this end—that, with you helping, the Manichean may introduce an alien one mingled into us; and so, while you leap forth, an unskilled assailant of him, you become an unwitting helper.

**V. 2.** *Julian.* This too that book shows which is called *Wisdom*, when, expressing the words of the impious, who, after the shadow of present things, behold the merits of the blessed laid bare: *Their life*, it says, *we esteemed madness; and how have they been reckoned among the sons of God?*[^5] Hence it is, then, that the perseverance of the faithful, contumacious against the iniquities of the times, and choosing to be afflicted with the people of God rather than to have the enjoyment of temporal sin[^6], is accused, by those who say: *Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die*[^7], under the name of pertinacity and contention; and nothing is thought more befitting cautious counsels than to buy the servitude of a degenerate soul, or the faithless quiet of moments. From this vileness of slothful hearts above all it has come about that the foul teaching of the Manicheans sails through the shipwrecks of the Churches. For if, in those who discharge the office of priesthood, a free and manly authority had stood firm, the inventions of the Traducianists, just as invincible reason has overthrown them, so public assent would have ground to pieces. But since nothing is esteemed by lovers of present things more vile than religion, recourse was had to accusations of God; so that necessity pressed upon us, by such long disputations, to approve our God, who is the true God, faithful in his words, just in his judgments, holy in his works[^8].

*Augustine.* If God is faithful in his words, why do you contradict him when he says: *I will repay the sins of the fathers upon the sons*[^9], and contend that this is not true? If he is just in his judgments, why do you not want this very thing—that the sins of the fathers are repaid upon the sons—to be just; and do you not fear to say that the children of Adam are pressed by a heavy yoke from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb without the merit of any original sin[^10]? If he is holy in his works, why do you refuse to discern the uncleanness of those being born—which compelled the man of God to say that no one is clean from the filth of sin, not even the infant whose life is of one day upon earth[^11]—from his holy handiwork by which he forms nature, though polluted by the contagion of origin; and so attribute all the vices both of wits and of bodies, so many and sometimes so great, all to his holy handiwork? Which, unwilling to attribute to the original merits coming from a nature vitiated by sin, you surely open a most ample entrance for the introduction of an alien nature of evil to the execrable Manicheans; whose nefarious teaching, shuddering, you as it were accuse, while you aid those erring.

**V. 3.** *Julian.* The work destined for these matters of my books the assertor of natural evil assails; with what strength, indeed, and with how consequent a response, I have more than sufficiently taught by the disputation of the preceding volumes. By the reading of which I do not doubt it is established to every prudent person (whom I have testified, by this preface, to be rare) that the enemy of truth studies nothing else than that the ears of the simpler be mocked, and that he may seem to have escaped, if in any way he may seem to have answered.

*Augustine.* That the prudent are rare, you testify by your preface; and what the cause is of this rarity, or why not even those very ones who, by a rare capacity of wit, have come to prudence, could attain useful knowledge without great labor, you neither say nor learn; you who are unwilling to confess that human nature was depraved through the transgression of the first man. And yet to the reading of your books you send none but those same prudent ones, whom you testify to be the rarest; of whose wit you think so well that, with them, you try to refute my one book with your eight, multiplying for them the labor of the children of Adam; by which labor they may learn that, even if no one had sinned, in paradise itself they would have been going to labor, in order to learn the books of the lettered, and first the letters themselves. For this is your splendid knowledge, comprehensible to no human beings, except the rare prudent ones, nor to these except laboring and wretched.

**V. 4.** *Julian.* Since, then, it is abundantly established that this has been accomplished by us; nonetheless, because a length is born to our work, let the wise reader understand that brevity was indeed desirable to us, but the necessity of the cause demanded that an error more widespread by the favor of the age be conquered by a broader edge of truth. It is not, then, for nothing that our discourse is extended. For with the help of Christ I presume it will come about that no part of the impiety against which we contend be judged either negligently sought out, or insufficiently found, or but moderately worn down. Nor, then, can we or ought we despair that, in the process of times, the storm that has been stirred up will subside, and that, by the authority of the wise, the slothful crowd which now roars about will be corrected; but the cause of the vow is less than that of the resolve; for whatever issue the state of affairs may be allotted, the reckoning both of kindness and of faith will stand firm for us. For we do not hang upon the outcome of popular prosperity. For that saying is well-known, of the three boys in Babylon, who, when they were compelled by a most proud king to adore a statue, most faithfully resisted, nor were terrified by the panting furnace which had been kindled for the devouring of the religious; and they answered as befitted both faith and constancy: *God is able, O king*, they say, *to free us from this furnace; but even if he should not free us, know that we do not worship your gods, nor adore the statue which you have set up*[^12]. How holy a vow they mingled with judgment, nor yet lightened the gravity of counsel by desire! The fortitude of faith they neither cast headlong by despair nor suspended by cupidity; they mingle vows indeed, but they do not abandon order; they console endurance, but submit milder things to the just: It is certain, they say, that our God can free us; but whether he will, is uncertain; and therefore, under the ambiguous outcome of affairs, the certain sentence of the devout stands firm—to reject idols, to bear punishments. Let it be seen what good our liberation may confer also on others; for us meanwhile let faith, they say, keep unconquered the true happiness; therefore he does not have too great a necessity of gratifying the softer, whose adversities trade for glory. From this discipline too, which those illustrious masters left, we understand that, in prosperous things, a moderation of vows is to be held; but in the teachings of faith, a perennity of counsels; and we wish, with the swelling of persecutions pressed down, to come to the aid of the peoples too; but if this does not happen, it stands firm to suffer whatever is bitter in insults and dangers, rather than not to turn away from the Manichean foulness and filth.

*Augustine.* How much you aid the Manicheans, when you do not attribute the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb[^13] to the just judgment of God on account of original sin, and so make room for an alien nature of evil, which their insane error hands down, both we have often admonished, and, where it shall seem opportune, we will not cease to admonish. Now, since you so boast that you are strong, that nonetheless you commend even your, though as you say "moderate," vows on behalf of many, by which, as you say, "with the swelling of persecutions pressed down, you wish to come to the aid of the peoples"; I ask of you whether you vow this from the Lord. For if you do not, these are not Christian vows; but if you do, how do you hope, your vows being heard, that the Lord will bestow this? Namely, that he turn the hearts of human beings which are adverse to you into favor and love of you. If you believe this, you have profited: he has now begun to convert you yourselves, to change you yourselves for the better. Think, I beg, of this, and retain it, and confess at last that the omnipotent God works the wills in the hearts of human beings, and converts the averse; thus you will be wise concerning his mercy and grace; and where he does not do this, judgments hidden, but just. Thus perhaps he will rather hear our vows, that he may convert you to the Catholic faith, as a little while ago your Turbantius, but now indeed already ours, by a like operation and commiseration of his.

**V. 5.** *Julian.* But let us now approach the cause. It became clear, both in the earlier and in the present work, that natural concupiscence, without which there cannot be the mingling of the sexes, was instituted by God, who is the founder both of human beings and of cattle[^14]. Which avails so much, by the confession too of my adversary, that without the disparagement of it—that is, of carnal concupiscence—and without the defamation of mingling, natural sin cannot at all be asserted.

*Augustine.* Whether you please to call concupiscence natural or carnal, we censure that concupiscence by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, and draws to unlawful things, unless the spirit too more strongly has desires opposed to it. This dissension we say was not in paradise, when those who were there were naked and were not confounded. That it began to be after sin, the matter itself cries out, since after sin they covered the shameful parts, which before were not shameful. Nor was it shamelessness that they were naked before, but innocence; for shamelessness too is a vice; but they, when it did not shame them to be naked, surely did not have a vice. This evil, then, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, the heretic Julian says is good; this evil, the other heretic, Mani, says is mingled into us from an alien nature of evil; this evil, saying that it turned into our nature through the transgression of the first man, the Catholic Ambrose conquers both.

**V. 6.** *Julian.* Which, since it was shown by us with that faculty which truth supplied, the prudent reader ought not to doubt further about this; wherever this occurs in the writings of the Traducianist (without which, nonetheless, he cannot gape), let it move the hearers in nothing, but let it protest to the shame of its author; but we, henceforth, if the matter compels, will touch it with a necessary brevity. He accuses, then, what I said: *That mingling of bodies, with heat, with pleasure, with seed made by God, is approved as in its own measure praiseworthy*. But he passed over what I subjoined: *But of a thing which, instituted naturally, becomes sometimes even an ample gift of the devout, the devil dares not, even with your brow, claim anything for himself*.

*Augustine.* He passed this over who sent the paper to which I was answering; perhaps understanding what you do not understand, you who spoke so incautiously as to say: "Of a thing instituted naturally, which becomes sometimes even an ample gift of the devout, the devil dares not claim anything for himself"; when we see that the devil claims for himself the very human beings, who surely were instituted naturally. Or perhaps they are not human beings, who are rescued from the power of darkness, of which the devil holds the principality? Or are you so out of your senses as to affirm that the devil does not claim for himself those whom he possesses, and holds subject to his power? But, to be silent about those whom you can say are possessed by the devil through their own evil will; what will you say of that one, of whom his father, with the Lord asking, answered that from his infancy he was vexed by an unclean spirit[^15]? Did not the devil claim for himself, to afflict, his members and senses—all of which were instituted naturally by God as author, and are common gifts of the impious and the devout? Which, although he could not do, unless he received the power from God, the good and just creator of man, he nonetheless does, and shows your words to be most empty, you who say: "Of a thing instituted naturally, which becomes sometimes even an ample gift of the devout, the devil dares to claim nothing for himself." For you ought to have said, of those good things which are instituted naturally, that the devil does not claim nothing for himself, but that he himself creates nothing. These things perhaps that man saw who excerpted from your books some things to send to his friend; and, in order to pass over these words of yours, he spared you. But I indeed give thanks, because you admonish me what I ought to say against your error. Inquire, then, the merits of the little ones, whom the devil claims for himself to be vexed; which, since you do not find proper, confess to be original. For if you persist in denying even these, you are convicted of accusing the judgment of God, who allows his image undeservedly to suffer these things from the devil.

**V. 7.** *Julian.* With these things, then, passed over, he accuses why I did not say: "with disordered desire"; and he subjoins, as the sharpness of his teaching befitted: "The gift of the devout is the fruitful propagation of children, not the shameful mingling of members; which a sound nature would not have in generating children, but now the same nature, vitiated, has. And through this, even he who is born thence needs to be reborn." How consistently all things! He calls the gift of the devout the existence of children; but this he establishes under the devil, which he affirms God gives, that is, children. But the disordered desire which is in the shameful mingling of members, he calls diabolical; which he does not deny is in the parents, whom nonetheless he absolves from fault. Would you say that this man was born of a man? What they do, he says, the parents, is diabolical, but they are not guilty; that children are born is a divine work, but they are guilty. And does he still think himself to have struggled not against God, but against the demon? They most deservedly suffer this fury, who think that there is natural sin.

*Augustine.* You rather rage against God, whom without doubt you slander as unjust, if the children of Adam, drawing from him—as you assert—no evil merits, he nonetheless presses with a heavy yoke from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb[^16]—which you are not permitted to deny; and, believing that the dull will read my words and yours, you say I said what I did not say. For when would I say: *What the parents do is diabolical*; when I proclaim that the thing chaste marriages do, mingled with the intention of procreating, is a good work? But that this mingling would not have been going to be shameful, if the sin of man, by which nature was vitiated, had not preceded; whence the concupiscence of the flesh was made such that no one uses that evil well, except by resisting, with the spirit having contrary desires, its movements striving to drag to unlawful things. We do not, then, say: *What the parents do is diabolical*; for to use an evil well is so far from being diabolical that God uses the very devil too well. But this we do not deny that we say: *That children are born is the work of God; but they are guilty*; not by the work of God, by which they are created, that they may be born; but by the origin of sin, by which they are bound, if they are not reborn.

**V. 8.** *Julian.* But that Adam was to be mingled with his wife in no other way than by that manner which grew up, both the form of the members, and the blessing of God brought forth no otherwise upon cattle than upon human beings, the very history too attests; which, just as it showed that the nature of bodies was formed, so it does not assert that it was changed. Against which testimony of universality nothing is found in the law of God; except in the books of the Manichean, who comments that this concupiscence was breathed in by the prince of darkness.

*Augustine.* Not by the form of the members, because it was not changed by the sin of the first man, does it assert that the concupiscence of the flesh was such before sin as it appeared when they covered the shameful parts, and at which you do not blush, they blushed—showing that, even with the form remaining, something in them was changed. Although even the members themselves, when they are born deformed and monstrous, shame compels you to confess that in no way, if no one had sinned, would such ones be born in paradise. But the blessing of God, by which it was said: *Increase and multiply*[^17], what wonder if even nature vitiated by sin did not lose? For it was not consequent, that because it had lost immortality and happiness, it should lose also fruitfulness, which was bestowed even on irrational living things; in which the flesh, even if it has desires, does not have desires opposed to the spirit; which most wretched war or most foul reign of the thing you have taken up you try to introduce into the place of that most blessed peace and liberty; since you contend that in paradise, even if no one had sinned, the human race would have been going to be such that we should either fight against disordered desire, or serve disordered desire, if we were unwilling to fight.

**V. 9.** *Julian.* But we, both by our work and by our discourse, attest that all must be reborn by Baptism; but not so that, by the imparting of this benefit, they may seem snatched by right from the devil; but so that those who are works of God may become pledges of God; and those who are born vilely, yet not noxiously, may be reborn preciously, yet not slanderously; and those who come forth from the institutions of God may be advanced by the mysteries of God; and those who bring works of nature may obtain gifts of grace; and their Lord, who made them good by founding, may make them better by renewing and adopting. Therefore it is rightly said—you must confess it of necessity—that the sin which the Manichean had feigned natural, but which you, with the name changed, call original, has perished. Nor does that Catholic faith believe this from of old, which does not doubt both that little ones are made by God, and that no evil is made by him; and through this it establishes the works of God, before the use of free will, by the prejudgment of nature, neither guilty nor under the right of the devil.

*Augustine.* We say original sin more significantly than natural, for this reason, that it may be understood to be not of the divine work, but of human origin; chiefly for signifying that which through one man entered into the world[^18]; which does not perish by Pelagian disputation, but by Christian regeneration. But why you say that all little ones must be reborn by Baptism, we know well enough; for thence you are heretics; and by a novel pest against the antiquity of the Catholic Church you dispute, saying that the little ones are not rescued from the power of darkness by the grace of the Redeemer; when the Catholic Church exsufflates and exorcizes in them, surely the power of the devil, for it is not the image of God. What is it, then, that you say: *That those who are born vilely, yet not noxiously, may be reborn preciously, yet not slanderously*; and you do not attend their very price, by which it comes about that they are reborn preciously? For what is it, but the blood of the immaculate Lamb? Why this was shed, the Lamb himself cries out. Does he not himself say: *This is my blood, which will be shed for many unto the remission of sins*[^19]? But you, a great worker of marvels, both say that that blood is shed even for little ones, and deny that any sins are remitted to them through it; you say they must be washed, and deny they must be cleansed; you say they must be renewed, and deny they must be cleansed from oldness; you say they must be adopted through the Savior, and deny they must be saved. But we, it seems, slander them, because we say they are dead in their offenses and in the uncircumcision of their flesh; and therefore are baptized into the death of Christ, that they may die to sin, who had been dead in sin[^20]; and you defend them, who, by denying them dead, bring it about that he who has the power of death be not cast out from them; and so they obtain no benefit from the death of Christ, who *one died for all*. Which when the Apostle had said, he at once concluded and said: *Therefore all died, and he died for all*[^21]. Whence he who so defends the little ones as to deny them dead, does not defend them from death, but presses them into the second death, whom he excludes from the benefit of him who is preached to have died for none but the dead.

**V. 10.** *Julian.* After these things, all that context about Abraham and Sarah, who, with their bodies now dead, received a son as a gift[^22]—whose example how much it supports the truth, not only a wise but even a moderate reader understands—he so passed over as to say that it avails not much against him. From which shamelessness even that sentence ought to have recalled him, which is so constructed: *And, that briefly*, I say, *the sum of this disputation may be closed; if through concupiscence was rendered him whom God promised, the good things which fulfilled the faith of God are without doubt; if without concupiscence, it cannot be noxious to the one begotten, which was present neither when he was conceived nor when he was born*.

*Augustine.* Who would say that the son of Abraham was sown without the concupiscence of the flesh? For this work would not come about otherwise in the body of this death, of which the Apostle says: *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^23]. But Abraham used this evil well in conjugal intercourse; which evil was not in the body of that life which was in paradise before sin. But if for this reason the concupiscence of the flesh seems good to you, because through it offspring was rendered which God had promised; let the devil too seem good to you, because through him the blood of Christ, by which we were redeemed, was shed, which God had promised. Or confess that even through some evil some good can be rendered.

**V. 11.** *Julian.* Those things, then, being passed over, this new natural philosopher pronounces false what we said: *Just as then the mud which was taken up was the matter, not the author, of man, so now that force of pleasure, the maker and mixer of seeds, does not fill the place of the divine operation; but offers from the treasures of nature that whence God deigns to work man*. Which nonetheless he attests was rightly brought forth by me, except for that which I said—that the seeds are made by the force of pleasure; and he philosophizes in this manner: *That pleasure*, he says, *of carnal concupiscence does not make the seeds, but those which are already in bodies, founded by the true God, come about not by pleasure, but are excited and emitted with pleasure*. This indeed appears that he brought forth not by fraud, but by defect of understanding. For I pronounced the force of pleasure to be the very reason of the virile body, on which I had to impose the name of virility. That very virility, then (since we have now worn this word smooth), consisting in the structure and health of the genitals and the inmost parts, which ministers the forces both of appetite and of efficiency, was named by me the force of pleasure and of concupiscence. For I preferred to call it not pleasure simply, but the force of pleasure, that I might show that whole ardor which is felt both before the work and in the work. For neither do the debilitated in their genital members, that is, eunuchs, have seed; although surely they are moved by certain embers of an extinguished fire; but because, by the virtue of those parts by whose ministry the seeds are made from the internal humors, they have lacked a special vigor, they do not become potent for generations. So, then, God instituted, that there should be a force in the body which, unfolded at its lawful times, if health were present, would arrive at the strength of fruitfulness. The seeds, then, are made in bodies, with the aid of ripe puberty. Hence it is that a premature pleasure indeed goads the unripe, but, without the laws of years, a sterile kindling sparkles. But that the seeds are mingled with pleasure is true; that there is another pleasure, however, which floats upon the senses, another more inward of the inmost parts and nearer to the effecting, is most widely disputed among the authors of the physicians; whence that Mantuan poet too, more skilled in natural things than the petty-philosopher of the Carthaginians, marks that the herds are made lean by thinness, so that, where pleasure, once known, solicits to first couplings, they may be kept off both from leaves and from springs. *Often too they shake them with running and tire them in the sun; when the threshing-floor groans heavily with the pounded grain, and the light chaff is tossed to the winds*. This, namely, lest by too much luxury the use be made too dull for the genital field, and clog the sluggish furrows; but let them suffer a certain dryness in the secret parts; and so let the thirsty one snatch love, and store it more deeply within[^24]. But in these too, not much necessary to the cause, let it suffice to have noted the man's sharpness.

*Augustine.* Your words, which you say I passed over, how empty they are, I have shown sufficiently above; which perhaps even that man saw who sent the paper, and, sparing you, passed over the same. But what, about the force of pleasure—which you said is the maker of seeds—you, a most loquacious man, having found occasion, dispute loquaciously, I have no need to resist you; for these things, you yourself too record, are not much necessary to the cause. For I had taken it that you wished the force of pleasure to be understood as that by which pleasure could do something, not that by which it itself came about. For thus we are wont to speak, that we call the force of any thing that by which it avails to do something, not that by which avails another thing which makes it. But you, as you have now expounded your words, assert that you said the force of pleasure to be that by which pleasure can be effected, not that by which it itself effects that which it can; just as if you said the force of fire to be that by which it is kindled so as to be; when all human beings call the force of fire that by which it burns, or warms whatever it can. In an unusual manner, then, you have spoken: but what is that to us? In whatever way we have learned, where there is agreement about the thing, not to contend about words. For it is agreed between us, that not only human beings are made from seeds, but the very seeds too are works of God, in whatever manner they are effected; lest we adduce physicians or poets, where there is no need, as witnesses; or contend in what manner one must speak, when both of us perceive that to be true on account of which we speak, that is, that the seeds of all natures are works of God. But it is false, what hence you try to show—that there are for this reason no vices of seeds, because God is the most supremely good founder of seeds; which you would not say, if you knew the reason of seeds in the way that he knew it who said: *Man was made like to vanity*; and, that he might teach this very nature, which has fallen into mortality itself, to have merited it, he added: *His days pass like a shadow*[^25]—when he knew man was made to the likeness of God, and yet discerned from the divine institution the vice of the depraved human origin. Which even in your very words by which you bit at me, you ought to have seen. For you said: *But in these too, not much necessary to the cause, let it suffice to have noted the man's sharpness*; signifying me to be dull, surely, because I could not understand your locution in matters not much necessary to the cause, as you confess. But I require of you whence human beings are born dull; for you are not yourself so dull as to deny that either dull or sharp wits pertain to nature; although even sharp ones themselves, as we said in the foregoing, on account of this corruptible body which weighs down the soul[^26], if they be compared to the wit of the first man, are dull; who surely did not receive such a body that by it his soul should be weighed down. And so I too would distinguish, in the nature of man such as he now is, what difference there is between the vice of the wit and the handiwork of so great a craftsman; to whom without doubt the vices of human wits, however great they be, are not rightly attributed; so that by this rule you may learn to discern from his institution the original sin, though congenital to man; nor for that reason deny that it is, because God makes human beings, who does not make sin; just as the congenital vices of human wits are not for that reason to be denied, because God makes human beings, from whose divine art all viciousness is alien. But God knows so to work human beings well from a substance vitiated by sin, as he knows to work well from the very sins of human beings, whose vices are voluntary. For we see how great goods he worked from the sin of the brothers, who through envy sold their brother[^27]; and many other things of which the sacred Letters are full.

**V. 12.** *Julian.* But I wonder everywhere at the constancy of the disputant, who pronounces *that the seeds were founded by the true God, by whom bodies too are founded, though they be emitted with pleasure*. He confesses, then, that the seeds are made by God, in which he says there is a diabolical evil; and he does not blush to believe that there is made by God an evil which is imputed to the innocent.

*Augustine.* By God an evil is not made, when from an evil a good is made. For an evil is the vice of origin coming from sin, with which man is born; a good is the work of God, not without an evil; whose guilt of evil is imputed not to the innocent, as you say, but to the guilty; so that, since it was drawn by being born, it may be loosed by being reborn. For so all were, by the reasoning of the seed, in the loins of Adam, when he was condemned, and therefore he was not condemned without them; just as the Israelites were in the loins of Abraham, when he was tithed, and therefore he was not tithed without them[^28]. For they knew better than you the reasoning of the seed, who said these things, and took care to commit to letters the things that would be read in the Church of Christ, in which those born of Adam are reborn, lest they remain damned in that stock.

**V. 13.** *Julian.* Disordered desire, he says, pertains in no way to the seeds, because it was made by the devil; but to this disordered desire the spouses serve; but the seeds and the little ones from the seeds God makes. But neither are they guilty, he says, nor are they punished, the parents, who do the work of the devil; but to crime and punishments are destined the little ones, whom God founded; and unpunished is what the devil did, that is, disordered desire; through which it is taught to be good, since it does not even merit punishment; but accused and condemned is what God does; through which it is taught to be wicked, since indeed it cannot be defended from punishment even by the shame of its own author; and let it be believed that the deity does this which not even the utmost captivity could sustain. They have this issue who bring war upon truth: that they say nothing not impious, not insane; while it is established that by no oration is the state of the innocent so defended as it is by the profanity of their accusers.

*Augustine.* Do you therefore make false what I say, because you say that I say what I do not say? For I do not say that disordered desire pertains in no way to the seeds, since those are not born without disordered desire who surely are born from seed; but I say that God works without any vice of his own, even from a vitiated seed. Nor do I say that they are not guilty, and are not punished, the parents who do the work of the devil; but I say that they do not do the work of the devil, when they use disordered desire not on account of disordered desire, but on account of offspring. For so is it a good work to use the evil of disordered desire well, which the married do, as, on the contrary, it is an evil work to use the good of the body badly, which the unchaste do. Nor do I say that disordered desire is unpunished, which will be destroyed with death, when this mortal shall put on immortality[^29]. For it is not except in the body of this death, from which the Apostle desired to be set free[^30]; nor was it, or was not such, in the body of that life which man, who was made upright[^31], lost by sinning; nor, like some substance, with us set free and separated, will disordered desire migrate into another place; but, like an infirmity, it will perish in the perfecting of our salvation; although already now it ceases to be after the death of the body. For neither can that be in a dead body which cannot be except in the body of death; but that which is to perish in the death of the body is not to rise again when the body rises again without death. How, then, will it be punished or unpunished, which by perishing will not be? But unpunished will be those who, by being regenerated, lacked its congenital guilt, and, when its movements rise and press to the accomplishing of unlawful things, do not yield; and if they do something not on account of offspring concerning it, but on account of it, with their spouses, they are healed by a following pardon[^32]. But that God creates little ones from an origin deservedly and justly condemned, the very thing which he creates is good; because he creates human beings, and human beings even evil are some good, insofar as they are human beings; nor does he withhold from them the goodness of creating, whom he foreknew would be damned—nay, knows to be already originally damned; whence we must give thanks that so many of them are set free from the due punishment through an undue grace. But if you judge it cruel that little ones are damned, whom you do not think draw original sin; let it seem cruel to you that little ones are not snatched from this life, who, according to you, have utterly no sin, whom God surely knows will die in many and great sins with no change for the better; for, according to human reasonings, it seems more cruel not to set free, when he could, one stained by no small or great sins, than to damn the offspring of a sinner. Further, since you cry out, by whatever voice you can, that the former is just; with what brow do you contend that this is unjust?

**V. 14.** *Julian.* After these things he tries to assail that which we proved by the testimony of the apostle Paul—that God works man from seeds. And he argues that I committed fraud, who wished to fit to this place those words which it was established were brought forth about grains; as though I either there, as he here esteems, made the sentence of the Apostle divided, or recalled the testimony for any other reason than to show that its end is that God must be believed the former of all seeds. For blessed Paul, after he had conciliated the faith of the resurrection by the examples of the daily multiplication, brought in that which could touch the whole of nature, saying: *But God gives it a body as he wills, and to each of the seeds its own body*[^33]; that is, to every seed he attributes the body which its property demands. Not, then, what was said of grains did I wish to be understood of man; but this which was said—that to each of the seeds its own body is conferred by God as author—I seized for your destruction, by whose teaching this is denied. By no means, then, as you suppose, did I superfluously make mention of that sentence, nor did I, as you lie, fraudulently abuse it; nor do you, as you forswear yourself, believe that man is made by God from human seeds; which I confirm not by opining, but by understanding your faith.

*Augustine.* In what manner you set down the apostolic testimony, which he used of the seeds that are sown in the earth, because they are not made alive unless they die (for this the disputation about the resurrection of bodies, in which he was engaged, demanded), let him who reads those things of yours understandingly notice, and what we answered you to them in the same book which you now try to refute[^34]; and he will find that you, as far as pertains to the matter, both there said nothing, and here say nothing. For if with great effort you strive to show that God works human beings from seeds, as though this were denied by us; and you adduce the Apostle as witness, where no necessity of the cause demands proof from you; and—what is more insipid—you wish it to be taken of the seeds of human beings, what he said of the seeds of grains, since the cause demanded it; and you say his words: *What you sow is not made alive*; and you are silent about what he connects, *unless it dies*. You are silent too about that which he adds and says: *And what you sow, you sow not the body that is to be, but a bare grain, perhaps of wheat or of some of the rest*[^35]; where he opens sufficiently of what he speaks. With which things kept silent, you thrust in what follows: *But God gives it a body as he wills, and to each of the seeds its own body*[^36]; and you do not want it understood here of which seeds, that is, *of wheat, or of some of the rest*, namely those which, when they are sown, are not made alive unless they die. And you seek to transfer this saying even to human seeds; of which, although it could be truly said that God gives them a body as he wills, and to each of them its own body; it cannot nonetheless be said that the seed of man, when it is poured into the lap of the woman, is not made alive unless it dies. But plainly it can be said of the body of man; for it will not rise again unless it dies, on account of which the Apostle said all that about the seeds of grains. Not without reason, then, did it seem to me that you for this reason kept silent, in this testimony, those words by which it would appear of what seeds he was speaking—the witness you adduced—lest a reader with a watchful mind be admonished (if at least you could foresee this) that human beings could so have been sown in paradise in the genital fields of women through the genital members of men, as grains are sown in the lands by the hands of farmers; so that to sow a man no goad of disordered desire would incite, just as to bear a man no pain would press. To whom this tranquility is displeasing, I ask, what in the flesh, except what shames them, pleases them? Nor surely would carnal concupiscence shame, if it freed only this, which the mind commanded, and when it pleased, and as much as it commanded. Now, then, since it is not such, why do you give it support against us, and not rather confess it with us, either born of sin, or vitiated by sin?

**V. 15.** *Julian.* But now who of the prudent would hold his laughter, when he comes to these examples which you subjoined? For you say: *By the very words of the Apostle would be refuted the namer—ashamed not of a religious will, but of a lustful pleasure—and the shameless preacher. For from the very seeds, which farmers sow in the fields, this man can be refuted. For why should we not believe that God could have granted to the blessed man in paradise, of his own seed, what we see granted to farmers of the seed of wheat; so that it might be sown in that way in which this is sown, without any shameful disordered desire?*. How charmingly the namer—ashamed, and the shameless preacher—gives altogether disordered movements, and speaks the songs of mountebanks. But that other thing cannot be read except most joyfully; since, if Adam had not sinned, the woman could have been prepared for fruitfulness like a cornfield; perhaps so that through all the joints and through the minute little pipes of bodies, which the physicians call *poroi*, the ears of children might burst forth; and so, joyful in all her parts, she might sweat out offspring instead of lice. But if some burst forth even through the eyes, the lights would be taken from her who bears; and if from the little balls of the pupils helmeted swarms came forth, blindness would without doubt imprecate bereavement upon her. Nor surely with difficulty would the offspring—not born, but sweated out—be killed, and there would be a race, as among the fables of the Myrmidons, so among the teachings of the Manichean, either lice-bearing or louse-producing. But this would be the childbearing of the woman; the man, however, what could he effect? He would apply, surely, not members, but tools, and, empty of genitals, would press in ploughshares and mattocks. Great and most ample thanks, then, are owed to the error of the first human beings, by which the torments of so blessed a nature were avoided. More mildly childbirth deals with women, and the husband, than if either they felt ploughs, or grew wild over their whole body with an importunate fruitfulness. Let the faces of the Manicheans be filled with ignominy, and let them seek your name, O Lord[^37]. O the portents of those who slander the innocent and God—the supports of arguments and testimonies! Why, he says, should we not believe that nature could have been made otherwise than it is beheld to have been made? As though it were asked what God could do, and not what he instituted. If it is pleasing, with the disordered desire of opining, to censure the things that were made, so that we say that, because they could have been made otherwise, therefore the things that were instituted do not render testimony to a good nature; let us pronounce that, because God could have made mortals two-headed, therefore the single-headed, and those who stand on their feet, were ill made; for they could have been made headed on either side; which form is wont to appear in certain little worms, whose belly a crown born on both sides encloses, so that, beginning from either shoulder, they are understood to have their end in the middle. Let these mockeries be admitted, and what will be the end of raving? God could, then, have brought it about that human beings should sprout from the earth with flowers; as far as power, I do not deny that he could; but he did not will it, except that they be born from the sexes. Now, then, it is asked what he did, not what he could do. In which place it is a furious response to say: It is evil that it is, because God could have made it otherwise; for this is to praise God to his own disparagement, and to proclaim his omnipotence to the stain of his counsel. Not only is it no praise, but even a huge contumely, to give to his powers what you would have detracted from his wisdom, and to say that fortitude was present to God, but counsel was lacking. The reproach of counsel reaches altogether to the denial of power; he is not omnipotent, if he cannot order well. Nay, if he is failed by the sublimity of wisdom, he retains nothing of the reverence of divinity; which, because to suspect is most profane, that recurs by which your traduction is throttled. God, who made all things very good, instituted nothing in such a way that, in that genus which was made, it is approved that it could have been made either more aptly, or more reasonably. For one endowed equally with wisdom and omnipotence would not institute what a little man could by right censure. Whatever, then, in absolutely all creatures is taught to be natural, has been so supremely made that an affected emendation in these things is taught to be foolish and profane. As, then, the form of a horse, the form of an ox, have indeed in mutual comparison a distance, yet in their own genera have been allotted a harmony apt on every side, so that neither ought nor could either horse or ox be instituted otherwise than it is seen to have been formed (by which rule it is allowed to run through all swimming, creeping, walking, flying things, finally the ethereal and celestial; the form of none, surely, is convicted of being able to be instituted better than for the genus for which it was prepared); so too man, whom above the generality of walking things has indicated, has been so formed through all things that he could be fashioned by none better; who prudently received in his body both places of comeliness and places of shame, that in himself he might learn both modesty and confidence; lest he should both seem deformed, if he were veiled through all things; and be rendered slothful and negligent, if he were always made common through all things. And through this, human fruitfulness ought to take on no other members of either sex than those it has, no other reasoning of the inmost parts, no other senses, no other pleasure. Let us admonish the Manicheans, then, to cease to censure the deeds of divine wisdom, that they may correct the depravity of their opinions; since it neither pertains to the question, if human beings be said to have been going to generate otherwise than universality is witness; nor that they could have been made better than they were made, as reason, so too Scripture attests, which cries out that God made all things not only good, but even very good[^38]. And through this, as through the whole work, so here too the teaching of the Manicheans has collapsed. But in the future time we confess that there will be more glorious bodies of the blessed, needing no help. But this very thing too was most excellently instituted by the most just and most wise God, that nature should by no means pervert the state of reward; but that there should be a first step, in which natural freedom should stand, and from which, by the right of free choice, it should either descend to the depths of punishments, or strive to the summit of glories by those ways which God instituted.

*Augustine.* You altogether, Julian, did not think that human beings would read both mine and yours; but you wrote only for those who, with mine unknown or neglected, and with both not diligently inspected, would care to read and know yours alone; and would not believe that I had said anything, except what, recorded by you, as though of mine, they found in your letters. For hence I see it has come about that, because I had said: "For why should we not believe that God could have granted to the blessed man in paradise, of his own seed, what we see granted to farmers of the seed of wheat, so that it might be sown in that way without any shameful disordered desire?", you, as though answering these words of mine, spread out your empty talk, advancing and diffusing yourself so far as to boast that I thought: "If Adam had not sinned, the woman could thus have been prepared for fruitfulness, perhaps so that through all the joints and through the minute little pipes of bodies, which the physicians call pores, the ears of children might burst forth, and she, joyful in all her parts, might sweat out offspring instead of lice," and the rest which it wearies me to record, but which it did not shame you to heap up. In which you said also of the man, that *he would apply, surely, not members, but tools, and, empty of genitals, would press in ploughshares and mattocks*. These things, certainly, and of this kind (which, when not just any readers, but lovers of yours read them, they blush on your behalf, if there is in them any human sense)—would they let you chatter as my words, which you for no other reason passed over and kept silent than that you might prepare for yourself in these things broader spaces of raving? For I said that man could be sown, with the genital members obeying the nod of the will; but you kept silent about the genital members, that you might go through the joints of the woman and the minutest pipes, sweating out sons through the pores of the body like lice, and bringing them forth through the pupils of the eyes with blindness following her who bears. The genital members, I say, you kept silent, as though we said that these, if Adam had not sinned, would be lacking to human beings; that you might be able to say ridiculous things, not by urbanity, but by triviality: *that the man, empty of genitals, would press in ploughshares and mattocks upon the wife to be impregnated*. Is there, then, a question about the number and figure of the members; which, created for generating, could in their own places, safe and entire, both not need the incitement of disordered desire, and serve the command of the will? Which therefore, recorded by me, you were unwilling to record, when you set down my words, lest you impose silence on yourself, and could not open your mouth to those things—about sons bursting forth through the whole body like lice, and about the tools of farmers to be applied for impregnating women—which seemed to you to sound with a most festive raillery, when they were said with a most inept vanity. Whence neither did you think that must be touched, that I, in the same place which you had undertaken as though to be refuted, set down about the pains of those giving birth. For if women bore without the torments of bringing forth, I think that not the genital members, but the penal torments would have been lacking to them. Further, the divine Scripture (which all who read know) attests that this kind of torment passed into the female sex from the sin of Eve[^39]. This you preferred to pass over rather than to handle in my words, lest it be said to you that, just as women could, with the female genitals safe and entire, bring forth without a groaning pain, so spouses could, with the genitals of either sex safe and entire, in that happiness of paradise, lie together without shameful disordered desire. But you prefer to place in the place of so great blessedness not only the torments and groans of those giving birth, but also the other labors and hardships of mortals—not from the time of their free choice, but from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb—rather than not place there your shameful thing you have taken up, by the very shame at least there. And yet you, who deny that nature was changed into this mortality after sin, confess that it must be changed, after the merit of a good will, into the glory of blessed immortality. To the summit of which glory the little ones—which you cannot deny—ascend by the merits not of their own, but of an alien will; whom you are unwilling to believe were cast down, by the merits of an evil alien will—but nonetheless of him in whose loins by the reasoning of the seed they were—into the depths of the miseries which we know.

**V. 16.** *Julian.* But let us now proceed to the rest. After he avoided the example of Abraham, which had been set down by me, he tried to assert that Abimelech too, who is reported, with Abraham praying, to have been healed together with his women, so that he could return to the work of generation, from which he had been suspended through a plague, can be understood to have had the womb of the women closed not by the withdrawal of disordered desire, but by some pain[^40]; as though it were asserted by us, at great cost, that natural appetite seemed to be restored to them; when I was content to prove by those testimonies only this—that intercourse, which could not be without disordered desire, was impeded through the wrath of God, and restored through the indulgence of God, whether with the obstacles removed, or with the customary incitements restored; that nonetheless it is taught to be not diabolical, but even through this pertaining to the work of God, who, among the moderate but harmless instruments of the body, would sin neither by species, nor by measure, but by excess alone.

*Augustine.* Who would not understand that, if, with God indignant, something befell the body of the women, whereby intercourse, and through this offspring (which surely could not be unless conceived by those coming together), was impeded, then, with that impediment removed, such intercourse was restored as is in the body of this death, that is, with disordered desire? For into such a state bodies are recalled when they are healed, as the nature of mortals has now been allotted after sin, which compels them into death. But in the body of that life, where man, unless he had sinned, was not going to die, there was without doubt another state; whence there was there either no disordered desire, or not such as now is, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, so that it should be necessary for it either to be subjugated or to resist; of which the one could not befit the honorableness, the other the peace of that blessedness. Do not, then, with heretical perversity, confound these two lives; in one way it is lived in a corruptible body which weighs down the soul[^41]; in another it would be lived in paradise, if the uprightness of man, in which he was created, had remained. There would be there too, then, the intercourse of spouses for the sake of generation; but either with the genital members serving the mind without any disordered desire, or with the movements of disordered desire itself, if there were any, never resisting the will; which, if it were such, would not be shameful; nor would it make the members of the body, which it would solicit by its own impulse, or move, to be properly called shameful, and compel them to be veiled; which that it came about after sin, and could come about only as a punishment of sin, the words of God attest. *Who*, he says, *told you that you are naked, unless because you ate of the tree of which alone I had commanded you that you should not eat?*[^42] Your nakedness, he says, would not have been told you, unless my law had been transgressed by you. But what is the nakedness told, which surely was not unknown to him, except that, by that movement goading, by an unwonted sight, it urged itself to be noticed, and struck shame? Since it had come about by sin that the lower part of man should have desires opposed to the higher, that is, the flesh against the spirit. But you close your eyes against all things, and, though God makes manifest that man would not be confounded at his nakedness unless he had sinned, you assert that it was so instituted that, even if he had not sinned, his nakedness would shame him. For God says: Who told you your nakedness, except that you sinned? And you say (that I may set down your very words, which a little before you spoke): *Man is so formed through all things that he could be fashioned by none better; who prudently received in his body both places of comeliness and places of shame, that in himself he might learn both modesty and confidence; lest he should both seem deformed, if he were veiled through all things, and be rendered slothful and negligent, if he were always made common through all things*. And through this, according to you, man was made far better by sinning; for unless he had sinned—whom God made upright[^43]—he would live imprudently, by not discerning in his body the places of comeliness and the places of shame, and shamelessly, by veiling nothing, and negligently, by making all things common; for he would not avoid these vices, unless his nakedness had been told him because he had sinned.

**V. 17.** *Julian.* Of which, since enough has been done, let us hasten to those things which the Manichean once objected acutely concerning natural evil, but, as I will prove, deceived by the perplexity of questions. Yet briefly first let us ventilate what Augustine reported against the testimony of the Apostle. I, then, when I had said that this known work of the sexes was instituted by God the author of bodies, and had said it was most plainly proved even by the testimony of blessed Paul, who, inveighing against the disgraces of those whom madness had cast headlong even into the intercourse of the male sex, says: *Having left the natural use of the woman, they were inflamed in their desires*[^44]—I inferred, with the Apostle as witness, that the use of the woman, naturally instituted, is approved. To this, then, this man rescribing: *The Apostle did not say*, he says, *the conjugal use, but the natural, wishing that to be understood which is done with the members created for this, that through them either sex might be mingled for generating; and through this, when one is mingled with a harlot too with the same members, it is a natural use, yet not praiseworthy, but culpable. Not, then, by this name, that is, the natural use, is the conjugal mingling praised; but the uncleaner and more wicked disgraces were noted, than if they used women unlawfully, but nonetheless naturally*. That is, this use of the woman, which the Apostle pronounced natural, is not understood as conjugal, so as to be taught good and lawful; but for this reason, he says, it was called natural, because it indicates the diversity of the sex instituted for this, that it might be prepared both for mingling and for childbirth. By which devisings, since he was helped in nothing, why did he dwell so long? Surely for this alone: that those who follow him may think dissolved what they shall have seen was contacted; for the rest, how nothing he said, a brief disputation will make plain. Surely the Apostle says that the use of the woman was naturally instituted, nor did he commend the other mingling first ordained; but, disputing about that use in which he knew disordered desire to have flourished in all times, he called it natural.

*Augustine.* The natural use of the woman is when her male uses that member by which the nature of living beings of the same kind is propagated; on account of which that very member too is wont properly to be called nature; whence Cicero says that a woman saw in dreams that she had been marked as having a nature[^45]. The natural use, then, is both lawful, as in marriage, and unlawful, as in adultery; but against nature it is always unlawful, and without doubt more disgraceful and more foul; which the holy Apostle reproached both in women and in men, wishing them to be understood more damnable than if, in a natural use, they sinned either by adultery or by fornication. The natural and likewise inculpable use, then, of those coming together both could have been in paradise, even if no one had sinned; for not otherwise would children be begotten for the multiplying of the human race according to the blessing of God; but that the Apostle called natural use that in which he knew disordered desire to have flourished in all times—who told you, except your heresy? For far be it that the Apostle should believe that the shameful disordered desire of human beings flourished even at that time when they were naked and it did not shame them. Yet even if the Apostle said what you yourself said—"that in the natural use of the woman disordered desire has flourished in all times"—in these words too I would have what I might rightly understand, lest I should, as you do, with a most foolish mind, a most loquacious tongue, a most shameless brow, place your shameful thing you have taken up in the bodies of that blessed life, which were not yet the bodies of this death. For in all times, from when the intercourse of either sex began to come about, without doubt the natural use of the woman could not be without this shameful disordered desire; for already they had the body not of that life, but of this death, when, after sin, having gone forth from paradise, male and female first mingled either sex naturally. But if they had done it before, there either would have been no disordered desire there, or it would not have been shameful; for it would not solicit the unwilling, nor compel the chaste to resist it; but either, without it, the genitals would perform their office, with the mind commanding; or it, if there were any, would rise when there was need, following the most tranquil nod of the will, nor oppressing the thought by a turbulent impulse of pleasure. That it now is not such, by its many importunate movements to be coerced it confesses; therefore it attests either to be a vice, or itself to be vitiated. Behold whence the Apostle said: *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^46]. Behold whence original sin is drawn from those being born. This evil conjugal modesty uses well; this evil widowed religious continence does not use, better; this evil sacred virginal integrity does not use, better still.

**V. 18.** *Julian.* This we understood, and, when we were speaking of the institution of nature, we brought forth what it was established the Apostle had thought. What, then, did you advance, that you should report that the conjugal use was not called by him, but the natural? Or with what face do you subjoin that, "since one is mingled with a harlot with the same members, it is a natural use, yet not praiseworthy, but culpable"? For, that here too we may show what we have frequently laid open—that not even a single sentence is brought forth by you which does not avail very much against you—if the use of fornication is called natural, yet not praiseworthy, but culpable, for this reason because it is the use of a harlot; without doubt you will profess the conjugal use, because it is honorable and lawful, to be not culpable, but praiseworthy.

*Augustine:* It is not because it is without evil, but because it makes good use of an evil, that conjugal intercourse is rightly called blameless. For it is good to make good use of an evil, just as it is evil to make bad use of a good. Thus married people make good use of the evil of lust in the same way that adulterers make bad use of the good of the body. I have already said this more than once, and I will not grow tired of saying it again and again, as long as you are not ashamed to contradict the truth.

**V. 19.** *Julian:* And where is that diabolical crime which you were striving to fasten upon it by the argument from shame? For lust is no longer being blamed—lust which, in both forbidden and permitted use, is felt as a condition of nature itself—if only its perversion, when it runs out toward what was not lawful, is faulted.

*Augustine:* It is not only "the perversion of lust when it runs out toward what was not lawful" that is faulted; rather, great is your own perversion, since you do not fault it even when it impels toward what was not lawful. For when it impels toward what is not lawful, it certainly runs out unless its depravity is resisted. And this is the concupiscence of the flesh, by which *the flesh lusts against the spirit*[^47]; and it is for this reason that *the spirit* too *lusts against* the flesh, that it may not run out to where lust impels. Therefore that which impels toward evil is itself evil; but if it does not run out, because the spirit resists it, the man is not overcome by evil. He will then be free of all evil when there will be nothing for him to resist. And when this comes to pass, an alien nature will not, as the Manichee madly holds, be separated from us, but our own nature will be healed. For if our nature now, just as it is healed of guilt by regeneration and the forgiveness of sins, were likewise already healed of all infirmity, the spirit would not lust against the flesh so that we might do only what is lawful; rather, the flesh would so consent to the spirit that nothing unlawful would be desired against it.

**V. 20.** *Julian:* And to put the question pointedly and briefly: when the apostle Paul named the *natural use of the woman*[^48], do you think he indicated both the possibility and the honorableness of the act, or only its possibility? That is, by this word "natural," did he mean us to understand a use that could and ought to take place, or one that could but ought not? If you say, One that could, though it ought not—such as goes on among adulterers—then that other shameful act too will not be against nature, since it is carried out by natural members. But if, taking fright, you reply, as the truth also holds, that the Apostle called natural the use destined for offspring, carried out honorably—indeed naturally, as it could and ought, that is, in the bodies of women whether one or several, permitted, however, by the dispensation of the times—then you will admit beyond doubt that you have argued ineptly, and that the blessed Paul, by the term "natural use," indicated not fornication, as you had supposed, but the honorable and lawful union of bodies that is open to fruitfulness. We therefore rightly defend in general what the Manichee attacked in general. For you say that this commingling of the sexes with pleasure was instituted by the devil, and is the cause of original sin and the necessity of all crimes; and through this you charge nature itself. What more consistent thing could we do than, with the Teacher of the Gentiles as witness, defend by the generality of nature, and ascribe to the work of God, what you called naturally evil? And so it has come about that what you call diabolical, we have proved, in refuting you, to have been naturally instituted. For this is the lawful and learned reply: that what is accused in the species be defended in the species, and what is charged in the genus be vindicated in the genus. This the Manichee too understood, whom you match in crime but do not match in talent; and so he assigns the whole substance of bodies to the devil, while you assign not the whole, but, as we said in the earlier book, the better part. Truth therefore has triumphed, hedged about with sacred defenses, which by the Apostle, proving the conjugal business natural and therefore pertaining to God, who is the author of nature, has shattered your fabrications, since you swear by what belongs to transgression, not to nature.

*Augustine:* We have already said enough above as to what use of the woman the Apostle called natural, and why he called it natural—namely, when it is done with those members of each sex which were instituted for propagating nature; whether such a use as could have existed in paradise, employing no evil, since either none existed or there was a lust that only followed the bidding of the will; or such as it now is from the time it began to be, whether lawful as in marriage, making good use both of the good of the body and of the evil of lust, or unlawful as in adultery, making bad use both of that good and of that evil—yet even this not departing from those members which are properly called by the very name of nature. There is therefore no reason for you to ask, as you say, pointedly and briefly, whether by the phrase "natural use" the Apostle meant the use which both could and ought to take place, or the use which could but ought not. For the Apostle was not, in saying this, looking to any of these, but only to the members of each sex that are genitally natural, that is, created for generating nature. For who does not know that the lawful use of the woman both can and ought to take place, while the unlawful use can take place, yet ought not, and that both are natural, since both are done by the genital members created in each sex for propagating nature? Take away your shifty evasions; remove the wordy and deceitful fog of your vanity. The lust of beasts is for this reason not a fault, because their flesh does not lust against the spirit; and if the Manichee had been able to discern this, he would neither have alienated the natures of beasts from the work of the true God, nor have thought human faults to be substances. But unless you think and hold with Ambrose and the other Catholics that, through the transgression of the first man, the discord of flesh and spirit passed over into our nature, then however much you may seem to detest the Manichees, you will without doubt remain their detestable helper: by asserting to be good what truth proclaims to be evil, and by denying that this evil comes from the perversion of our nature vitiated by sin—so that the Manichee, with you as his helper, may introduce into us a commingling of an alien nature.

**V. 21.** *Julian:* With like sharpness you try to undermine this also, which I said—that on the gospel testimony a tree ought to be known by its fruits—so that I might show what is plain: that marriages cannot be taught to be good, nor indeed nature itself, which is supplemented by the operation of marriages, nor can it be vindicated as the work of God, if crimes were said to sprout from it. To this, then, you replied: *Was the Lord speaking of that, and not rather of two wills of human beings, the good and the evil—calling the one a good tree, the other an evil tree, since from a good will good works are born, and evil works from an evil will? But if we understand marriage to be the good tree, then on the contrary we shall have to set fornication down as the evil tree. Moreover, if he should say that adultery is not to be put there in the place of the tree, but rather human nature, from which a human being is born, then here too the tree will not be marriage, but human nature, from which a human being is born.* You are mistaken: the Lord is not there speaking of two wills, but of his own person. For when he was bestowing countless benefits on the Jews, they never ceased from their accusations against him. Since they could not censure his works, which even glorified him, they alleged that he—a Samaritan and full of a demon and of the spirit of Beelzebul—was mocking them. Then, therefore, the Lord said: *Either make the tree good, and its fruit good, or make the tree bad, and its fruit bad; for the tree is known by its fruit*[^49]. That is: Either disparage my works, which proclaim themselves good by infirmities driven out and health restored—so that by the testimony of my own works you may prove me wicked; or, if you do not dare to censure these great benefits, render to the good tree, namely to me, the testimony of my fruits, and love the benefactor, you who proclaim the benefits. There, therefore, Christ commanded his person to be recognized from his works; and this rightly supported us, that we might teach that nature too and marriages are to be judged from the quality of their fruitfulness, so that if the poison of crimes flowed from them, the root too should be judged criminal. See, then, how blind you are in understanding, you who thought to lighten the weight of my objection by setting fornication opposite to marriage, so that, as marriage is a good tree, so fornication might seem an evil tree—from which, that is, from fornication, no fruitfulness ought to exist, lest it be convicted of being evil, if good marriages were proven good by good offspring—whereas a human being, whether born of marriage or of adultery, comes forth not from the shameful deed but from the nature of the seeds. For the shameful deed, committed by the will of those who commit adultery, does not disturb the institutions of the substance; rather, nature exercises itself through its own materials, and, while the sin remains with the author of the unlawful will, an innocent offspring breaks forth from the work of the Creator. This indeed you too saw must be granted; but the prudent reader should observe how you have tried to evade it. For you say: *If adultery is not to be put there in the place of the tree, but rather human nature, from which a human being is born, then here too the good tree will not be marriage, but human nature, from which a human being is born.* This, then, is what you tried to say: As a human being is not imputed to fornication, but to nature, so the sin that is drawn from lawful parents is to be imputed not to marriage, but to human nature, which the devil infected with ancient crime. In adulterers, then, you faulted the will of the wanton, but praised human nature, from which a human being is born even through unlawful intercourse; in lawful parents, however, you praised marriage, from which you say sin does not come, but you found fault with nature, from which you say a dreadful crime is poured in. Let my reader therefore be watchful here. If you judged human nature praiseworthy in fornication, since it made the cause of the one being born unpolluted by the crimes of those uniting, how do you blame this same nature in the matter of marriage, which you say furnished the cause for a natural crime? Therefore you have professed not marriage, indeed, but human nature to be both a great good and a great evil. For what is more wicked than it, if it engendered crime? What more detestable, if it is possessed by the devil? Whatever artifice it has in the seed, let it see to that itself; meanwhile, in the matter of quality, in which all good or evil lies, it is proven utterly base, if it is itself guilty, and the begetter of guilt, and convicted as a satellite of diabolical tyranny. Rightly, then, ought a tree to be recognized by its fruits, so that what is the cause of evil may with the best right be named evil.

*Augustine:* That neither marriage nor adultery is the cause of original evil, the matter itself sufficiently declares; since that which is good in human nature is born of a human being by God's creating, and that which has the evil on account of which it must be reborn is drawn from a human being. The cause, moreover, of this evil is that *through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed to all men, in whom all sinned*[^50]. With how much wordiness you have labored in vain to twist these words of the Apostle into another meaning, those see who read your writings and ours with understanding. What good, then, does it do you? Why, I ask, did you bring up the gospel testimony that *a good tree bears good fruit*[^51], when you were speaking of the good of marriage, and wished its fruit to be human beings—as though thereby showing that they are born without evil, because marriage is good, and *a good tree cannot bear bad fruit*[^52]—when human beings are born of human beings, either with the original harm, whence the Apostle says *the body is dead because of sin*[^53], or without any harm, as you contend against the Apostle, and that not through conjugal intercourse alone, but also through shameless intercourse? And sometimes marriages are sterile and adulteries fruitful? Now whether the Lord wished to suggest by the two trees two wills, as we say—one good, by which a human being is good, which cannot do evil works, that is, bad fruits; the other evil, by which a human being is evil, which cannot do good works, that is, good fruits—or whether, as you say, he spoke these things to the Jews about himself, those who wish to know read the Gospel and disregard you. For the Lord, when he showed that we must beware of those who come in sheep's clothing, but inwardly are ravening wolves, said: *By their fruits you will know them. Do men gather grapes from thorns, or figs from thistles? So every good tree bears good fruit, but the bad tree bears bad fruit. A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit*[^54]. And according to Luke, when the hypocrites were being rebuked, these two trees were mentioned, and at once plainly expounded, the Lord going on to say: *The good man out of the good treasure of his heart brings forth good, and the evil man out of evil brings forth evil; for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks*[^55]. But where he says, *Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad* (which you suppose he said about himself), what he meant he at once makes clear, saying: *For the tree is known by its fruit. You brood of vipers, how can you speak good, when you are evil? For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. The good man out of his good treasure brings forth good, and the evil man out of his evil treasure brings forth evil*[^56]. Do you see that it is you who are mistaken, not I? Return then to the cause of evil work, and you will find an evil will; return to the cause of original evil, and you will find the evil will of the first man, and a good nature vitiated by it.

**V. 22.** *Julian:* But we say these things to show what the end of your faith is. For the rest, that stands unshaken which was established in the preceding disputations: that there is nothing evil except the work of the will, which the justice of the one committing it forbids; and that what is natural cannot be convicted of being evil. There remains, therefore, this unshaken tower, from whose height the robberies of various errors are repelled.

*Augustine:* What is it you say? Or what are your preceding disputations, but most wordy vanities? What is it you say: *that there is nothing evil except the work of the will, which the justice of the one committing it forbids*? Then the evil will itself is not evil, if there is nothing evil but its work? For it does not follow that an evil will should also have the capacity of carrying out the work; and so, on your authority, it will not be evil for a man to will badly when he cannot do it. Who could bear such folly, or rather such madness? Where we may also point out that, if there is nothing evil but the work of the will which the justice of the one committing it forbids, then whatever men do or suffer unwillingly will not be evils; that will not be evil which the Apostle cries out about: *For I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^57]; the punishment of eternal fire will not be evil, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth[^58]—because no one suffers it willingly, and it is not the work of the will which the justice of the one committing it forbids, but the punishment of one who is unwilling. When would you understand such things, if you were not so wonderfully out of your senses, or rather raving? What too is it that you say, "nor can what is natural be convicted of being evil"? Is it really so (to say nothing of the countless natural faults of the body) that natural deafness is not an evil—deafness which hinders even that faith by which the righteous man lives[^59], since *faith comes from hearing*[^60]? But unless you were inwardly deaf, you would hear with the ears of the heart the Apostle saying: *For we too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^61]. But go on still, and cry out with blind and deaf hearts: It is not an evil to be naturally forgetful, naturally dull, naturally irascible, naturally lustful. Why indeed do you not securely fling out these foolish words, by which not even natural foolishness itself is an evil? To such a degree, indeed, do you who deny that all original evil is deserved find yourselves compelled to praise all natural faults, that you say that not only those deformed, weak, and monstrous in body, but even the foolish, would have arisen in paradise had no one sinned there—while yet you place amid the delights of that most blessed place your shameful protégée, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit.

**V. 23.** *Julian:* But now let us pursue what the topic requires. It is plain, most subtle disputant, that you have extolled one and the same thing—human nature, namely—with great praises, but stained it with greater charges; and since this cannot happen at one time, by one act, and by one design, so natural reason in no way admits these things, even under contrary alternation, but always full of one thing, that is, of the estimation of the good, never on account of the evil, since its institutions do not, through their author, admit of unworthiness, unless it be bitten by the most polluted tooth of the Manichees. But this being finished, I inquire this: which smoke-screens of marriage you think are to be followed. For if you confess that marriage is not the cause of the human being, but nature; and that the cause of sin too is not marriage, but nature; then this marriage, to which you were giving praise, has utterly vanished. To what thing, then, is it ascribed, if it is the cause neither of your evil nor of my good? If you remove the human being from the honorableness of marriage, lest you be forced to ascribe him to fornication as well; if you remove sin from the necessity of marriage, lest you seem to condemn marriage—what has remained worthy of praise in the possession of wedlock? Why do you fear to violate by speech what you have utterly undermined by your disputation once brought to bear? Of what thing, then, will it be said to be the cause, if it shares in neither the natural evil nor the natural good? Does the name and honor of wedlock, then, now do nothing in human affairs? But you are distressed, and grievously so; help, then, must be brought to the panting old man. There has remained something you can give it, without which, however, nothing else is found—namely, that you say this marriage stands at the door, so as to let no rumor of obscenity rush in upon the pleasure of those uniting, but to claim for that business, by its own title, honorableness and modesty. Without cause, then, did you wish by treacherous praise to corrupt the trustworthiness of marriage; to none is it more hostile than to you. It utterly drives you off, and does not let the tongues of the Manichees creep in to tear at the commingling committed to its protection. They have, it says, in their own teaching, brothels worthy of them, in which they may be satisfied in the hours of the night; the watchmen of marriage guard that pleasure of the bashful, crimes are kept off, and the honor of honorableness is admitted; the privileges granted to it by the Apostle defend honorable marriages and undefiled beds; but *fornicators and adulterers God will judge*[^62]. Where, then, is the criminal commingling, if the authority of wedlock, which you were praising, attends upon its business and its secret?

*Augustine:* You indeed, in saying that a tree is known by its fruits, wished this gospel testimony to serve not nature but marriage. For these are your words: *If, then, original evil is drawn even from marriage, the coming together of marriage is the cause of evil, and that must be evil through which and from which evil fruit appeared, as the Lord says in the Gospel:* " *The tree is known by its fruits* "*. How*, you say, *are you to be thought worthy of a hearing, you who say that marriage, from which you declare nothing but evil to come forth, is good? It is established, therefore*, you say, *that marriages are guilty, if original sin is derived from them, and that they cannot be defended unless their fruit is approved as innocent; but they are defended and pronounced good; their fruit, therefore, is approved as innocent.* Surely by these words of yours it is clear enough that you wished the tree to be understood as marriage, and the fruits of the tree as those offspring who are born of the union of married people. But since you were shut out from this by the most manifest reason—for such offspring arise from adulterers too—you thought you must flee to nature, in whose depths you might lie hidden; though you were not treating of nature when, for the sake of the good of marriage and its good fruits, you blindly applied the likeness of the gospel tree. Defend nature, then, against original sin: leave marriages aside, call nature itself the good tree, because whether from marriages or from adulteries, it begets human beings; whom you therefore call the good fruits of a good tree, lest they be believed to have drawn from a depraved origin, by generation, any guilt to be loosed by regeneration—lest they need a savior, lest they be redeemed by blood poured out in the forgiveness of sins. Do these things, as the detestable heretic you are; fill the paradise of God, even if no one had sinned, with the lusts of those desiring, the struggles of those contending against lusts, the pains of those giving birth, the wailings of crying infants, the sicknesses of the ailing, the funerals of the dying, the grief of those mourning. So act—this befits you; for such, on your authority, are the punishments that follow the good fruits of the good tree, and introduce a paradise of delights—but a Pelagian one. Indeed you, a sharp dialectician, even mock my disputation, saying that I extolled one and the same thing, namely human nature, with great praises, but stained it with greater charges. But I delight to have as my master not Aristotle or Chrysippus, much less vain Julian with his wordiness, but Christ; who surely, unless human nature were a great good, would not have become man for it, since he was God; and unless it had died by the great evil of sin, would not have died for it, since he himself had come and remained without sin. Yet again, as though human nature were not enough for you—being such, born of adulterers, as it is born of married people—you think we must be pressed about the goodness of marriage, asking what marriages do in human affairs, if neither the evil is to be imputed to them, which is drawn not from them but from the origin vitiated by sin, nor the good, since a human being is born even of adulterers. And because we have found the honorableness of marriage to be distinct from the baseness of unlawful intercourse, you suppose it is thereby concluded that no original evil is drawn from conjugal intercourse; not seeing that if the good of marriage were the cause why those born of married people should draw no evil, then surely the evil of adultery would be the cause why those born of adulterers should draw evil. Marriage therefore has its honorable place in human affairs, not so that human beings might be born—who would be born anyway, even if, by no law of marriage, each sex came together indiscriminately by natural use—but so that they might be born by an ordered propagation; and that, as mothers are made certain by the birth, so fathers too may be held certain by the fidelity of marriage; and that your shameful protégée may not stray through any women whatever, the more basely the more freely. But it is not because a human being is born from marriage with a certain father that he therefore does not need a savior through whom he may be reborn, so as to be freed from the evil with which he is born. There is therefore in marriages no criminal commingling, as you slanderously claim we say; but the chastity of married people is to be praised for this reason, that it alone can make good use of the evil which you praise in unseemly fashion.

**V. 24.** *Julian:* These things, then, being settled, I will show, if only briefly, how deep in the abyss of ignorance you lie—you who up to now were held to be most subtle and most acute. You say you ascribe to nature the crime that passes into offspring, yet not to marriage; just as, on the other side, you attach human beings to nature, not to the shameful deed. And so you pretend, indeed, to praise wedlock, lest you be openly caught as a Manichee; but you find fault with nature, in which you confess that evil is present, and from which you confess evil is propagated. Have you, then, never been able to hear the rules of disputation and the established principles of the soundest reasoning? For in all the categories the genera embrace more than the species; and from this too arise subaltern genera; while the species embrace more than the individuals; and there are genera by which species are contained, and special species by which individuals are enclosed. The shattering, then, of the lower does not shake the higher; but the higher, by their falls, involve all that they embraced. For example, "animal" is a genus; but by the breadth of its meaning it comprises diverse species, namely of man, horse, ox, and so on. If, then, one species perishes, that genus does not feel destruction; for suppose the nature of oxen should fail from the world; the genus is surely not destroyed, the nature of the other animals remaining. But on the contrary, if that which is "animal" be removed from the world, all the species which were enclosed under this genus undoubtedly perish; for no species of living thing will remain when "animal" is utterly destroyed. Those things, then, which are higher impart their outcomes and merits to the species which they embrace; yet it does not run back the other way, so that they should be changed by the varieties of their species as of certain dependents. And to apply the example to the matter: the generality of human nature, of the institutions placed beneath it, is a kind of genus; this has, as it were, species in posture, in members, in arrangements, in movements, or in other things of the kind. It therefore imparts its quality to all the species placed under it; yet it would not be made liable to itself, so as to be mixed up with the perils of things lower than it. If, then, nature be found fault with, and be believed liable to the devil and guilty, marriage too, which is under it, and fruitfulness, and the whole substance will be condemned. Marriage, therefore, which is done according to nature, cannot be praised, if nature itself is blamed. The beauty of the cut shoots must necessarily die together with their root; and, to put the matter more plainly, the business of marriage cannot be called good, if the natural commingling is impugned; since what is devalued in the genus, to which it indivisibly adheres, cannot be honored in the species. Moreover, when an evil will uses the natural instruments for shameful deeds, that force of pleasure and of seed, which is never varied according to the will of those uniting, suffers no association with the crime; rather, it furnishes the matter to God who is at work, and the shameful deed convicts only the desert of the adulterer, not of nature. Since, then, we were disputing about natural things, you most dully praised marriages while condemning nature; whereas it is irrefutably established that the genus shares with its species whatever it takes on; and therefore either the use which the Apostle calls natural will be judged good and lawful, and marriages will be honorable, and there will be no natural sin; or, if nature is believed to be diabolical, so that there is original sin, the use of marriage too will be pronounced damnable. And then indeed not soberly, but openly, the teaching of the Manichees is taken up; which, since it is deadly, and there is among them nothing of truth, or honorableness, or faith, while among no others can the opinion of natural sin exist—then just as we are Catholics, so you are established to be Manichees.

*Augustine:* You certainly spoke of a tree, and of the fruits by which a tree is known, when you had thought that marriage and offspring were to be understood in this likeness; and being shut out from there, because such fruit can arise from adulterous intercourse too, you fled to nature. Nor could the passage of your flight lie hidden from us; for in your words, which I shall now recall, it appeared plainly enough. For you said, speaking to me myself: *With like sharpness you try to undermine this also, which I said—that on the gospel testimony a tree ought to be known by its fruits—so that I might show*, you say, *what is plain: that marriages cannot be taught to be good; nor indeed nature itself, which is supplemented by the operation of marriages, nor can it be vindicated as the work of God, if crimes were said to sprout from it*. By these words of yours you opened the passage of your flight, adding, after naming marriages, and saying: *nor indeed nature itself, which is supplemented by the operation of marriages*. You therefore distinguished these two, and showed clearly enough that nature is one thing, marriages another, by whose operation nature is supplemented. What is it, then, that afterward you wish nature to be the genus, and marriage its species? Is any genus supplemented by the operation of any species of its own? Surely not: for neither is "animal," which is a genus, supplemented by the operation of man, or horse, or ox, or any other beast, which is a species of that genus; since even if some species be lacking, and be removed from the world, the genus yet remains which embraces the other species, as you yourself also argued. And this genus would surely not remain whole, if it were supplemented by the operation of the species which has been removed; for it is no more a genus if it has more species, and no less a genus if it has fewer—although if all the species be removed, neither will there be a genus, just as, the genus being removed, there will be no species. Marriage, therefore, is not a species, nor nature a genus, if nature is supplemented by the work of marriages; just as agriculture is not a species of the crop, since the crop is supplemented by the work of agriculture. Then again, if you say nature is the genus and marriage its species, you are doubtless compelled to say that every marriage is nature. For every horse is an animal, though not every animal is a horse, precisely because the horse is a species, the animal a genus. Therefore no nature is produced by a human being; for although, as it is written, *a wife is joined to a husband by the Lord*[^63]—since it is not done without his help, when it is rightly done—who yet does not know that marriages are the works of human beings? Moreover, if a human being does not make nature, marriage is not nature, because a human being makes marriage. And thus, since marriage is not nature, it can in no way be a species of nature, as though nature were the genus. Marriages, therefore, pertain to the morals of human beings, but human beings themselves to nature. It is permitted us, then, to find fault with the evils of a vitiated nature, and to praise morals that make good use of the goods and evils of nature. I praise marriages, then; but far be it that I should praise the evil by which the flesh lusts against the spirit—without which evil no human being can be born; whose guilt cannot be loosed except by being reborn; whose good use is praised in conjugal intercourse. Accordingly, original sin, which is drawn not from the commingling of an alien nature, but from the perversion of our own, is asserted not by the Manichees, but by the Catholics; and by denying it you are heretics.

**V. 25.** *Julian:* To ward off the odium of this name, you make mention, quite in vain, of various heresies. For you say: *Just as the Arians call Catholics Sabellians, although they make a definite distinction of persons while preserving the community of nature, so the name of Manichees is imposed upon you by us; since you do not say that marriage is evil, as the Manichees say, but you say that evil passes into all human beings by the condition of nature*. But these arguments of yours, I do not doubt, will be laughed at by prudent men. For as the Arians call the true Catholics Sabellians, when by us the distinction of the persons of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit is made without any confusion and without any change of substance, while they most dully slander us, as though there were no difference between one and three—so too the Catholics rightly and deservedly pronounce you Manichees, because your faith compels this to be said by us. For the Manichees assert sin to be natural; you say it is natural. The Manichees say that the lust of bodies was planted by the devil; you confirm this with multiplied disputation. They contend that evil cannot be guarded against by free choice, since it is natural; you too, in the same terms, devise a free choice, but one through which evil can be done, while one cannot desist from evil. The Manichee says the seed is accursed; you try to prove it by the authority of the Scriptures. The Manichee says that wickedness is unconvertible; you cry out that it is so. But you say that Adam alone was of a better nature; the Manichee too says, in his letter to Patricius, that one ought to believe him better than those who followed, as though compounded from the flower of the first substance. You say that the commingling, on account of its natural motions, is diabolical, and that human beings are rightly plucked by the devil like the fruits of a shrub he himself planted; the Manichee says this too, since it is from him that you learned to believe and affirm it. The Manichee says that both nature and marriage are evil; you, however, say marriage is good, but nature guilty. You are not here more religious, but he is more prudent. As, then, it is false, what the Arians say, that the Catholics are Sabellians, so it is most true, what the Catholics say, that the Traducianists are nothing other than Manichees; and that the distinction between you came about, indeed, by an illusion, not from your faith, but from inexperience. You and the Manichee, therefore, are of one faith; but he is found less shameless than your gravity; for one will not easily find a Manichee, or another Melitidian, who would say that he condemns the nature of human beings, but does not defame marriages.

*Augustine:* Why I mentioned the Arians and Sabellians, whoever reads that book of mine with understanding soon finds out; and he sees through you as acting deceitfully, since you were unwilling to recall the whole of that same matter. For I said to you: *Just as the Arians, while fleeing the Sabellians, fell into something worse, because they dared, in the Trinity, to distinguish not persons but natures, so the Pelagians, while trying to avoid the plague of the Manichees in a perverse way, are convicted, on the matter of the fruit of marriage, of holding things more pernicious than the Manichees themselves, by believing that little ones do not need Christ the physician*. You should have set before yourself these words of mine, or at least this sentiment of mine, if you had been willing to answer me in any way; but, passing these over, you say to yourself whatever you please, so that you may seem to have answered me—not by refuting what was said by me, but by not keeping silent. And now, in saying that I hold what the Manichees hold, you are greatly mistaken; or rather, you deceive whom you can. For the Manichees say that evil is coeternal with God, and that this same evil is a substance, a certain alien nature, which can in no way be changed into good, neither by itself nor by the good God; by the commingling of which unchangeable evil they declare the good soul—which they dare to believe is of the nature of the good God—to be defiled and corrupted; and on this account to have need, at every age of a human being, of a savior, by whom, cleansed and restored and rescued from such captivity, it may be set free. But you, while fleeing the Manichees in a perverse way, have fallen into these shadows of impiety, so that, asserting little ones to be safe from all evil, you think a Savior unnecessary for the wretched; and, by helping the very Manichees you flee, you somehow meet your own error by a roundabout way, denying the evil of our vitiated nature—so that whatever evils are rightly believed, or plainly found, in little ones may be attributed (as they themselves wish) to the commingling of an alien nature. But the Catholic Church, that it may avoid both Manichees and Pelagians, says that no nature and substance is evil; but, by a voluntary evil which through one man passed into all men, it does not deny that our nature and substance, being changeable precisely because it is not the nature of God, has been vitiated; and to that evil, which is not incurable for God, so that it can be done away with, it confesses a Savior to be necessary at every age. Accordingly, neither of natural sin, nor of the lust of bodies, nor of free choice, nor of the accursed seed, nor of unconvertible wickedness, nor of the nature of the first man, nor of the commingling of the sexes, nor of the devil's power over human beings, can we say the same things as the Manichees; we who do not say that there are two natures and substances, one of good, the other of evil, everlasting without beginning of time, and commingled from a certain beginning of time—lest, among many absurd and senseless things, we should also say that the nature of God is befoulable and corruptible. But as for your asserting that I alone say that marriages are good and nature guilty—to say nothing of others, I give you the apostle Paul, whom you too proclaim a praiser of marriages, yet who says of the guilt of our original nature that *the body is dead because of sin*[^64]. I give you another who understood this, my teacher, the Catholic Ambrose, who, though he praises conjugal chastity, yet says: *We are all born under sin, whose very origin is in fault*[^65]. Slanderously, contentiously, with a wagging tongue, what more do you seek?

**V. 26.** *Julian:* But against these mockeries enough has been done: let us come to that most perplexing question, which I mentioned above, which by its very subtlety deceived your own preceptor. For you tried to meet our objection not by explaining, but by posing something more difficult. For when I had taught that, in human beings of more mature age who work evil by their own will, both the harmless beginnings of nature were praised and the byways of their actions were rightly censured—and that there are two things which can be applied to contraries; but that in little ones there is one, namely nature, since there is no will; and that this one thing must be reckoned either to God or to the demon; and I had concluded that, if nature subsists through God, there could be no original evil in it, but if it were assigned to the devil through an inborn evil, there would be nothing by which the human being might be vindicated for the divine work—when, then, you had reached these passages, you answered, with your accustomed assurance, that I had concluded truly; but that in little ones too there are two things, that is, nature and sin. Which sin, however, that you may recall your earlier definitions, is nothing other than the will of retaining or committing what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. With sin, then, established as nothing other than the choice of a depraved will, you answered, O Epicurus of our time, that in little ones there is sin, but there is no will: of what disgrace this is, the fourth book has already shown. In these passages, then, when I had said, "If sin is from the will, the will that does the sin is evil; if from nature, the nature that does the sin is evil," you tried to meet me with that question which, it is plain, was not thought up by you. For when some years ago we were at Carthage, this very thing was put to me by a certain Honoratus, a friend of yours, likewise a Manichee, as your letters indicate. I have made mention of this matter only for this purpose: that it may be clear that this is the question which deceived both Manes and Marcion so many ages ago. So, then, you speak against what I had said—"If sin is in nature, the nature that does the sin is evil": *I ask of him, let him answer if he can, that just as it is manifest that from an evil will, as from an evil tree, all evil works, its fruits, come to be, so let him say whence that evil will itself, that is, the evil tree of evil fruits, arose. If from an angel, what was the angel himself but a good work of God? If from a human being, what was the human being himself but a good work of God? Indeed, since the evil will arose from an angel in the case of the angel, and from a human being in the case of the human being, what were these two, before these evils arose in them, but a good work of God, and a good and praiseworthy nature? See, then, evil arises from good, and there was nothing at all from which it could arise except from good; I mean the evil will itself, which no evil preceded—not the evil works, which exist only from an evil will, as from an evil tree. Nor yet could an evil will arise from good because the good was made by the good God; but because it was made from nothing, not from God. What is it, then, that he says: If nature is a work of God, the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God? Was not the work of the devil, when it first arose in the angel who became the devil, a work that arose in a work of God? Wherefore, if evil, which was utterly nowhere, could arise in a work of God, why could not evil, which already was somewhere, pass through a work of God? Are not human beings the work of God? Sin, then, passed through human beings, that is, the work of the devil through the work of God; and, to say the same thing in another way, the work of God through the work of a work of God.* These many things, which I have set down from your discourses, have fully laid open the very head and fountain of the ancient error; nothing sharper have you brought forward in your sayings, nothing more perplexing in your reasoning; tossed about over the whole field of disputation, and driven by the weapons of hostile truth from every place where you had tried to take your stand, you have come at last to that cave which the Manichee had dug amid the shadows of his questions. You gave to this proposition the testimony of its own difficulty, saying, "I ask of him, that if he can, he may answer"; and therefore, since it is agreed between us both that the place is difficult, I warn the reader to attend with his whole mind. The distinctions, subtle as the condition of the matter requires, which with Christ's help I trust to make rich, let him follow, first attentive, then at ease. You asked, then, whence evil comes; I ask, what evil you mean; for this is a name common to fault and to punishment; though punishment is called evil only by misuse, since it is vindicated by the gravity of the judgment by which it is inflicted. You answer that you speak of sin, not of punishment.

*Augustine:* You have fully laid open your folly, as much as you could, you who confess that to sin is an evil, yet say that to burn in the punishment of eternal fire is not an evil, but is called evil only by misuse. But you give a remarkable reason for so great an absurdity: *Punishment*, you say, *is called evil by misuse, since it is vindicated by the gravity of the judgment by which it is inflicted*. If, then, to say this, you weigh the penalty of the condemned not from the misery of the one suffering, but from the justice of the one condemning, say more openly that the punishment is good, which you say is called evil by misuse. For punishment is the penalty of sin, and the penalty of sin is surely just; the punishment, therefore, is just; and everything that is just is good: the punishment, therefore, is good. Do you not see that, unless you so distinguish the condemned from the one condemning that you assert the condemnation itself—which is the punishment and the penalty of sin—to be indeed a good work of the one condemning, but the ruin of the condemned to be an evil; do you not, I say, see that, unless you distinguish these things by this reasoning, you are led to this: to say that by their evil works human beings come not to the graver evils that they suffer—which is true—but rather to goods, which is so false, and is said with such vanity, that to think such things is as great an evil as is even the punishment of a blind heart itself? Therefore it is not called evil by misuse; rather, it is altogether evil to the one suffering, but good to the one inflicting it, because it is just to impose punishment on the sinner. If you do not wish to rave, distinguish these things.

**V. 27.** *Julian:* You ask, then, whence comes this evil which is rightly called evil, that is, sin. I answer that it is too unskilled to ask the origin of a thing about whose definition there is as yet no agreement. Let us see, then, first whether it is, then what it is, and last whence it is. I did this indeed in the first booklet of the present work; but there, on one side, free of care: let it be debated, then, whether evil is.

*Augustine:* If you said that evil is, while I denied it, then the question would be turned between us whether evil is, in which question you would undertake to show that evil is, because it had been denied by me; but since neither of us denies it, and neither is in doubt about it, why do you demand that what is not in doubt be put in doubt—except from a lust for talking, so that you may glory not in the conviction of my words, but in the multitude of your books?

**V. 28.** *Julian:* But that it is, frequent vices and severe judgments bear witness; it is established, then, that sin is. We ask what it is: whether it is some body, which would seem composed of many things; or something single, like some one element, or one purged by thought from communion with the rest. But it is none of these. What is it, then? An appetite of free will, which justice forbids; or, to use the earlier definition: *The will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain*. Consider, then, whether sin can be found outside the bounds of this definition, lest that wander elsewhere which we suppose to be enclosed. Let us therefore consult the justice of the one judging, that by his testimony too it may be clear whether the whole genus of sin is well girded by these limits. Does God impute what he knows cannot be avoided? But there is no justice in that, and the utmost deformity; indeed, if this were done, sins would not be punished, but would increase. For by a just judge fault is wont to be punished; if that fault, by corruption, should make its way into the very arbiter of justice, then it has been avenged of the judge, not punished. Justice, therefore, does not impute as sin anything except what one is free to abstain from. But it cannot be called free unless it stands in the right of an emancipated will, without any unavoidable compulsion of natural things. Excellently, then, and fully, has it been defined: Sin is the will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. These parts, then, being settled, let it be asked whence it is—what, before these definitions, had been inquired into in the most confused way. Whence, then, is sin? I answer: From the free will of the one doing it.

*Augustine:* Is it really from the free will of the one doing it, where it is said: *But if I do what I do not will, it is no longer I that do it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^66]? Do you see that, when you ask whence sin comes, and answer, "From the free will of the one doing it," you are thinking of that sin which is not also the penalty of sin; but that this—where a man does what he does not will, and yet the Apostle proclaims it to be sin—pertains not at all to this answer of yours, nor to that definition which you recalled, saying that sin is what is the will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain? For how is one free to abstain, where it is cried out: *What I do not will, that I do*[^67]? In one way, then, human nature sinned, when it was free for it to abstain from sin; in another way it now sins, having lost its freedom, when it needs the help of a liberator. And the former was sin only; but the latter is also the penalty of sin.

**V. 29.** *Julian:* But let us see whether that which the foregoing definition has accomplished is confirmed by the assent of all. Surely no one prudent, nor any Catholic, can doubt this: that there is no sin except what can be guarded against, and no justice except that which imputes this to him who could have guarded against it, what by his free will he himself committed, when he could have guarded against it.

*Augustine:* This was imputed to the first man, that he could guard against it, if he willed; but by his sin all nature, even in his descendants, having been vitiated, has need of a Savior, so that it may be able to guard against sins, when an age has also arrived at which it can use the office of reason; but before this age there is present, from the origin, a guilt contracted by generation, to be removed by regeneration. By denying this, you most openly say that Christ Jesus is not Jesus to little ones; who is therefore, on the angel's testimony, so called because *he will save his people* (among which people you do not wish little ones to be) *from their sins*[^68].

**V. 30.** *Julian:* But just as these things are no longer slippery to us, so they are repelled with equal resolve by the Manichee and the Traducianist. Let us see, then, what they themselves say. The Manichee writes that sin is natural; Augustine agrees that sin is natural. Both, therefore, dissent from the definition which we set forth, and have a fellowship in the naming of natural sin. Let us see what they say also about the genus of sin, that is, what they think sin is, which both affirm to be natural, lest perhaps they dissent even at the next step. What does the Manichee write to his daughter? That the concupiscence of the flesh, and that pleasure destined for the work of fruitfulness, are proved diabolical by the fact that their work avoids the public gaze. What does Augustine say? The same in everything: *That concupiscence of the flesh is the shrub of the devil*; *the cause, the law of sin, which*, he says, *everywhere avoids the gaze, and seeks a shameful secrecy*. Therefore they disagree neither about the first nor about the second account of evil. What of the third? When it is now asked whence evil comes, the Manichee says, "From the eternal nature of darkness." What does Augustine say? "My master," he says, "goes too far, who thinks that evil never began; it began through the will of the first man, indeed already through the will of a higher nature, that is, the angelic; but from that time it became natural." His preceptor surely thrashes him, and most authoritatively drags him into court. What will the Catholic judge between these two? The Manichee, indeed, beyond doubt most stupid, who thinks sin natural; but, set beside the genius of Augustine, he seems most acute. For to whom does it agree that sin is natural, and yet confess that this sin is of one species, by which they think the whole genus of human beings is possessed? But after this, for the disciple to dare to say that in one alone it is not natural, which he confesses to be inborn in all—without doubt branded with boyish whippings, they will subject him to a master who blasphemes more consistently. But both together, disciple and master, they will banish from the assembly of the pious. See, then, what we grant. It does not please you that the Manichee says evil is natural; say, then, that no one is born guilty, and you have escaped, denying, of course, original sin. But you do not say it; you confess, then, that you wish neither to desist from your master, nor to be joined to the Catholics.

*Augustine:* Ought we therefore not to say that the good God made the world, because the Manichee too says this? But when it is asked from what he made it, there we are distinguished. For we say: From those things which were not; since *he himself spoke, and they were made*[^69]; but he says: From two natures, namely of good and of evil, which not only already were, but always were. These things, then, which we do not say together, do not allow us to be their associates on account of what we say together. Likewise, if we are asked whether God is, both we and the Manichees answer: God is; and in this we both are separated from that fool who *said in his heart: There is no God*[^70]; but when it is asked what sort God is, we are distinguished by a great difference from the impious fable of the Manichees. For we say he is incorruptible, and we defend it; but they fable a corruptible God. Asked also about the Trinity itself, both we and they say that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are of one and the same nature; nor yet are we therefore Manichees, nor they Catholics; for they say other things about the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, in which we are most diverse from them and most opposed to them. Hence these things which we say together, we confidently assert against the errors of others who deny them; nor do we fear that they should call us Manichees, and on that account prefer themselves to us, because we say with the Manichees what they themselves, since they refuse to say it, are on that account refuted by us. As, then, the Arian is a heretic because he does not say with us the one essence of the Trinity, which the Manichee too says with us, so you too are heretics, by not saying with us natural sin, which the Manichee too says with us; but we are not therefore Manichees; for what he too says, we do not say as he says. For we say that our good nature has been vitiated by the voluntary sin of him from whom we are born, whence we are all born under sin, and our very origin is in fault, as Ambrose says[^71]; but he brings to bear upon us an alien evil nature, by whose commingling he affirms that we sin. Finally, we offer even little ones to the Savior, for our nature to be healed; but he thinks Christ necessary not in order to heal our nature in us, but to separate an alien nature from us. You see surely, even in the fact that we say together that sin is natural, by how great a diversity we stand apart. Likewise also, in the fact that we say together that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh lusts against the spirit, is evil—when it is asked whence this evil comes, we are found most distinct. We say also with Ambrose that this foul discord of flesh and spirit passed over into our nature through the transgression of the first man[^72]; but he, with his followers, says that, so that flesh and spirit oppose each other, an alien nature, which was always evil, clung to us. Hence we ask for a Savior, that this fault of ours may be healed; but he, that the alien nature, which can in no way be healed, may be taken away from us. Why too do you not attend here, in the fact that we say together that the concupiscence of the flesh resisting the spirit is evil, by how great an unlikeness we are separated? Why do you not attend that neither are we Manichees, because we say some things with them; and that you are heretics, because you do not say those things with them? For if you said with them that natural evils exist, and said with us against them whence they come—because they are not from an alien nature coeternal with God—you would not be Pelagian heretics; but as it is, by denying that the concupiscence by which the flesh lusts against the spirit is evil, and is from our vitiated nature, you cause them to conclude that it is from an alien nature; and so you are both new heretics, and helpers of the old heretics whom you perversely flee. Cease, then, to set against me my master the Manichee, but rather follow Ambrose with me; and look to the Arians, and in this at least, in which they are wiser than you, imitate them; for they do not say that we are Manichees, although we say with the Manichees that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are of one nature, where they most contentiously contradict us.

**V. 31.** *Julian:* But you thought it a forceful objection, when you said whence, in the first man himself, or in the devil who had been made an angel, an evil will had arisen; which, however, you say arose in a work of God, that is, either in the angel or in the human being, not because it was a work of God, but because it had been made from nothing. See, then, lest you too, by other paths, should say that the necessity of evil was eternal. For if this was the cause of evil's arising in the work of God—because it was established that it had been made from nothing; and before that which was came to be, this nothing always was, that is, before that which was came to be, it was never anything, and this which never was is said to have been nothing; from eternity, therefore, that never was which was not before it was made by God, whose substance alone is without beginning; this void, then, that is, nothing, before it was bounded by the existence of things, always was. This nothing, therefore, was not made; rather, creatures were made, and that nothing ceased to be. In that creature, then, which was made from nothing, you accordingly devise that evil arose because it was made from nothing. Evil, then, arisen in the human being, you have reckoned to his origin; and the origin, that is, nothing, is said by you to have been the cause of sin. For it is not, you say, because he was made by God that evil arose in the human being, but because he was made from nothing. If, then, evil arose because the condition of the preceding nothing demanded it; and this nothing was eternal; then by altered paths you have stumbled, and you hang altogether in the snare of your preceptor, so that you both confess evil to be from eternity. But in this too he is more prudent; for, introducing natural sin, he said that the substance of darkness was eternal, which compelled this evil to be present without the will of the sinner. To the thing, then, whose necessity he was contriving, he gave an author, so that the evil which pervaded substances might seem to have something compelling it; but you, with the unbearable lead of your wit, confirm the necessity of evil, but deny an author of the necessity. And as in little ones, so in the very first worker of sin you leave a work; and you say that some great thing can be understood—how that nothing was worth very much, when it was nothing.

*Augustine:* You are worth nothing; but it is you, in asserting that nothing, when it is nothing, is worth something; nor do you understand that, when God is said to have made from nothing the things he made, nothing else is said but that he did not make them from himself; for before he made anything, it was not coeternal with him to make something. That, then, is from nothing which is not from something; because, even though God made some things from other things, these very things from which he made them, he had made from no things. But no thing could sin, if it had been made from the nature of God; nor would it now have been made, but whatever it was would be from him, and that which he is; just as the Son is, and the Holy Spirit, since they are from him what he is, the one by being born, the other by proceeding; and they are so from him that he himself was never prior to them. And therefore this nature cannot sin at all; because it cannot desert itself, nor does it have a better one to which it ought to cleave, and by deserting which it might sin. Yet the rational creature was not so made that it had a necessity of sinning; but neither would it have had the possibility, if it were the nature of God; since the nature of God neither wills to be able to sin, nor can will it.

**V. 32.** *Julian:* For you affirm that this nothing, from which all things were made, was the cause of sin. As much, then, with you does the power of this nothing accomplish, as with the Manichee the power of the prince of darkness. Both of you, then, say that there was a necessity even of the first evil; but he gives it solidity, though an evil solidity; you, an emptiness, but equally evil: he, then, speaks of a violent substance; you of one equally violent, but nothing. See, then, the conclusion of your syllogism: nothing, surely, when as yet nothing had been created, was the token of an eternal emptiness; but this very nothing, that is, emptiness, was bounded when creatures arose; for it ceased to be nothing, when something began to be. The nothing itself, then, even when it was, was not; since it is understood to have been when as yet there was not anything. But after things were made, this token of emptiness, that is, nothing, just as it had never had substance, so also lost its very name; and it came about that what had never existed in reality, lost even its very name. By the violence of this, then, you devise that evil arose both in the angel and in the human being; than which what can be said more frenzied?

*Augustine:* It is rather you who seem to rave in your zeal for cursing. I did not say a violent nothing: for there is no thing that could be violent. Neither angel nor human being was compelled to sin by any force; nor would they have sinned, if they had not willed to sin—they who could even have not willed; but indeed the very ability to sin would not have been in them, if they were the nature of God.

**V. 33.** *Julian:* A thing which was not, you say, had great force, for this reason alone, that it had never been; indeed, then it began to be worth very much, after it had lost even its very name; and this nothing obtained great dominion, after even its appellation had perished.

*Augustine:* If that which is nothing were something, it would, with you, be said to have obtained great dominion; since vanity, or falsity, so lords it over you that it compels you to keep chattering these empty things for so long.

**V. 34.** *Julian:* Bravo for the prowess of your wisdom—you who, by the rules of a disputation new and first invented by yourself, embrace the second things by the denial of the first, and join bodies, lopped off, to heads. We do not envy your subtleties; rather, with Christian kindness we pity you, that you have found an end worthy of your dogma: that, having set out from charging the innocent, you should arrive at nothing.

*Augustine:* It is rather you who have arrived at nothing, which so delights you that you are unwilling as yet to withdraw from it or return; you who say that I called that which is nothing something, in order that you might say so much that is nothing.

**V. 35.** *Julian:* O the soundness, O the elegance of the disputant! *Not on this account*, he says, *did evil arise in the human being, because he was made by God, but on this account, because he was made from nothing*. We have already shown the acumen by which the greatest force was substituted for this nothing; now I point this out, which the earlier conflict too made understood: that the first evil was not taken up by the will of the sinner, if the condition of a stock coming from nothing demanded that it be born.

*Augustine:* No condition of a stock coming from nothing demanded that sin be born; since that is demanded which is compelled to be rendered or to be done; but the angel or the human being, by whom and in whom the first sins arose, no thing compelled to sin; rather, they sinned by free will—which they could even have not willed, since they were not compelled to will; and yet they could not have willed it, if they had their nature from God and had not been made from nothing.

**V. 36.** *Julian:* You have given, then, a nature even to the first evil, but emptier than the Manichee's, yet equally eternal. There is no need to fight over this; it is altogether clear that there remains between you a covenant which the chain of natural evil and eternal evil binds together.

*Augustine:* Most insipid one, that cannot be eternal which is nothing, that cannot be eternal which is no thing, and finally that cannot be eternal which is not.

**V. 37.** *Julian:* I have acted, surely, as the good faith of disputation befitted; and that which you had effected by arguing—namely, that an evil will arose in the work of God because the human being had been created from nothing—reason, searching it through, has ground to powder; showing, that is, that you, with the words altered, said the same thing that the Manichee invented and believed, that is, that the first sin too was begotten by the violence of eternal darkness.

*Augustine:* I have already warned you above that what is not cannot be eternal; what is it, then, that you say—that I am to be compared to the Manichee, because the Manichee said the first sin was begotten by the violence of eternal darkness? To those shadows he gave a substance; but I could not give a substance to nothing, so that on this account I too should, as it were, set up eternal shadows, that is, an eternal nothing. But just as I could not give substance, so I could not give violence or eternity to nothing; for in no way, as we have already said, can that be either violent or eternal which is nothing. In vain, then, did you wish to dispute against me about that which is nothing.

**V. 38.** *Julian:* But lest, seeing yourself detected, you try to break out by this, namely to report that you did not say, "Evil arose in the work of God because it was made from nothing," but, "It was able to arise, because it was made from nothing"—it must be shown by how much more violent snares you are bound. For if you say that you imputed the possibility of evil, not its necessity, to the force of that eternal nothing, we reply that the fact that an evil will could arise in the human being is surely nothing other than free choice; for an evil will could arise precisely so that a good one too could arise. This is the liberty in which reason exercises itself, on account of which the human being is asserted to have been made to the image of God, and through which he excels the other creatures. If, then, the fact that an evil will could arise in the human being is nothing other than the liberty of choice, and this is so great that by its tokens he stands before the other living things; you, who profess that this possibility was in the human being not because he was made by God, but because he was made from nothing, by a new prodigy of dogma pronounce that nothing, that is, the ancient emptiness, to be the cause of so great a good, that is, of free choice. Finally, that the matter may be made clear by a brief question: this which you said—*Nor yet could an evil will arise because the human being was made by God, but because the human being was made from nothing*—this very thing, I say, that a will could arise, do you believe to be good, or evil? That is, the very faculty of a will's arising, which you reckoned to nothing, do you judge upright, or most base? If you call it good, then God is not the cause of the good, but nothing is. But if, seeing this to be most insane, you pronounce it evil—the very thing which you say is to be reckoned not to God but to nothing—you will testify that we concluded nothing against you craftily, but that with the good faith of disputation we have undermined the bad faith of your dogma. There stands, then, unshaken what we have done: namely that you, like the Manichee, imputed even the evil will of the first man to the necessity of an eternal origin.

*Augustine:* What might be answered to you, you have somehow seen; but in vain have you tried to withstand the truth, as though answering my words, and not answering my words. For you disputed as though I had said, "Nor yet did an evil will arise from good"—which I did not say; but I said: *Nor yet could an evil will arise from good because the good was made by the good God, but because it was made from nothing, not from God*. Just as you yourself also set down these same words of mine. What is it, then, that you thought you must answer as though I had said, "Nor yet did it arise"—when I said, "Nor yet could it arise"; and you spoke so long against one who said that the necessity of evil's arising from good rushed in for this reason, that the good itself was made by God from nothing, not from God—when I attributed to such a cause not the necessity, but the possibility of evil; because I did not say it therefore arose, but therefore could arise from the good, the evil? And you accused nothing for so long, and made nothing violent; as though nothing had compelled the angel and the human being to sin by an unavoidable necessity. Now, then, come back at last to my words, as you have begun to come back. For you set yourself a question, as though it had suddenly come into your mind what I could answer; when I set this down long before in the very book you resist. For you say: *that I can report that I did not say, Evil arose in the work of God because it was made from nothing, but: It was able to arise, because it was made from nothing*. This very thing I said: I said, *It was able to arise*; I did not say it arose; I gave to this cause the possibility of evil, not the necessity. For the rational creature, when it was first made, was so made that, if it did not wish to sin, it was urged by no necessity to will it, or even, not willing, that is, unwilling, to sin, and not to do the good it willed, but to do the evil it did not will—where there is now not that sin which is simply called sin, but also the penalty of sin. Yet to will anything evil, or even, unwilling, to do anything evil, it could in no way, unless it had been made from nothing, that is, if it were the nature of God. For the nature of God alone was not made from nothing, because it was not made at all; and therefore it can in no way be changed. When we say this, we do not give powers to nothing, as though it could have made something, or made something, when it is nothing; but we say it is not the nature of God which was able to sin. And it follows that the nature which is not the nature of God was made; for it is not coeternal with God; and if it was made, it was made from nothing; because even those natures which were made from other natures draw their stock from nothing; since the natures from which they were made, before they came to be, were nothing, that is, were not at all. But, you say: *An evil will could arise so that a good one too could arise*. As though the angel or the human being were not made with a good will. *He was made upright*, as Scripture said[^73]; it is not asked, then, whence the good will could arise in him, with which he was made; but whence the evil, with which he was not made. And you say, not attending to what you say: *An evil will could arise so that a good one too could arise*; and you think this pertains to the nature of free choice, that it can do both, namely both sin and not sin; and in this you suppose that the human being was made to the image of God, when God himself cannot do both. For neither would anyone, even mad, say that God can sin; nor do you dare to say that God does not have free choice. Free choice, then, is the gift of God, not of nothing; but in God himself free choice is supreme, who can in no way sin. For if he could be unjust, then God too could surely not be; for if he is God, most consistently he is just; and therefore, while supremely and most greatly having free choice, God nevertheless cannot sin. The angel, then, or the human being, was able to sin for this reason—that is, was able to misuse this gift of God, which is free choice—because he is not God, that is, was made from nothing by God, not from God himself. Understand, and be silent; or speak what you understand, not what you do not understand.

**V. 39.** *Julian:* But not content to have conquered in one way, I will convict of falsity the argument whose impiety I have exposed. For when you wrote: *An evil will could not arise in the work of God because it was made by God, but on this account, because it was made from nothing*; you ought to have seen how powerfully the examples of other creatures prescribe against you—creatures which, produced just as much from nothing, are yet not capable of an evil will. Finally, the very elements, which were truly made from nothing, cannot have the consciousness of any will, so as to attest the necessity of their origin by evil motions. But living things, and the rest with which the world is filled, came forth not from nothing, but already from something. Where, then, is the force of the ancient emptiness, which compelled an evil will to exist, since it is plain that, apart from the rational animal, no one can sin?

*Augustine:* You might think me convicted of falsity if, when I had said that our bodies can be wounded because they are earthly, you showed many earthly bodies which cannot be wounded. For there can be no wound except in the body of a living thing, which is called flesh. Here I would have to warn you that you could not see that this statement does not run back the other way; because it is not, as it is true that "Everything that can be wounded is an earthly body," likewise true that "Every earthly body can be wounded." Why, then, in matters of logic did your little vaunted cleverness slumber, so that you did not notice that, where I said the rational creature could sin because it was made from nothing, I meant to be understood that everything which can sin was made from nothing, but not that everything which was made from nothing can sin? As though I had said this, you objected to me other things, and the very elements of the world, which, though made from nothing, cannot sin; since only the rational animal can sin. Now, then, wake up, and observe that everything which can sin was made from nothing; nor does it therefore follow that everything which was made from nothing can sin. Let not other things, then, be brought forward to me, which were made from nothing and cannot sin; since I do not say: Everything which was made from nothing can sin; but I say: Everything which can sin was made from nothing; just as if I had said: Every ox is an animal, there surely ought not to have been mentioned against me many animals which are not oxen; since I did not say: Every animal is an ox, but: Every ox is an animal. Again, then, I say: Everything which can sin was made from nothing; I do not say: Everything, therefore, which was made from nothing can sin; and, as though I had said this, you mention many things which, although made from nothing, yet cannot sin. Take away your craftiness, by which you mock dull wits; or your blindness, by which you do not see plain things. But when I say: The nature which was created rational could sin because it was made from nothing, not from God—attend to what I say, lest again with empty wordiness you fan nothing at me, and affirm that I said that which is nothing has the force of doing something. This I say: that the nature which was created rational could sin because it was made from nothing; which is nothing other than that it could sin because it is not the nature of God. For if it had not been made from nothing, it would naturally be from God, whatever it was; if it were naturally from God, it would be the nature of God; if it were the nature of God, it could not sin. For this reason, then, it could sin, although it was made by God, because it was made from nothing, not from God. If you understand this, and are unwilling to fight against the truth, you will cease from this contention in this question.

**V. 40.** *Julian:* Since, then, this so great matter was plain, what was it that persuaded you to believe that the violence of the ancient nothing was the cause of the evil will? Surely it was that we might understand that you believe all things which were made from nothing to be guilty, and hand over the whole world to the devil. Since, therefore, it has become clear to me that an ancient concord of dogmas remains between you, hence I now answer you and the Manichee alike. You surely ask—since I, who deny that there is a nature of evil, am to answer if I can—whence that evil will itself could arise in the first human being. But I reply that you do not understand what you say. For the will is nothing other than a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling it.

*Augustine:* What is a motion of the mind, but a motion of nature? For the mind is without doubt a nature; accordingly the will is a motion of nature, since it is a motion of the mind. But you, when above you were setting nature down as a genus, and subjecting its species to that genus, surely spoke thus: *The generality of human nature*, you say, *of the institutions placed beneath it, is a kind of genus; this has, as it were, species in posture, in members, in arrangements, in movements, or in other things of the kind*. By this disputation of yours, then, you asserted a motion of nature to be a species of nature; whence it follows for you, what you do not wish, that every motion of nature is a nature, if nature is a genus, and a motion of nature is its species; just as every horse is an animal, in that an animal is a genus, and a horse a species of this genus. And through this the will, since it is a motion of the mind, and is thereby shown to be a motion of nature, is, on your own disputing, a nature; because you subjected a motion of this kind to nature as to a genus. Why, then, do you find fault with what is called natural sin, which an evil will did—which very will you are convicted of saying is a nature? But suppose the will is not a nature; surely, nevertheless, it cannot exist except in a nature; for so far as pertains to the human being, it is a motion of the mind, and the mind is a nature. Permit it now, I ask, that sin be called natural, since when a human being sins, nature surely sins; for a human being is a nature: just as it can rightly be called also spiritual sin, when the spirit sins. For the Apostle did not err where he said: *spiritual wickedness*[^74]; which spiritual things are without doubt natural, because the spirit is without ambiguity a nature, whether it be creator or created. Yet not because it was committed by the will of an angel or a human being—since a nature sinned (for both the angel and the human being are natures)—do we so call this natural sin as to say that what was done by free will was done by necessity. For he who sinned for this reason, that he willed it, could also have not willed to sin; and the human being was so created that he could both not will and will, and have either of these in his power. But original sin is another thing, which, although those being born draw it without their own will, yet through the will of the first man the very origin is vitiated; just as there is also another thing in the grown human being, on account of which he says: *For I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^75]; yet this necessity too is not incurable for him to whom it is said: *Deliver me from my necessities*[^76].

**V. 41.** *Julian:* You ask, then, the necessity of a thing which cannot be if it suffers necessity. To this free motion of the mind, restless without the compulsion of origin, if a cause more ancient than the motion itself be given, it is not begotten at all, but abolished. For the very name of will has no other force than that it does not owe to matter the fact that it is moved. When, therefore, you ask for something more ancient than the will itself, as the source whence the will arose, you seek not its beginning but its end; for it is in no way understood to be, if it be assigned either to darkness or to nothing; nor can that any longer be called will which cannot subsist except in a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling. If, then, someone compels, there is indeed a motion; but it is not will, whose force that second part of the definition completed, that is, "with nothing compelling." If, then, the will is nothing other than a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling, the origin of the thing is altogether wrongly sought, whose condition perishes if it is forestalled. Weigh, then, what it is you ask: *Whence*, you say, *could that evil will itself, as an evil tree, arise in the first human being?*—you who confess that the will came about from origin. For the will is a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling. All natural things compel that which follows to be; but the will, if it be compelled by preceding causes, at once ceases to be will, and loses its condition, if it has received an origin.

*Augustine:* If the will has no origin because it is not compelled, then the human being himself has no origin, so as to be a human being, because he was not compelled to be. For how could he be compelled, who was not? And surely the human being is a nature; and you said: *All natural things compel that which follows to be*. I ask you, attend to what you say; do not move your tongue with your eyes shut, like one who talks in his sleep. No thing that is not can be compelled. See, too, how insane it is to deny that things which have arisen have an origin; since the very word "origin" is derived from "arising." For what is, and has no origin, always was; but if it was not, and is, it has arisen; if it has arisen, it has an origin. The will of sin, therefore, which was not, and is, has surely arisen; for if it is, and has not arisen, it always was; but it was not always: therefore it has arisen. Now then cry out against the most manifest truth: for this befits your empty wordiness; and say: It has arisen indeed, but has no origin; or, what is more insane: It both was not, and is, and yet has not arisen. But if you do not say this, lest you be judged most insipid and altogether foolish, ask whence the evil will of the human being arose, which you cannot deny has arisen, because you cannot deny that it was not and began to be; ask, I say, whence it arose; and you will find the human being himself; for from him arose the evil will, which before was not in him. Ask too what sort the human being was before the evil will arose from him; and you will find him good; for it was by that will he was made evil, which, before it arose from him, was such as he had been made by the good one, that is, good. This, then, is what my teacher and your destroyer Ambrose says: *From goods, therefore, evils have arisen*[^77]. By denying this, and saying: *The nature of things does not allow that anything evil be produced from good, or anything unjust from the just*, you only help the Manichees toward introducing a nature of evil, from which they say evils arise—so that they congratulate you as the patron of their error, unless you too be conquered along with them. For it is you who, by a marvelous eloquence, or rather madness, so defend little ones that you separate them from the Savior; you who so assail the Manichees that you raise them up against the Savior.

**V. 42.** *Julian:* Since, then, the will has been well defined—a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling—why do you seek higher up for causes which the definition of the will has excluded? Weigh, then, what the will is, and you will cease to inquire whence the will comes. For the will is a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling; if you try to go up so much as half a fingernail higher, you at once tear down what was established. What, then, does the Manichee say? But this motion arose because the human being was made from the nature of darkness. What do you say? Because, you say, the human being was made from nothing. The one, then, says: The will is evil because the human being was made from nothing; the other: The will is evil in the human being because the human being was made from darkness. Both of you, then, deny that supplement of the will, that is, "with nothing compelling." For if there was as great a force in nothing as in something, which compelled this will to exist, it excluded from it its own condition, by which it had been said, "with nothing compelling." But it drove off no less the infamy of evil; for that is not sin which does not come from a free motion of the mind; and it came about that, with the ruin of truth, the odium of all evil perished; and the nature of evil vanished, when the crime of will vanished; but the crime of will vanished, when the definition of will was cut away. Of both sin and will, then, it has become clear that the condition is such that, if it be reckoned to preceding causes, it loses both its right and its crime. Where, then, will be the nature of evil, when it shall have been established that evil is not?

*Augustine:* It cannot be said how I marvel at your effrontery—how you can say there is a nature of evil, you who do not say evil is natural; or how you can say evil is not natural, you who say there is a nature of evil. But what is emptier than your definitions, you who think that whence the will comes is not to be sought, because it is a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling? For if it be said, as you suppose, whence it is, then what was said will not be true, "with nothing compelling"; because that whence it is compels it to be; and therefore it is not from anywhere, lest it be compelled to be. O singular folly! The human being himself, then, who was not compelled to be, is not from anywhere; because there was no one who could be compelled, before he was. By all means the will both is from somewhere, and is not compelled to be; and if its origin is not to be sought, it is not to be sought, not because the will is not from somewhere, but because it is manifest whence it is. For the will is from him whose will it is: namely the will of an angel from an angel, of a human being from a human being, of God from God. And if God works a good will in a human being, he surely does this, that a good will may arise from him whose will it is; just as he brings it about that a human being arises from a human being; for it is not because God creates a human being that a human being is therefore not born from a human being. But of his own evil will each one is the author, because he wills evil. But when it is asked why a human being can have an evil will, although it is not necessary that he have it, it is not the origin of the will that is sought, but the origin of the possibility itself; and it is found that this is the cause: that although the rational creature is a great good, it is yet not what God is, whose alone is an unconvertible and unchangeable nature. And when the cause of this thing is sought, this is found: that God begot it not from himself, that is, from his own nature and substance, but made it from nothing, that is, from no thing. Not because nothing has some force; for if it had, it would not be nothing, but something; but because this is, for every nature, to have been made from nothing: that it is not the nature of God, which alone is unchangeable. Nor are those things which were made from certain things excepted from this origin; since the things which were so made that from them others might be made, were made from no existing things, that is, altogether from nothing. But whatever other things can be changed by their diverse and proper qualities; while in respect of the will, which uses reason, the rational creature alone is changeable. Whoever attends to these things carefully and intelligently will recognize that you have said much about nothing—nothing that pertains to the matter.

**V. 43.** *Julian:* For what is evil, that is, sin? The will to follow what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. What is the will itself? A motion of the mind, with nothing compelling. If, then, sin is born of the will, and the will of a motion of the mind with nothing compelling; neither the condition of nothing nor of darkness made this motion to be, which is therefore compelled by no one, so that it can be "with nothing compelling." And through this there is no natural, no original sin: because these two names indicate one thing, that is, that sin is not voluntary; but truth has prescribed that there can be no sin unless it is voluntary; and therefore he who says that to be evil which is established to be inborn does not convict sin of being in nature, but shows himself, by the depravity of his judgment, to be criminal. Behold, an answer has been given to that to which you believed no answer could be given. The question was altogether a paradox, which you judge unconquerable.

*Augustine:* You exult in vain, and say, "Behold, an answer has been given," where anyone who reads these things of yours, if he is acute, or even not very dull, will soon find from mine that you could not answer. For however great the perplexity with which you try, not to unravel what is tangled, but to entangle what is plain, the sound cannot deny that each one's will arises from himself, and that a human being's will arises only from a human being. And through this, since the evils of human beings began to be by an evil will, and we know that before the evil will there was the good nature of human beings, evils have arisen from goods. This Ambrose says; by this the Manichee is destroyed; this Julian, on behalf of the Manichee, denies against Ambrose, saying: *If nature is a work of God, the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God*; so that the Manichee may say that human beings are not the work of God, through whom the Apostle says sin and death passed[^78], which is the work of the devil; since, according to Julian, *the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God*; and the Apostle says the work of the devil passed through human beings; therefore human beings are not the work of God. This conclusion is the Manichee's, coming to him, Julian, from your help. But the Apostle, contending for the truth, both says that human beings are the work of God, so that he may overthrow the Manichee, and says that the work of the devil passed through the work of God, that is, through human beings, so that with him he may overthrow you too.

**V. 44.** *Julian:* Yet I point this out, that you too are wont, in your writings, to say that darkness is not a creature, but that obscurity remains by the absence of light; so that a being-darkened is nothing other than the exclusion of brightness. That, then, which is excluded, you call a creature; what remains, darkness; which is indeed a commonplace among the philosophers; nor do I now ask whether it is thought to be true or false; but I drive home this: you say that darkness is nothing other than nothing; and yet you argue that in the human being, that is, in the work of God, evil arose because he was made from nothing; you therefore confirm that this nothing was the cause of evil, which nothing you also pronounce to be darkness. And so you say that the necessity of evil descends from the condition of darkness. Therefore in this too you do not dissent from your preceptor, since you both equally reckon an evil will to eternal darkness.

*Augustine:* A little before, I already answered you, as openly and briefly as I could, when you were saying nothing about nothing; and now in vain have you wished to flee to darkness. You will not hide: for the light of truth pursues you, saying that creatures were made from nothing, things which are not what he himself who made them is, so that this nothing is not supposed or understood to be something, nor to have any force for making; because if it had, it would not be nothing. And through this, nothing is neither any body, nor any spirit, nor anything accidental to these substances, nor any formless matter, nor empty space, nor darkness itself, but altogether nothing; for where there is darkness, there is some body lacking light, whether air, or water, or something else; for by a corporeal light nothing can be either illumined so as to be bright, or deprived of it so as to grow dark, except a body. And through this the maker of this corporeal darkness is none other than he who made bodies, on which account, in the hymn of the three young men, *light and darkness bless him*[^79]. God therefore made all things from nothing; that is, all the things that he made so that they might be, if we look to their first origin, he made from those things which were not; this the Greeks express: ex ouk onton. And lest this be believed of the Only-Begotten, who is God from God, light from light, and therefore not from nothing, the Catholic Church vigorously resists the Arians. Therefore, when we say that an evil will could not have arisen from good because the good was made by the good God, but because it was made from nothing, not from God, we do not give any nature to nothing, but we distinguish the nature of the maker from the nature of the things that are made. For these can be changed for this reason, whether by will, as the rational creature could be, or by their own proper qualities, as the rest; because they were made from nothing, not from God—although by none making them but God; that is, because they are not what that nature is which was not made, and on this account is alone unchangeable. If, then, you wish either to avoid or to conquer the Manichees, understand this, grasp this—by understanding, if you can; by believing, if you cannot—that evils have arisen from goods, and that malice is nothing other than a lack of good.

**V. 45.** *Julian:* But as truth has uncovered and laid both of you low, so the consideration of our office demands that we explain what it was that had made obscurity for this question, which is now disarmed. All things that come to be are said to exist either from the necessary or from the possible. By "necessary" I mean here, not what we are wont to call useful, but what has been compelled by higher causes. We therefore call necessary, not what is within the right of the will, but what suffers a force of existing. We call possible, however, that which experiences a necessity neither of existing nor of not existing in either direction, but in certain ways can both be and not be. Let our reader hold, then, what here we call necessary, what possible. And to begin with great examples: that God made the world came from the possible, not from the necessary; that is, it was possible for his omnipotence to create what he founded, yet it was not necessary—namely, he was not compelled by anything to make it, but made it because he willed, which he certainly would not have made had he not willed. But this which was present to the author from the possible, was, in the work, made from the necessary; that is, it was not possible for the world to be and not to be, which was bidden to be by the Omnipotent, but it was compelled to exist, to which the Omnipotent commanded being.

*Augustine:* How was the world compelled to exist, which was not before it existed? How is anyone compelled, if he is not? Was it not better for you to say: The world was made by God's will, not its own? But go on; let us see what you try to show from this distinction of possible and necessary—which we could understand better, if you had wished merely to mention it, and not to expound it. For who does not see that everything which must come to be can also come to be; but not everything which can come to be must also come to be? If, then, it pleased you to call possible that which can come to be in such a way that it is not necessary, and necessary that which not only can come to be but must also; speak as you wish, where things are apparent there is no controversy to be made about words. It is enough to know that everything necessary is possible, but not everything possible is necessary.

**V. 46.** *Julian:* It passed, then, into the necessity of the thing founded, what had come from the possibility of the founder. He also made diverse natures and diverse species within the natures, that order being kept which flowed from the beginning of things, so that some should be necessary, others possible. Whatever, then, creatures have naturally, they have received from the side of the necessary.

*Augustine:* If whatever creatures have naturally, they have received from the side of the necessary, then human beings do not have naturally that they have intercourse, but that they can have intercourse; nor is that which the Apostle mentioned the natural use of the woman[^80], but the possibility of it; for if a human being does not will it, the use itself does not occur, although it can occur if he wills; the possibility of it, therefore, is natural, but it itself is not; for it is not necessary, since it is also nothing, if we do not will it—and the Apostle erred who said there is a natural use of the woman. Where too is what you said before, that nature is the genus, and marriage its species; since marriages are joined not by necessity, but by will? Or had it perhaps not yet come into your mind, when you said this, the distinction of these two, that is, of the necessary and the possible? And that a human being should be born after the intercourse of the sexes, is not natural, because it is not necessary? For it is not necessary that conception and birth follow, when a male and female have had intercourse; but you defined this to be possible, not necessary, which can come to be, but is not necessary. And do we not eat naturally? Because this too, if we do not will it, does not happen; and therefore it is possible, not necessary. But to deny that these things are natural is nothing other than to wish to take away a great part of nature. It is false, then, what you said: *Whatever creatures have naturally, they have received from the side of the necessary*; since both these things which I mentioned, and others which it would be long to mention, they have naturally, and yet they have not received them from the side of the necessary.

**V. 47.** *Julian:* But things that have sensation, in their process, take not always from the necessary, but much from the possible. This one may see in all bodies, but a longer disputation arises; let us give, however, a few examples. It is the nature of bodies that they grow together by joining, and are dissolved by division; they are therefore capable of a wound, which suffer destruction. That they can be wounded, then, they have from the necessary; that they be wounded, however, from the possible. So the nature of possibility is from the necessary, while the effect of possibility is not necessary. For example, a horse, an ox, and similar living things have a woundable nature, and therefore are capable of harm from the necessary; but that they be wounded is not always necessary. For if by the diligence of keepers they are defended from blows, they can be not wounded; but if they are not kept, they can also be wounded. There is, then, the greatest difference between those things which come from the possible and those which come from the necessary; and unless this distinction is kept, one goes into countless errors. That this may become clear by comparison: certain assailants of medicine have fallen, arguing that this art has no usefulness; and they dispute in this manner: Does medicine help those about to die, or those about to live? If those about to die, it accomplishes nothing; if those about to live, it bestirs itself in vain. For those who were about to die will die even with this art laboring; those about to live could be safe even without the benefit of it. How neat, indeed, how clever a conclusion! But it is undone by the defenders of medicine in this manner: This art, they say, profits neither those about to live nor those about to die, that is, from the necessary, but those about to suffer either of these from the possible; medicine therefore does not help him who is beyond doubt about to die, since it cannot make him immortal; but neither does it aid him who is beyond doubt going to be unharmed; rather, it aids him who, if he is not treated, can be endangered, and if he is treated, can be freed; just as, then, it can profit neither one about to live nor one about to die from the condition of the necessary, so the learned art can profit both one about to die and one about to live, but from the possible. Those earlier objectors, then, to the labors of healers, beginning their proposition from the possible, closed it with the end of the necessary; which kind of disputation extends to countless cases. For example, the law forbids murder, and forbids also that occasion be given for the negligence by which one rushes into dangers, as in the case of a goring bull[^81], and of the parapets of houses[^82]. But it can be said: Does this diligence help one about to live or one about to die? If one about to die, it accomplishes nothing; if one about to live, it counsels superfluously; if both this and its contrary, they will necessarily follow both through hindrances and without hindrances. But this is false: for by the best right it is provided for mortals that what they could suffer without diligence, they may escape by the resources of diligence. One thing, then, is from the possible, another from the necessary. Let us now show what we are helped by these prefatory remarks. God made the human being of free choice, of a good nature; but one that was capable of the virtues which it would acquire for itself, by a mind given over to itself; which free choice could not stand in any other way than that it have also the possibility of sinning. Liberty, then, it has from the necessary; will, from the possible. It cannot not be free; but it cannot be compelled into either will, and the possible effect of a necessary thing has been made. In the possible, then, one can sin; in the necessary, one cannot; because in the necessary not the agent, but the author himself is reckoned; and this, that the human being can, is wholly God's, but from the possible the agent himself is brought to account.

*Augustine:* What do you say of the devil, of whom it is written: *The devil sins from the beginning*[^83]? Does he have a possibility of sinning, or a necessity? If a necessity, you may see how, according to your disputations, he is excused from crime; but if a possibility, then he can also not sin, can have a good will, can do penance and obtain the mercy of God; because *a contrite and humbled heart God will not despise*[^84]. This indeed seemed so to certain people, on the authority, as it is reported, of Origen; but this, as I think you know, the Catholic and sound faith has not received; whence some prove, or wish, that even Origen himself was a stranger to this error. It remains, therefore, either that even before the punishment of eternal fire, this very necessity of sinning is a great punishment to the devil for his great sin, nor is he hereby excused from crime, since this too is a vengeance for the greatest crime—that malice alone should delight him, and justice cannot delight him. But to this now penal necessity of sinning he certainly would not have come, unless he had first sinned by free will, with no necessity. And so that definition of sin, when one does what justice forbids and from which one is free to abstain, pertains to that sin which is only sin, not that which is also the penalty of sin.

**V. 48.** *Julian:* Therefore he does both the evil and the good by his own will; but his good he owes also to God, who supplies to this part, not indeed a prejudgment, yet a help.

*Augustine:* The human being, you say, does both the good and the evil by his own will, and the possibility of either is balanced in him in an equal scale, and for doing good God supplies a help; why, then, is the nature of mortals more prone to sinning, if original sin did nothing? Although it is not hidden what sort you say this help is, which you are compelled to confess God supplies. For you say it is the law, not the Spirit, although the apostle Paul teaches that we are helped by the supplying of the Holy Spirit[^85]. I thought this should be mentioned, lest perhaps those who hear or read your opinion about the supplying of divine help forget your heresy.

**V. 49.** *Julian:* So great, then, is the force of this distinction, that if, not knowing it, we conclude into the necessary what begins from the possible, all crimes run back to God. Seeing this, the Manichee devised darkness as the author of sin; for he could not distinguish between the possible and the necessary. Everything, then, which a human being has naturally, he has received from the side of the necessary; because he could not be otherwise than he was made.

*Augustine:* I have already shown a little before how empty this opinion is; for it is most stupid that human beings should have naturally the possibility of eating, but not naturally eat foods suited to nature; or that human beings should have naturally the possibility of intercourse, but not naturally have intercourse with the genital members of each sex. Who would say this, who considers at all what he says? For both are natural—both that these things can come to be, and that they come to be; but the former is so even when we do not will it; the latter is not, unless we will it.

**V. 50.** *Julian:* But the evil that he does, he does from the possible.

*Augustine:* Attend to him who says: *The evil I do not will, that I do*[^86]; and answer whether he does not have a necessity of doing evil, who does not the good that he wills, but the evil that he does not will, that he does. And if you do not dare to oppose the Apostle, behold, a human being doing evil from the necessary shatters and scatters your definitions: for by necessity he does evil who does not will it, and yet does it. But this which he does unwilling, if it is only to desire by the flesh, without any consent of the mind and operation of the members, then the concupiscence of the flesh is evil, even if it is not consented to for evil—which concupiscence you yet delight to praise; but if he who cries out, *The evil I do not will, that I do*[^87], is so far compelled that he even offers his members as weapons to sin, then not only are evils desired from the necessary, but they are even committed. Where are your definitions, which you distinguish with such wordiness? Surely they have failed and perished like smoke. You commend that the necessary and the possible must be distinguished with great care, saying that the necessary is what must come to be, but the possible is what can come to be, but is not necessary. Accordingly, you attribute necessity to the necessary, but constrain the possible by no necessity. Evil acts you give not to the necessary, but to the possible; and you say, speaking of the human being: *But the evil he does, he does from the possible*; lest anyone be said to do evil by necessity, not by will. But there comes forward into the midst one who most strongly contradicts you, and says: What is it you say? Behold, I *do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^88]. It is known that the first man did evil by will, not by necessity; but this man who says, *The evil I do not will, that I do*, shows himself to do evil by necessity, not by will; and weeping over his miseries, he laughs at your definitions.

**V. 51.** *Julian:* Unless, then, there had been a necessary possibility, the possible effect would not have been. That he can do evil and good, then, is necessary; but that he do evil, he owes not to the necessary, but to his own possible. But where there is a possibility of either part, there is necessity of neither. Thus it comes about that sin is nothing other than the will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain. But if the will is nothing other than a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling, how then, just as God, in order to make the world, had it from the possible, while the world itself, in order to exist, suffered it from the necessary, so also in the image of God a like thing is understood. For that he have whatever will he has chosen, he is not compelled; but it comes to him from the possible; but that which he has done badly has a necessity of guilt. So crime incurs horror from the necessary, although it arose not from necessity, but from the possibility of the one doing it. The work, then, is the testimony of the possibility of a free living being.

*Augustine:* Whoever reads those things which we answered you above now disregards you altogether. For he who says: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*, indicates clearly enough that he is pressed by necessity to do evil, and shows it to be false what you say: *That he do evil, he owes not to the necessary, but to his own possible*; and the other such things you chatter in vain. So it comes about that that definition, by which it is said that sin is the will to do what justice forbids, and from which one is free to abstain, embraces that sin, as I already pointed out above, which is only sin, not that which is also the penalty of sin. For by such a penalty this man was doing the evil he did not will; whence, if it were free for him to abstain, he would by no means say: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*. As, then, we recognize the human being blessed in the body of that life, where he was free to do what he willed, whether good or evil; so you too recognize the human being wretched in the body of this death, where, having lost liberty, you hear him say: *I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do;*[^89] and: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do;*[^90] and: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death?*[^91]

**V. 52.** *Julian:* The censure of this does not run back to the necessary; because whatever reaches the necessary strikes the author himself.

*Augustine:* Does this evil of the human being, where he says: *The evil I do not will, that I do*, strike the author of the human being himself? And yet it is plain enough that he who thus does evil has reached the necessary; for by necessity he does what he does not do by will.

**V. 53.** *Julian:* As, then, what comes from the necessary cannot be ascribed to my possible, so what comes from the possible cannot be ascribed to the necessary. That is, as the nature of my body and mind cannot be applied to my will, so that I should seem to be thus because I willed it—when I could not will before I was—so the evil of the will cannot be brought to nature, so that the works of possibility be mixed with necessity.

*Augustine:* From necessity, indeed, that is clearly and openly distinguished which can so come to be that it is not necessary; this you call possible, as though that were impossible which not only can come to be, but must also. But since it pleased you to impose such names on these two, let us understand as we can, and bear with it. But what is it you say: *The evil of the will cannot be brought to nature*? When an angel, or a human being, wills something, does not a nature will something? And are not the angel and the human being natures? Who would say this? If, then, the angel and the human being are natures, surely a nature wills whatever the angel wills, a nature wills whatever the human being wills. How, then, can the evil of the will not be brought to nature, when only a nature can will something? Or else let the sin of his will not be imputed to a human being; because a human being is a nature, and the evil of the will, as you say, cannot be brought to nature. Or does your vanity go so far that you say that ought to be imputed to nature which cannot be brought to nature? For who would say that what is imputed to a human being is not imputed to nature, unless someone so foolish that he denies a human being is a nature? Do you see how much you talk, not knowing what you talk? If, then, you say: The will cannot be brought to necessity—not even this is universally true. For sometimes we will what is necessary; as it is necessary that those who live well perseveringly become blessed; sometimes too it is necessary that we will something; as it is necessary that we will blessedness; whence there is also a certain blessed necessity, because it is necessary that God always and unchangeably and most blessedly live. But since there are also certain necessities so alien from wills that there is necessity where there is no will, and will where there is no necessity, it is at least in part true what is said: The will cannot be brought to necessity. But he who says: An evil will cannot be brought to nature, let him show us, if he can, a will, whether evil or good, where there is no nature; or that any will can exist, if there is no nature which wills something. Attend, then, how shut off you are from the truth. You say: An evil will cannot be brought to nature; but truth says: As long as there is any will, it cannot be separated from nature.

**V. 54.** *Julian:* Not looking into this subtlety of divisions, the Manichee brought forth to us the swarms of the Traducianists. For he argues in this manner: Whence comes evil? Surely from the will. Whence comes the evil will? He answers: From the human being. Whence the human being? Through God. And he concludes: If evil is from the human being, the human being through God, then evil is through God. And after this, as though religious, lest he make God criminal, he gives the nature of darkness, to which we should ascribe evil. Hence Augustine too: Whence comes evil? From the will. Whence, he says, the will itself? From the human being, who is the work of God. And he gathers: If evil is from the will, the will from the human being, the human being the work of God, then evil is through God. And as though trying to resolve this, lest he seem to call God criminal (which his offshoot affirms), he offered us a nothing equally violent in place of God, that is, darkness to which we should ascribe this evil. *For it is not*, he says, *because he was a work of God that evil was born in the human being, but because he was from nothing*. As though truth could not answer: And with what shamelessness does your God first lie that there is a will in the human being, then condemn it, since he knew that this evil, namely sin, came about from the necessity of darkness, that is, of the ancient nothing? We have declined "nothing" through its cases, that the virtue of the Traducianist who places his hope in nothing might appear. See, too, the weakness of the God whom the Traducianist introduces. He could not overcome the nothing itself; and when he had made the human being from nothing, he could not strip him of the condition of evil, which came from nothing; but, made the more bitter by the difficulty of the matter, he charges the human being with his own faults, and condemns the offenses of nothing by the destruction of his own image. The old man Manes deals more kindly with him, in saying that he was not everywhere laid waste by the race of darkness; whose so great feebleness the Traducianist introduces, that he devises that God was overcome by nothing.

*Augustine:* No one is overcome by nothing; but you are overcome by saying "nothing"; nor did I place my hope in nothing; but you have brought your wordiness to nothing. Indeed, if you understand rightly what you say perversely, in this way God is overcome by nothing, since no thing overcomes God; for what is nothing, except no thing? In this way too God cannot overcome nothing; because he does not overcome any thing, who overcomes all things; for he is above all things. But: *The Manichee*, as you say, *did not look into the subtleties of your divisions, and therefore says: If evil is from the human being, but the human being through God, then evil is through God*; so that, terrified by this conclusion, we should deny either that the human being is through God, or that evil is from the human being, or, like him, say that both are false; and so introduce to us some substance of darkness, which made the human being, and which is principally evil, whence all evil is believed to come. You, then, most subtle divider, by what cleverness do you think this craftiness must be resisted? *I will say*, you say, *that evil arose from the human being from the possible, not from the necessary*. As though he could not answer you: If evil is from the possible, possibility from nature, nature through God, then evil is through God. This conclusion, if you do not fear it, neither do I; since we both confess that the first man sinned not from the necessary, but from the possible. For we, in saying that the human being could sin for this reason—that his nature was not made from God, although it could not exist at all except by him creating it—do not say this in such a way that from this we say a necessity of sinning was forced upon him, as you slanderously claim. He could altogether both sin and not sin; but if he had not been made from nothing, that is, if his nature were from God, he could not sin at all. For who is so mad as to dare to say that the unchangeable and unconvertible nature which is God can in any way sin—of whom the Apostle says: *He cannot deny himself*[^92]? We therefore resist both Manichees, saying that the human being was not so made by the good and just God that it was necessary for him to sin; and that he sinned because he willed, who could also have not willed. But because we behold in his offspring evils so great and so manifest—not the voluntary evils of human beings, but those with which they are born—when you deny that these come from an origin vitiated by sin, your heresy surely sets up the Manichee, as if in a citadel, for introducing a nature of evil by whose commingling the nature of God is corrupted; from which citadel truth casts down both him at once and you.

**V. 55.** *Julian:* All who declare war on truth suffer this ignominy. Let us, then, gather up what we have done. It is asked whence that first evil will arose in the human being. We answer: From a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling. It is objected: Even if it appeared in the work of God? We assent that it is true. It is asked how we do not refuse this, who deny natural sin. We answer: Because in the work of God this sin exists from the possible, not from the necessary. Natural things, then, are necessary, but possible things are voluntary.

*Augustine:* There are also voluntary things that are necessary, as we will to be blessed, and it is necessary that we will it; there are also possible things that are natural; for it is thus possible that a woman who has intercourse with her husband, by her own and his genital members, should conceive, if neither she be sterile nor he; but it is not necessary; for it can come to be, but is not necessary; and yet it is natural. Be silent, I beg; your definitions are empty; your divisions are not subtle, but childish.

**V. 56.** *Julian:* And through this, just as we ascribe sin to the free motion, so we ascribe nature to God the founder. Human nature, then, is a good work of God; the liberty of choice, that is, the possibility either of doing wrong or of doing right, is equally a good work of God. Both of these come to the human being from the necessary; neither of these two things is the cause of evil. But thus far those necessary things have come; now will indeed arises in these, but not from these. For they are capable of will, not full of it; nor do they make, but receive, the diversity of merits.

*Augustine:* You most rightly confess that nature and free choice are good works of God; but in asserting that will arises in these indeed, but not from these, what more insane thing can be said? Is it really so, Julian, that a human being's will does not arise from a human being, when a human being is a good work of God? Finally, could it ascend into your heart that a human being's will arises indeed, but not from his free choice? Say, then, whence—if not from nature, that is, not from the human being himself; if not from his free choice, say, I ask, whence a human being's will arises. You have said where it arises; say also whence. Nature and free choice are good works of God: *In these*, you say, *will arises, but not from these*. Whence, then? Say; let us hear, let us learn. Or show that something has somewhere arisen, when there was nothing whence it could arise. The world indeed arose from nothing, but with God making it; for if it did not have God as its maker, it could not arise at all from no things. If, then, will too arose in the human being or in his free choice from nothing, who made it? Or, if not made, but arisen, who begot it? Or is it alone among the things which began to be, made by no one, born of no one? Why, then, is the human being condemned on account of it, in whom an evil will arose without his willing—of which he was only capable, not effective? But if, so that he may be justly condemned, it arose in him with his willing, why do you deny that this very will arose from him, with whose willing you do not deny it arose, and without whose willing you do not deny it could not arise? But when it arose from him, it arose from nature, because a human being is a nature; and because he could also not will what he willed, it arose from his free choice, which itself, as you confess, pertains to nature; why, then, do you deny with closed eyes things that are plain—that a human being's will arises from a human being's nature—while you fear lest the Manichee accuse the author of this nature? To refute that pestilence it is enough that Catholic truth proclaims that the human being was so founded by the best God that he did not have a necessity of sinning, nor would he sin if he did not will to sin; while he could also always not will it. For who is so blind of mind as not to see that, as the human being was first founded, it is a great good of nature, to be able not to sin; although it is greater not to be able to sin; and that it was most orderly established that the former should be first, whence a human being's merit might come, and the latter should be afterward the reward of one who has merited well?

**V. 57.** *Julian:* The good possibility of evil and good, then, does not compel the will, but permits it to arise. No one, therefore, is good because he is endowed with free choice; for there are many human beings equally free, but yet most wicked; but neither is anyone evil because he is of free choice; for there are many equally partakers of this liberty, yet most excellent. A human being, then, is neither good nor evil because he is free; but he could be neither good nor evil unless he were free. There is, then, this possibility, which is shown by the name of liberty, so established by the most wise God that without it there is not that which through it is not compelled to be. For by the one capacity for contraries it is vindicated from the prejudgment of either; that is, it cannot be called the cause and necessity of an evil will, nor of a good one, which so receives both that it compels neither to exist. The course of necessary things, then, is single, and is, as it were, one thread, like that geometrical length without breadth, nor can this unit be divided. As long, then, as it extends single, it holds the force of its nature; but where there meets it something by which it is split into diverse parts, that necessary thing is at once terminated. That is: the good God made the human being good.

*Augustine:* Why, then, did you say that a human being is neither good nor evil except by his own will; and that whatever he has from God, he has from the necessary, not from the possible? By which you wish it understood that he has it from nature, not from will; so that a human being is good through himself, not through God, or at least better through himself than through God. For these are your words: *No one*, you say, *is good because he is endowed with free choice*. And a little after: *Nor is anyone evil*, you say, *because he is of free choice*. By which words what do you say, but that God made the human being neither good nor evil, but he makes himself either of these, when he uses free choice well or badly? What is it, then, that you now say: *The good God made the human being good*; if he is neither good nor evil by having the free choice which God made in him, but by using it well, that is, when now he himself wills well, not when he has the possibility of willing well? And how will it be true: *God made the human being upright*[^93]? Or was he upright not having a good will, but the possibility of it? Then he was also depraved not having an evil will, but the possibility of it; and the good will is his from himself, and it is falsely written: *The will is prepared by the Lord*[^94]; and: *God works in you also to will*[^95]. Although you do not say that the good or evil will is his from himself; but that it arises in him, not from him. So it comes about that, by your marvelous wisdom, neither did God make the human being upright, but one who could be upright if he willed; nor does he make himself, but by some chance becomes upright, because the will by which he becomes upright arises in him not from him, but from I know not where, or in I know not what way. This is not the wisdom that comes down from above, but is earthly, animal, diabolical[^96].

**V. 58.** *Julian:* And both to begin, and to begin well, the substance is directed by the union of the necessary. It also receives the liberty of choice; it is still likewise contained by the line of the necessary; but now is the end of necessary things; from here wills are split into contraries. To the union of the necessary, then, the nature of division does not pertain. Thus we are compelled to have possibility; but to use that possibility either well or badly, we are not compelled. So it comes about that even the possibility of sinning is capable of good and evil, but of voluntary good and evil; since it could not be capable of its proper good, unless it were also capable of evil.

*Augustine:* Say rather, if you wish to say what is true, that human nature was first made capable of good and depraved; not that it could not be made capable of good alone; but that from that very step it ought most orderly to rise, so that if it did not sin when it could sin, it would arrive at that blessedness where it could not sin. For, as I have already said, both are a great good, though one is less, the other greater. For to be able not to sin is less, but not to be able to sin is greater; and it was fitting that from the merit of the lesser good one should arrive at the reward of the greater. For if, as you say, human nature *could not be capable of its proper good unless it were also capable of evil*, why, after this life is piously spent, will it be capable of good alone, and not of evil—free, that is, from all sinning that is alien, not only of will or necessity, but even of possibility? Or is it to be feared, lest then too we should perhaps sin, when we shall be equal to the holy angels? Of whom it is without doubt to be believed that they received the inability to sin as the reward of their perseverance, since they stood firm, when, others falling, they too could have sinned. Otherwise it would still be to be feared lest this world should have many new devils, and their new evil angels. The life of the saints too who have departed from their bodies will be suspect to us, lest there too, where they have come, they perhaps have sinned, or perhaps do sin; if in the rational nature the possibility of sinning remains, and it cannot be capable of good, if it be not also capable of evil. Since these things are vehemently absurd, this opinion is to be spat out; and it is rather to be believed that this nature was first made capable of both good and evil for this reason, that by loving the one of these it might acquire merit, by which it might afterward become capable of good alone, or of evil alone; yet so that, if it were punished with perpetual condemnation, it would be compelled only to suffer evil, not also permitted to do it.

**V. 59.** *Julian:* But as great as is the difference between the full and the empty, so great surely is the difference between possibility and necessity. For the possibility of that thing of which it is said to be capable is shown to be empty; since, if it were not empty, it would not be capable. For who would contain what he already had? But necessity indicates not emptiness, but fullness; for it cannot, as it were empty, contain what it already compels to be, as it were full. As much, then, is there between necessary and possible things as between the crammed and the void. From the prejudgments of either quality, then, of which it is equally capable, it is defended by the very reception of the opposites. It has, therefore, the necessary good in nature only so far as pertains to the honor of its author; which is innocence mixed naturally with no evil, the receiver of its own work, for the property of good and the accusation of evil. That, then, which comes to it from its own, anyone sinning can wound; but this which it has received from the work of God, he cannot discolor. There remains, therefore, even in evil human beings, the estimation of the natural good; nor will it ever be an evil to have been able to do good and evil; but it will profit nothing that person who does not indeed condemn the institutions of his necessary, but yet has compelled them not to profit him. As, then, in the human being in whom liberty is already unfolded, when he sins, we ascribe the evil to the will, but nature to God the author of his condition; so if a little one, who has no use of will, and shows nothing beyond the institutions of nature, be said to be full of crime, and to have from the necessary the evil which another takes from the possible; without doubt he is charged as the author of the crime, who is the author of nature.

*Augustine:* We openly break your rules in the grown, so that you cannot transfer them to little ones. He was no little one who said: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^97]. In him was not the emptiness of possibility, but the fullness of necessity, to speak of these matters in your manner; that which contained was not empty, but stuffed with the evil it had taken in. For he does not say: I can do good and evil; which possibility of human nature was no evil, nor of the will; but he says: *I do not do the good I will*. Nor only that; but he even added: *but the evil I do not will, that I do*. Behold, both that he does not do the good, and that he does the evil, he owes not to the possible, as you establish, but, as he suffers and confesses, to his own necessary; weak indeed to take away his miseries, but plainly a most firm hammer to grind your rules to powder. He wills and does not do what is good, he does not will and does what is evil; whence this necessity? Catholic teachers indeed recognize it, who understand the apostle Paul to be saying these things even of himself, and do not doubt that it comes from the law which in the members fights against the law of the mind[^98], without which no human being is born; and therefore they see that it is said even by the saints: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*; because they see how great a good it is not even to desire by the flesh what they turn from in mind; and that they will this, and do not do it; and that it is an evil, although the mind does not consent, yet by the flesh to desire such things; and that they do not will this, but do it, without any condemnation indeed, since, the guilt of this sin being blotted out by regeneration, they resist with the mind, lest they accomplish what they desire by the flesh; but not without some evil of their own, because not an alien nature is commingled with them, but it is their own nature both in mind and in flesh. This pious and true sense against your protégée you are unwilling to admit; as though for this reason you might bring it about that, in the judgment by which it is defended by you, nothing should be recited against it, not only from the writings, but even from the very morals of human beings and the groanings of the saints, against your will; and that it be recited with so great a manifestation of truth, that not your eloquence, but only your shamelessness remains, by which you cannot defend it, but wish to. For what are you doing, when you try to cloud plain things with the storm of your turbid wordiness? The Apostle cries out: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*. He cries out also above: *It is no longer I that do it, but the sin that dwells in me; for I know that there does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh, the good*[^99]. What is: *what I do not will, I do*? And what is: *it is no longer I that do it*? What is it, but what he consistently expounds? For by saying, *what I do not will, I do*, he shows that he does it; and again by saying, *it is no longer I that do it*, he shows that not the consenting mind, but his own desiring flesh does it; for the flesh acts by desiring, even if it does not draw the mind to consent. On this account he adds: *I know that there does not dwell in me*. And expounding what he means by "in me": *That is*, he says, *in my flesh, the good*. But suppose these words are not the Apostle's, as you wish, but of any human being weighed down by his evil habit, which he cannot conquer by will; are not these too so robust that they break and grind to powder your arguments about the possible and the necessary, like childish and playful little tablets, by their robustness? Since there is, what you are unwilling, not only a voluntary and possible sin, from which one is free to abstain, but also a necessary sin, from which one is not free to abstain, which is now not only sin, but also the penalty of sin. Nor do you wish to attend that what is done in each one through the violence of habit (which certain learned men have called a second nature), this has been done through the penal violence of that highest and greatest sin of the first man, in all who were in his loins, destined to arise through his concupiscence, when the human race was propagated—which concupiscence the shame of the sinners covered in the region of the loins.

**V. 60.** *Julian:* But what have we to do with little ones, when the question of the Manichees says that not even a human being of perfect age sins by will? For if evil arose in the human being because he was made from nothing, but the human being had from the necessary that he be made from nothing; without doubt he received evil not from the possible, but from the necessary. Since this has been destroyed by a long disputation, let us still discourse a little on this same matter, that it may grow clearer and clearer by repetition. You ask, then, whence that evil will arose in the first man. I answer: From a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling. You ask too whence the motion itself. I answer: What do you seek? Whence it could be, or whence it was compelled to be? If you say, as you also wrote: Whence it was compelled to be, I will report that you speak inconsistent and contradictory things. For you ask who compelled what cannot be, except with nothing compelling; which, since it is dissolved by its own self-contradiction, the question has no force which has no order. Therefore you ask most stupidly whence the evil will itself comes. For this which you say, "Whence," seeks not its occasion, but its origin, that is, its nature; but this, as was treated above, if it receives a nature, loses its definition, by which it was said, *with nothing compelling*; but if it keeps its definition, it excludes the prejudgment of birth. Not for this reason, then, did the human being sin, because he was made from nothing; not for this reason, because he was made by God; not for this reason, because he was made from darkness; not for this reason, because he was made of free choice; but he sinned for this reason, because he willed; that is, he had an evil will for this reason, because he willed.

*Augustine:* We, or rather Truth itself, says that some human beings of perfect age do evil by will, some by necessity, or that the same persons do evil in some things by will, in some by necessity. And if you think this false, look at him who cries out: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*. Whom you must so often have thrown back in your face, as often as, saying these things, you either pretend not to see him, or perhaps do not see him. Why do you wrap yourself in twisting evasions? It is not said to you: A human being had a necessity of sinning, because he was made from nothing; but you say this to yourself. He was certainly so made that he had the possibility of sinning from the necessary, but sin from the possible. Yet he would not have even the possibility of sinning, if he were the nature of God; for it would surely be unchangeable, and could not sin. Not for this reason, then, did he sin, but for this reason he could sin, because he was made from nothing. Between "He sinned" and "He could sin" there is the greatest difference; the former is fault, the latter nature. Nor could everything which was made from nothing sin; for wood and stones cannot sin; but yet the nature which could sin was made from nothing. Nor is it a great thing to be unable to sin; but it is a great thing to be unable to sin together with blessedness. Just as it is not a great thing to be unable to be wretched; because all things which do not have the capacity for blessedness cannot be wretched; but it is a great thing for a nature to be so blessed that it cannot be wretched. And though this is greater, neither is that small: that a human being's nature was founded in such blessedness that, if it willed, it could be not wretched. But all things are said to be made from nothing, that is, from those things which were not, in this sense, that we may understand that whatever was made from that which already was, is to be referred to the first origin. For flesh is from earth; but earth from nothing. For so we say also that all human beings are sons of Adam, when each is a son of his own father. Yet all things which were made are changeable, because they were made from nothing, that is, they were not, and by God making them they are, and they are good; for they were made by the good one; nor would any changeable goods exist at all, insofar as they are, unless there were he by whom they were created, the unchangeable good. All evils, therefore, which are nothing other than privations of goods, have arisen from goods, but changeable ones; for both the angel and the human being, from whom evils arose (which yet could also not have arisen, if they had not willed to sin, since they could also not will it), we can rightly call good natures, but not rightly unchangeable. But God is so good that he uses even evils well, which the Omnipotent would not allow to be, if by his supreme goodness he could not use them well; and hence he would rather appear powerless and less good, by not being able to use even evil well. Accordingly, you are not allowed to deny that he who says: *The evil I do not will, that I do*[^100], has now taken evil from the necessary, not from the possible. Not, then, as you say: *Every action of evil is not from the necessary, but from the possible*; rather, some is found also from the necessary. See now how your so labored contrivance has collapsed. But to one asking whence the evil will arose in the first man, you think you answer more cautiously: *From a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling*; as though you would not answer more quickly and readily: From the human being himself. For that which is added, *with nothing compelling*, could have been added by you here too, with no one resisting you. For who would resist you saying what is true, if you said: An evil will arose in the first man from the human being himself, with nothing compelling? But now, fearing to blame nature, as though hence any injury ran back to its author, you have at last said what you long wished to say, and have not withdrawn from nature. For the mind is a nature; and indeed in the constitution of the human being a nature better than the body, from whose motion, *with nothing compelling*, you said an evil will arose. Do you see that it could not exist except from somewhere, which you are not allowed to deny was not before it existed? But what need is there to ask whence the motion of the mind comes, when it is plain enough that the motion of the mind could not exist except from the mind? But if you most shamelessly and most insipidly deny this, it is still asked of you whence an evil will arose in the first man; nor are you any longer permitted to say: *From a motion of the mind, with nothing compelling*; because the motion of the mind itself, with nothing compelling, is the will. Wherefore, to say this—The will arose from a motion of the mind—is to say: The motion of the mind arose from a motion of the mind; or: The will arose from the will. Or do you say that this motion arose from itself, not from the mind, lest hence the good nature, that is, the mind itself, be blamed? Then let it not hence be condemned; for who would bear that the mind be justly condemned for that for which it cannot be justly blamed? But you say: *The human being sinned for this reason, because he willed; he had an evil will for this reason, because he willed*. You say most truly; but if the clearest light is not darkness, an evil will arose from him, because he willed. For we do not say, as you slanderously claim, and as you also lie that we wrote: *whence that motion was compelled to be*, but, with no one compelling, whence it arose; because, even with no one compelling, it yet arose, and could not exist except from somewhere, which before it existed was not. If, then, the human being willed, it arose from the human being; and what was the human being, before it arose from him, but a good nature and a good work of God? Which even an evil human being is, insofar as he is a human being and a work of God. Let Julian, then, be confounded by his own vanity; since Ambrose said truly that evils have arisen from goods[^101]; but because it was with no one compelling, God is uncharged; but in that he permitted them to exist, he is more signally praised by his just and good use of them.

**V. 61.** *Julian:* The will, then, which is nothing other than a motion of the mind with nothing compelling, owes its possibility to nature, its effect to itself. For it arose in nature, but from the possible, not from the necessary. Here if someone says: But it is an evil nature, which could have an evil will; I answer: But it is a good nature, which could have a good will. At one time, then, it will be called at once best and worst. But the nature of things does not allow that at one and the same time one and the same thing be filled with contrary qualities of merits. If, then, it be thought evil because it could do evil, let it be believed good because it could work good. But why, one says, could it also do evil, which was working good? I answer: Because this good which is called virtue could not be proper, unless it were voluntary; but it would not be voluntary, if it had a necessity of good; and it would suffer a necessity of good, if it did not have a possibility of evil. So that the right of good might stand, the possibility of evil was admitted.

*Augustine:* As I see, you are unwilling to attribute even a good will to nature, when the human being was first founded; as though God could not make a human being of good will, in which, however, he would not compel him to remain, but it would be in his choice whether he wished always to be in it, or not always, but should change himself from it into an evil will, with no one compelling, as in fact happened. For the human being did not first lack the will of not sinning, and begin his life from the will of sinning, the life in which God founded him upright; certainly such a one as could already use reason. For who would bear it, if he were said to be made such as infants are born? That perfection of nature, then, which not the years gave, but the hand of God alone, could not but have some will, and that not evil; otherwise it would not be written: *God made the human being upright*[^102]. The human being, then, was made of a good will, ready to obey God, and obediently receiving the precept, which he would keep without any difficulty as long as he willed, and would forsake without any necessity when he willed; nor was he going to do the former without fruit, nor the latter with impunity. Whence by a pious and sober consideration it is gathered that the first good will is the work of God; for with it he made the human being upright; for no one is ever upright, except by willing what is right. On which account a good will is not restored, once lost, except by him by whom it was founded; nor is it to be thought that the necessity of sin can be healed from anywhere else, except by the mercy of him by whose deep and just judgment it followed the descendants of him who sinned without any necessity. Whence that man, after he had wept over the necessity and penalty of the sin dwelling in his flesh, by which he was compelled to do the evil he did not will, soon showing to whom one must flee for refuge, said: *Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^103]. You see surely how that possibility, by which you think you have found something great, does not aid this man; for it is now lost, when evil is done by necessity, and under a necessary evil a human being cries out: *Wretched man that I am*; but plainly let him aid him, by whose grace even those things are overcome which you call necessary for this reason, that they cannot be otherwise. For what is impossible for human beings is easy for God. To whom it was not necessary that a camel could not enter through the eye of a needle, but rather possible that it should enter; just as flesh and bones were sent through closed doors[^104]. In vain, then, do you try to defend a vitiated nature. If you seek to do something useful for it, act so that it be healed, not that it be excused. Allow that it itself did that for which it would be deservedly condemned. For whence the evil will arose, whatever else you say, and you deny that it arose from it, you affirm its condemnation to be unjust. For what else do you say, but: It itself did not do that for which it is condemned? What, then, is more iniquitous than that it be condemned for what it itself did not do? But if it itself did it, why do you try to excuse it from the possible, whence you are convicted of accusing it more inexcusably? But you say: *An evil will arose in nature indeed, but from the possible, not from the necessary*. If this possibility is outside nature, let it rather, whence the evil will arose, be condemned, not nature. But if the possibility too pertains to nature, then nature rather made an evil will for itself, because it could also not make it; for this the definition of possibility commended by you shows. No one says to you: *It is an evil nature, because it could have an evil will*. We at least, against whom you now speak, do not say this; why do you linger over superfluous things? But in that which you said: *This good which is called virtue would not be voluntary, if it had a necessity of good; but it would suffer a necessity of good, if it did not have a possibility of evil*; you have altogether forgotten God, whose virtue is the more necessary, the more he so wills it that he cannot not will it. For you too said, in the first book of this work of yours: *That God cannot but be just*[^105]; which, if it is to be called necessity, let it be called so by all means; provided only it be established that nothing is happier than this necessity, by which it is as necessary that God not live badly, as it is necessary that he always and most blessedly live. For such a necessity does not fear your words, in which you were unwilling to say: But it would have a necessity of good, but preferred to say: *It would suffer it, if it did not have a possibility of evil*; so that, namely, God might seem to have spared the human being, lest, as though suffering something burdensome, he should bear a necessity of good, by not having a possibility of evil; which is so great a good that it was reserved for the saints as a reward—whom likewise you have forgotten, as you have God. For neither shall we then live without virtue, when it shall be granted us that we can never depart from the Lord, since we shall not even be able to will it. For so will the good be certain for us, with which, as was promised, *we shall always be with the Lord*[^106], that we shall not will to depart from him, nor be able to will it. There would not, then, be virtue in us otherwise than if we so did not have an evil will that we could have it; but for the merit of this lesser virtue, a greater virtue ought to accrue to us in reward, that we should so not have an evil will that we could not even have it. O necessity to be desired! Truth will bestow it, that there may be a sure security, without which there cannot be that fullness of our felicity, to which nothing is to be added.

**V. 62.** *Julian:* But this can be twisted to the contrary, so that it be said: But nature was fitted to evil; for since evil could not be voluntary, if there were a necessity of evil, on this account a possibility of good was given, that a property might be prepared for evil. This indeed is acute, but insane; for all things are estimated from their better part: here too the dignity of the author, that is, of God, comes in, who made a free living being not on account of the things he was going to punish, but on account of the things he was going to reward, and gave the possibility of contraries. Yet over this I am unwilling to fight; but I permit some slander, so that I prejudge nothing about the authority of the maker. This, however, follows of necessity: that this possibility of doing good and evil be removed from the effect of either will. And through this, neither is the cause of virtue from the necessary, nor of vice. For—to do the honorable this injury, that from the fact that they seem to contend with the wicked, neither the merit of a good nor of an evil will be ascribed to it—it has, then, the testimony of its own nobility, that it is innocent, because it is full neither of good nor of evil, voluntary at least. Ascribe, then, the possibility of will to nature; but the will, neither good nor evil, to nature. It has, then, been concluded invincibly that an evil will arose indeed in the work of God, but from the possible, not from the necessary; which cannot be reckoned to the giver of the possibility, but to the governor of the possibility itself.

*Augustine:* You grant that a will, neither good nor evil, is to be ascribed to nature, but only the possibility of a good or evil will, although both the angel and the human being are a nature. To which, if a good or evil will is not to be ascribed, as you say, then it is neither to be honored for a good, nor to be condemned for an evil will. For what is more iniquitous than that we should judge it must be condemned for an evil which is not to be ascribed to it? Or are the angel and the human being not natures? Who says this, but one who does not know what he says? That, then, is ascribed to nature which is ascribed to the angel; that is ascribed to nature which is ascribed to the human being; but to a nature which was founded good by the good God, and was made evil by its own will. And through this, most rightly the evil which is ascribed to these natures is not ascribed to him by whom they were founded; because he did not so found them, when he first founded them, that there should be in them any necessity of having an evil will, but only a possibility; where a good will, not deserted, might acquire and find merit, the reward; deserted, the punishment. Why, then, do you seek to excuse nature by the malice of the will, whose it is to will or not to will? For there is no evil will but of an angel or a human being willing, which we can in no way say are not natures. Why, I say, do you ascribe an evil will to a human being, so that he can not unjustly pay penalties for the merit of an evil will; and what you ascribe to a human being, you are unwilling to ascribe to nature, as though a human being could in any way not be a nature? How much better it is for you to speak soundly, and to say that a human being's evil will is only of some nature, because every human being is a nature; but that this nature, when it first did evil, had an evil will not from the necessary, but from the possible; since by these names it pleased you to distinguish these two, in one of which is understood that to come to be which is necessary, in the other that which can indeed come to be, but is not necessary, because it can also not come to be? For this is said most truly of the sin of the first man, or of the first human beings; there remains he who cries out: *I do not do the good I will, but the evil I do not will, that I do*[^107]. For this man does evil from the necessary, who does not will and does it; and he breaks your rule, which you fashioned by rash wordiness, saying that *neither the cause of virtue is proved to be from the necessary, nor of vice*; when the cause of this is proved to be from the necessary. For either an action of evil is not a vice; or to not do the good one wills, and to not will evil, and yet do it, is not necessity; or, on the contrary, there will not be for us also a happy necessity of virtue, when our nature shall be filled with so great a grace, and God will be *all in all*[^108], that it cannot will anything evil. For virtue is justice; and a new heaven and a new earth are promised us, in which justice dwells[^109]. Or, if perhaps, disturbed, you say that you fashioned this rule for the present life, not the future; I do not contend with one conquered; you at least do not deny that this pertains to this life, where you see a human being will, and not do good; and not will, and do evil; and you are compelled to attribute this vice, against your own rule, to necessity, not to will. But from this necessity, with which even little ones are born, but which begins to appear with the advance of age, who frees the wretched human being, except *the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^110]? Of which grace you, enemies, trust in your own virtue; and against the divine words, which condemn those who trust in their own virtue[^111], you dispute with impious pride.

**V. 63.** *Julian:* Let it cleave, then, to the mind of the prudent reader that there is a great difference between those things which come from the possible and those which come from the necessary; and let him apply all natural things to necessary things, but voluntary things to possible ones; and let him conclude all questions from the side on which they began. For if through both he wanders with blind judgment, he is hurled into countless errors through almost all his words; and since this has become clear enough, it appears that you have been altogether too much in the dark, you who concluded: *As evil, which was nowhere, could arise in the work of God; so also that which already was passed naturally through the work of God*. For see in what error you are entangled: sin, conceived by the first will, which came from the possible, you say was changed into necessary things, so that, just as a free motion of the mind could arise, so without a free motion it passed into the necessity of natural things. But understand that God is the author of necessary things. If, then, God does in natures what the mind did in faults, it is necessary that he be guilty in the same way as he whose will he censures. Indeed more so; for as much as the necessary is more than the possible, so much more wicked is it to engender than to usurp sin. Although the nature of things does not admit this; yet here I would have shown that your opinion about God is much worse than the Manichee's. For the Manichee's God was maimed by sudden wars; but yours was corrupted by ancient and multiplied crimes. And through this you dissent from the Catholics not only about the question, but altogether about God; you do not worship him whom we venerate as most just, most omnipotent, inviolable in the Trinity. The thing of the will, then, could not permeate through nature; and we rightly said that the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God. For the work of the devil and of the evil human being is sin, which can exist in none without a motion of the free will. This work comes, and comes, to both the devil and the human being from the possible; but the work of God is nature, in which a human being subsists not from the possible, but from the necessary; which nature is for many times without will, since its force is felt at a certain age. As long, then, as it is a nature without will, it is only the work of God; and this, what it has not done, it cannot have as sin. Irrefutably, then, has it been said that the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God. But no less false than profane is what you said: *The work of a work of God passes through the work of God*. For this is to say: God too sins, because the human being whom God made sinned. For just as sin is nowhere except in the work of the human being—for neither, when a human being has sinned, is anything added to his substance, so that sin should seem to stand out there, but only an evil work, committed from an evil will, gains an evil merit for him too by whom it was done, so that he is called evil who did evil things—so too your God, if he did evil in his work, nothing indeed accrues to his substance, just as neither to the human being's; yet a most deformed merit is gained for him, that he be called evil by doing evil. The little one indeed is proved not to be guilty even then, because he has malice from the necessary, which, unless the devil had it from the possible, he could not be guilty. But God, who is the true God of Christians, does not do evil; the little one too, before the choice of his own will, has nothing except what God made in him. No sin, therefore, can be natural. But since we have most carefully burst forth into the caverns of the ancient error, nor has anything remained hidden on this question; let the diligent reader hold the distinction of the necessary and the possible; and he will laugh at the inventions of the Manichees no less than of the Traducianists.

*Augustine:* Among human beings who so understand what they read that they understand even these things you say, you have accomplished nothing else, by repeating the same things through the same with such great perplexity, except to make it appear that you wished to obscure our answer in my one book—which you undertook to refute with your eight—since you could not dissolve it. But among those who do not understand these things, you have at least accomplished this: that they think you have said something for the reason that they do not understand it. Whence they must be briefly admonished for the present about this matter now in hand, so that, the fogs of your wordiness removed, they may look at that very thing of mine, and see how unconquerable it is. For you had said: *If nature is a work of God, the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God*. To this I said: *What is it that he says*, I said, *if nature is a work of God, the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God? Was not the work of the devil, when it first arose in the angel who became the devil, a work that arose in a work of God? Wherefore, if evil, which was utterly nowhere, could arise in a work of God, why could not evil, which already was somewhere, pass through a work of God; especially since the Apostle uses this very word, saying: " And so it passed to all men "*[^112]*? Are not human beings the work of God? Sin, then, passed through human beings, that is, the work of the devil through the work of God; and, to say the same thing in another way, the work of a work of God through the work of God. And therefore God alone is unchangeable and of most powerful goodness; who both, before there was any evil, made all good works, and from the evils which arose in the goods made by him, works well through all*. Disturbed by this evidence of things, you thought the eyes of human beings should be darkened by a long and empty disputation about the possible and the necessary, so that, this fog being thrown up, you might not withdraw your empty opinion, lest it be destroyed, but conceal it, lest, being destroyed, it be seen to lie prostrate. For whether from the necessary or from the possible, what does it matter to the matter about which we inquire? The angel certainly and the human being sinned; either, then, dare to say that the angel and the human being are not natures; or, since you are not so mad as to dare, you are convicted that, the angel sinning, a nature sinned; you are convicted that, the human being sinning, a nature sinned. But from the possible, you say, not from the necessary, he sinned. This is true; yet the angel sinned, yet the human being sinned, yet a nature sinned; and so the work of God, which is the angel, which is the human being, sinned—not with God compelling, but by its own evil will, which it could also not have had. To that nature, then, belongs the ignominy, which, although it was well made, and was not compelled to do evil, yet did evil; but to God the glory, who both made the nature good, and from the evil which he himself did not do, does good. Since, therefore, by these and such-like true and Catholic reasons, the creating nature can both be defended and proclaimed, and the sinning nature can be charged and reprehended; and the sinning nature itself can both be praised insofar as God made it, and be blamed insofar as it fell away from him, with no one compelling, and received its merit in its descendants (for this same nature is born without will in each individual, which sinned by will in one); who hurled you headlong to say, who drowned you to write: *If nature is a work of God, the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God*? O deaf to the holy words! O blind in your own inventions! Is not sin the work of the devil? Did it not pass to all human beings, who are the work of God? Is not death through sin—that especially which you say alone happened through sin, that is, not of the body, but of the soul—the work of the devil? Did it not pass to all human beings, who are the work of God? But by imitation, as you say, it passed. Yet it passed through human beings, who are the work of God. But from the possible, not from the necessary, it passed. Say whatever you please: yet it passed through human beings, who are the work of God; but you, without any exception, said: *The work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God*; and this so transparent vanity of your opinion, to make yourself the vainer, you tried, with such great wordiness, not to clear up so that it be resolved, but to cover so that it not be seen. If the apostolic words had not come into your mind, which would forbid you to say this; why did you not attend, I beg, that it is more for the work of the devil to exist in the work of God, than to pass through the work of God? Since, then, you confess the former, why do you deny the latter? Or can what you wish come to be, while what you do not wish cannot? May God have mercy on you, that you cease to be vain. But this opinion of yours the Manichee gladly embraces, as his friend, and argues thus: If the work of the devil is not permitted to pass through the work of God, much less is it permitted to exist in the work of God; whence, then, comes evil, except whence we say? But we answer him: Say these things to Julian, not to us. The prince of this opinion has been cast outside; far be it that he should prejudge against us, who is to be conquered along with you, or rather already conquered by us along with you.

**V. 64.** *Julian:* That other thing, too, you brought forth out of the same dullness. For you say: *God so creates the evil as he feeds and nourishes the evil*, because it is written in the Gospel that *he makes his sun rise on the good and the evil, and rains on the just and the unjust*[^113]. For far and wide contrary is that which you thought joined. For that God feeds even sinners, and is kind toward the ungrateful and the evil, is a testimony of his goodness, not of his malignity; who indeed *does not will the death of the dying, but that he turn back and live*[^114]; nor does he quickly punish the erring; nor does he do this for any other reason than that his goodness may grant a time for repentance. For so says the Apostle: *Or do you not know that the goodness of God is leading you to repentance? But by your hardness and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself*[^115]. He disputes also among the Lycaonians and the Areopagites that God, even in the times of past ignorance, did not withdraw the proofs of his providence. *For he did not leave himself without witness, giving*, he says, *rains from heaven and fruitful seasons, filling their hearts with food and gladness*[^116]. This, then, that he rains on the good and on the evil, is a proof of his kindness, which therefore bears with and awaits the erring, that they may at last withdraw from evil and do good. So far, then, is he from willing that evil be done, that out of love even for human emendation he feeds even the ungrateful. While that is the mark of perfect piety, this which you say—*He creates the evil*—is the testimony of perfect iniquity. See, then, how you do not know what you say, you who wished to prove cruelty from an example of mercy. For it is good to feed even the evil, that, if they will, they may be corrected; but it is wicked to make little ones evil, so that those who cannot will may yet be compelled to be unjust. Liberality, then, exercised toward sinners leads them away from evils, does not compel them into evils. But the condition of the evil does not lead away from the worst things; rather, it compels both the work and the worker into all wicked things. You are insane, then, when you say: God creates evil; but you are more insane when you try to affirm it by the testimony of the Gospel, and by that testimony in which there is a great argument of the divine goodness. Attend, then, how much more strongly it is now turned back; God, who feeds even the evil, that by his patience he may make them good, it is manifest does not create the evil. But if he creates the evil, he neither loves nor rewards the good, nor, finally, can he himself have anything of good; since the creating power harms more effectively and more violently than any evil will, not only of possible, but even of necessary evils. Since this does not befit the God of Christians, that is, him who is called the Father of mercies and the God of consolation[^117]; whose judgments are all proclaimed equity[^118]; who is declared to have made all things in wisdom[^119]; you have, as Manichees, no fellowship with us in the estimation of our God; to some other author altogether, whom you worship, yet feigned by the frenzy of Manes, these men have drawn their foolish inventions and inborn sins.

*Augustine:* I will do what—why you did not do it, what concern is it of mine even to say? Let those who read judge; for because you had said *that, according to our sense, God makes human beings over to the devil*, it came about that one came to these words by my answer, from which you set down what you wished; but I will recall, even against your will, what you thought should be passed over. Among other things, then—all of which it would be too long to say: *Or does he indeed*, I said, *feed the sons of perdition, the goats of the left hand*[^120], *for the devil, nourish and clothe them for the devil, because he makes his sun rise on the good and the evil, and rains on the just and the unjust? So, then, he creates the evil as he feeds and nourishes the evil; because what he bestows on them by creating pertains to the goodness of nature, and the increase he gives them by feeding and nourishing, he bestows as a good aid not, surely, on their malice, but on the same nature which the good one created. For insofar as they are human beings, it is a good of nature, of which God is the author; but insofar as they are born with sin, destined to perish if they are not reborn, it pertains to the seed cursed from the beginning*[^121], *by the fault of that ancient disobedience. Which yet the maker uses well, even in the vessels of wrath, that he may make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy*[^122]; *lest anyone attribute it to his own merits, if, belonging to the same lump, he be freed by grace, but let him who glories, glory in the Lord*[^123]. Having said these things, I added these: *From this faith*, I said, *apostolic and Catholic, most true and most well-founded, this man, departing with the Pelagians, is unwilling that those being born be under the devil, lest little ones be carried to Christ, that they may be rescued from the power of darkness and transferred into his kingdom*[^124]. *And so he accuses the Church spread throughout the whole world, in which everywhere all infants to be baptized are, for no other reason, exsufflated, except that the prince of the world may be cast out of them*[^125]; *by whom the vessels of wrath are necessarily possessed, when they are born of Adam, if they are not reborn in Christ, and, made vessels of mercy, transferred by grace into his kingdom*; and the other things which he who wishes and can may read there, or hear read. Now, then, since, leaving aside these things by which what you set down is built up and fortified, you thought those things should be set down in such a way that you might invade them robber-like, as though in a wilderness with no one defending; let those read these things who wish to know what you have done, or rather let them turn back to the consideration of the same book, from which I have set these down again, and let them see that those things remain most firm which you tried, as though weak, to undermine. What good, then, does it do you that you objected to me those whom God's patience awaits to be corrected by repentance, and therefore makes his sun rise upon them and rains; when I objected to you the goats of the left hand, whom he who foreknows all things to come cannot be ignorant will live to the end without repentance in their impiety and crimes, and therefore are to be punished with eternal punishment? Nor does he withhold from them the good of his creation, to whom it would not be expedient even to be born; nor the good of his nourishment and vivification, for as long as it shall please him, even prolonged, to whom it would be expedient to die as soon as possible; among whom there are certainly very many who, if they were snatched from this life as infants, according to your false heresy, would be taken away utterly from all; but according to the Catholic faith, from a most grievous condemnation. What, next, is this: that among these goats of the left hand, destined in the foreknowledge of God, which cannot be deceived, for perpetual fire, there are many who, washed by the laver of regeneration, afterward either perish by apostasy, or live so wickedly and shamefully that they are without doubt reckoned to the same left hand; nor are they snatched away, like some, *lest malice change their understanding*[^126]? Neither is he prevented by any fatal necessity from conferring on them this so great benefit; nor, in conferring it on others, is he changed by any respect of persons. What do those possible and necessary things of yours do here, which you commend as needing to be carefully distinguished, where, while you do not know what you say, he knows what he does, whose judgments can be hidden, but cannot be unjust? It is not unjust, then, that goods be conferred on the evil; but it is unjust that evils be inflicted on the good. Say, then, by what justice little ones suffer such great evils, which it wearies us to mention so often; you who are not ashamed to bring those same evils into paradise, even if no one had sinned. *They are not created evil*, as you say, that is, drawing original sin; by what justice, then, are they pressed by a heavy yoke, from the day of their coming out of their mother's womb[^127]? In which yoke there is so great misery that we can more easily deplore than explain it. You say that sin cannot be changed from the possible into the necessary, that is, from the voluntary into the non-voluntary; which we have shown could be, in him who says: *The evil I do not will, that I do*[^128]. This you attribute to the violence of habit, not to the bonds of a vitiated origin; yet you see that sin could be converted from the possible into the necessary; and you do not blush at your distorted and deceptive rules. You are unwilling that something of the kind could have happened to the whole human race through one man, in whom all were; and yet you do not deny that, under the care of the most omnipotent and most just God, infants pay so many and so great penalties; since they shut your mouths and strike your eyes, if you should deny it. Do you not, then, attend whom you make unjust, beholding most evident penalties and denying any evil merits of little ones? It seemed to you false and profane, what I said: *The work of a work of God passes through the work of God*. Because, namely, the angel is a work of God. Sin, then, which is the work of the angel, is the work of a work of God, not of God himself. And about these words you charge me as though I had said: God too sins, because the human being whom God made sinned; which I did not say. For the work of God sinned, that is, the angel, or the human being; but they sinned by their own work, not by the work of God: for they themselves are the good work of God; but their sin is their own evil work, not God's. But which is worse: to say, God too sins, because the human being whom God made sinned (which I did not say); or so to deny original sin that the unjust penalty of a little one is nothing other than the sin of God? But if this cannot fall upon God, then the penalty of the little one is just; and if it is just, it is for sin. No one, therefore, can proclaim God just amid the so many and so great penalties of little ones, while denying original sin. It would be, he says, a testimony of iniquity to create the evil, if he himself had created the evil by which they are evil; but now, since human beings are evil, and he creates this, which is that they are human beings, but it follows them from their vitiated nature that they are evil, through sin; surely even when he creates the evil, he himself is good, in that which he creates; since they are evil by a fault which is no nature, but he himself creates a nature which is no fault, even though it is vitiated. But to bestow the good of creation on a vitiated and justly condemned stock is like bestowing on an evil human being also the good of life and health, on account of which he is a human being, not on account of which he is evil. But you say it is *wicked to make little ones evil, so that those who cannot will may yet be compelled to be unjust*. To be compelled to anything, those can in no way who are not. But if they already are—not indeed in the property of their own person and condition, but in the most hidden principle of the seed—as Levi was in the loins of Abraham[^129]; there, by the fault of nature coming from the sin of the first man, they are already evil; not by the creation of God are they who cannot even will compelled to be evil. See indeed the marvels of the grace of Christ, of whom you are the wretched enemies. Behold, little ones, who can neither will nor not will good or evil, yet, when, struggling and crying out with tears, they are reborn in holy Baptism, are compelled to be holy and just. For without doubt, if they die before the use of reason, they will be holy and just in the kingdom of God, by that grace to which they came not by their own possibility, but by necessity, leading their holy and just lives without end, treading down and breaking your rules about the possible and the necessary. But without doubt it is more to be unwilling to do evil than neither to be unwilling nor to will; and yet he was unwilling and did it, who said: *The evil I do not will, that I do*. I am not insane, then, nor do I say: God creates evil. For he creates good, who even from a vitiated nature creates not a fault, but a nature. But that nature draws the fault, not by God's making, but by his judgment. But attend whether you are not insane, if you are not greatly insane, you who say that God does not the penal evil which is just, but the evil which is called iniquity; for what else does he do, if on guiltless little ones, with no evil, he either inflicts so great evils, or permits them to be inflicted? But you are not to be addressed and refuted by us; rather you ought, if it were possible, to be exsufflated and exorcized by the whole Church, by which you say infants are exsufflated and exorcized in vain.
