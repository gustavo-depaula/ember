# Book Four

**IV. 1.** *Julian.* I think that an attentive reader may wonder why, although I have divided my little work into a definite number of books, in accordance with the customary practice of writers, I nonetheless resolve the questions of one book in another; whence it is that the meaning of the Apostle, who said that through one man sin entered into the world[^1] (by which number he crushed the opinion of original evil), which I disputed in the third volume—and that the generation of the Jews is reported to have descended from the one Abraham[^2]—contributed nothing, even slight, to that shaken position. But let that reader, whom I now set out to satisfy, suppose that this ought to have been placed in the second volume. Let him know, then, that this fidelity of response, by which even those things which are thought to have been omitted are supplied in a later book, neither hampers the contest in any way, nor arises from any disturbance of haste, but as much from the necessity of the causes as from the reasoning of design. There is, indeed, an abundance of great talents whose examples would vindicate such consistency of style; but it is an inept boastfulness, for the defense of a simple business, to summon the resounding endorsements of ancient names, and to render the security of a deed suspect by a defense. The second book, then, which I had destined for asserting the opinions of the Apostle, I terminated, by a necessary disputation, lest the addition of light questions should draw it out to too great a length. But because, the duty of a serious treatise having been fulfilled, it pleased me to meet even inept questions, and to have regard for any of the slowest who can be moved even by frigid objections, I showed in the third book in what manner many are said to have been born from one. Yet it is also most fitting, if the following volume pays the debt of the prior, that there may both seem to be a great concord among the books, and the readers be invited to the knowledge of the whole work; since, even if at first they have judged some things deferred, they understand that they have not been passed over. Having shown, then, that what we have done agrees with the faith and diligence of the work, let us join battle with the Manicheans on behalf of the works and laws of God—repelling from the former the deformity of natural evil, from the latter the iniquity of cruel judgments—teaching that in the precepts of the latter justice has lost nothing of its good, that in the seeds of the former the devil has mingled nothing of his evil; that these things, finally, are worthy of God the ruler, those most worthy of God the founder.

*Augustine.* The Manicheans would take your joining of battle as a great help, lest they be overcome in our joining of battle, were not the Catholic faith overcoming you too together with them; since, when you begin from the weepings and calamities of little ones, you are not permitted to attribute the discord of the concupiscences of flesh and spirit, and the miseries of the human race, of which the nature of mortals is full, to the mingling of the two natures which they introduce—because all these things are attributed, by divine authority and most true reason, to a nature corrupted by sin, which God founded good and, though corrupted, does not desert with the gift of fruitfulness and his good handiwork. And in denying this, you indeed try to break the arms by which they are conquered; but they are so firm and unconquered that, whether, fixed in them and pierced through, they reach you, or, fixed in them, they reach the Manicheans, they lay both of you low.

**IV. 2.** *Julian.* If you believe these two things—namely, that the works of God are not evil, nor his judgments unjust—the whole teaching of traduction is crushed; just as, on the other side, when the impiety of traduction is taken up, these two, the founding by God and the judging, through which alone God can be understood, are destroyed.

*Augustine.* The works of God are not evil, when even out of any evils whatever he works good, and to the little ones—what he has well worked out of the mass lost through the transgression of the first man—he kindly brings help, even to those to be healed; nor are his judgments unjust, when by a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day of their going forth from their mother's womb[^3] he avenges nothing but the deserts of sins. When these things are believed and understood, both the error of the Manicheans and that of the Pelagians is extinguished: that of the Manicheans, namely, because they want these evils of the human race to be attributed to some I-know-not-what principle of evil coeternal with the eternity of God; but that of the Pelagians, because they do not wish to impute them to sin.

**IV. 3.** *Julian.* Now, then, let us weigh those things which the lacerator of natural things has woven together; but, so that for our reader both understanding and discernment may be supplied of matters that are entangled by mutual response, I admonish him by what manner of thing he struggles. He professes to oppose our writings, which he says were sent to him on a short paper, and he sets down some particles of my sentences and pricks them—things which are not held in my work.

*Augustine.* Thanks be to God that to those very four books of yours, from which that man, whose paper reached me, excerpted what he wished, as he wished, I have answered all things in my six books. I think that you are not going to say there that I wished to refute some things which are not held in your work; and even if you do say it, and prove it, I ought even to be grateful that you did not say things you ought not to have said; and would that, whatever words of yours are most rightly censured, you had said none of them at all.

**IV. 4.** *Julian.* He speaks, then, to Valerius: *Attend to the things by which he supposes himself to agree with us against this title set before him: God*—he says—*who had fashioned Adam from the mud, built Eve from a rib, and said: "She shall be called Life, because she is the mother of all the living"*[^4]. *It is not indeed so written, but what is that to us? The memory is wont to fail in words, provided the meaning is held. Nor did God impose the name Life upon Eve so that she should be called Life, but her husband did. For thus it speaks: "And Adam called the name of his wife Life, because she is the mother of all the living"*[^5]. Stupendous erudition of the teacher, who does not allow even a little departure from the wording of the Scriptures! He charges us with unskillfulness, but deigns to pardon the forgetfulness by which it happened that I said the woman was named Life by God; and so, by a brief occasion, he was eager to appear both learned and kindly. But just as the diligence is not to be admired in having found out the namer of the woman, so the shamelessness is not to be borne—to want to pardon what you could not blame. For it is not so read in my work as is held in this man's fabrications. For I, having set down the testimony of the law, by which the Creator of things is shown to have said: "*It is not good for man to be alone; let us make for him a helper like himself"*[^6]. *What is this*—I say—*"It is not good for man to be alone"? Had God made anything that could not rightly be called good*—*especially he who had made all things not only* *good but even very good? How, then, does he say: "It is not good for man to be alone"? By which words the status of the creature is not censured, but it is shown that the union could have been ruinous to the human race, unless succession had budded forth by the ministry of a different sex. For although Adam too is hoped to have been able to become immortal, had he not transgressed*, *yet it is manifest that he could never have become a father, unless he had found a wife*, *who, taken from the side of the sleeping man, heard at the first address for what work she had been prepared: "She shall be called Life, because she is the mother of all the living"*. *By which words it was shown that thereafter no one of human beings could either be or live who had not subsisted by the benefit of conception*. Since, then, it is clear that in a matter indeed of no great weight, nothing was nonetheless brought forth by me in such a way that anyone, except by a singular shamelessness, could accuse it, the mark of public falsehood has been branded upon the most destitute of all men, who, when he wishes to give what he does not hold, at one and the same time offends both by poverty of right and by ambition of largess.

*Augustine.* If you did not set down the words of the divine Book as I found them in that paper, I have pardoned not you, but the one who so wrote; whom we both ought to pardon. But if you suppose that I did not find this in the paper, but, as though I had found it, set down those words mendaciously, so that there might be something I could ascribe as though to your error, now certainly I pardon you for having thought so badly and falsely of me.

**IV. 5.** *Julian.* After he has censured this with such gravity, he proceeds to the rest and warns his patron to attend to what follows. *God, then*—he says—*the creator of male and female, formed members suited to generation*. But inserting only this much of the head of my words, with very many lines passed over entirely—by which is especially driven home the newness of souls owing nothing to flesh or seed—he subjoins that word of mine: *So he ordained that bodies be begotten from bodies; in whose efficiency, however, the power of his operation intervenes, administering all that is by that power by which he founded it. If, then, there is no offspring except through sex, no sex except through body, no body except through God, who can doubt that fruitfulness is rightly reckoned to God?* After he had thus set down these things from mine, he commends that he too confesses them to be said in a Catholic sense. Who, then, would not think the man had changed? But he does not become unmindful of himself; rather, he judges the condition of a depraved teaching and of shame to be alike, so that even the latter is restored to a manifest ruin, just as here it hardens by constant attrition. He approves, then, my opinion, and fixes the iron of prejudgment into his own faith with a spontaneous embrace; but after this, as though whole in strength, he reckons that there remains something to fight with. For thus it follows: *After these things which were said truly and in a Catholic sense—nay, truly written in the divine Books, yet not said by this man in a Catholic sense, because they were not said with the intention of a Catholic heart—now, on account of what he said them, the Pelagian and Caelestian heresy begins to be introduced. For attend to what follows*. Here again he subjoins our words: *What, then, does the devil recognize as his own in the sexes, by which he may rightly possess their fruit? Diversity? But this is in bodies, which God made. Mingling? But it is vindicated no less by the privilege of blessing than of establishment. For it is the voice of God: "A man shall leave father and mother and cling to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh"*[^7]; *it is the voice of God: "Increase and multiply and fill the earth"*[^8]. *Or perhaps fruitfulness itself? But this is the very cause of the institution of marriage*. To which he answered that the devil recognizes neither the diversity of the sexes, nor the mingling, nor the fruitfulness in the sexes, by which he might rightly possess their fruit; but, all these being absolved, he found what to assign to the devil, and assailed our honorableness under the name of fear—us who, among so many gifts of bodies and sexes, were afraid to name the concupiscence of the flesh. Thus, then, he speaks against me to his patron: *But this man, in all these, was unwilling to name the concupiscence of the flesh, which is not from the Father, but is from the world*[^9]; *of which world the devil is called the prince; who did not find it in the Lord, because the Lord, as man, did not come to men through it. Hence he himself says: "Behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he will find nothing"*[^10]; *nothing, surely, of sin—neither what is drawn by one who is born, nor what is added by one who lives. This man was unwilling to name it, among all these natural goods which he recounted—it, on account of which even marriages are confounded, which glory in all these. For why is that work of the married withdrawn and hidden even from the eyes of their children, except that they cannot be in the praiseworthy mingling without shameful disordered desire? At this even those first blushed who first covered the shameful parts*; *which were not shameful before, but were to be proclaimed and gloried in as the works of God*. I treated indeed, most blessed father Florus, in the four little books of my first work, the cause of marriage, the cause of mingling, the cause of bodies, the cause of the sexes, the cause of the work of God, the cause, finally, of the esteem of God—which necessarily, just as it is praised by the goodness of his works, so is wounded by their disgrace; and through the whole work of my writings, an utterly unobscurable truth approved that the devil added nothing either to the formation of the body or to its sensation; and therefore it was thoroughly taught that the opinion of traduction was drawn from the mud of the Manichean.

*Augustine.* How you acted in those four books of yours, and how you were confuted by our response, so that it became clear that you accomplished nothing, those who read both mine and yours understand without difficulty. And although even when yours are not read, but only mine, it appears in them that your heretical teaching is destroyed; yet when, to my one book, you had so answered those four of yours that you scarcely touched a third part of it, and refuted none of it, you presumed so much from them that against my one book, against another of mine in the same cause, you composed these eight of yours—as though you supposed that I must be terrified not by the firm foundation of disputations, but by the number of volumes; for so far did you perceive that you accomplished nothing by that fourfold response, that this eightfold one seemed necessary to you. If, then, your loquacity advances by such increments, whom would the number—not the truth—of your books not terrify, which it wearies me to count? For you, a man eloquent with stupendous abundance, who first thought four of yours should be rendered to my one, and eight to another of mine—who would not fear that perhaps you are meditating to render more than a thousand of yours to my six books, if to the one which is first you now render sixteen, and so pursue each of the following ones with the preceding number doubled, showing us how much you talk by not understanding what you say?

**IV. 6.** *Julian.* Yet, since it is established that this was most fully accomplished there, it shall be repeated, though briefly, in the present disputation too. Let us turn, then, to the man himself, with whose erudition we contend. You grant, surely, that it was rightly gathered by us that if offspring exists only through sex, if sex only through body, if body only through God, it could not be doubted that fruitfulness pertains to the works of God, and that the prince of darkness recognizes nothing of his own in the sexes, by which he might retain their fruit by the assertion of his own right; since both diversity pertained to sex, and mingling to the diversity of members, and the fruit of fruitfulness to God, who was also the institutor of those who generate; and through this nothing remained on account of which the fertility of mingling should be adjudged to the devil. As for what you think is conferred upon you, whether as solace or as defense, by the honorableness of my discourse—because I was unwilling in this place to name the concupiscence of the flesh? Even if I had passed it over in silence throughout the whole work, that matter would suffer no censure or injury, which was vindicated by more honorable names and indeed by the clearest expressions of understanding. Grant, then, that I wished, more prudently than the reasoning of the business I had undertaken demanded, to cover with silence the name of that thing which we cover with a garment; is the loss of all reasoning, of all truth, therefore to be made? Will the intellect, the arbiter of affairs, therefore lie prostrate, because there is something which, just as it can be brought before the eyes, so cannot always be decently brought to the ears? Yet what deformity is there in naming the concupiscence of the flesh, which I too have named when the place required it, and which you, although you affirm that you perceive it to be untamable, nonetheless frequently mention?

*Augustine.* I call the flesh tamable, that is, but by those who war against it, not by those who praise it.

**IV. 7.** *Julian.* Then, a little after this: *Through what, then, are little ones under the devil, who are born of bodies that God made, of the mingling of a sex which he distinguished by forming, but joined by blessing, and multiplied by implanting fruitfulness—and the little ones themselves God made from the matter of the seed? If you confess that the matter of bodies exists through God, the genitals of bodies through God, the mingling of the genitals through God, the very force of the seeds through God, and through God too the form and life of all that is born; what do you think remains, on account of which you transcribe so many works of God to the devil?* Therefore you call the body of another disputation a heresy, all of whose members you confess to be upright and Catholic.

*Augustine.* The Novatians, the Arians, the Eunomians, and several others—do we not call them heretics even when they have confessed the whole Creed? So then, to be silent about other things pertaining to your heresy, how do you not want us to call you heretics, who deny that little ones are rescued from the power of darkness when they are transferred to Christ—little ones in whom, as they are about to receive the Holy Spirit, the whole Church from of old, which praises the name of the Lord from the rising of the sun to its setting, exsufflates and exorcizes the unclean spirit?

**IV. 8.** *Julian.* For after you praised our gathering—which is fortified by so great a truth that it was carried wholly against you, nor could you violate it even with an insult—with God compelling you, you nonetheless accuse its effect.

*Augustine.* What you call its effect, you do not understand to be your defect. For from these true things which you say, as though Catholics, that thing is not effected by which you are heretics. For as Catholics you say that offspring is through sex, sex through body, body through God, whence fruitfulness is rightly reckoned to God. But is it therefore not the case that there is born thence such a man as is not without the filth of sin, even if his life upon earth be of one day[^11]? Not, therefore, by saying those true things, but by denying this, which is likewise true, are you heretics. For surely God is not the author of foolishness, and yet he is the author of offspring, even when a foolish one is born. Understand thus, if you can, that there is born in man, from his origin, a defect of which God is not the author, although the creator of the man himself is none but God. Remember, indeed, that you have deviated from your own teaching, by which you deny that God works the will in the minds of men. For I praised your words, which I confess are said by Catholics too, by my will—words which, however, you say I praised with God compelling me. See how God works in us even to will[^12]—which you are accustomed even against the Apostle to deny.

**IV. 9.** *Julian.* *If, then*—I say—*there is offspring only through sex, sex only through body, body only through God; who can doubt that fruitfulness is rightly reckoned to God? This is so certain that it has shaken even your mouth to its praise*. But after the encomium rendered by you to this passage, you warn that a heresy is being introduced, when the things I subjoined doubled nothing other than what had been set before, with an explanation applied.

*Augustine.* Surely, in those things which I conceded you said truly, had you said: *What, then, does the devil recognize as his own in the sexes?* For here you began to introduce the meaning of your heresy; as though the devil could not recognize something of his own in the sexes for this reason, that body and sex are through God the creator—when the devil recognizes both the good of God and the evil of the devil even in himself: the former, namely, in his nature, the latter in his defect. So too in the sexes he recognizes the things that are God's, just as the very sex, and body, and spirit; but he recognizes also his own, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit; for those things are from the Creator, whose vengeance he could not avoid; this is the wound which he inflicted.

**IV. 10.** *Julian.* For I say: *What, then, does the devil recognize as his own in the sexes, so that he may, as you say, rightly possess their fruit? Diversity? But this is in bodies, which God made. Mingling? But it is vindicated no less by the privilege of blessing than of establishment. Or perhaps fruitfulness itself? But this is the very cause of the institution of marriage*. What, then, was new here? What, dissenting from the earlier conclusion which you had approved, that you should think it must be censured after praise? Surely nothing; and through this, what is gathered from so foul an inconstancy? Since indeed the disputation has not been renewed by me, but the estimation of my words has been changed by you—that your intention is most foul, and your reasoning feeble.

*Augustine.* You wonder that after the praise of the works of God I censured your insidious questioning; for, questioning, you said: *What, then, does the devil recognize as his own in the sexes?* And, wishing to persuade us that he recognizes nothing of his own in the sexes, you mentioned those things which do not truly pertain to the devil—namely, diversity, by which the feminine sex is diverse from the masculine; and mingling, by which, that children may be generated, each sex is mingled; and fruitfulness, by which the children themselves are generated. All these things we confess would have been in paradise, even if no one had sinned; but that thing would not have been there which they felt when they covered the shameful parts—who, before they sinned, were naked, and were not confounded[^13]. This concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit[^14], without which no man is born, and this discord of flesh and spirit, which the Catholic Teacher learned—so excellently praised by the mouth of your Pelagius—turned into our nature through the transgression of the first man; this, then, why were you silent about? You who, asking what the devil recognizes as his own in the sexes, named other things which are not the devil's, and were unwilling to render to your question this which is his. This deceitful question of yours I censured, not the divine works, which I rightly praised.

**IV. 11.** *Julian.* And that what you praise as Catholic belongs to your fear, not to your faith.

*Augustine.* He recognizes our faith in the Catholic praise who, through it, displays and storms your error.

**IV. 12.** *Julian.* That you therefore censure what you praised belongs not to judgment, but to fury.

*Augustine.* I do not censure what I praised; rather, I praised the things you said truly; but I censured the fact that you questioned insidiously, seeing what should be answered you, and so passing over this in silence as though it were not. Whether this belongs to judgment or to fury, you will see, if you grow calm from your fury.

**IV. 13.** *Julian.* Of which fury you will indeed never be free, unless you first reject the obscene teaching; because, suffering grievous Symplegades between the shame of penance—which yet was never shunned by Christians—and the poverty of argumentation, it is necessary that the good words by which you are overwhelmed you either follow or tear to pieces.

*Augustine.* They are not good words, Julian, to deny that Christ is Jesus for the little ones, or to confess that Jesus, that is, the Savior, is for the little ones in the same way as for the whole mortal creation, whence it is said: *You will save, O Lord, both men and beasts*[^15]. He did not, in coming in the likeness of the flesh of sin, receive such a name on account of that; but the angel said: *You shall call his name Jesus; for he will save his people from their sins*[^16]. They are not good words, to separate the little ones from this people, and so to say that Christ is Jesus even for them because he saves them—not from their sins, but from their little scabs. Recover your senses, I beg you; I think that you were not born of parents who believed these things; and certainly you were reborn in a Church that does not believe them.

**IV. 14.** *Julian.* Let us see, however, what of my sentences you have seized, to assail under the name of error. *But this man*—you say—*was unwilling in all these to name the concupiscence of the flesh, which is not from the Father, but is from the world; of which world the devil is called the prince; who did not find it in the Lord, because the Lord, as man, did not come to men through it*[^17]. You said, surely, that I introduce a heresy; to prove which you subjoin no words of mine.

*Augustine.* I subjoin those words of yours by which you asked *what the devil recognizes as his own in the sexes*. For deceitfully you said these things, when you saw the concupiscence of the flesh—by whose movements even chaste marriages must often resist—and were silent, and pressed me with a most fraudulent question, as though there were nothing that could be answered. Or, if you did not see it, surely by that very blindness you were introducing your heresy.

**IV. 15.** *Julian.* But you say I was unwilling to name the concupiscence of the flesh. I who was unwilling to name it, kept silent; and if I kept silent, I spoke nothing you could censure: who, then, persuades to error by being silent? O new and hitherto unheard-of portents of crimes! He says that a perverse teaching is composed by my silence!

*Augustine.* Not by silence, but by a deceitful question, as I showed above. Although silence too is deservedly censured, when what ought to have been said is not said precisely lest it be seen that the question could be answered.

**IV. 16.** *Julian.* Let all prudence weigh this, and laugh at the odium of a speech shown under the name of taciturnity. You confess, then, that I said nothing you could accuse.

*Augustine.* Nay rather, what you said in questioning is most rightly accused. For, that it might be thought there was nothing that could be answered you, you were unwilling to say that which could be said. Or at least why should I not accuse the very blindness by which you could not even see it?

**IV. 17.** *Julian.* You, indeed, by harassing our silence, have brought forth what once could not be defended; but now, after the letter of the Manichean, whose sentences I set down in the third book, has come to the public, it can no longer be concealed.

*Augustine.* Neither does the Manichean truly censure the concupiscence of the flesh, nor do you truly praise it: he, because he does not know whence this evil is; you, because you deny it is evil; he, because he attributes it to an alien nature mingled into us; you, because you do not want it to be of our corrupted nature; he, because he believes that through it a part of God is corrupted; you, because through it you try to dishonor even the happiness of paradise.

**IV. 18.** *Julian.* *He was unwilling*—he says—*to name the concupiscence of the flesh, which is not from the Father, but is from the world; of which world the devil is called the prince; who did not find it in the Lord, because the Lord, as man, did not come to men through it*. By the very words, then, of the Manichean, you profess that the concupiscence of the flesh was not made by God, but founded by the world, of which world you nonetheless call the devil the prince.

*Augustine.* Do I say that the devil is the prince of the world, and does the Lord himself not say it? Would I have said this, had I not read that he himself said it? And since you too have read it, why did you think it must be objected to me? Yet the devil is not for that reason the prince of heaven and earth, and of every celestial and terrestrial creature, according to what is said, *The world was made through him*[^18]; but because by the name of the world is called the circle of the earth in the human beings of which it is full, according to what is said, *And the world did not know him*[^19]; according to what is said, the devil is *the prince of this world*[^20]; according to what is said, *The world is set in the evil one*[^21]; according to what is said, *But because you are not of the world, therefore the world hates you*[^22]; and innumerable such things. And through this, with the holy Scriptures teaching, we take the world, according to the difference of the statements, now in a good sense, now in an evil. To its goods pertain heaven and earth and every creature of God in them; to its evils pertain *the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the ambition of this age*[^23]—or, as you yourself set it down, *the pride of life*. As, then, the world is read now in a good, now in an evil sense, so read, if you can, the concupiscence of the flesh, or the concupiscence of the eyes, somewhere placed in a good sense; but you will not find it so, just as you will not find the pride of life, which is added as a third to those two evils.

**IV. 19.** *Julian.* But I said that you used the words of the Manichean altogether, because, although some of these words are held in the Letter of the apostle John, yet since it is certain that that Master of the Church did not there think anything of the flesh, or of the sensation of the flesh, or of nuptial concupiscence, such as the Manichean composes by the usurpation of his words, I said not unjustly that the words were his whose meaning they served, so that words are deservedly reckoned to the intention of him who uses them; and just as in holy John they are to be revered with apostolic dignity, because they instill the truth, so in the Manichean they seem the obscured tokens of those words, not their genuine expression.

*Augustine.* Why do you add *nuptial*, and say *of nuptial concupiscence*, so as to clothe this shameful thing you have taken up with the honorable name of marriage? The apostle John said the concupiscence of the flesh, not nuptial concupiscence—a concupiscence which could be in paradise, even if no one had sinned, in the appetite of fruitfulness, not in the itch of pleasure; or at least always subject to the spirit, so that it would be moved only with the spirit willing; never having desires opposed to the spirit, so that the spirit too would be compelled to have desires opposed to it. For far be it that in a place of such great happiness, and in human beings there so happy in such great peace, there should be any discord of flesh and spirit.

**IV. 20.** *Julian.* The apostle John, indeed, when he summoned the faithful to the summit of all the virtues, and extended the zeal of holiness to the height of the Lord's manner of life, comprising in brief, under the name of the whole world, all the incitements of present goods and delights: *Do not*—he says—*love the world, nor the things that are in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him; for all that is in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the pride of life; which is not from the Father, but is from the world; and the world passes away, and its concupiscence; but he who does the will of God remains forever*[^24]. As far as the surface of the words looks, he proclaims hatred for all the elements, and declares that the world and all that is in the world are not from the Father and ought not to be loved.

*Augustine.* You say these things in vain; no Catholic, not even an unlearned one, takes the world in this passage so as to think anything of the elements. For neither, where he says of the Lord Christ, *He himself is the propitiation of our sins; not of ours only, but of the whole world*[^25], does anyone so lose his senses as to think that here too the sins even of the elements are to be understood. This whole, then, which is in this way called the world, is understood only in the human beings who are throughout the whole world, that is, throughout the whole circle of the earth, in whatever direction it is inhabited. Finally, that human life by which one lives not according to God but according to man, the Apostle in this place called the world. Therefore he forbids it to be loved, and says: *All that is in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the ambition of this age*, or *the pride of life; which is not from the Father, but is from the world*[^26]. You, if you can, show the concupiscence of the flesh somewhere named in a good sense, in any place of the holy Scriptures; and do not pour clouds of loquacity over open matters.

**IV. 21.** *Julian.* Surely he is an Apostle, and one specially loved by the Lord Jesus with love; yet, unless his intention—either the Gospel which he wrote, or the gravity of his Epistles—indicated it, these words which we have set down could so bring no prejudice to things that they would be compelled to yield to all the Scriptures, which assert that the world was made by God. But he himself fortified himself by the reverend opening of the Gospel which he composed. *God*—he says—*was the Word; through him all things were made, and without him nothing was made*[^27]. And thereafter: *It was the true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world; he was in this world, and the world was made through him*[^28]. And likewise: *The Word was made flesh, that he might dwell among us*[^29]. By which testimonies he left no obscurity in his meanings; but he showed that he knew and asserted God to be the founder of the whole world, and of all things that are in the world, and most thoroughly taught that no access lay open to the Manicheans for their opinions. Since whoever asserts God to be the creator of all natures uses the names of substances, without loss of faith, for the signification of immoderate desire.

*Augustine.* But you do not want the concupiscence of the flesh to be understood as evil even when it is immoderate, but as a good, and rather that those use a good badly who use it immoderately. And through this, if the concupiscence of the flesh is a good, then surely the moderate is a moderate good, and the immoderate an immoderate good; but to use it moderately is, as you say, to use a good well; and to use it immoderately is, as you say, to use a good badly. Just as wine is without doubt good—for *every creature of God is good*[^30]—and he who uses a little wine uses a good well, but he who uses immoderate wine uses a good badly. But John would never say that wine is not from the Father, as he said that the concupiscence of the flesh is not from the Father. You do not, then, find what concupiscence of the flesh is not from the Father, since even the immoderate is with you a good, and rather he is not good who uses an immoderate good, that is, who uses a good badly. Why, then, do you hesitate to say briefly and openly what you say obscurely by long windings—that what John said is false, and that you say what is true? For it is false, what he says: *The concupiscence of the flesh is not from the Father*—from whom are all natural goods—if the concupiscence of the flesh, as you say, is good even when one uses it immoderately, and only he is evil who uses a good badly.

**IV. 22.** *Julian.* He commands, then, that the world not be loved, nor the things that are in the world; and he says that the love of God cannot be in the lovers of this age—but not in order to make another than the true God to be understood as its founder by this name of love to be withdrawn from the age; rather, that the faithful may recognize that no desires of the present life are to be preferred to the virtues, lest their mind, occupied either in acquiring riches or in seeking out pleasures, be led away from the vigor of the Christian—which is the true—philosophy. *Since all that is in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the pride of life; which is not from the Father, but is from the world; and the world passes away, and its concupiscence; but he who does the will of God remains forever*[^31]. By the name of the world, then, he indicated the morals of human beings who claim that there is nothing after this life, and the various pomps and luxuries of mortals.

*Augustine.* Therefore, if *by the name of the world he indicated the morals of human beings who claim that there is nothing after this life, and the various pomps and luxuries of mortals*; among these are those things which he said are in the world, and are not from the Father; in which the first place is held by that beautiful thing you have taken up—namely, the concupiscence of the flesh. But you, it seems, wished, in the morals of human beings who suppose there is nothing after this life, the concupiscence of the eyes to be understood, since by clinging to those things which they see, they refuse to believe the things they do not see; but in the pomps of mortals, the ambition of this age or the pride of life; and in the luxuries, the concupiscence of the flesh—so that you may seem to have embraced all three things which John set down; as though there could be luxury, which you censure, unless consent be given to the concupiscence of the flesh, which you do not think should be censured, and which, by calling it a good, you even praise besides. But what is more insane than to call luxury an evil, and the concupiscence of luxury a good? And to think that the Apostle of Christ, under the name of the concupiscence of the flesh, accused not the concupiscence of the flesh itself, but rather luxury, which is altogether nothing unless one is enticed, dragged, possessed by the concupiscence of the flesh? As though so great a teacher did not find by what to accuse, not it, but the luxurious man by its name; since the luxurious man would not be accused, unless he obeyed its desires. Stop saying much and being wise about little. You will never effect, by however great a river of your loquacity, into whose depths you are swept; you will absolutely never effect that luxury be an evil, and that to desire the things that pertain to luxury be not an evil, even if no consent be given to such concupiscence, lest an evil be perpetrated.

**IV. 23.** *Julian.* For so too in his Gospel he had said: *The world was made through him, and the world did not know him*[^32]; not, surely, so that the elements, lacking reason, could be seen to be able to know or deny Christ, but so that by the name of the world the throng of unbelievers might be signified. In the same manner, then, in the present passage too, all that is in the world—that is, all kinds of human beings so clinging to pleasures that they measure all the goods of a rational living being either by the trappings of powers or by the burdens of riches by their senses—he says swelled up with hateful pride, which is not from God, that is, does not please God, but is from the world, that is, conceived by the depravity of the human will. And therefore, he says, it ought not corrupt you by emulation of it; because he who does the will of God becomes a partaker of eternal blessedness, and does not fly past with the fragility of present things. The apostle John, then, commanded the world to be held in hatred, just as the Lord too in the Gospel showed that not only the body, but even our very soul must be held in hatred: *Whoever*—he says—*does not hate his father, or brother, and besides even his own soul, is not worthy of me*[^33]. Although surely the mind of the faithful cannot hate itself, since by a most prudent love it purchases its happiness even with sorrows and dangers. What, then, did we accomplish? Namely, that the apostle John, by the word of the world, in the manner of the Scriptures, struck not the nature of things, but the defects of wills; and so denied that the concupiscence of the flesh is from God, just as all the things that are in the world. Which the Manichean, seizing upon as though consequent, not according to the Apostle's meaning but according to his own perfidy, affirms that not the concupiscence of the flesh, nor the flesh itself, nor finally the whole world, was effected by God; and you, following him, believe that the concupiscence of the flesh was founded not by God, but by the devil.

*Augustine.* This concupiscence of the flesh, then, I say is evil, which John says is not from the Father, and which Ambrose says turned into our nature from the transgression of the first man; whence John, wishing human beings to be understood, says it is from the world. This concupiscence of the flesh the Manichean too calls evil; but he does not know whence it is; you, however, call it good, because you too do not know whence it is; and by denying it to be whence Ambrose says it is, you bring it about that the Manichean seems to himself to attribute it rightly to a nature of evil, which he raves to be coeternal with God. Therefore, that both you and the Manichean may be refuted, that which the apostle John says, the bishop Ambrose expounds. For what turned into nature through the transgression of the first man is surely not an evil coeternal with God; let the Manichean, then, be silent: and yet it is an evil; let Julian too, then, be silent.

**IV. 24.** *Julian.* It is established, then, that blessed John gave you no occasion of error; but that you imbibed what the Manichean brought forth. And, the Apostle's estimation now defended, let us lay hands, even if briefly, upon this point: what do you suppose holy John intimates here, when he pronounces the concupiscence of the flesh and the concupiscence of the eyes not to be from God? The very genus of concupiscence, which falls into vice not by the moderation that is granted, but by the excess that is forbidden—or only that excess, which is found to be not natural but voluntary?

*Augustine.* If it falls into vice, then at least concede that it is evil; but why do you then too contend that it is good, and say that he uses a good badly who exceeds its granted measure? For thus it is not the concupiscence itself, but he who uses it badly that falls into vice. Do you see how you do not know what you are saying, when you do not even stand by your own definition?

**IV. 25.** *Julian.* If you say that by the name of concupiscence is meant even that very measure by which one is affected by the lawful pleasure of natural things, so that the concupiscence of the flesh seems reproved in its entirety; then profess too that the sense of the eyes, and the world itself, and whatever is in the world, was founded by the devil, since all are alike said not to be from God. If indeed you grant this, you will not be made a Manichean—for you are one even now—but you will show that you are one not only by your arguments, as before, but also by profession. But if, dreading your naked error, you say that the Apostle, by the name of the concupiscence of the flesh and of the concupiscence of the eyes, and by the name of the world, noted not the things themselves, which, when they are held within the granted bounds, are harmless, but, when they run out into unlawful things, become reprehensible; it will be clear—what we treated in the first work—that of natural concupiscence not the genus, not the species, not the measure, but only the excess comes into fault. And therefore for the rest beware modestly of this testimony of the Apostle, lest, if you pollute it even by a light mention, you, defended in nothing, be approved as openly betrayed.

*Augustine.* But, contentious man, the measure of concupiscence which you say is granted is not preserved when consent is given to it as it impels, or yielded to, and one goes into its excess; lest one go into it, the evil is resisted. For who would doubt that it is evil, which, if you obey it, you do evil; if you fight against it, good? Whoever, then, wishes to live temperately, let him not consent to the evil which you praise; and whoever wishes to live faithfully, let him not consent to you when you praise the evil; accordingly, that he may beware of you, let him know that the concupiscence of the flesh is evil; but that he may beware of both you and the Manicheans, let him know whence it is.

**IV. 26.** *Julian.* I treated this place in the second little book of my earlier work, and I showed that the concupiscence of the flesh, which excites the appearance of taste and of the eyes, was earlier in man than the fault.

*Augustine.* To this second book of yours I answered sufficiently in my fourth. For just as there, so here too you chatter empty things. For in no way did you show that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, began to be in man earlier than the fault. Whatever that thing was that those two human beings, after the sin, felt as new in their members—whence their shame covered the shameful parts—without doubt the fault contracted it.

**IV. 27.** *Julian.* Here, however, I necessarily require by what dreams it was revealed to you, that by the name of concupiscence you should think the disordered desire of those coming together is meant. For, with the resources of truth preserved—which a long disputation has laid open—let us suppose that it has not yet become clear that the sense of the flesh pertains to the same author to whom its form belongs.

*Augustine.* The senses of the flesh are one thing, by which the flesh, in a certain way, announces present corporeal things to the spirit; and the movements of carnal concupiscence are another, by which the flesh, having desires opposed to the spirit, hurls into all unlawful and dishonorable things, unless the spirit too has desires opposed to it; which discord of flesh and spirit is attributed, not to the creator of flesh or of sense, but to the evil persuader and to the man who transgressed, by those whose sound faith condemns the insane error of the Pelagians and the Manicheans.

**IV. 28.** *Julian.* But if this is feigned to be doubtful, at once a most dense cloud of doubt will envelop you. For by this concupiscence of the flesh no indication of the genitals at all is held. I shall say, then, that the ears are beaten by this word, thirsting for various songs; I shall say that the palate of gluttons is chastised by holy John; I shall say that the nostrils dying upon odors are blamed; in short, everything except what you suppose: the choice is free, where there is no pressure of a special word. Either, then, deny that the things we have mentioned are desired, and contradict, as you are accustomed, the conscience of all; or, if shamelessness has not yet grown so callous as to try to resist these things, assent that the condemnation of genital pleasure is found in no discourses.

*Augustine.* You say this as though we said that the concupiscence of the flesh seethes only in the pleasure of the genitals. Absolutely, in whatever sense of the body the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, it is recognized; and since, if the spirit does not more strongly have desires opposed to it, it drags to evils, it is convicted of being evil. On account of it Scripture says: *What has been created more wicked than the eye?*[^34] And surely God, the creator of all bodies and senses, created the eye, not wickedness. Behold whence you may understand (if you do not resist the truth) that there is evil in our nature, even when it is created, although it is created good, well, by the good. But whence this evil is, learn from Ambrose, lest you furnish support to the Manichean for introducing another nature, that is, of evil, coeternal with God.

**IV. 29.** *Julian.* What, then, can it profit you, that I, amid the mingling of the sexes, the fruitfulness of those coming together, was unwilling to tear it to pieces under the name of concupiscence, since you could not, with the Manichean, approve it as diabolical; and since now, by the very consequence of the foregoing disputation, it had become clear that the sense by which the body of those mingled is affected recurs to the work of him to whose institution bodies, marriages, seeds are referred?

*Augustine.* The power of sensing is one thing, the defect of desiring another; distinguish these two carefully, do not err deformedly. The power of sensing is one thing, I say, the defect of desiring another. Read the Gospel: *Whoever*, it says, *has looked at a woman to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart*[^35]. It did not say: Whoever has looked—which is to sense through that sense of the body called sight; but: *whoever has looked*, it says, *to desire*, which is to look toward evil. Sight, then, the sense of the flesh, is good; but the concupiscence of the flesh is an evil movement. If a married man uses this evil well, he does not make it good; but he compels it to serve a good work. For he does nothing but something good even through it, if he does nothing on account of it; but if he does something on account of it, yet within his spouse, the Apostle would not grant him pardon by the grace of marriage[^36], did he not recognize it to be sin.

**IV. 30.** *Julian.* It is fitting indeed to dispute nothing, after the first work which I published to holy Turbantius, about the necessary shame of natural things; because there it was treated so fully that, unless minds were laid utterly aside, no ambiguity could arise from this.

*Augustine.* Perhaps holy Turbantius, having read that very work of yours which you record you wrote to him, even for this reason breathed again into the Catholic faith—because he recognized that you had failed in such a cause.

**IV. 31.** *Julian.* Yet, since the defender of traduction cannot turn aside from it by so much as a fingernail, and harasses my modesty with rhetorical elegance, why should it help to decree the very business of mingling, with the sparingness that befits things done, for different people? In this place too, then, and in those below, where I said: *"For this reason a man shall leave father and mother and cling to his wife; and the two shall be one flesh"*[^37]; *so that the Prophet, in order to express the faithfulness of the works, drew near to the peril of shame*—as though finding plunder, he exults, he leaps, and he cries out: *An open confession, wrested out by the force of truth*—he says. *Let the cause be told, why, in expressing the works of God, the Prophet drew near to the peril of shame. Are human works, then, not to be shameful, but plainly to be gloried in, while divine works are shameful? Is it true that, in uttering and expressing the works of God, not the love or labor of the Prophet is honored, but his shame is endangered? For what could God have made, that his proclaimer should be ashamed to tell? And, what is graver, that a man should be ashamed of some work which not man, but God made in man—when all craftsmen, as much as they can, by labor and industry, act so as not to blush at their own works? But surely it is that which shames us, which shamed those first human beings, when they covered the shameful parts; that is the punishment of sin, that is the wound and the trace of sin, that is the enticement and the tinder of sin, that is the law in the members resisting the law of the mind. Of this we are ashamed, and rightly ashamed. For if this were not so, what would be more ungrateful for us, what more irreligious, if in our members we were confounded not by our defect or punishment, but by the works of God?* It became clear, surely, how he exults; he cannot put reins on his joy; he holds my sentence, by which he can show natural concupiscence to be evil and assign it to the works of the devil; which sentence he says was wrested from my mouth by the force of truth, and he harangues that it is most wicked and most profane, if we should confess that the things we say were made by God must be covered by shame. But, too moved by alacrity, he could not weigh what he was saying; for, asserting that in the works of God there is nothing shameful, but that the work of the genitals is shameful, and therefore cannot be ascribed to the works of the Founder, he has suddenly confessed it to be not only honorable but even just—not only with God creating, but even with God judging—allotted to our bodies. I showed in the third book of my earlier work that the punishment cannot be the same as the fault.

*Augustine.* You did not show what you vainly boast you showed; nay rather, you yourself in a certain place, forgetting what you had said before, confessed that the fault is also a punishment of the one who sins. Which I, when I answered you above, had shown evidently enough, proving also through the Apostle that sins are punished by sins; who, when he had said of certain people that they had changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of the image of corruptible man, and of birds, and of four-footed beasts, and of serpents, at once, showing this sin to be punished by other sins: *Therefore*, he says, *God handed them over to the desires of their heart, unto uncleanness; that they should afflict their own bodies with insults among themselves*[^38], and the rest which he there wove together. Nor would it be said in the Psalm, *Add iniquity upon their iniquity, and let them not enter into your righteousness*[^39], unless, by the just judgment of God, preceding sins were punished by added sins.

**IV. 32.** *Julian.* And therefore one need not labor over this; yet I commend the disputant's sharpness, who does not want what he says was rendered by God to be founded by God; pronouncing that what he wants to befit his judgments does not befit the works of God.

*Augustine.* It appears that you do not understand how it was said, *God did not make death*[^40]—by whose judgment, nonetheless, he who sins dies. It befits his judgment, then, that the sinner die; and yet death does not befit his work, because *God did not make death*. For it is his just judgment that each one perish by his own sin, although God does not make sin; just as he did not make death, and yet he slays the one whom he judges worthy of death. Whence it is read: *God did not make death*[^41]; it is read: *Death and life are from the Lord God*[^42]; which two things he surely sees are not contrary to each other, whoever discerns the divine judgments from the divine works—which if you had been able to do, you would not have said these empty things.

**IV. 33.** *Julian.* Therefore in his works there is no shame; but in his sentences the utmost deformity. It is certain, surely, that guilt deserves punishment; yet does the confusion due to fault therefore recur in the avenging, so that what the guilty man did is called shameless, but what God did in judging cannot be told without ignominy?

*Augustine.* Why do you entangle what is unfolded, and roll up what is unrolled, so that to slower wits—of which there are many among human beings—you may seem to say something, when you say nothing? For you are a man who tries to persuade rather by shamelessness than by eloquence: that the deformity is either none or slight, that, when the flesh ought to be subject to the spirit, it has desires opposed to the spirit; or that it is not the just judgment of God to abandon the one who offends, so that he may be a punishment to himself, to whom God had been the true happiness; or that each one ought to be confounded by his sin, not by his punishment—when most people are not ashamed of their sins, if no punishment which they feel has followed, and the punishment makes them confounded, which impunity would not have done. But against most open matters who would care to be eloquent, except one deserted by the truth? Both of these we say freely: both what man did willing, and what he suffered unwilling; that is, both the disobedience of the spirit, and the concupiscence of the flesh against the spirit; but one of these you blushed to say, lest you warn us whence we might convict your error. And now, when you already name the concupiscence of the flesh and disordered desire, lest you be said to blush at the name of the thing you have taken up, you fear more to blush, and do not fear to err.

**IV. 34.** *Julian.* Yet it is established that our accuser is rolling in the very mud he shuns. For when he has feigned that the punishment of disordered desire was justly rendered, and drives home that this justice befits the divine sentence—while in the pronouncing of the sentence he does not deny it to be a work of God, from which he says disordered desire arose, which he confesses must be blushed at—he has surely brought back upon the work of God that shame which he had thrust away.

*Augustine.* Already it has been said to you: *God did not make death*[^43]; but in the pronouncing of the sentence it is God's: *You shall die the death*[^44]. Behold, God brought it about that this be rendered to the sinner, which God himself did not make. And yet, because *God is the God of vengeances*[^45], he says that he creates evils[^46]; and certain things in the book of *Sirach* are said to have been created for vengeance[^47]. But when sin is also the punishment of sin, God does not make sin by an unjust work, but, that sin be the punishment of the sinner, he makes by a just work. For who would deny it to be a sin to believe lying prophets? And yet this was the punishment of king Ahab, inflicted by divine judgment, as the history of the Kingdoms attests[^48]. Nor for that reason does anyone so lose his senses as to think that the lies of the false prophets are to be praised, or to call God the author of a lie, when by a just judgment, where he perceives one worthy of such a punishment, he causes one to be deceived by a lie. Read, and understand; and do not, lest you understand, drown yourself out with the tumult of loquacity.

**IV. 35.** *Julian.* To which, however, as though a corollary of a special crime, he added, so as to say that such a punishment was inflicted by God as would be the enticement and tinder of sin, the law in the members fighting unconquered against the law of our mind; by which kind of vengeance God would multiply outrages, not punish them; and he who was angry at the evil will by which there had been straying would make for the rest a necessity of sinning. Yet what kind of judgment this is, let the fury of the Manichean see; while it stands established that this judge, who is feigned by Augustine, pretended to abhor sin, but for the rest clings to sinners with such affection that they could not find a more diligent foster-father.

*Augustine.* Read what is written: that, since *they did not approve of having God in their knowledge, God handed them over to a reprobate mind, to do the things that are not fitting*[^49]; and see that certain sins are also the punishments of those who sin. But that you may understand how God does this, reread what I admonished you of above concerning king Ahab, whose sin, surely, was to believe the false prophets; and yet this sin, with God avenging, was also the punishment of the one who sinned. Consider these things, and do not bark against the truth, lest in you too this punishment be recognized.

**IV. 36.** *Julian.* What, then, do you want, most subtle disputant? That shame in the works of God is sacrilegious, because all the things that God made ought to be done everywhere openly, lest our trepidation seem to accuse the craftsman? The apostle Paul erred, then, who, describing the works of God, says: *Our more shameful parts have greater honorableness; and God tempered the body, giving more abundant honor to that which lacked it, that there might be no division in the body*[^50].

*Augustine.* Read carefully, and inspect the Greek codex; and you will find that the Apostle said *uncomely* what you say are *more shameful* parts; and, asking why they are uncomely, which before had been so comely that those two were naked and were not confounded[^51]; you will find, if the animosity of contention does not blind you, that, in order that this should follow, sin went before; and that God made nothing uncomely in the first human beings—just as God did not make death, since God alone makes the body; and yet, *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^52], the truthful Apostle says.

**IV. 37.** *Julian.* The caution of honorable people too errs, then, which holds out the coverings of modesty for natural things. You, then, yourself drive out before the eyes of the peoples the remains of foods, which you certainly confess do not pertain to disordered desire; nay, that you may win authority for your teaching by example, do in church all the things which you say are done at the nod of the will, and, content to hide only the work of mingling—from which, perhaps by an old resolve, and now perhaps by old age, you have desisted—say that it pertains to great sacrilege if a thing which has God for its founder shuns the public gaze. Eat, then, in the forum, or in the synagogues, in which your disputations resound; and when garments are a burden under the scorching sun, covering only that part of your body which the devil moved, walk naked for the rest; since you pronounce it ungrateful and profane if anything be covered that is ascribed to the works of God. And since you do none of these things (if at least you do not do them by way of making progress), by these very deeds you confirm one of two things: either that all these things pertain to the devil, which you do not perform under the public gaze; or that your teaching, if not by your heart, has fallen at least with your belly as witness.

*Augustine.* Of the things which shame conceals, some are horrible, others desirable; the former are concealed lest they be abhorred, like the discharges of the remains from food; the latter, lest they be desired, or lest they prompt to be desired that which is wont to be done through them, like those members which are properly called shameful by shame itself, or if by them the very work of concupiscence is performed. For even the other parts of the body, more remote from these, when one is ashamed to leave them naked or to bare them, this pertains to the fact that the concupiscence of the flesh is fed more widely through the eyes. Hence those shameless men wished, as far as they could, to strip the chaste Susanna[^53]. It can therefore well be understood that God too commended care for this same shame, when those who had made themselves girdles, confounded at their nakedness, he even clothed with tunics; and these indeed of skins[^54], so that death adjoined might be signified by bodies now corruptible. But custom forbids eating in public—those whom it forbids—and it is deservedly shameful to act against custom. For the ancient Romans, as you yourself too have read, used to dine and lunch in the open. What is it, then, that you wander about in the void, not by reasoning but by reviling? Look back upon those first parents, who were naked and were not confounded; attend to what they covered, and confess what they felt. From girdles it came to tunics; thence began, and grew, the covering of the human body, where to the human members the name of shameful parts was given. There the care of shame is greater, where chastity resists concupiscence. For a rational nature is ashamed to have something in its flesh, where, if it does not wish to be dishonored by unchastity, it is necessary to resist—both for the married, lest they be defiled by an unlawful one, and for the continent, lest by any lying together. This discord of flesh and spirit—far be it that it could have been in paradise, if no one had sinned; nor did it possess us from the mingling of an alien nature: whence, then, did it descend to us, unless from the transgression of the first man?

**IV. 38.** *Julian.* They are inept matters, and such as would make our pen more an occasion of reviling than of labor; but they necessarily follow upon your faith. Yet still, that the reader may be attentive, I admonish him. You attempted, surely, to assert that the work of God is proved not to be shameful; but just as you could not effect this, so you pledged absolutely that you would withdraw shame from disordered desire, if it should be taught to be the work of God; which in the first work I so approved that I do not think you will doubt it when you read it; yet, since this contrivance of yours signifies that those books have not yet come to your hands, it will not irk me to show this same thing in the present too. The irrational animals, then—by whom do you think they were founded, which at certain times are moved by a most ardent disordered desire, so that it even sharpens the ferocity of each? Then the wild boar is savage, then the tigress most fierce. Before all, the frenzy of mares is notable[^55]. Truly the grasses swell. In spring the abundance of tender moisture overflows. And on certain days the herds repeat their coupling[^56]. It is long to go through each one: all the kinds which flight suspends, which swimming has plunged, which wandering has scattered, through the air, through the seas, through the groves, and yet which neither reason has lifted up nor fault depressed, are inflamed with the known pleasure of bodies to be mingled. That ardor of the sexes, then, which they suffer, did they receive it by the work of God or of the devil? You will cry out, without doubt, of God. God, then, kindled sex with natural pleasure; which surely the Manichean denies more consistently than you. For he—from whom you learned to condemn the concupiscence of the flesh—weighing what he had to say, pursues the thing he had defined as diabolical, and removed from the works of God, wherever he could find it; and so, just as through concupiscence he assigned the bodies of human beings, so through human beings he assigned all living creatures to the devil as author. But you, while remaining thus far in the camp of the Manichean, and bearing his greatest dragon, from which you breathe lethal venom into unhappy minds through natural evil and the crimes of marriage; yet you do not want to hurl into all natures the darts supplied by your master, and, more familiar with the brutes (which yet you spare for this reason, that you may more authoritatively charge the state of rational beings), you consent that God made in their bodies what the devil made in ours; though you confess that the same thing, but more mildly, is in human beings than in cattle. So that the prudent reader may grasp what is effected, then, you do not deny that the disordered desire of bodies in animals was made by God. That affection is therefore not unworthy of the works of God, which is found sharper in those substances which have snatched nothing from the evil of the devil, even by a slight will. Therefore, since concupiscence has been defended by the example of animals, defended too by the dignity of its author, it is neither evil nor diabolical, which God the former of bodies made, and which that nature holds which is free of sin; since this became clear, then, I inquire whether you consent that God made this disordered desire which human bodies feel. If you grant it, the contention is removed, you are corrected, and the Manichean will remain ground down. But if you say, In the bodies of human beings it could not be made by God, I reply, you think that pleasure and concupiscence of the flesh unworthy of the works of man, not of God. For it cannot be doubted that, if what you confess to have been made everywhere by God, you say could not be made in human beings, you do not take from concupiscence its due testimonies, but procure for the human body things undue to it. See, then, of what profanity is the end of your sect. You say that what was not unworthy of the work of the Founder is unworthy of mortal flesh. Therefore in this sense you have not disparaged disordered desire, but exalted the man whom you wished to accuse. Such is the constancy and such the reward of one who declares war on truth, that he is always struck by effects contrary to his attempt. I, then, now more consistently with your words, as all prudence recognizes, rise up. Is it so, that in appraising and expressing the works of God, reason is not heard, the examples of all natures are not considered, but to such a degree is the madness of man borne up, that he thinks unbecoming to his own inmost parts what he sees, through the work of God, flourishing in the partners of his nature? For neither is the origin or the reasoning of our bodies other than that of one another's.

*Augustine.* Say, then, that resurrection and everlasting incorruption are not owed to human bodies, because, like the bestial, they too are themselves earthly; say that the end cannot be different where the origin is not different; say these things, if you please; and, against the evangelical Letters, show, by the rush of empty loquacity, how much you have profited by secular letters. But if you will not dare to say this, confess, according to the Christian faith, that even this is a punishment of man—that he was compared to senseless beasts and was made like them[^57]. To him, then, this is a misery, since beasts cannot be miserable; so too the concupiscence of the flesh is a punishment to man, not to a beast, in which the flesh never has desires opposed to the spirit. Or does it please you so to equalize mortal natures that you assert that in beasts too the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit? But if you do not do this—lest, like the horse and the mule, you have no understanding[^58]—acknowledge that disordered desire such as we speak of, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, would not have been in paradise, if no one had sinned. For such a thing is not in cattle, from which you provided such patronage for the thing you have taken up, where, with those mute creatures, you could be wordy. For if the flesh did not, by human disordered desire, have desires opposed to the spirit, but disordered desire in human beings were such that it rose up at the nod of the will, when there was need, but, when there was no need, supplied no incitements with which our will would fight to restrain and rein them, we would accuse you of nothing for being willing to place it in paradise, that is, in the dwelling of such great happiness, so unhappily.

**IV. 39.** *Julian.* For just as we received the image of God by the reason of the mind, so we perceive a communion with the cattle by the affinity of the flesh; of which, although the form is diverse, there is nonetheless one substance from the matter of the elements—a flesh which, for the merits, surely, of the rational soul, is to see eternity, either wretched with hardships or glorious with rewards.

*Augustine.* If, for the merits of the rational soul—as you confess—earthly flesh, and corruptible like that of cattle, is nonetheless to see eternity with a far different end, why do you not accept that, for the merits of the image of God, which had been deformed by no sin, flesh was so founded at first—though of earthly matter—that, if no one had sinned, it would be going to remain in eternity and in incorruption; nor would the corruptible body be weighing down the soul, that is, the image of God, but so subject to it that, for the sake of children to be generated, just as the other members by which we work anything, even the genital members would be moved at the nod of the will; or such would be the concupiscence of the flesh that it would not rise except with the soul, that is, the image of God, willing, nor would it submerge the thought of the mind by an overflow of pleasure? For if it were even now such, it would not be said of it that it is not from the Father; but it would be from the world[^59], that is, from human beings, who through it, and with it, are born to the world, and without doubt are going to perish unless they are reborn to God. Of the bodily matter, then, common to us and to the cattle, it is fittingly believed that, for the merit of the image of God, before sin had begun, the beginning was different, just as, after sin was taken on, the end is different.

**IV. 40.** *Julian.* By what reasoning, then, would it be unworthy—not of the image of God, since the substance of the soul is other than that of the flesh, but of the dissoluble bondsman of the image of God—what was not unworthy of the founding of God himself? God, then, made bodies; God distinguished the sex of bodies; God made the genital members; God implanted the affection by which these bodies might be mingled to one another; God gave also the force of the seeds; God works in the secrets of nature from the matter of the seeds: but God makes nothing evil, nothing guilty.

*Augustine.* *God makes nothing evil, nothing guilty*—but insofar as God makes it, not insofar as the mass is corrupted and condemned, from which he makes it.

**IV. 41.** *Julian.* God made disordered desire, as in cattle, so in human beings; but in cattle he sent forth the affections uncurbed, while in rational man God instituted a measure. Prudence and honorableness procure for him a garment, which God gave. Therefore God blames not the measure, not the genus, but its excess, which, arising from the insolence of free will, accuses not the state of nature, but the merit of the doer.

*Augustine.* Why, then, does disordered desire resist the spirit in man, which it does not do in a beast—unless because it pertains to the nature of a beast, but to the punishment of man, either because it is, which would be none, or because it resists, which would be subject, if it had not been made or corrupted by sin? For if *in cattle God sent forth the affections uncurbed*, surely you confess that the disordered desires—since you call these affections—are curbed by man; but in no way would they be curbed, unless they were moved viciously. For behold, you say that God instituted in man a measure for disordered desire; why does it not stay in the measure in which it is divinely instituted, but goes beyond it unless it is curbed? How, then, is it called good, which urges and compels man to do evil, if it is not resisted? Do you see that the excellent thing you have taken up was, in the nature of man, either born from sin, or corrupted by sin; and that hence comes that fact—that the first human beings, after sin, covered the shameful parts, who, before sin, were naked and were not confounded? For what is it that you say: *Prudence and honorableness procure for man a garment, which God gave*? Were the human beings, then, foolish and dishonorable, and imprudent and shameless, before sin, when their nakedness did not shame them? Thanks to sin! For so they would have remained. But if this is said most absurdly, surely natural prudence and honorableness covered the shameful parts; but parts which, before sin, were not shameful. The excess of disordered desire, accordingly, is in sin; but even its impulse is in vice. Of this impulse they blushed, who were unwilling to leave naked the members which this thing you have taken up was soliciting against their will.

**IV. 42.** *Julian.* Attend now, indeed, to what is gathered from your opinion: namely, that whatever is believed to be good ought not to be covered by shame. But we have taught that that will, naturally implanted in the sexes, is no more evil than it is pertaining to the work of God. And through this you will either desert your impiety, or your shame. But what is this to us? *If the Ethiopian will change his skin, or the leopard his spots*[^60]; so too will you be washed clean of the mysteries of the Manicheans.

*Augustine.* Nay rather, you will not cease to aid the Manicheans, unless you say, with Ambrose and with all Catholics, that this thing you have taken up—which even the Manicheans convict of being evil—turned into our nature from the transgression of the first man; lest, according to those whom, unknowing, you aid, the impious heretics, so manifest a vice of man be believed to have a beginning coeternal with God.

**IV. 43.** *Julian.* It follows, then, that you lay aside modesty, and, with friendship remaining toward your master, ally yourself with the Cynics; whom, however, the arguments even of some of the Stoics accompany, as Cicero relates in the *Offices*: *For they fault common honorableness, because we hold to be disgraceful in word things that are not disgraceful in fact, while things that are disgraceful in fact we call by their proper names. To commit robbery, to practice fraud, to commit adultery is disgraceful in fact, but it is spoken of not obscenely; to labor at the begetting of children is honorable in fact, obscene in name. And many things to that effect are disputed by the same men*—he says—*against modesty. But let us follow nature, and flee everything that shrinks from the approval of the eyes and ears*[^61]. And you, then, to whom this natural reasoning, the sister of honorableness, is displeasing, either say that to labor at the begetting of children—since robbery, sacrilege, and parricide all have the greatest disgrace in fact, none in their names—is fouler and more profane than the duty of modesty demands; or, if you blush to accuse marriage to such a degree that it would even outweigh crimes when set beside them, exhort spouses to call that mingling by name as securely and as freely as we are accustomed to name parricide and robbery. But if you should add that the union of bodies destined for offspring, in order to mock—as you are accustomed—Christian ears, is stained by no iniquity, but can be reckoned good in its own measure, embrace that deed of Crates of Thebes, a rich and noble man, to whom the sect of the Cynics was so dear to heart that, leaving his paternal wealth, he migrated to Athens with his wife Hipparchia, a follower of that philosophy with equal spirit; with whom, when he wished to lie together in public, as Cornelius Nepos relates, and she, for the sake of concealment, drew over the covering of a cloak, she was beaten by her husband: *By your own opinions, surely*—he said—*you are still too little taught, who dare not exercise in the presence of others what you know you do rightly*. Such a manner is altogether owed to your flocks, so that natural things—because, by this, they are proved good, since they are founded by God for the multiplication of bodies—may be usurped without any obstacle of shame. Restore to the four-footed beasts their thanks, then, so that, because by their examples they guard the sense of your bodies from the possession of the devil and the mouth of the Manichean, you too may follow their liberty in coupling as an attestation of a good thing. For it is fitting that those should furnish a teaching of morals to whom they furnished a patronage of members. And, that what we have done may be assigned by repetition: you said that disordered desire, if God had made it, was to be wrapped in no shame; we, although in the four books of the earlier work we treated this fully, nonetheless in the present too, by the testimony of all animals, which for the moment you confess were founded by God, have shown that the disordered desire of the sexes was created by God—which nonetheless we confess must be overshadowed by shame in human beings; your teaching, then, follows, that you should present to us, by a long restoration of the Cynics, their seemliness, and exercise the things that are natural, because they were made by God, in the eyes of the city. Do you perceive with what wrapped-up eyes you leaped upon my sentence, by which I said: *So that, in order to express the faithfulness of the works, the Prophet drew near to the peril of shame*? For just as your preceptor consistently accuses these things in accordance with his fables—whose fabrications I set down in the earlier book, who utterly denies that all bodies were made by God—so you have dared shamelessly to handle them, you who confess God to be the creator (though by fearing) of bodies, the sense of which you ascribe to the prince of darkness; which surely was worn down by so great a power of truth as the faith with which it was said by the Evangelist, that through God *all things were made, and without him was made nothing*[^62].

*Augustine.* Could you compare beasts with human beings in the concupiscence of the flesh or disordered desire, unless you believed that those first parents of the human race too, bearing as it were corruptible bodies, would have been going to die even if they had not sinned? And this, in the new error of your heresy, the Catholic Church so condemns that Pelagius, your prince, condemned it for himself, among other things objected to him before fourteen Eastern bishops, by whom as judges he was heard, through fear of his own condemnation. In which judgment, surely, even you yourself were condemned, who say that Adam was so made that, whether he sinned or did not sin, he would be going to die; contradicting the Apostle who says: *The body is dead because of sin*[^63]. Further, if, with no sin preceding, those bodies were not going to die, surely they were not even corruptible, lest they weigh down those blessed souls: *For the corruptible body*, as it is written, *weighs down the soul*[^64]. And through this, just as death and corruption could be not common to bestial and human bodies, although the earthly matter was common to them, so disordered desire could be not common to them in propagating children; but either none in human beings—so that, just as they used the other members at the will's motion for fitting works, so for the work of generating they would use even the genitals moved by the will—or not such as in beasts, but serving the nod, and never laying aside the mind from the vigilance of thought, nor at last by pleasure itself. But now, with sin preceding, by which human nature was changed for the worse, those things became penal for it which are fitting to the nature of a beast; but in this evil, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, there is a greater cause of shame, because between two things, each of which pertains to our nature, and of which the one ought to command, the other to serve, there has arisen a discord not only to be grieved over but even much to be blushed at. What, then, have the irrelevant aids of the Cynics, summoned by you, profited you—since, with you in this way of your error failing from the way, not even the beasts of burden you compared to human beings could help you?

**IV. 44.** *Julian.* The time admonishes that we pass on to other matters; but since it is certain that you have nothing to peddle to unskilled ears except the shame of natural things, I shall touch, as briefly as I can, what is thought to remain. Who, then, would deny that this honorableness, by which we cover our genitals, varies according to persons, places, offices, and customs? Thus in public gatherings nakedness is more obscene, which has nothing of disgrace in the baths. Thus the bedchamber dress is one thing, half-naked and unconcerned; the dress of the forum another, more careful and fuller. What of the fact that, by frequent attestation of familiarity, negligence of caution looks on, and the more unfamiliar or honored the person present, the more diligent the dress procured? What of the fact that to sailors and most workmen no one has counted nakedness a fault? And, lest this simplicity be imputed to persons rather than to occupations, in the custom of all even the apostle Peter, after the Lord's resurrection, fished naked in the boat[^65]. Direct your eyes from here to the gifts of physicians; they lead their art, for the zeal of health, into places of shame. For athletes nakedness is even seemly. And now not only among young men, and those joined to themselves in wanton company, but even among whole nations, both sexes are uncovered, and there is mingling without choice of secrecy. Yet what wonder is it, if the Scot and the barbarism of neighboring nations does this; when even the philosophy of which we made mention above held the same view, and the teaching of the Traducianists has come to this point? What measure of shame, then, will be held, or what boundary among so many of its diversities, and among undertakings partly necessary, partly defended by society, through which it could be taught that the devil mingled a natural ardor into the sexes? And through this, just as the support of your opinion is established, by offices, places, customs, arts, and whole nations together, to be dissolved, so there has remained unconquered what we defend by reason itself and with the apostle Paul teaching: that God, the author of the universe, created all bodies, all members of bodies, and all senses of bodies; but so ordained that shame should cover certain things in our members for the opportunities of the times, and natural honorableness should present certain things, to which it would be as unbecoming to apply a covering as importunely to make common the secrecy of the genitals.

*Augustine.* You rather importunely sprinkle the fault of importunity upon those of whom the divine Scripture says: *They were naked, and were not confounded*[^66]. And surely they were then so upright as they had been made upright: for *God made man upright*[^67], as we read. At that time, then, of so great uprightness of theirs, were they so depraved as imprudently, shamelessly, dishonorably, importunely to make common the secrets of the genitals? Acknowledge, then, that there was not yet a cause for shame, when those members were not yet shameful which now are properly called shameful. For there did not dwell in the members a law fighting against the law of the mind, without which no one is now born. There had not yet been rendered, by the just judgment of God, who deservedly deserted the deserter, to the disobedient man the disobedience of his own flesh. For it is indeed the disobedience of the flesh to have desires opposed to the spirit, although, even with the spirit having desires opposed to it, it is not permitted to accomplish what it attempts. This, then, was not yet, when they were naked and were not confounded. They did not, therefore, by a true nakedness importunely make common the secrets of the genitals: but as yet they had felt nothing importunate in the genitals. Why do you gather empty words, like light leaves, with which you too may cover your carnal contention against spiritual authority—as it were, flesh having desires opposed to the spirit? Why do you ask what measure of shame must be held, or what boundary, among so many of its diversities, which arise from various necessities, arts, opinions, customs, whether upright or perverse? Behold, you have human beings, not of any nation, like the Scots, but the parents of all nations; not depraved by any perverse opinion, like the Cynics, and any others befouled with the deformity of similar irreverence, but founded upright by God; not constrained by the work of any necessity, like the nakedness of Peter, by which you thought yourself should be veiled, but free in the paradise of delights; look at them—before sin, displayers of liberty, after sin, teachers of shame. Before sin, they were naked and were not confounded; after sin, they were confounded at their nakedness. Before sin, they left the genitals, not yet shameful, without a covering; after sin, they covered the genitals, now shameful. These, then, are witnesses sufficiently fit, and they convict both the obstinacy of the Pelagians and the shamelessness of certain nations and of the Cynics, by their nakedness, before not shameful and afterward shameful.

**IV. 45.** *Julian.* This, then, in the present work, being settled as far as the matter at hand has allowed, I come to that which you said—that in Christ this natural concupiscence was not. For thus you speak against me: *But this man, in all these, was unwilling to name the concupiscence of the flesh, which is not from the Father, but is from the world; of which world the devil is called the prince; who did not find it in the Lord, because the Lord, as man, did not come to men through it*[^68]. You pronounced, then, that Christ—whom the Catholic faith confesses to be in all things a true man—did not have in his flesh this concupiscence of which the apostle John speaks. But John, as his words have taught, pronounces that the concupiscence of the flesh and the concupiscence of the eyes, with the whole world, are not from the Father[^69]; how this ought to be understood, we have shown.

*Augustine.* You have shown indeed, but how you understand it, or rather do not understand it, not how it ought to be understood; concerning which matter I have answered you in that disputation of yours, not truthful but talkative, contending for the truth.

**IV. 46.** *Julian.* But you, having embraced the very name of concupiscence, want both the sense of the eyes and that of the inmost parts to have been absent from the body of Christ.

*Augustine.* You would not say these things, if you had a sound sense—not of body, but of mind.

**IV. 47.** *Julian.* Here, then, I admonish the reader to be present with his whole mind; for he will see the heresy of the Apollinarists, but, through you, restored with the addition of the Manichean. For Apollinaris is reported first to have introduced such an incarnation of Christ as to say that only a body seemed to have been assumed from human substance, while in place of the soul there was the deity itself; and that Christ seemed to have taken on not a man, but the corpse of a man. After this began to be shaken, both by the attestation of reason and of the Gospel—namely, because by this very thing it was necessary that all that falsity be convicted, that Christ had said he was a man, whom the Jews persecuted as having spoken the truth, if flesh alone had been assumed by him, since a man cannot be except soul and body at once; or because in the Gospel he had said: *I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again*[^70]. For what soul would be laid down, if it had not been assumed? When, then, he had been overthrown by the authority of such testimonies and of clear reason, he devised something else, from which his heresy, which endures to this day, would be born; and he said that there was indeed a human soul in Christ, but that the senses of the body were not in him, and pronounced him to have been impassible to all sins.

*Augustine.* Epiphanius, the Cyprian bishop of holy memory, in the little work which he published on heresies, said that some of the Apollinarists said that in the Lord Jesus Christ the body was consubstantial with the divinity; but others denied that he assumed a soul; others, on account of what was said, *The Word was made flesh*[^71], contended that he did not assume flesh from created flesh, that is, of Mary, but that the Word was made flesh; but afterward, thinking I-know-not-what, said that he did not assume a mind. But what you affirm—that the Apollinarists asserted that the senses of the body were not in Christ, and that they pronounced him impassible—I have neither read anywhere, except in this book of yours, nor ever heard from anyone. But since I see that you seek where to spread out your empty talk, so that, being wordy, you may seem to be copious, I answer quickly: Whoever believes either those things which I recounted above the Apollinarists to say, or that Christ did not have the senses of the body, and was impassible, let him be anathema. But that you too may recognize yourself: Whoever believes that the flesh of Christ had desires opposed to the spirit, let him be anathema.

**IV. 48.** *Julian.* Not one who, by the power of judgment, had avoided faults; but one who, by a felicity of flesh set apart from our senses, could not have felt the desire of vices.

*Augustine.* We do not say: *That Christ, by a felicity of flesh set apart from our senses, could not feel the desire of vices*, but we say that he, by the perfection of virtue, and by flesh procreated not through the concupiscence of the flesh, did not have the desire of vices. For it is one thing not to have had an evil desire, another not to have been able to feel it; for he would have felt it if he had had it, since the sense by which he would feel it was not lacking to him, but a will was present by which he would not have it. And do not wonder that Christ—though a true man, yet through all things good—was unwilling to have an evil desire. For who, except you, denies that disordered desire is evil, by which evils are desired? Who, I say, except you, tries to persuade that what he confesses to be the disordered desire of vices is not a vice, and that what, if it persuades and is consented to, becomes an evil work, is not an evil? This disordered desire Christ both could feel, if he had it, and could have, if he willed; but far be it that he would will it. Yet if he had an evil desire, and—to use your word—a *desire of vices*, it would have begun to be in him from his own will, since he was not born with it, as we are. And through this, his virtue was this: not to have it; our virtue is: not to consent to it; and in this to imitate him, that, just as he committed no sin by not having it, so neither do we commit any by not consenting to it; and as he willed and was able not to have it, so let us will to be free of it, because we shall be able. For his grace will set us free from the body of this death—that is, from the flesh of sin—he who came to us in the likeness of the flesh of sin, not in the flesh of sin[^72].

**IV. 49.** *Julian.* By so grave a flattery toward the faith of the Catholics, as the matter shows, not only is it in no way necessary, but it is even profane. For when he wishes to show that there was something more in the body of Christ, lest it grow cheap by communion with our flesh, he defrauded him of the integrity of natural senses; not seeing into how great a destruction the loss of truth, even by flattery, would go when corrupted. For the prescription roused up by the Catholics roared out; because, with such a faith, the mysteries of Christ would suffer greater loss than his members. For if, they say, Christ was for this reason made of the seed of David[^73], made of a woman, made under the law[^74], that he might give us an example, and that we might follow his footsteps, who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth[^75]; and yet did not put on through all things the property of our substance—if he bore either flesh without a soul, or a man without the senses with which nature imbues us—he is not taught to have fulfilled the form of an example and of the law. For what was praiseworthy in despising the enticements of the senses, of which he was incapable by the benefit of nature? What wonder is it to restrain the eyes of his flesh, not wandering by his aid? What great thing to lead away the nostrils from enticing odors, to which they could not be open? What stupendous thing to keep an arduous sparingness daily at banquets, whose incitements he could not feel? What, finally, would there be of arduousness in fasting prolonged to the fortieth day, if hunger could not be troublesome? What veneration would the discipline of the ears deserve, ever the receiver only of honorable voices, if it suffered a native deafness to unbecoming ones? What glory of chastity, if manhood was absent more than the will, and what was thought to be done by vigor of mind came from weakness of members?

*Augustine.* These things are deservedly said, not against Apollinaris or any of the Apollinarists—whom I do not think to have said that the flesh of Christ did not have the senses of a human body—but against anyone whatever who says this. We, however, say that he both sensed beautiful and deformed things through the eyes, and sensed sweet-smelling and ill-smelling things through the nostrils, and perceived by the sense of the ears melodious and absurd things, and discerned bitter from sweet things by tasting, and distinguished rough and smooth, hard and soft, cold and hot things by touching; and whatever else can be sensed and perceived by the sense of the body, he could sense and perceive; nor was the possibility of generating lacking to him, if he had willed; and yet his flesh never had desires opposed to the spirit. But if for this reason it is a great good, as you suppose, to abstain from faults, because disordered desires are not lacking which may be conquered, and it would not be a great thing if they were lacking; then each one will be the more praiseworthy in virtue, the more lustful he was in the flesh. And through this Christ, according to that horrible and detestable depravity of yours, just as he was the greatest of all human beings in virtue, so ought he to have been the most lustful of all in the flesh. With how great an impiety this is perceived, if you perceive it, do not delay to change your opinion; and to distinguish disordered desires from the senses, which are sensed rather in any things than themselves sense; lest each one seem to be the more lively in sense, the more ardent he was in disordered desire; and lest Christ be believed to have burned with the sharper disordered desires, the more he flourished with the purer senses.

**IV. 50.** *Julian.* What, finally, palm of endurance, if the pain of wounds and beatings, with the path of the senses intercepted, could not reach the soul? To what, then, did the flattery of Apollinaris profit? Namely, that all the beauty of the virtues which Christ had expressed in himself, emptied of the praises undue to his nature, would wilt; and, stripped of all the splendor of its truth, would offer the sacred teaching office of the Mediator to derision. To this was added that not only did he lose the marks of his works—being happier by birth than by virtue—but he was even pressed by charges of fraud, if he should declare to mortals: Stretch yourselves to the endurance of a man feeling nothing, and come through true crosses to the virtues of a false body feeling no pain; or: Imitate the chastity, conquering the certain enticements of your nature, of him whom weakness made seem chaste. Surely nothing more profane, nothing more wicked, can be devised by such fabrications. And Apollinaris had not said all these things; by that one thing which he had pronounced—that those senses were lacking to the man Christ which, implanted by nature, slip into vices not by use but by excess—he mutely incurred all these things which were reported by the Catholics in prejudgment of his opinion. For by the affirmation of all these the Catholic faith is built up, by whose denial or consequence the heresy of Apollinaris was compacted and condemned. You, therefore, conjecture what ought to be judged of you, who condemn the mingling of the sexes, like a Manichean; set apart the nature of the flesh of Christ from communion with human beings, according to the Manicheans; accuse the concupiscence of the flesh, according to the sayings of your preceptor the Manichean; say that the concupiscence of the senses was not in the body of Christ, either according to the Manicheans or according to the Apollinarists; and yet you want not to be called by us either an Apollinarist or a Manichean. Although I yield the point, and grant the favor; nor does our liberality repent us; through me it shall be permitted that you not seem a follower of Apollinaris—for he is the inventor of a lesser impiety; but through you it is not permitted that you be called anything other than a Manichean.

*Augustine.* Neither an Apollinarist nor a Manichean was Ambrose, but the vanquisher of heretics, who, through this thing you have taken up, said that whatever of human beings is born cannot be without fault, understanding and expounding, as he ought, the apostle Paul. But you so much the more aid the Manicheans, as I have most often shown, the more you suppose yourself alien from them; you are, however, a new Pelagian heretic, most loquacious in disputation, most slanderous in contention, most fallacious in profession. For, finding nothing to say, you say many empty things, you object a false crime to the Catholics, you lie that you are Catholic.

**IV. 51.** *Julian.* I showed, indeed, in the third little book of my first work, that it must irrefutably be said that Christ too drew guilt from the flesh of Mary, if natural sin were believed; and that he is placed by you under the power of the devil, you who affirm that the whole substance of human beings belongs to the right of the devil.

*Augustine.* What you record you showed in your third book, I convicted you in my fifth of not having shown.

**IV. 52.** *Julian.* And therefore, leaving that aside for the present, I demand this: where have you read that Christ was a eunuch by nature?

*Augustine.* Where have you read that I said this, except that you slander, as you are accustomed? The possibility of sowing children is one thing, which we do not say was lacking to the flesh of Christ, just as we know it to be lacking to the flesh of eunuchs; and the desire of vices is another, which you try to persuade Christ had, and you boast that you are a Christian; because, namely, as you said a little before: *Christ too would be pressed by charges of fraud, if he said to mortals: Stretch yourselves to the endurance of a man feeling nothing*; as though it follows that one does not feel inflicted pains who wills and is able not to have evil desires; or if he said—as you yourself compose your own words: *Imitate the chastity, conquering the certain enticements of your nature, of him whom weakness made seem chaste*. For you are so excellent a lover of chastity that to you one seems chaster who desires unlawful unions but, that he may not perpetrate them, resists his own disordered desire, than one who does not even desire such evils—not through impossibility of the flesh, but through the summit and perfection of virtue; nay, that the former is chaste, but the latter not chaste at all, but only seems to be; since, according to you, if he were chaste, he would desire these evils by nature, but would curb that same natural disordered desire by vigor of mind. So it comes about that that horrible absurdity, as I have already shown above, follows you: that each one is by will the chaster, the greater the disordered desire of his nature he has conquered, and has not permitted to go out into any excesses of luxury, however great it be; but that he who curbs a lesser disordered desire of disgraceful acts is less chaste; since he to whom what is not lawful is not pleasing is, according to your wisdom—or rather your madness—not chaste at all. Behold what you insanely try to fasten upon Christ: that he was by nature the most lustful of all, in order that thus, by the virtue of his will, he could be the most chaste of all. For in him, as you dispute, the spirit of continence would be the greater, the greater the concupiscence of the flesh he curbed. To this destruction your thing you have taken up, loved too much, has led you.

**IV. 53.** *Julian.* Who, although for the sake of a sign he was born of a Virgin, yet so did not turn away from the male sex that he took on its truth, whole through all his inmost parts, whole in body, a true man, a perfect man, if the apostle Peter is to be believed disputing in the *Acts*[^76]; conspicuous in untouched chastity, guarding both his mind and his eyes, with the vigor of his heart never relaxed; but that he accomplished all this by virtue of mind, not by infirmity of flesh, both his sleep, and food, and beard, and sweat, and labor, and cross, and lance attest, and the concupiscence of the flesh mingled into the senses of his whole body, and the truth and health and condition of his members. He did not, then, lack the senses of the body, but he ruled them. This is whence the faith of the Catholics insults the Gentiles, insults the Manicheans, because the word of his cross, as of his flesh, *is to those perishing folly, but to those who shall be saved the power of God*[^77]. This is what commended his love among us; since whatever the impiety of the Manichean tears to pieces, all this the piety of the Mediator assumed. Nothing, then, shames me in my Lord; in the things into which he came for my salvation, I hold the truth of his members, that I may take on the solidity and citadel of his example.

*Augustine.* The truth of his members, which every Christian acknowledges in Christ, is one thing; the disordered desire of sins, which you wish to fasten upon Christ, is another. For you say that the concupiscence of the flesh—that is, disordered desire, which you more gladly call natural concupiscence—is a good; and so you accuse its excess, so that whoever permits it to transgress the granted bounds contracts fault, as one using a good badly; but whoever advances it only up to lawful and granted things, and permits it to proceed no further, is worthy of praise, as one using a good well. Accordingly, since we see that some are so born that they are urged by greater disordered desire, others by less, if both, resisting it, be chaste, you are compelled to say that the former use a greater good well, the latter a lesser. There will, then, with you as teacher, be each one the more abounding in this good of yours, the more lustful he is; and the more laborious in fighting for chastity against his own disordered desire, the more abundant in this natural good; and therefore even the more praiseworthy in this virtue, the more strongly he struggles against a greater good than if he struggled against a lesser. Since, then, Christ in mortal flesh lived without doubt the most chaste of all, you will give him so much the greater natural disordered desire, the stronger you cannot find anyone in curbing disordered desire. For thus, without a charge of fraud, he will say to his own: Imitate my chastity, conquering the certain enticements of your nature; for these enticements are good, but nonetheless to be curbed and conquered; just as I had them, but curbed and conquered greater ones; lest you should say to me: For this reason you conquered, for this reason you lived most chaste in your mortal flesh, because by the felicity of nature you had the least disordered desires, which you would conquer most easily. Be chaste, then, because, in order to take away from you the obstacles of excuse for imitating me, I willed to be born more lustful than you, and yet I never permitted my greatest disordered desire to cross the granted bounds. These horrible monsters your heresy has brought forth.

**IV. 54.** *Julian.* I proclaim that all sanctity in him stood by the benefit of his mind, not by the prejudgment of the flesh. For thus both nature is defended, as much by his condition as by his assumption of it, and the life of human beings is directed by the imitation of his virtues. Of these two, the one cannot be praised without the truth of the other; there will be as much of dignity present to the sacred work as there is of truth present to the human body; and there will be as much present to the defense of the flesh as the sanctity of his manner of life shall have demanded. And, on the other side, the disparagement of one shares in both; since as much will be taken from his virtues as from his limbs; and if anything be scraped from the solid of his substance, all the ornaments of his morals perish, and the losses of the one being born recur to the injury of the one enduring; finally, if the substance of his flesh be thinned by any snatching-away of natural things, the whole pomp of his virtues vanishes. I will deny nothing in the members of the Mediator made of a woman that is established to be natural. And see how diverse are the bounds of reason, how of shame; the faith of Christians does not blush to say that Christ had genitals, while we nonetheless conceal them in ourselves as honorably as we can.

*Augustine.* The faith of Christians does not indeed blush to say that Christ had genitals; but you ought to have blushed—or rather to have trembled—lest you should say that the genitals of Christ were sometimes, and that even with him unwilling (for he ought never to have willed this, who led a celibate life), nonetheless moved by disordered desire, and that, against his holy resolve, that part of his holy body raised itself up to certain unlawful uses. For every kind of saint suffers such disordered desire as you try to bring upon the Holy of holies. But if you do not dare to say that the genitals of Christ were wont to be moved and raised by disordered desire with him unwilling, why do you dare to believe, unhappy man, why do you dare to attribute such disordered desire to the nature of Christ, that what you do not dare to say, you compel men to think?

**IV. 55.** *Julian.* Thus nature has prescribed that there be certain things which reason, as well as faith with religion, may pronounce upon; and yet shame and the decency of conduct do not permit them to be exposed to the eyes. In this manner the Teacher of the Gentiles too assigns truth to his flesh and sanctity to his spirit. *Great*, he says, *is the sacrament of piety, which was manifested in the flesh, justified in the spirit, appeared to angels, was preached among the Gentiles, was believed in this world, was taken up in glory*[^78]. Which, just as he commended to be believed with truth, so he announced that its assailants would be born at the end of the age; for at once there follows: *But in the last times certain ones will depart from the faith, attending to spirits of seduction, to the teachings of demons speaking lies in hypocrisy, having their conscience seared, forbidding to marry, to abstain from foods which God created to be received with thanksgiving by the faithful, and by those who have known the truth; since every creature of God is good*[^79]. This contamination of the Churches, then, which you vomited up from your following of the Manichean, and this transgression of those departing from the faith—which consists in the preaching of natural evil and in the condemnation of conjugal mingling—the Apostle both foresaw and punished, showing not only what would be said, but also what would follow.

*Augustine.* And yet you, who can do nothing but belch slanders and vomit insults, did not dare to contradict the words of the Catholic man of God—whom you cannot call a Manichean—who chokes you, and who says that, from the mingling of male and female, no one can be born free of fault[^80]. For these words of his I both recorded in my first book to Valerius, which—having tried to refute it with four books—you feared to touch anything thence; and in this one, to which you now respond, I did not pass over those same words in silence, and against them, still mute, although with the name of him you do not openly dare to oppose unspoken, you do not fear to accuse me of this.

**IV. 56.** *Julian.* For what he says—that it would have to be taught to abstain from foods—does not, surely, in the judgment of a prudent man, accuse the sparingness of Christians, nor announce that human beings can exist who would point out the peril of starvation; but he shows that, since there would arise those who would say that all animals created by God for the food of mortals, because they were born of concupiscence and the mingling of sex, were polluted by a diabolical evil, he expressed what would follow: namely, that one must renounce foods, if their propagation were believed diabolical. Whence you too remove this defamation of the mingling of bodies from animals, so that a reason for your pretense may subsist. Yet human beings, who were made to the image of God, you say pertain, on account of this very concupiscence of those generating, to the right of the demon. Both of you, then, you and the Manichean, have one cause for disparaging substances and transcribing them to the devil; but he, on account of this affection which is felt in the natural work, condemns all living things; you, however, not all, but—what is fouler—the better ones. For you absolve swine, dogs, and asses, so that you may seem to avoid the Manicheans; but you condemn, on account of this same thing which the Manichean does, all human beings who were made to the image of God; and, establishing natural evil nowhere except in the image of God, you harangue against us, an accuser of the saints and a patron of asses.

*Augustine.* What is this you say, slanderer of the Catholics and helper of the Manicheans? What is this you say? A man who ought to be ashamed of so great a dullness, even if he had the brow of an ass. Could not one who said that human beings can be made miserable by ignorance of the truth, but that asses cannot be made miserable by not knowing the truth, be called by a man of such a heart as you yourself are, "an accuser of the saints and a patron of asses"? And yet he would be saying what is most true. Why, then, do you not understand, ass, that it is likewise true that human beings cannot be born free of fault from the mingling of male and female, but asses can? Or do you think that you escape the pursuing blows of authority and reason because, in the company of disordered desire, you yoke human beings and asses to the chariot of your error? Ambrose was speaking not of cattle, but of human beings, where he says: *It has been so arranged, then, that from man and woman, that is, through that mingling of bodies, no one is seen to be free of fault*[^81]. Was this man, this Teacher of the Church, then, an accuser of the saints and a patron of asses? Surely the body both of the ass and of man is corruptible; and yet it weighs down not the soul of the ass, but of man; for of man Scripture says: *The corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^82]. So then, in disordered desire too acknowledge both the nature of the beast and the punishment of man, if you do not have the soul of an ass.

**IV. 57.** *Julian.* Christ, then, no less a true man than a true God, had nothing less of natural things; but it was just that he who gave an example of perfection should excel in all the pursuits of the virtues, and his chastity, lofty with continuous integrity, moved by no appetite of disordered desire—a virgin of holy mind, and a greatness of soul that was the tamer of all the senses, and the conqueror of pains—should be made, to all the faithful, both imitable in its humanity and admirable in its sublimity.

*Augustine.* You say the chastity of Christ was lofty with continuous integrity; but you are a man to whom chastity does not seem integral where, by the greatness and perfection of a good will, unlawful things are not only not committed, but not even desired. For one who desires evils, even if, resisting his own concupiscence, he does not perpetrate them, fulfills indeed what is written: *Go not after your concupiscences*[^83]; but does not fulfill what the law says: *You shall not covet*[^84]. Christ, then, who fulfilled the law most perfectly, desired no unlawful things; because the discord of flesh and spirit, which turned into the nature of human beings from the transgression of the first man, he utterly did not have, who was born of the Spirit and of a Virgin, not through the concupiscence of the flesh. But in us the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit for unlawful things, so much so that it would altogether accomplish them, unless the spirit too had desires opposed to the flesh so as to conquer. You say that the mind of Christ was the tamer of all the senses; but that must be tamed which resists; the flesh of Christ, however, had nothing untamed, nor did it in anything resist the spirit, so that he should need to tame it. By which proposed example of perfection, to this every imitator ought to stretch himself: that he strive and desire utterly not to have the concupiscences of the flesh, which the Apostle forbids to be accomplished[^85]; for thus he can by daily progress lessen those which he would have none of by a perfected salvation.

**IV. 58.** *Julian.* You, therefore, as in all things, have spoken most profanely, that in the flesh of Christ there was not what belongs to human nature; which surely you drew from no vein of reasonable Scripture, but deceived, from the mud of the Manicheans alone. But, that a greater abundance of defending the truth may appear, let us grant that you dreamed that in Christ there was not the concupiscence of the flesh which you speak of—which surely was condemned first in the fury of the Manichean, afterward of Apollinaris; yet how could it support your teaching, since it would not at once follow that a thing is evil, if he had been unwilling to assume something? Since, with the merits of the good increasing by degrees, he would be said to have gone through the best things, and not for that reason to have condemned good things placed below himself by his choice of the higher. For just as he did not defame marriage by following integrity, so neither would he have condemned the sense of genital flesh, if he had been unwilling to have its possibility even in his own substance.

*Augustine.* I have already said above that Christ not only did not perpetrate, but did not even desire unlawful things, so that he fulfilled the law that says: *You shall not covet*[^86]. This, surely, flows into the hearts of the faithful from the vein of holy Scripture, not from the mud of the Manicheans—the thing which your heretical teaching extinguishes. You say I dreamed that there was not in Christ a concupiscence of the flesh resisting the spirit; you spared not even its dreams. For we know that Christ slept; in whom, if this thing you have taken up was present, surely it sometimes deluded his lulled senses through such dreams, so that he too seemed to himself to lie together; and so his flesh, stirred by this good of yours goading it, would both stretch out the genitals in vain and pour forth seed in vain. But if you tremble to believe this of the flesh of Christ (for you are not so stony as not to tremble at what I, though to refute you, nonetheless did not say without trembling of heart), surely you ought to confess that not only was such concupiscence of the flesh lacking to the nature of Christ without loss, but even with the praise of perfect virtues—such concupiscence as we know is not lacking to the flesh of the rest of human beings, and even of the saints themselves. But what you say—that it does not follow that this is evil, even if you grant that Christ was unwilling to take it on; just as he did not for that reason condemn marriage, because he was unwilling to have a wife—this can be said of the disordered desire of beasts, to which for this reason it is not an evil, because they do not have the good of reason, whence their flesh does not have desires opposed to the spirit; but what resists the spirit of a man wishing to do good, with however great loquacity it be defended by you, is not good. So then Christ abstained from sin, so as to abstain even from all disordered desire of sin; not so as to resist it existing, but so that it should utterly never exist; not that he could not have it if he willed, but he would not rightly will it—a thing which the flesh of sin, which he did not bear, did not compel him to have even against his will. Accordingly that perfect man, born not through concupiscence, which indifferently desires both unlawful and lawful things, but of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, whatever he desired was lawful; whatever was not lawful, he did not desire. For, born of flesh with the Holy Spirit conceiving, far be it that he should have in himself the discord of flesh and spirit.

**IV. 59.** *Julian.* And, that a plain matter may be confirmed by some example: it is better to have reason than not to have it; but human beings were made rational, the cattle irrational; yet not for that reason, because human nature stands before the four-footed beasts, are the four-footed beasts to be said to be either some evil or the work of the devil. Grant, then, that since Christ, when he formed his members, was unwilling to mingle into them the sense of the genitals, which he was not going to use; did he therefore, when he fashioned the members of Isaac, Jacob, and of all, and gave them both sex and sense, make something evil? Or, coming to these things, did he summon the devil to his aid, to mingle into the members which he himself had compacted the necessary delights? You have therefore been able to obtain absolutely nothing at all, even slight (as we have shown both in this and in the earlier work, with God helping), through the person of Christ against the divine works. For it became clear that the body of the Savior had nothing less of the nature of human beings.

*Augustine.* Nay, it became clear that human nature now has all these things less than that integrity, uprightness, health in which it was first founded. This Christ came to restore, to correct, and to heal—whole without any corruption, upright without any depravity, sound without any disordered desire of sin.

**IV. 60.** *Julian.* And through this, in that nature there cannot be sin, since in him, in whom it is whole, no iniquity was found.

*Augustine.* Monstrously, Julian, you blaspheme, equating the flesh of Christ with the flesh of the rest of human beings; and not seeing that he came not in the flesh of sin, but in the likeness of the flesh of sin[^87]—which would in no way be true, unless the flesh of the rest were flesh of sin.

**IV. 61.** *Julian.* The disordered desire of the sexes, then, whether it was in the flesh of Christ or was not, is not taught to be evil and diabolical.

*Augustine.* The concupiscence of the flesh is evil even when consent is not given to it for evil; for it is itself that by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit; even if, with the spirit having desires opposed to it, it does not accomplish what it attempts, that is, the evil work.

**IV. 62.** *Julian.* I hasten to other matters, but I am much fixed here in wonder at the matter. What so great a fury is there against another, that you neither understand the Scriptures nor weigh your own words? But you argue ever in such a way that whatever you have hurled recurs upon you with a greater impetus. You said, surely, that there is no cause of shame except the concupiscence of the flesh appearing in the movements of the genitals.

*Augustine.* I did not say this. For there are also other causes of shame, whether lest something be done that is unbecoming, or because it has been done. But of this shame of which we now treat, when the cause is sought, that most true one is found, by which it came about that these members were properly called shameful, which before were not shameful, when those upright and perfect human beings were naked, and it did not shame them. But if you had been willing to think prudently, you would not yourself have shamelessly resisted a most manifest truth.

**IV. 63.** *Julian.* And that it is sacrilege to judge anything shameful which is believed to have been made by God, except this concupiscence of the flesh; but that Christ did not have this concupiscence of the flesh, which makes for mortals a cause of shame. What, then, would meet these things, you did not see: namely, that he—that is, Christ—naked ought to have mingled in the gatherings, and to have no regard for modesty, lest he incur the sacrilege which you contrive—being ashamed, namely, in his own flesh, which had no concupiscence, of his own and his Father's works. But if there was in him both no concupiscence, and yet he discharged the office of shame, it is irrefutably approved, even according to you, that the modesty is owed to the human body, not to the heat.

*Augustine.* By this reasoning of yours, so sharp and exquisite, it ought to be denied that the baptism of John was given for the penance of sins, because Christ was baptized by it, who had no sin at all. But if he could be baptized by another cause, not that by which the rest are—that is, not on account of the flesh of sin, which he did not have, but on account of the likeness of the flesh of sin, which he had taken on for the flesh of sin to be set free—he could also cover his members, not by that cause by which the rest do, but so that he might thus accord with those covering the shameful parts, having nothing shameful; just as, baptized, he accorded with the penitent, washing off nothing to be repented of. For the things of which the flesh of sin had need befitted the likeness of the flesh of sin. Although the very appearance of the naked human body, wherever it is unusual, offends the human gaze. Whence even the angels who, like men, appeared to men, willed to appear clothed, as human custom demanded. But if we recall whence this custom took its origin, the cause of the first sin presents itself, in those human beings who, before they sinned, were decently and honorably naked in a place of so great blessedness—not yet, namely, confounded by their flesh, which was rendered disobedient to the disobedient, having desires opposed to the spirit. Nothing, then, does the garment of Christ help you, so that you may not be a shameless defender of the concupiscence of the flesh.

**IV. 64.** *Julian.* You see, then, that you have called it sin in vain, if in these works which God made we confess that a covering must be applied—a covering which our Lord and the former of man both instituted and, made man, practiced. Let the reverence of the Redeemer grant pardon, since, for asserting the truth of the mystery and destroying the disgraces of the Manicheans, we speak boldly of his flesh; which, did not faith demand it, modest reverence would not touch.

*Augustine.* You do not speak boldly true things of the flesh of Christ, but unhappily false ones; not, as you flatter yourself, for destroying the disgraces of the Manicheans, but rather for aiding them. If you think to conquer the Manichean, do not say that what is evil is good; but say whence is the evil which you cannot show to be good. For while you are unwilling to say with Ambrose that this is from the transgression of the first man, you surely effect that the Manichean boast he speaks the truth about an alien nature.

**IV. 65.** *Julian.* *But this man*, you say, *was unwilling to name the concupiscence of the flesh; he is silent because he is ashamed, and—a thing wondrous, if it can be said, the shamelessness of shame—he is ashamed to name what he is not ashamed to praise*. It displeases you, then, that we know our more shameful parts, according to the Apostle, must be veiled by a necessary honorableness[^88]; because in this we follow the counsel of the Founder, that what he placed in a secret part of the body, we likewise cover with a modest covering.

*Augustine.* You say wondrous things—that Adam and Eve followed the counsel of the Founder then, when they deserted his precept, by following the counsel of the deceiver. For before they had perpetrated this evil, when they were still upright and perfect, were they not following the counsel of the Founder, in leaving naked what he placed in a secret part of the body, and not caring to cover it with a modest covering? Most shameless man, to praise what they felt when they blushed is worse than to lay it bare.

**IV. 66.** *Julian.* These diversities of expressions, however—which you not only do not imitate but even accuse, by whose offices we are aided—the cultivation not of all languages, but of the Greek and Latin, devised. For the other languages, which are called natural, because the pursuits of wealth or refinement have brought to them nothing either of resources or of polish, abuse the members altogether by their simple names; whence even among the Hebrews, in whose speech the purity of the Scriptures is contained, all things are indicated by their proper words.

*Augustine.* You are much deceived—as though in Hebrew speech there were no tropical expression, in which surely the words are not proper but transferred. But however this may be, what does it help you? In the Hebrew sacred Letters surely it is read both when those first human beings were naked, and it did not shame them; and when they were confounded at their nakedness, and what members they covered[^89]; so that we might be able to know what they felt, and whence they were confounded. You, if you wish in this cause to cover yourself, by whom you are confounded, at last be silent.

**IV. 67.** *Julian.* And with the same security with which the feet and knees are named, the genitals too of either sex are named. We, indeed, even when this authority supports us, have not neglected the aids of a more modest speech; because the neglect of decorum, without any compulsion of the business, lies open to a most just censure—a decorum which, no less in words than in works, as far as the reason of the case shall bear, must be retained. Therefore, because natural concupiscence could not be convicted even by the reproach of shame, and was defended by the dignity of its Founder, it was vindicated from the right of the demons for this alone—not as though a great good, since it is common to human beings and cattle, but as a necessary instrument of the sexes—so as to be located among the works of him who made the world and bodies, but accused by none except the Manichean and his heir the Traducianist; it appears that the whole fabrication of natural sin, whose every support clung to the disparagements of nature, has vanished.

*Augustine.* Behold, you still say *natural concupiscence*; behold, as far as you can, you cover the thing you have taken up, lest it can be understood what it is, with the ambiguous garment of words. For why do you not say: The concupiscence of the flesh, but say: *Natural concupiscence*? Is not the concupiscence of blessedness a natural concupiscence? Why do you speak ambiguously? Call by its name the thing you have undertaken to defend; what do you fear? Or, perhaps disturbed in its evil cause, have you forgotten what it is called? Nay rather, with your memory awake, you do not want to name it the concupiscence of the flesh; for you know that those are offended by its praise who have read this name in the holy Scriptures only in the signification of an evil thing. But using this name, and calling it *natural concupiscence*, you try to locate it among the works of him who, as you say and as is true, *made the world and bodies*, while John says it is not from the Father[^90]. God indeed made the world and all bodies whatever; but that the corruptible body weighs down the soul[^91], and the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit[^92], is not the preceding nature of man as established, but the consequent punishment of the condemned. *It is accused by none*, you say, *except the Manichean and his heir the Traducianist*. I rejoice to receive your insults together with those whom you dare to blame in me, and dare not to name. Does he, then, not accuse the thing you have taken up, who says that the concupiscence of the flesh against the spirit turned into our nature from the transgression of the first man[^93]? And who is this? He, namely, whose faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures, as your author Pelagius proclaims, not even an enemy dared to censure. Against this accuser defend your thing you have taken up. Revile this teacher of mine, and your teacher who praised him, on its behalf, as much as you can, that you may show your patronage to be free enough and faithful; lest disordered desire desert you, its defender, as though timid—although it will not find another who would blush less for it.

**IV. 68.** *Julian.* And through this I necessarily asked what the devil recognized as his own in the sexes, by which he might rightly pluck their fruit—he who had neither compacted the flesh, nor formed the members, nor given the genitals, nor distinguished the sex, nor ordained marriage, nor either honored with fruitfulness or sprinkled with pleasure the mingling, without which marriage cannot be.

*Augustine.* None of these things, indeed, did the devil make, but he persuaded disobedience to the human mind, which the penal and shameful disobedience of the flesh would follow; whence original sin would be drawn, through which whoever was born would be made subject to the devil, to perish with the same devil unless he were reborn.

**IV. 69.** *Julian.* And so, you, attempting to go to meet these disputations with the fear of a stag and the cunning of a fox, so imposed upon your patron, to whom you write, that you reported that, on account of the natural sense of the body—that is, the concupiscence of the flesh—the prince of darkness lords it over the works and image of God; when it was necessary that the sense of the flesh should look to the same author to whom the nature of the flesh too looked.

*Augustine.* You do not know what you are saying. The sense of the flesh is one thing, the concupiscence of the flesh another, which is sensed by the sense both of the mind and of the flesh; just as the pain of the flesh is not itself the sense, but unless the sense be present, it cannot be felt. By the sense of the flesh, then, which is called touch, rough and smooth things, like the rest, are differently sensed; but by the concupiscence of the flesh unlawful and lawful things are indifferently desired, which are judged not by desiring but by understanding; nor is one held back from unlawful things, unless the concupiscence be resisted. Evil works, then, are not guarded against, unless the evil concupiscence be reined in—which by you, with a horrible shamelessness, or rather madness, is called good; nor do you blush, nor shudder, that you have come to so great a disgrace, that no one is set free from his own evil, unless he refuses to consent to your good. The concupiscence of the flesh, then, by which the things that are forbidden are desired, is not from the Father. In vain you think, or rather wish it to be thought, that where the apostle John said this, the concupiscence of the flesh was placed for luxury. Surely, if luxury is not from the Father, neither is that from the Father which, if consented to, conceives and brings forth luxury. For by its own movements, which we are commanded to resist, what, I ask, does it attempt, but to arrive at luxury? How, then, is it good, which attempts to arrive at evil? How is it our good, which attempts to arrive at evil? How is it our good, which compels us to evil? This evil, then, Julian, must be healed by the divine goodness; not praised by human vanity, iniquity, impiety.

**IV. 70.** *Julian.* It appears, then, that you have not striven to refute the objections, but pitiably to mock your patron, to whom you were writing; and that he might think you had brought forward something crude which you rightly bit at, when you had already praised it both by the foregoing concession and by the institution of bodies.

*Augustine.* I praised the institution of bodies, which is good even in an evil man, not the evil, without which no one is born; which, while you are unwilling to say with Ambrose whence it is, you aid the Manichean who says that it is from an alien nature.

**IV. 71.** *Julian.* Yet I confess that you have thought too much about what you said; and your wit appears, and your zeal; most skillfully indeed you weighed what must be asserted on behalf of the traduction of sin; no other could have written more elegantly on behalf of natural evil; which indeed not even you yourself could have accomplished so urbanely, had not your wit been aided by the contrivances of your old master.

*Augustine.* I glory that my master, both against you and against the Manichean, is plainly Jesus, whom I confess (which you deny) to be the Jesus even of the little ones; because *Adam perished, and in him all perished*[^94]; nor are they saved from perdition except by him who *came to seek what was lost*[^95].

**IV. 72.** *Julian.* You understood, surely, that to the little ones, deserving nothing of their own, a crime could in no way be fastened without the condemnation of bodies; and therefore that there was need of the help of the Manichean, who would exclude the concupiscence of the flesh from the works of God, and reckon both marriages and the bodies themselves to the devil as author.

*Augustine.* I have no need of the help of the Manichean, but rather, fighting against him, I storm with the help of God the help which you furnish him. Which God furnishes me also through his most illustrious companions, not only the Prophets and Apostles, whose words, perverse, you try to pervert; but even the later teachers of his Church—Irenaeus, Cyprian, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Basil, John, and very many others, most whole in faith, most keen in wit, most rich in teaching, most renowned in fame; who all, with the praise of bodies and of marriages preserved, confessed original sin, knowing that Christ—which you impiously deny—is the Jesus even of the little ones. For he himself saves his people, among whom those little ones too are, not from fevers and other pests and mishaps of this kind, which he bestows even on non-Christians by an abundant goodness over all flesh; but, as he saves Christians, from their sins[^96]. These so many and so great sons and fathers of the Catholic Church, who taught this when placed on its summit, which they learned at its breast, you do not shrink to call Manicheans; and while you seem openly to attack me alone, them the more obliquely, the more insidiously; and the more insidiously, the more wickedly you slander. In this so great crime your own words altogether convict you. For you slander me by no other so nefarious a name except because of original sin I say what they say.

**IV. 73.** *Julian.* Therefore, that you are poured out from the truth, that you are put to flight, the deformity of the cause you defend has caused. For the rest, if you were willing to cross over to the Catholics, how much more elegantly and fully would you yourself pursue that which we assert!

*Augustine.* O brow full of tongue, and mind blinded! Are they not, then, Catholics to whom I cling in the fellowship of this faith, which I defend against your empty words and curses? Are they not Catholics—to be silent about others and to repeat the same ones—Irenaeus, Cyprian, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Basil, John of Constantinople; and are Pelagius, Caelestius, Julian Catholics? Dare to say these things, if you can. But if you do not dare, why do you not rather return to these, from whom I do not withdraw? Why do you admonish me to cross over to the Catholics? Behold the Catholic lights, open your eyes; you cross over to these, against whom you slander me; and at once you will check yourself and grow dumb against me.

**IV. 74.** *Julian.* Although I would not dare to pronounce you endowed with a sharp and vigilant wit, whose judgment in the choice of the business to be taken up I see to be so slow and deformed.

*Augustine.* Say whence those of slow wit are born: for Adam was not made such; you who do not want human nature to have been changed for the worse through his sin, and that this hampers those who are born, unto all the vices they suffer.

**IV. 75.** *Julian.* For if, with faith most unimpaired, in the scholastic manner, only to show the powers of your erudition, you were attempting to shake things that are unconquered; yet, with the sport of the contest finished, you were about to give the palm to those whom you opposed; we would approve the zeal for letters, but chastise the irreligious example. But since you pursue—with a most foul obstinacy—a teaching not even tinged at least with a corrupted color of verisimilitude, which by no testimonies of the law (since these never prejudge reason) can you defend, and which moreover, horrible in its impiety, lying in its folly, pierced through everywhere—except amid the groins of wills—by honorableness, by disputation, and by the sacred law: it follows from three things, one—that you be judged to suffer either a leaden edge of understanding, or the chains of that sorcery by which we have heard tell men are held in the mysteries of the Manicheans, or both at once.

*Augustine.* However much, under the name of reason, you may seem to fight against the divine testimonies which we bring forth to you, you do not overthrow them; but they vex you, when you kick against the goad. Say, as much as you can, amid the groins of wills, to him who tells you that no one conceived by the mingling of both sexes is free of fault[^97]. This is Ambrose, Julian; he himself conquers you, whom you do not dare to deny is a Catholic, whom you will certainly never call a Manichean. Nay rather, you affirm that I am either of a leaden edge for understanding, or held by the sorcery of the Manichean, or suffer both at once. Concerning the sorcery of the Manichean, or your reviling, we have already often answered you, and, more opportunely, when it shall seem necessary, we will perhaps answer still. Now, concerning the leaden edge for understanding, you wordy heretic, answer. There is certainly no one who would not wish, if it were in a man's power, to be born lively in wit and most sharp for understanding; and how rare these are, who does not know? Who, however, even those most rare—if they be compared to the wit of the man who was first made—are judged leaden. For not then, as now, did the corruptible body weigh down the soul[^98]. For either it was not corruptible, because Adam was not going to die unless he had sinned; or if, as you new heretics say, even if he had not sinned, he would have been going to die; yet he was not so established—he whom God had first made, and who had as yet done nothing evil—that his soul should be weighed down by the body. For who would deny this to be a punishment, except one who is more weighed down than the rest? If, then, the Manichean should ask whence this evil of slowness is—not in bodies, but in those very human wits, where is the image of God—which slowness has come by degrees even to a foolishness to be laughed at, or rather, as Scripture admonishes, to be lamented[^99]; we answer that both these and all other evils, with which we cannot deny or doubt that human beings are born, must be attributed to the sins of the first two and then of the other parents; since they cannot be attributed to the will of those who are born. For other animals—that some are born with the defects of their kind—what wonder if noxious spirits receive these into their power, as we know they received the swine[^100]? Which malignant spirits can, just as men can corrupt the members of irrational animals, so can corrupt even their seeds. But the question is about human beings, in whom the image of God would never suffer the punishments of the various vices with which we see them born—which it could not suffer unjust, unless the sins of those generating preceded; which you, in denying, both desert the Catholic faith and aid the nefarious teaching of the Manichean as much as he did not dare to wish—so that it may seem to him certain and secure that the maker of human beings does not confess the true God, but introduces a race of darkness.

**IV. 76.** *Julian.* We, indeed, have nothing that we should believe admirable in our wit, because we understand that there can exist neither sin without will, nor bodies without God, nor the senses of bodies without bodies, nor marriages without mingling, nor those who are born without God as the worker; and we hold it beyond doubt that neither is that divine which is taught to be unjust, nor can what is divine be taught to be unjust. And no less than the things we have said does it shine forth to be iniquitous, if the sins of some be imputed to others, who were not even there, much less consented. Accordingly, illumined by these suns, we by best right despise the caverns of the Manicheans, who think either that there can be sin without will, or that human beings are not made by God, or that the sense of bodies belongs to one author and bodies to another, or that there is a God who is oppressed by charges of iniquities, or that he is pressed by the crime of iniquity who is esteemed the eternal founder of all things, or that justice retains any shadow at all, if the wills of some be applied to the births of others. And through this we impute the merits of deeds to the business, not to the wits.

*Augustine.* I have already answered you on all points: nor are they made firm against me for this reason, that they are so often and so hatefully repeated by you. Say rather, if you can, whence human wits are born with so great viciousness for the most part; when it is agreed between us that the just God is the maker of the whole man, and you deny any original sin? But you would not say that we impute the sins of some to others who were not there when they were committed, if you recalled what is written, that Levi was in the loins of Abraham, when Melchizedek, the priest of God most high, took tithes of that same Abraham[^101]. For there you would see, if obstinacy did not blind you, that the human race was in the loins of Adam, when he perpetrated that great sin.

**IV. 77.** *Julian.* But, to return to the cause: after you said that I *was for this reason unwilling to name concupiscence, because it was not from the Father, but from the world; of which world the devil is called the prince; who did not find it in the Lord; because the Lord, as man, did not come to men through it*[^102]. Where it must be noted that, when you were speaking of a natural thing, and had said it was from the world, you subjoined that the devil must be believed the prince of the whole world; so that you confessed him to be the author not of voluntary acts, but of natural things—of natures, surely.

*Augustine.* Where I put: *the concupiscence of the flesh*, you took away from it: *of the flesh*, which I said; and where I said: *of which world the devil is called the prince*, you added: *the whole*, and said: *of the whole world*, which I did not say. Do as you please, say what pleases you, or even those whom you please. I said the concupiscence of the flesh, which John says is not from the Father, but from the world[^103], that is, from human beings, who are born in the world, to perish unless they are reborn in Christ. This concupiscence of the flesh is not luxury when it is resisted, but when it is accomplished, that is, when it arrives where it impels. Whence also the apostle Paul: *Walk by the Spirit*, he says, *and do not accomplish the desires of the flesh*[^104]. He did not say: Do not have them; for he knew this gift would indeed be ours, but not of the present life. I said the devil is the prince of the world, just as the divine Scripture calls him[^105], not as your vanity suspects or slanders. I did not, then, call the devil the author of natures, but the prince of the world, that is, of human beings, with whom, in the circle of the earth, the world is filled, who are born in the world and are not reborn in Christ. For from those who are reborn in Christ, the prince of the world is cast out; which the mysteries signify, when even the little ones to be baptized are exorcized and exsufflated. To these things answer, if you can. Do not want to make the reader vanish away by your most loquacious vanity, and be turned aside and seduced from the matter at hand. Say, if you dare, that it is good to desire evil. Say that evil works indeed are not from the Father, but the concupiscence of evil works is from the Father. Say that the devil is not called the prince of the world. Say that human beings established in the world are not called the world. Say that by the world in an evil sense cannot be understood unbelieving human beings, with whom the world in the circle of the earth is filled; and again, in a good sense, faithful human beings, with whom, though fewer, yet with these too in the circle of the earth the world is filled, are likewise called by the name of the world—just as it is not absurd that a tree full of fruits be called full also of leaves. Say that the little ones, when they are baptized, are not rescued from the power of darkness, and that, with great injury to God, throughout the whole Catholic Church images of God are exorcized and exsufflated; or say that they are possessed by the devil without any obligation of sin. If you will dare to say these things, you will soon appear; if you will not dare, not even so will you lie hidden.

**IV. 78.** *Julian.* After you uttered this, then, you brought in: *Whence the Lord himself too says: Behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he found nothing*[^106]; and you add of your own: *Nothing, surely, of sin—neither what is drawn by one who is born, nor what is added by one who lives*. Show, then, that the Lord said in the Gospel that he did not have sin which would be drawn by one being born.

*Augustine.* You show that the Lord said he did not have the filth without which Job says there is not even any infant whose life upon earth is of one day[^107]. And yet where he says, *Behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he will find nothing*[^108], we understand not even that filth itself, if we rightly understand. For if, from what he said, that which he did not say is not to be understood—neither did he name the devil, but the prince of the world: *And in me*, he says, *he will find nothing*; he did not say: In me he will not find sin. And yet we say those things which he did not say; but understood from these things which he did say.

**IV. 79.** *Julian.* Why do you circumscribe unhappy souls, that you may lie that what is not has been indicated? The Lord says in the Gospel: *Behold, the prince of this world comes, and in me he found nothing*[^109]. It is certain that the devil came upon nothing of sin in him; since in every temptation—whether of him hungering, or of him afterward preaching, in which he had brought as it were his own persecutors—he was overcome. The Savior, then, professes that the devil discovered nothing of sin in him. In whom surely he would have found it, if anything were drawn from the condition of the flesh; since he too had been made of a woman, of the seed of David and born of the stock of Adam.

*Augustine.* But the Virgin had not conceived him through the concupiscence of the flesh; and therefore into him, without the propagation of the sin of the flesh, the propagation of the flesh passed, so that there was in him not the flesh of sin, but the likeness of the flesh of sin, about to save the flesh of sin[^110]. Accordingly Adam, before he sinned, had neither the flesh of sin nor the likeness of the flesh of sin; because he was not going to die, unless he sinned; but after he sinned, the flesh of sin now begot the flesh of sin, because through that concupiscence of the flesh he begot it which before sin was either none at all in him, or did not resist the spirit, on account of which it shamed him in nothing to be naked. But Christ, who was not born through that concupiscence of the flesh, was born without the sin which all flesh of sin draws, on account of which without doubt all die—he himself not having the flesh of sin; but nonetheless he too died on account of the likeness of the flesh of sin. For if he did not even die, he would not only not have the flesh of sin, as he did not have it, but neither would he show its likeness, which he had taken on for our salvation. You, therefore, who cannot deny that Christ came not in the flesh of sin, but indeed in true flesh, yet in the likeness of the flesh of sin, ought to show the flesh of sin; since, if there is none, surely neither is there any likeness of it. Further, since Christ alone had the likeness of the flesh of sin, which was not the flesh of sin, because he was not born of the mingling of the sexes; what remains, except that there be the flesh of sin of all who are born of such a mingling, pertaining to the world, of which the devil is the prince, from which evil they are not set free, if they are not reborn in Christ?

**IV. 80.** *Julian.* The devil, then, would have found in him a natural crime, if there were any; he would hold his body liable, if he had poisoned it either in his first parent, or in her who bore him; nor would it matter whether, by the conditions of his matter, the intention of the will rose up late and ineffectual; striving against nature, it would not expiate the one born, but exasperate the tyrant; saving that the will could not be free, if the nature had been captive. If, then, there were sin in the very sense and condition of the flesh, if the very nature of human beings pertained to the dominion of the demon, Christ would either be guilty, or not be a man. Therefore, if a curse is ascribed to the nature of humanity, to him too who was made flesh that he might dwell among us[^111], either a crime will be given, or his humanity taken away.

*Augustine.* Neither will a crime be given to him in whom the prince of the world could find no sin; nor will his humanity be taken away, in whom there was both a soul and human flesh; although it was not the flesh of sin, but the likeness of the flesh of sin[^112].

**IV. 81.** *Julian.* Both of which the Manichean did, who, having contrived that there is evil in the flesh naturally, said that there was no flesh in Christ, lest he should confess that there was iniquity in him.

*Augustine.* The Manichean heretic denies the flesh of Christ; the Pelagian heretic wants to equal the flesh of sin to the flesh of Christ; the Catholic Christian distinguishes the likeness of the flesh of sin from the flesh of sin, lest he blaspheme the flesh of Christ.

**IV. 82.** *Julian.* But the Catholic faith ground him to pieces in both: pronouncing, namely, that there is no evil in the flesh naturally, and on account of this that nothing of humanity was lacking to Christ, nor anything of iniquity present. He cries out, then, conscious of his dispensation: *Behold, the prince of this world comes, and he found in me nothing*[^113]; nothing, surely, that he could accuse, because he could not defame a nature whose will he had not bent to sins.

*Augustine.* Nay rather, nothing of sin—neither what is drawn by one being born, because he was conceived in no iniquity; nor what is added by one living, because he was deceived by no temptation; of which two, the one is that on account of which we read, *In iniquities I was conceived*[^114]; the other, that on account of which we say, *Lead us not into temptation*[^115].

**IV. 83.** *Julian.* The very cause too of the devil's tempting Christ was this: that, after the manner of his art, he wished to take him by persuading, since he could vitiate no one by founding him.

*Augustine.* He does not vitiate by founding, who founds no one; but by persuading badly he vitiated the good thing founded which he found. For he is not the author of the nature, which is founded in man by the goodness of God; but of the fault, with which man is born of his parents from the propagation of the vitiated first human beings. But what wonder is it, if he tempted him in whom there was not the flesh of sin—which was not even in those whom, by first tempting, he cast down; through whose concupiscence of the flesh, of which they were confounded, the flesh of sin was propagated, which the likeness of the flesh of sin, having no evil, would heal from this evil?

**IV. 84.** *Julian.* Accordingly the incarnation of Christ protects the work of his divinity, who, bringing to me my nature and his own will—of which he offered me both a mirror and a rule—and pronouncing that the devil found nothing of sin in him, showed that guilt is taken on not from the founding of the flesh, but from the will alone. Finally, just as nowhere in the Scriptures is it read that Christ fled sin, which he knew was drawn by those being born; so too that is most thoroughly taught by a clear testimony—that the righteousness of the man assumed subsisted not from a diversity of nature, but from a voluntary action.

*Augustine.* *Nowhere is it written*—you say—*that Christ fled sin, which he knew was drawn by those being born*; for why should he flee what he himself had not drawn, but had come to save those who would draw it? Why, I say, should he himself flee what is fled by no one, except when there is refuge to him? You also say: *It is most thoroughly taught by a clear testimony that the righteousness of the man assumed subsisted in the Son of God not from a diversity of nature, but from a voluntary action*. Is it so, then, that not even this did Christ have diverse in nature—that he was so born of a Virgin as to be now not only the Son of man, but also the Son of God? Did this assumption, then, which made God and man to be one person, avail nothing to that man toward an excellence of righteousness which you say subsisted in him from a voluntary action? Does the defense of free choice so drive you headlong against the grace of God, that you say even the Mediator himself, that he might be the only Son of God, merited it by his own will, and that what the whole Church confesses is false—that it believes in Jesus Christ, the only Son of God the Father almighty, our Lord, born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary? For according to you, the man was not assumed by the Word of God, that he might be born of the Virgin; but, born of the Virgin, he afterward profited by the vigor of his own will, and brought it about that he was assumed by the Word of God; not having such and so great a will by that assumption, but coming to that assumption by such and so great a will; nor was the Word made flesh in the womb of the Virgin; but afterward by the merit of the man himself, and of his human and voluntary vigor. From which it follows for you also this: that, as you believe him to have been assumed by the Word of God for this reason, because he willed; so you believe that many could likewise have been assumed, if they too had likewise willed, or can be, if they will; and through this, that it is by the sloth of the human will that he is the only one, when more could be, if men willed. If you say these things, where is your brow? If you do not say them, where is your heresy?

**IV. 85.** *Julian.* For the apostle Peter says: *Christ died for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his footsteps; who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth*[^116]. Surely the sentence of the Apostle agrees with the voice of the Lord. He says in the Gospel: *The prince of this world comes, and in me he found nothing*[^117]. This same thing the Master of the Church commends, and says that in Christ there was no offense; but through what he did not have sin he commends by a truthful attestation. *Because*, he says, *he did no sin*. He did not say: Because he took on no sin, but: *Because he did no sin*.

*Augustine.* Surely he who did not draw original sin had no sin, because he did none; just as Adam himself, because he did not have original sin, would have had none, if he had not done any.

**IV. 86.** *Julian.* But if there were a crime in nature, the sentence of Peter was unskilled, who judged, namely, that it could suffice for a testimony of immaculate sanctity if it vindicated his acts from crimes; when it would be believed that sin dwelt in him through a natural poison. Therefore, if he had thought anything of natural evil, he would have recorded this part more anxiously and more closely, so as to write: Christ left us an example, who neither did sin, nor gathered what we draw by being born; and so it would rightly follow that no guile was found in his mouth. Further, if he had thought this, he would never have made mention of an example; for whom would he show to be imitable by human beings, if an alien nature of flesh had distinguished him, and a diversity of substance had argued against the censure of his teaching office?

*Augustine.* How you say nothing, others surely understand, if you do not understand. For the apostle Peter, setting forth in Christ an example to be imitated by human beings, what need was there that he should say anything of original sin—as though anyone, by imitating Christ, could effect that he be born without it, as he was able? Just as he could not effect, by imitating Christ, that he be born, as he was, of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary. Accordingly, that we may imitate Christ, our will is formed; but that we may be set free from original evil, our nature is regenerated.

**IV. 87.** *Julian.* To this was added that the opinion of natural sin would not only consume the force of emulation, but would press the fraud of the very encomium which was rendered to him. For with what gravity, with what brow could it be said that there was no guile in his mouth; when, if he had come in another condition of flesh than ours is, he would be convicted of having done malicious guile not only by teaching, which is lighter, but even by being born, which is graver? For what sort of thing would it be, that he should compel human beings—established in natural evil and in the kingdom of the devil, and sinning under the pestilent necessity of an inborn corruption, or naturally serving the law of sin which reigned in their members—to his own emulation, even under the threat of punishment, and should impute his own righteousness to the men of that flesh, by whose evil he had been so terrified that he himself, who under the appearance of it had wished to express the form of honorableness, nonetheless guarded against the truth of its nature? How much more rightly would the sickness of those who sin and the security of those who are compelled say to him: When we are well, we all give right counsels to the sick; you, if you were so, would feel far, far otherwise? To what, then, did your impiety profit? Namely, that it follows by necessity that, if it is believed that sin is naturally in our flesh, Christ too either took on this flesh, and is held liable to this evil; or did not take it on, and is bound by the inextricable iniquity not, indeed, of birth, which in him seems mendacious, but nonetheless of voluntary fraud. Which, because the whole thing is so squalid with such great filth of blasphemies that it can scarcely be set forth without horror, even when it is torn apart, let the dignity of the Mediator be present, by his example and his work and our faith—which, vindicating the truth as much of himself as of his Apostle by their words, never ceases to preach that Christ, a true man, took on flesh of the same nature as ours from the inmost parts of Mary, a true man through all things, had no sin; for the indicating of which it suffices the apostle Peter to say that he did no sin, teaching that he who had done none could not have it. No guile was found in his mouth; he furnished a holy example to the partners of his nature, which he himself had made in all. And therefore it is established that sin is not inborn, since Christ did not have it, who, even with the honor of his deity preserved, was for this reason made carnal, that he might be imitable by us.

*Augustine.* For what, O man of much speaking and little wisdom, if human beings should say to Christ: Why is it commanded us that we imitate you? Are we, then, born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary? Finally, can there be in us virtue so great as is in you, who are so man that you are also God, and this coeternal with the Father and equal in omnipotence to the Almighty, omnipotent? Ought he not, then, to have been so born, or so assumed into unity of person by the Word of God, that to human beings unwilling to imitate him such an excuse should not be given? But just as he himself set the Father before us to be imitated, who certainly was never a man, and they imitate him without loss or weakening of his divinity, whoever, by his grace, both will and are able, so that they love their enemies, do good to those who hate them[^118]; nor do they say to him: You can do this for the reason that you are God, and because your enemies can do you no harm, but we are weak human beings, and are commanded to love those who heap so many and so great evils on us by their persecutions; so to Christ his imitators do not say: We cannot do those things which you exhort us by your example to do, because your excellence is far more powerful than our weakness. Not, then, ought he, born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, to have had concupiscence by which he would desire evils, even if, by resisting it, he did not do them, lest human beings say to him: Have first evil desires, and conquer them, if you can, that by conquering ours we may be able to imitate you. Then what do you say, Julian, of him who says: *I do not the good I will; but the evil I hate, that I do*[^119]; whom you assert to be pressed, not by the concupiscence with which he was born, but by an evil habit, even to that necessity? Did Christ not furnish to human beings of this kind an example, that they should imitate him? Did he despise them, and will them to be alien from following his virtues? If, then, they should say to him: You do not know what we suffer by the weight of habit by which we are pressed; you are not pressed, therefore you speak; easily we all, when we are well, give right counsels to the sick—would it please you that Christ too should be pressed by such a habit, and overcome it, that he might be, with their excuse removed, imitable by such people? Or will you now laugh at your empty talk, and be silent from us?

**IV. 88.** *Julian.* But, these things being fulfilled as the matter demanded, it helps to confront you even moderately: with what confidence you, while you praise the writings of Jerome, say that there was no sin in Christ, when he, in that *Dialogue* which he composed under the name of *Atticus and Critobulus*, with a wondrous charm—and such as befitted such a faith—strives to show, even by the testimony of a fifth Gospel[^120], which he says was translated by himself, that Christ had not only natural but even voluntary sin, on account of which he knew himself must be washed by the baptism of John. From another testimony too, of John the Evangelist, he sews onto him the disgrace of falsehood. Of which work you boast in that letter which you sent to Alexandria, so as to say that Pelagius, oppressed by the heaps of Scriptures by him, cannot vindicate free choice. But that work was met by a Catholic man who had been assailed. Now, indeed, I have made mention of it only for this purpose: that you might recognize yourself to be in harmony not only not with the holy Scriptures, but not even with the very helpers of your teaching.

*Augustine.* If you had set down the words of Jerome, perhaps I would show how they ought to be taken without the blasphemy which you try to fasten upon him; and if I could not, I would nonetheless not think his faith—which he held with other most illustrious teachers of the Catholic Church—should for that reason be repudiated, because he would be found to have said something, if indeed it were found, in which he did not harmonize with them. But this is enough for me against you from the person of this man: that, since he holds of original sin what you so turn away from that you call it Manichean, you nonetheless do not dare to call him this. Whence it appears that you are deceived by imprudence, but slander me against your prudence. I, indeed, set against you not a sentence of Jerome, but of Ambrose; not one recorded in my words, but expressed in his own[^121], by which he says that Christ could not otherwise be free of fault, unless he were free of that conception which the mingling of man and woman works. Where you discern it to be consequent that, if I am for the sake of this sentence a Manichean, Ambrose too is one; but since he is not, this sentence makes or shows no one a Manichean. When you see this (for it is not such that you cannot see it, or can not see it), you are nonetheless so deserted by the goodness of your cause that you slander me with the name of Manichean, not by an imprudent ignorance, but by a shameless deceit.

**IV. 89.** *Julian.* For you desert the Manicheans in the disparagement of swine and goats, whom you accompany in the disparagement of human nature; with whom too, in Christ, you overthrow not the appearance of flesh, but the reasoning of an example. You remove also from Christ, in words at least, natural sin, lest you should seem to place even him under the power of the devil—which not even the Manichean himself did. But you praise Jerome, who did not so fear to blaspheme him as to say that he entered into familiarity even with voluntary crimes. Thus, rolled without deflection amid the diverse filths and fictions of your friends, you are made savage only against the injuries of the Catholics, because they say that God is not the author of evil, that human beings who are made by him are not naturally evil, that the laws of God are just, that his image can decline from evil and do good; that Christ perpetrated no sin, neither in his members, nor in his precepts, nor in his judgments. And therefore, if you sicken at the assertion of truth, we trust nonetheless that the prudent are instructed, and that some even of those whom your lies have wounded can be healed.

*Augustine.* Concerning the example of Christ I have answered you above: that we ought neither to deny his excellence, by which, although a most whole man, yet born of the Spirit, and conceived with no flesh having desires, he led here the most righteous life above all human beings; nor, on account of this excellence, to excuse ourselves, so as not to study to imitate him according to our measure. For faithful spouses do not imitate his celibacy; and yet, that they may not perpetrate adulteries or any unlawful unions, they imitate him; nor, if they imitate him more who lead the celibate life holily, can they for that reason imitate him in this too, that he not only did not do, but did not even desire unlawful things. But to a holy celibate life every union is unlawful, to which not even the conjugal is lawful. What wonder, then, if, born of the Spirit and of a Virgin, he had in himself no evil? But who, except those pressed down by graver evils, would deny it to be an evil, against which even the saints daily pray to the Father from the teaching of the Lord himself? For when we say: *Bring us not into temptation*[^122], we pray to God against our own concupiscence. *For each one is tempted*, as it is written, *drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence*[^123]. Let him, then, whom we ask, the Father, turn away from us this, that we should dare to say that what is not from the Father, to which the concupiscence of the flesh draws us, is from the Father—that very thing which draws; or that the evil to which it draws is evil, and that the thing itself which draws is not evil. Further, if truth cries out that it is evil, surely he who was born without any evil did not have it, and through this, just as he did no sin, so he desired none. We, therefore, imitate him, when we do no sin—not by not having the concupiscence of sin, but by not consenting to it; although we so imitate that Holy of holies, when we live well, that there nonetheless is not lacking whence we should say in our prayer: *Forgive us our debts*[^124]. But I did not praise Jerome in the way Pelagius praised Ambrose, that his faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures not even an enemy dared to censure. And through this, if anything displeases me, I censure it in the writings of a friend, as in my own. But it is one thing somewhere for a Catholic to err, another to found or hold even the heresy of a great error.

**IV. 90.** *Julian.* But let these things suffice from here: let us now come to that in which a great part of the business is engaged; by whose power the legate of the Manicheans, examined and unstrung, brought forth proofs as much of a faithful response as of an elegant wit. For after he praised and approved my words, which I set down above, with no new thing intervening, he came to that part in which the disputation promised by us was being supplied; for when I asked through what they were under the devil, whom God made; to which I answered in his name: *Through sin, not through nature*; and reported of my own: *But, as you yourself grant, just as there cannot be offspring without the sexes, so neither can there be sin without the will. Therefore the little ones have, at the time of their conception, a will—they who as yet have no soul; or they have, at the time of their birth—they who as yet have no use of reason*. When, then, he had come to this place, this alone of our objections he set forth, that is: *But just as there cannot be offspring without the sexes, so neither can there be sin without the will*; and he answered: *So indeed, so it is; for thus "through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned"*[^125]. *Through the evil will of that one man all sinned in him, when they all were that one man, from whom for that reason individuals drew original sin*. I exhort the reader, then, to inspect our conflict vigilantly. What did it profit you, most learned of bipeds, not to supply my sentence; since, even if in that part which you suppressed the explanation of the prior saying was held, yet even in that which you objected to yourself the whole force likewise stands? For I set forth faithfully—as your assent too manifested—what is wont to be reported by you, and, asking through what the little ones, whom God had made, were under the devil, I answered in your stead: *Through sin, not through nature*. Certainly, you see that I committed no fraud here. I said what is in the mouth of the Traducianist, though this is not in his teaching. To which I reported: *But just as there cannot be offspring without the sexes, so neither can there be sin without the will*. You made this divided by a shameless fraud; for, recalling what we had said—that just as there would be no offspring without the sexes, so neither sin without the will—that which followed about the will of the little ones you kept silent, and you answer: *So indeed, so it is; for thus "through one man sin entered into the world"*[^126], *through the evil will of that one man all sinned in him*. Is this to answer? Is this to dispute? Is this, finally, to have any regard even for sobriety? The cunning of sophisms is laughed at by the learned, where, through the community of names, the simplicity of the respondent is mocked; but those very sophisms, although they are not solid in truth, are nonetheless colored with an urbane surface; but this, which is a monster of disputation, is neither solid in truth nor polished by a sophism. I said, surely, that there cannot be sin without a free will; which being granted, your teaching is consumed, you who think there is in the nature of human beings a fault without any will of the guilty.

*Augustine.* I had read only that of that work of yours which the paper sent to me contained. For after I found your books, from which someone or other excerpted that, I answered all things. But we too say that there cannot be sin without a free will; nor for that reason, however, as you say, is our teaching consumed, when we assert that there is original sin; because to this kind of sin too one has come by a free will—not the proper will of him who is born, but of him in whom all originally were, when by an evil will he vitiated the common nature. The little ones, then, do not have, at the time of their conception or birth, a will to sin; but he, at the time of his transgression, committed by a free will that great sin, from which the contagion of original sin human nature so contracted that a holy man could most truly say: *In iniquities I was conceived*[^127]; and another holy man likewise: *For who is clean from filth? Not even the infant whose life is of one day upon earth*[^128]. These utterances of truth deride the vanity of your loquacity.

**IV. 91.** *Julian.* With what shamelessness, then, do you both approve my sentence and not desert your teaching; when the sentences of each part can so be coupled by no compact that the opposites of the propositions do not equally not share in the truth? Just as, then, if I were to consent that sin is natural, it would be necessary that I fall away from the right of the sentence which prescribes that there cannot be sin except in a free will, so you, on the other side, consenting that I said well, who pronounced that there is no sin without a will, ought at once to reject that opinion by which you believed sin to be natural.

*Augustine.* It is one thing that there cannot be sin without a free will, which we too say, because original sin too could not have been without the free will of the first man; and another, as you yourself said: *that there cannot be sin except in a free will*, which we do not grant. For original sin is not in the will of the one being born, nor is it even in the will of the first man, although it could not be without it. It is one thing, then: Sin cannot be without a will; and another: Sin cannot be except in a will. For if we rightly say: Birth cannot be without conception; it is not therefore rightly said: Birth cannot be except in conception. But this is so diverse that neither in conception can there be birth, nor in birth can there be conception. But sin can be in a will, as was that of the first man; it can also be not in a will, as the original sin of anyone being born, which is altogether in the will of no one; but not without the will of that first one. That holy man too who said to God: *You have sealed up my sins in a bag, and you have noted whatever I admitted against my will*[^129], surely did not have in his will the sin which he committed against his will. What of him who says: *I do not the good I will*, and at once subjoins, but *the evil I do not will, this I do*[^130]? Must he be said, according to your very selves, to have sin in his will—you who want him to be understood to be compelled to sin, unwilling, by the force of habit? Cease, then, to creep up from the neighborhood, and bit by bit to transgress the boundary, so that, because we say without a free will, you affirm us to say, except in a free will, sin cannot be; just as if we said, coals cannot be without fire; and you affirmed us to say, coals cannot be except in fire. If you did not know these things, confess that you were not an understanding disputant; but if you did know them, that you hoped to have an unintelligent reader.

**IV. 92.** *Julian.* For whatever is natural, it is manifest, is not voluntary.

*Augustine.* If whatever is natural, it is manifest, is not voluntary; then it is not natural that we will to be saved, that we will to be blessed. Who would dare to say this, except you? Or, when admonished, perhaps not even you.

**IV. 93.** *Julian.* If, then, sin is natural, it is not voluntary; if it is voluntary, it is not inborn. These two definitions are as contrary to each other as necessity and will are contrary, whose confirmation is generated from mutual negation. For just as the voluntary is nothing other than the uncompelled, so the compelled is nothing other than the unwilling. To exist together, then, and as it were to live, these two cannot; because their nature is such that one lives when the other is extinguished.

*Augustine.* Why do you not attend that there is sin even not voluntary—surely in him who says, by whatever cause he says it: *But if I do what I do not will, it is now not I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^131]? Why do you not attend that there is also a necessity by which it is necessary that we will to live blessedly; and, with closed eyes, you so set the one against the other, as though will could not be of necessity, or necessity of will?

**IV. 94.** *Julian.* Since, then, this is so unshaken that it can be shaken by no argumentation, choose which you prefer, and constantly affirm either our sentence or yours; so as to impute the fault either to necessity or to will. But when I say, which you prefer, I do not so admonish as though your opinion still lay hidden—you who learned to bring in the crimes of nature through the Manichean; but for this reason I admonish, that the truth of your disputation may be noted.

*Augustine.* Like it or not, since you often say this, often must you hear it: that he was not a Manichean who said that the dissension of flesh and spirit turned into nature through the transgression of the first man[^132]. Which cause of this evil you deny, while you are not permitted to deny that every man is born with this evil; you bring it about that the Manichean says that the mingling of another nature is in us, and conquers—unless, conquered together with you, he perishes.

**IV. 95.** *Julian.* What, then, you have answered me, let a prudent and careful hearer weigh. We are dealing, surely, with little ones, in whom there is no will of their own mind, concerning whom it is asked through what they are under the devil, whom God made, since you consent that they themselves did nothing evil. You harangued that they belong to the right of the demon on account of sin, not on account of nature. Against this our response rose up: *But just as there cannot be offspring without the sexes, so neither can there be sin without the will*. To which you reported: *So indeed, so it is*; that is, there cannot be sin without the will. But you who had confirmed this, with what shamelessness did you subjoin: *But through one man sin entered into the world, through the will of that one*[^133]?

*Augustine.* Was it because we were dealing with little ones, that therefore what you said truly I ought not to show does not hamper even my sentence—that is, that there cannot be sin without will; since even original sin has no cause except from the will of him from whom the very origin is derived? That, then, although you said it with an adverse intention, I answered was nonetheless not adverse to me, and granted to be true; and what you had not seen—how it was not adverse to me—I showed. For you could have said, according to your intention, that there cannot be sin without the proper will of him whose sin it is; which, if you had said, I would by no means grant; for without the proper will of the one being born, original sin is drawn. But nonetheless you said truly that there cannot be sin without will; because original sin too the will of him effected, who first sinned by that will by which human nature was vitiated; so that whatever of human beings was born through the concupiscence of the flesh, which is veiled by the confusion of the guilty, would not become free from guilt, unless it were reborn through the grace of the Spirit.

**IV. 96.** *Julian.* Were you asked about the works of Adam, or was it inquired whether he had sinned by will? Which indeed will soon be asked against you. So be it, let it delight you to mock others; of what deformity is it that you impose upon yourself? For I cannot persuade myself that you suffer so great a deformity not by fraud but by opinion. In one and the same line you assent that there cannot be sin without will, and you subjoin straightway that the sin which you had not said could remain without a free motion of the mind reigns in all human beings without will.

*Augustine.* Why do you substitute your word as though mine, so that, with the one who hears or reads this not perceiving it, you withdraw my meaning? I did not say that sin cannot remain without will, but cannot be; and how much there is between these, I will open from your words. For where you said: *There cannot be offspring without the sexes*, who would not consent that you said truly? For otherwise offspring does not exist, except from the sexes of male and female. But if you had said: Offspring cannot remain without the sexes; who would grant you this to be true? For offspring remains without the sexes of the parents, without which nonetheless it could not be; nor do they at all make it remain, who made it be. So, then, sin, which cannot be without will, can remain without will. And so the sin too of Adam—since it is itself that which originally remains in his posterity, except in those to whom it is remitted in Christ—surely when it is said to be in them too not without will, is referred to the will of that one, by which it came about that there should be that which, remaining, would be in his posterity too, not by which it came about that there should remain what could already be even without will. But if you say that to be is this, which is to remain; I do not contend about words, but plainly say, as far as pertains to remaining, that every sin can be without will. For who is there who would even will that the sin which he did not do without will should remain? And yet sin remains, with the sinner unwilling, which was committed by the will. It remains, then, until it is remitted; and if it is never remitted, it will remain forever; for it was not mendaciously said in the Gospel: *He will be guilty of an eternal sin*[^134].

**IV. 97.** *Julian.* Then, because much diversity is found in the propositions, you had said: *That there is not sin without will*, and you answered: *but through the will of the one there is sin*. Does the response that followed, brought in through the preposition of the accusative case, harmonize with the prior definition, which is fortified with the preposition of the ablative case? It had been asked whether there could be a crime without will, which had been established could not be; you subjoined that through one man sin entered; when it did not matter through what it began, but without what it could not be.

*Augustine.* I said that sin cannot be without will in the way that we say that fruits or grain cannot be without roots; where, without any offense to the grammarians, we can also say that fruits or grain cannot be except through a root. Since, then, each is rightly said, although the one is expressed through the preposition of the ablative, the other through that of the accusative case; what is it that you stretch out snares for us by the cases of nouns, as by the webs of spiders—the more subtly, the more weakly? Seek dying flies, which you may catch by these little nets. Not such were those whom we follow, that we should scatter your snares. Not such was the Apostle, who said: *The body indeed is dead because of sin*[^135]. Not such was Hilary, who said: *From sin is all flesh, derived, namely, from the sin of the parent Adam*[^136]. Not such was Ambrose, who said: *We are all born under sin, whose very origin is in defect*[^137]. Would that you rather were caught tenaciously and wholesomely in the nets of these fishermen of Christ; then, corrected, you would better decline the accusative case, by which you were accused by your own self, and the ablative, by which you were carried off from the Catholic Church. But if you follow the prepositions rightly and entirely, why do you not, having laid aside your elation, set these teachers of the Church before yourself?

**IV. 98.** *Julian.* A detestable theft appears, surely, which was indeed straightway punished by the vengeance that clings to sins: namely, that before it advanced into the pest of its hearers, it surrounded and ruined its own author. For behold, it is not denied that the first man incurred some sin; but it is asked how this sin could be found in those who are born. Define what you think the condition of the first sin was; you say: *The will was free, for there cannot be sin without will*; this we approve. But you subjoin: *But this sin, which cannot be without will, clings to those being born without will*.

*Augustine.* It clings through contagion, not through choice.

**IV. 99.** *Julian.* It is false, then, what you had conceded—that there is no sin without will; if, although it was committed through will, it could nonetheless pass over to others without will.

*Augustine.* It is not false what I had conceded, because without the will of him from whom is the origin of those being born, original sin was not made; but it could pass over to others through contagion without will, which could not be committed by that one without will. And through this, it could not be without will, that there should be what would pass over into others without will; just as grain could not be without roots, that there should be what could pass over into other places without roots.

**IV. 100.** *Julian.* There is, then, now sin without will, since it is found in those from whom you snatch away the evil will.

*Augustine.* There is altogether sin without will, that is, it remains. For it would not remain, unless there were surely that which would remain; but that there should be, without will, what would remain, was made only by will; if at least the sin be sin only, not also a punishment; for by this each one sins unwilling. So, then, both are true—both that sin cannot be without will, and that sin can be without will; just as both are true—both that offspring cannot be without the sexes of the parents, and that offspring can be without the sexes of the parents. For the former was said because without these it cannot exist; the latter, because without these it can remain. Well indeed you yourself, in one place, spoke both of sin and of offspring, saying: *Just as there cannot be offspring without the sexes, so neither sin without will*. As, then, we understand that offspring cannot be without the sexes of the parents for this reason, that without these it cannot exist, so as to be; and that it can be without the sexes of the parents for this reason, that without these it can remain, when it now is; why do we not so understand that sin too both cannot be without will, because without it it cannot exist, so as to be; and can be without will, because without it it can remain, when it now is?

**IV. 101.** *Julian.* See, then, to what your sharp points are carried: you try to persuade that a thing is not through that through which it can be.

*Augustine.* For what do you, that great one, even against the grace of God, the defender of free choice? Will you deny that some sin is not through free choice, which can be through free choice? For it comes about that there is sin through free choice, when a man sins if he wills; and it comes about that there is not sin through free choice, when a man does not sin if he does not will. Behold, we find something—and this very thing about which we dispute, that is, sin—not to be through that through which it can be, that is, through free choice. What is it, contentious one? To this are my sharp points carried? Or have your lights here lost their edge? Do not be headlong: it is better to attend to what you say, than to contend that you may contradict.

**IV. 102.** *Julian.* That is, that sin is without will through that through which it cannot be without will.

*Augustine.* Sin is not indeed without will through that through which it cannot be without will, but by diverse causes; nonetheless both are possible; for without will it cannot be, because without will it cannot exist so as to be; but without will it can be, because without will what exists can remain.

**IV. 103.** *Julian.* So that it loses its own condition by its own condition, and is without that without which it was defined that it cannot be. This is to deny things. What such did Anaxagoras, who said that snow is black? You comment that the nature of some thing is denied by its own fruits; and, since necessity and will are contrary, so that, as we showed above, they consume each other by mutual attack, you by a new and impossible compact and a monstrous bond subject one to the effect of the other, and say that necessity arose from the fruits of will, so that the will deleted itself by its own multiplication, and, having worked, changed its own state; and, to express the thing itself by its own words, after the will began to be, the will ceased to be. Than which what can be thought—I will not say more obtuse, but more furious? Since, then, these two cannot be at once, that is, necessity and will, and you have confirmed what we said, that there cannot be a fault without will, and you assent too that there is not will in the little ones; with twisted throat you are compelled to grant that there is no sin in them, which you professed cannot be without will.

*Augustine.* You would not say that necessity and will cannot be at once, if it were given you to know what you say. For since there is a necessity of dying, who would deny that there can be also a will? Whence the Apostle says that he has a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ[^138]. When, then, he wills to die, whom it is necessary to die, necessity and will are at once; which you had denied could come about by an empty will, by no necessity. But that there comes about even from will a necessity often contrary to will, is altogether foolishly denied. For he who, willing to die, strikes himself mortally, dies even if he is unwilling. Likewise he who willingly committed a sin, unwillingly has the sin; willingly unchaste, unwillingly guilty; for the sin remains with him unwilling, which would not come about with him unwilling. And through this, both sin cannot be without will, because it is not done except by will; and sin can be without will, because there remains even without will what was done by will; and there is now a necessity even without will, which will, without necessity, made. For even he who says: *I do not the good I will*[^139], is certainly, according to you, pressed by the necessity of habit; but this necessity—lest you take away free choice—you contend that he made for himself by will; and you do not believe that some such thing was done in human nature, that from the will of the first man, from whom is the origin of the human race, there came about a necessity of original sin in his posterity. Behold, those things which you set forth as impossible have, in the force of habit, been made possible—a force which was not without cause called by some a second nature. You had said that we say something more absurd than he who said that snow is black: *that sin loses its own condition by its own condition, and is without that without which it was defined that it cannot be*. Does it not lose its own condition by its own condition, so as to come about without will by the force of habit, since habit was made only by will? Is not the nature of this thing denied by its own fruits? Since habit is the fruit of will, because it is generated from will; which nonetheless denies that it does, by will, what it does. You say that *necessity and will are contrary, so that they consume each other by mutual attack*; thence accusing us, that *we subject one to the effect of the other, saying that necessity arose from the fruits of will*; when you see that the necessity of habit is the most manifest fruit of will. Is it not, what seemed to you impossible, that *the will deleted itself by its own multiplication, and, having worked, changed its own state*—a will which, multiplied, made the necessity of habit; if necessity, with you as author, consumes will? But if it does not consume it, surely in a man pressed by the weight of habit, both the will of righteousness and the necessity of sin can be at once. Since: *To will is present to me*, is a profession of will; *but to accomplish the good, I do not find*[^140], is a confession of necessity. But you said that will and necessity cannot be at once; when you discern them to be at once when they agree, at once when they fight. That, moreover, is ridiculous, which you set down as impossible, saying: *Nothing*—you say—*can be thought more obtuse, nay, more furious, than that, after the will began to be, the will should cease to be*; as though this did not come about, when a man, when he has begun to will something evil, soon repents and ceases to will. Yet, saying such things, with—as you say—a twisted throat, you *compel me to grant that there is no sin in the little ones*; when you, not even with a twisted throat, break the bond of Catholic truth, by which, unless you consent to it, you are most wretchedly strangled.

**IV. 104.** *Julian.* But what you added: *Through one man sin entered into the world*[^141]—just as it was here shown to be most unfittingly placed, so in the second book it was expounded how it is to be understood. But now, the present disputation being supplied, it pleases me to handle again that most acute sentence; for thus you say: "*Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*"[^142]. *Through the evil will of that one all sinned in him, when they all were that one, from whom for that reason individuals drew original sin*. *Then*, you say, *they sinned, when they all were that one*. But this I had marked that you wrote to Marcellinus; by which testimony you are convicted of believing and embracing a traduction of souls received specifically from the inmost parts of the Manichean (whose sentences I inserted in the third book). Which is surely so deformed that, although you signify it, you nonetheless by no means dare to profess it. But let us for the present defer this, which is throttled both by the obscenity of its prior assertor and by the fear of the following one, that is, yours. But it helps in the present to descry this: in how great a perturbation of disputing you are tossed. For you say: *Through the evil will of that one all sinned in him, when they all were that one*. If all were that one, how through the will of that one did all sin; when all these, whom you say were in him, could have sinned by their own will? Nay, that we may turn it back, that one is more unhappy than all, who alone bears the odium, when all sinned in him according to your teaching. The little ones, then, had a will, not only before they were born, but before their great-grandfathers were generated; and they used the choice of election, before the seeds of their substance were founded. Why, then, do you fear to say that there is in them, at the time of their conceptions, a free will, by which they do not draw sin naturally, but commit it of their own accord; if you believe that they, conceived today, had, so many ages ago, sense, judgment, the efficacy of will? Which indeed in the books you published to the name of Marcellinus you did not hesitate to put—plainly for showing by what madness the enemies of God are held. For there you say thus: *The little ones, who sinned in Adam, that they might be created like him*. Than which what could resound more mendaciously, more madly, and more foully, than that *they sinned before, that they might be created*? That is, by doing they merited that there could be those who would work something; and their action was older than their substance. Which fabrications, fitter for the Orgies and Thyrses than for letters, let it suffice to have indicated. Thence, then, flowed that response, by which you say: *All sinned in him, when that one was all, from whom individuals drew original sin*. For here one need not labor to teach that, since will is the work of a person, there cannot be a will before the person whose will it is; but I wish this above all to be understood: that not even according to this opinion is there original sin. For if all were there who sinned, they drew nothing of original evil, because all perpetrated it by their own pursuits. The traduction of sin, then, is destroyed not only by Catholic truth, but even by all the arguments of its own patron; which indeed the nature of lies has—that it does not preserve the harmony of pretense, but, prodigal of modesty and greedy of another's, is detected in all the things it has usurped.

*Augustine.* That through one man, in whom all sinned, sin entered into the world, the Apostle said, Ambrose understood; but those same apostolic words Julian tries to pervert into his own perverse sense. Why does not Ambrose himself rather answer him? Hear, then, Julian: *All*, he says, *die in Adam; because "through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned"*[^143]; *the fault, then, of that one is the death of all*[^144]. Hear yet another thing: *Adam existed*, he says, *and in him we all existed; Adam perished, and in him all perished*[^145]. Say to him, if you dare, that with one soul sinning by its own will, so many souls, not yet having their own wills, could not perish. Accuse my hesitation about the origin of souls, because I do not dare to teach or affirm what I do not know; bring forth from the so profound obscurity of this matter what pleases you; provided only that this sentence be fixed and unshaken—that the fault of that one is the death of all, and that in him all perished; whence came the last Adam, to seek and to save what was lost[^146]. Say to him: These too, then, sinned by their own will, whom you say perished in him who sinned by his own will. But Ambrose could understand this, which you cannot—that this is said not on account of the choice of individuals, but on account of the origin of the seed, whence all were going to be; according to which origin all were in that one, and these all were that one, who in themselves as yet were no one. According to this seminal origin, even Levi is said to have been in the loins of his father Abraham, when Abraham was tithed by Melchizedek; whence Levi too is shown to have been then tithed, not in himself, but in him in whose loins he was; nor did he will, nor refuse to be tithed; since there was no will of his, when according to his own substance he himself as yet was not; and yet, according to the reasoning of the seed, it was said not mendaciously nor emptily that he was there, and was tithed[^147]. Whence from this tithing of the sons of Abraham, who were in his loins when he gave tithes to the priest Melchizedek, that one priest alone was excepted, to whom it was said: *You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek*[^148]. Who, although he too is according to the flesh the seed of Abraham—since the Virgin Mary, of whom he took flesh, was propagated from that same seed—is nonetheless not liable to the same seed, he who was conceived not from male seed, free from the bond of the disseminating concupiscence. Say no longer to Ambrose, then, as I was saying, but to him who wrote to the Hebrews, and says: *And, so to speak, through Abraham even Levi, who receives tithes, was tithed; for he was still in the loins of his father, when Melchizedek met him*[^149]. Slander this man with your blind loquacity, and say, if you dare: Since father Abraham was tithed by his own will, how could Levi be tithed through his will, that is, give tithes, whose will was none, because he himself as yet was not at all? By that reasoning, surely, or rather by that error, you say to us too: Since the first man sinned by will, how could those sin at once in him through his will, who as yet did not have their own, because through their own substance they were as yet no one? Cease, rather, to chatter empty things, and understand, if you can—that all who were as yet not born could do nothing of good or evil by their own wills, but were able to sin in the one, in whom by the reasoning of the seed they were, when he by his own will sinned that great sin, and vitiated, changed, made liable to guilt human nature in himself—with the exception of the one man who was procreated indeed from that very seed, but not by the seminal reasoning; if you can, understand; if you cannot, believe.

**IV. 105.** *Julian.* But after this response he tries to wash away another point where he had been struck by me. For I, after I had inquired about the will of the little ones, went on: *But you deny this—that is, that a sinning will is in those being born—and yet you say that the little ones are under the devil, nor do you dissemble through what you judge them to pass their life under his power; for you say: Because they are born of the mingling of both sexes, they are under the adverse power*. By the testimony of his own words, then, it appeared that he vindicates the little ones to the demon for this reason, because they had been begotten of the mingling of both sexes; through which I taught that he had made over to the devil marriages, which were instituted by God, which cannot be without mingling.

*Augustine.* This you could by no reasoning teach, although you attempted it with much contention; which those can see who read both your slanders and my refutations.

**IV. 106.** *Julian.* This, then, he now set forth for himself, and he prefaces my question. *You say, then*, he says, *that they are under the devil for this reason, because they are born of the mingling of both sexes?* To which what manner of satisfaction he reports, let us hear. *I say*, he says, *plainly that they are under the devil on account of sin; but that they are not free of fault for this reason, because they are born of that mingling which cannot, even what is honorable, be worked without shameful disordered desire. This too the most blessed Ambrose said*[^150]. O calamitous depravity of the man! O nefarious intention! O falsehood to be blushed at!

*Augustine.* Cry out, cry out as much as you can; add to your cries: O the violence! For you, an innocent man, suffer force, that you should be compelled to call Ambrose a Manichean. Far be it, you say, that I should say it. Why, I ask? Or do you here show how great is the strength of free choice, when you suffer such force that you should say, and yet do not say? Why, then, do you say that I am what you do not say that he is, when he so long before said what I now say, and in this sentence, on account of which you call me a Manichean, the cause is common to me and to him? Or, since you do not find by what to escape, do you feign anger indeed—not, however, angry, but disturbed—and cry out? But in your very cries I hear: *O calamitous depravity of the man!* Because, namely, I am a calamitous and depraved man, who choose to consent to Ambrose; but I would be blessed and upright, if I chose to consent to Julian. I hear: *O nefarious intention!* For our intention is nefarious, because we set Ambrose against Julian; but it would be allowable, if we set Julian before Ambrose. But what is this that I hear, your third cry: *O falsehood to be blushed at!* Do you say that the sentence of Ambrose is false, that is, that he himself thought falsely? Or that we falsely attribute it to him, since he himself did not think this, nor said at all what we say he said? Or that we, not understanding, opine falsely about it, when it itself is true? But you do not speak so contumeliously of Ambrose as to attribute to that man a falsehood to be blushed at. But that we composed it, and feigned that it was uttered by him, you did not dare to say; for the writings of that teacher are known to so many that you feared to hurl yourself headlong into this precipice. So open, indeed, is that sentence that whoever understands it can seem not acute, but superfluous, who thought it must be expounded. Finally, that what I say may be attended to, let me here too set down the very words of the most blessed Catholic bishop. He says, then, he—he whose faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures your Pelagius says not even an enemy dared to censure—when he was speaking of the nativity of the Lord: *And therefore, as man, he was tempted through all things, and in the likeness of men sustained all things; but, as born of the Spirit, he abstained from sin. For every man is a liar, and no one is without sin except one God. It has been observed, then*, he says, *that from man and woman, that is, through that mingling of bodies, no one is seen to be free of fault; but he who is free of fault is free also of this kind of conception*[^151]. Since, then, you do not deny that Ambrose said this, and you perceive that it is plain and open, what is it that you cry out: *O falsehood to be blushed at!* To whom, I ask, to be blushed at? To him, or to me? If to him, see to whom you are contumelious; if to me, see how slanderous you are. But this, you say, you too say. I say it plainly, because it is true; but if you do not think it true, why, in one and the same sentence, which he says, and I say, is he not a Manichean, but I am? How much more justly do we here cry out: O the respecting of persons to be blushed at! Which without doubt would compel you to blush, were not, in your person, your brow like your mouth.

**IV. 107.** *Julian.* That this man should dare to say that he is not a condemner of marriage, and to mock unskilled ears with so great a crime, and that he should say he has departed from the company of the Manichean—he who has, by a manifold profession, established the mingling of the sexes, the work of marriage, the affection and sense of parents in the kingdom of the devil; and, joining to these inventions of the Manichean the aid of his own sharpness, pronounces indeed the mingling to be diabolical, and that it is both in the work and in the flesh of the spouses, and on account of it adjudges them to the kingdom of the devil—the operators nonetheless being absolved, the innocent. Thus, surely, ever hostile to God, he protects those serving the devil, as he says, through disordered desire.

*Augustine.* You err, and you send others who consent to you into error; they do not serve the devil through disordered desire, who use the body of their spouses for the sake of procreating children, that those who must be regenerated may be generated. Nor for that reason, however, is the evil of disordered desire defended; but those are defended who use the evil well. For there is also a good use of evil. For even of Satan himself uses are found in the holy Scriptures—his reproach remaining, surely, but the praise being of one who uses the evil well.

**IV. 108.** *Augustine.* But the little ones, whom he says are made by God, he assigns to the dominion of the enemy, and thus convicts not the work of the demon, whose ministers he absolves from fault, but the work of God, to which the pleasure, conscious of a diabolical service, could not attain. The accuser, then, of the conjugal work, but the expiator of disordered desires, the assailant of innocence, and the defamer of divine equity, did not fear to write: *I say plainly that they are under the devil for this reason, because they are born of that mingling*. Which crime of his sentence, when he saw it laid bare, attempting to defend it by some authority which he could not bring forth from the Scriptures, he subjoined that bishop Ambrose too thought similarly. It is no wonder indeed that he accuses even the dead, when he accuses the innocent.

*Augustine.* Whoever hears these things, what does he think you object to us, except that this sentence of the most blessed Ambrose which we set down is not his, but that we feigned it as though it were his? For I too, when I read these things, supposed you were accomplishing nothing; but after I came to those things you add, and you do not deny that Ambrose said this, I found you rather a horrible accuser of that so great teacher. For whatever you say against me—since I say that, through the virile mingling and the conception of the woman, no one is free of fault—surely you say also against him who said and wrote this before me. But I, when refuting you and resisting you, assert that the little ones, on account of original fault, are under the devil unless they are reborn in Christ, defend from your nefarious accusation not myself alone, surely, but Ambrose too, and others his companions, and partakers and teachers of this faith, and the whole Church of Christ, which attests that it received this, holds this, faithfully believes this, by the exorcism and exsufflation of baptized little ones.

**IV. 109.** *Julian.* How much more rightly would you say: This same thing Mani too said in the letter to Patricius; he said it also in the letter which he wrote to his daughter Menoch, he said it also in many other writings, which you have thoroughly imbibed? But you try to draw the bishop of Milan into your company, and because you cannot have a defense, you wish to find a solace.

*Augustine.* Very much against the Manichean is what Ambrose said. For the Manichean says that an alien nature of evil is mingled into us; but Ambrose says that our own nature is vitiated by the transgression of the first man; while in this sentence of which we now treat, Ambrose, distinguishing the nativity of the flesh of Christ from the flesh of the sin of the rest, defends it, which the Manichean utterly denies. What Ambrose believes, then, this I too; but what the Manichean believes, neither he nor I. What is it that you strive to separate me from Ambrose, and join me to the Manichean? For if to say that original sin, not from the mingling of an alien nature, but from the depravation of our own nature, is drawn by those being born, is the teaching of the Manicheans; this Ambrose says with me; why do you not try to join us both to the Manicheans? But if this is not the teaching of the Manicheans, as it is not: this I say with Ambrose; why do you not deign to disjoin us both from the Manicheans? Why, then—as you say—*do I try to draw the bishop of Milan into my company*; when you in vain try to draw me away from his company? What is it that you say: *That because I cannot have a defense, I wish to find a solace*? To me, with Ambrose, like it or not, in the Catholic faith, Christ is the common defense. For this reason plainly Ambrose is my solace, because with him I receive your insult; nor does he alone in this cause console me most, but Cyprian too, and Hilary, and others like them, whose Catholic faith you lacerate in me. Do not, then, envy that Ambrose, Cyprian, and Hilary are solaces of my injury; for you are compelled to see, unwilling, how much it differs that Pelagius and Caelestius, and any other if there be, are solaces of your condemnation. What of the fact that I show Ambrose warring against the Manicheans on behalf of the Catholic faith, and you in this contest, against Ambrose, furnishing to the Manicheans either a solace if conquered, or—what is worse—an aid even to those resisting? For the Manicheans say that there is a proper substance and nature of evil, coeternal with the good substance and nature of God. For it is impossible, they say, that evils can arise from goods; Ambrose contradicts, and says: *From goods, evils have arisen; for there are no evils, except those that are deprived of goods; yet through evils it came about that goods stood out; therefore the lack of good is the root of malice*[^152]. You, amid these, what do you say? *The reasoning of things does not allow*, you say, *that from a good an evil, and from a just thing something unjust be brought forth*. These words of yours on behalf of the Manicheans against Ambrose, from that famous work in which you wished to answer my one with four books, we have set down. If in this controversy you were judge, surely by your sentence Ambrose would be said to be conquered by the Manicheans. And do you not blush, when you are the slanderer of those whom you most openly accuse, the flatterer of those whom you obliquely accuse alike, the helper of those of whom you accuse others?

**IV. 110.** *Julian.* Do the writings of disputants, then, prejudge the law of God or the work of God?

*Augustine.* Here now you begin to confess that this was not feigned by us as though it were Ambrose's, but that it is truly his sentence; since you so try to free yourself from him as to say: *Do the writings of disputants prejudge the law of God or the work of God?* But go on, say the rest, by which you may be judged a more shameless enemy of the Catholic faith.

**IV. 111.** *Julian.* Meanwhile it abundantly suffices me to prove, even through this very thing, that you have nowhere read in the sacred Letters what you believe—since you, in such a cause, brought forth nothing except the bishop's, as you call them, little sayings; with which, if you had been able to come upon anything more authoritative, you would without doubt have refrained from these.

*Augustine.* Let those who read see whether I have not brought forth divine testimonies; or whether you have vainly attempted to deprave those brought forth by me.

**IV. 112.** *Julian.* But it is well that you first relieved us of the burden of such persons. For in the book which you wove against free choice to Timasius, when holy Pelagius had made mention of the venerable men, both Ambrose and Cyprian, who had commended free choice in their books, you answered that you were burdened by no authority of such men; so that you said that they, by the progress of a better life, had expiated, if they had thought anything badly[^153]. Let your own words be reported back to you for this, that you may blush to stir up odium from names alone. For the rest, either the sayings of Ambrose, or of others whose fame you strive to stain by the company of your own, can be defended by a clear and kindly reasoning.

*Augustine.* That so great a blindness of heart could befall you, who believes, except one who reads these things? You say that, if I had been able to come upon, that is, to find, something more authoritative, I would have refrained from the sayings of disputants, or, as you say, little sayings; and at once you yourself say that Pelagius, whom you call holy, in the defense of free choice adduced the venerable men Cyprian and Ambrose as witnesses; nor do you attend how, by saying this, you refute your master and your very heresy. For according to your sentence, if Pelagius had found anything more authoritative, in the canonical Scriptures, for that which he was defending, he would have refrained from the testimonies of disputants. When would you say these things, unless Ambrose so disturbed you that Pelagius suddenly found an adversary? *But I first relieved you*, as you say, *of the burden of such persons*—of Ambrose, namely, and his companions. Plainly you are so pressed by this burden that you are not only crushed, but even ground to pieces, and become the dust which the wind drives from the face of the earth[^154]. For those so many and so great and so holy and illustrious bishops of God, sons of the Catholic Church by learning, fathers by teaching, did not so speak of the sin of the first man and of his liable succession of mortals, that one differed from another, or any of them from himself; but altogether so that, by their consensus and constancy, holy Scripture is to be understood concerning this matter in no other way, nor any other faith to be thought Catholic—whoever does not read them with a heretical mind cannot doubt. By whose weight you are now yourself so broken that you have undertaken to defend the sayings which you contradict *by a clear and kindly reasoning*. Let us hear, then, the clear and kindly reasoning; by which reasoning of yours, if those sayings are defended, why are your curses heaped upon me from them? For altogether, what you detest and accuse in me are those very things which you defend in their disputation. But if they are not defended, but under the name of defense more cunningly accused; far be it that this be a clear and kindly reasoning; but rather a derisory flattery, which is for this reason called a defense, lest the offense of the Catholic peoples, by whom those same men are venerated, could not be borne.

**IV. 113.** *Julian.* Because, namely, they both said certain things more simply, and, contemplating something else, did not have the necessity of meeting questions arising at their side. For when they often praised marriages, and supposed no affection in bodies inserted by the devil, nor subjected divine works—that is, the members of nature—to the kingdom of the devil, but unfolded both marriages instituted by God and endowed with blessing, and free choice, as opportunity brought it; it is human that they not be judged to stand in the company of your crime, if in their writings you catch certain things placed either ambiguously or negligently. For just as it does not prejudge the holy Scriptures that all heresies conceal themselves under some of their testimonies and strive to defend themselves; so we will not allow the fame of Catholic men to be condemned on account of certain things poured out more incautiously. For they had no intention either of condemning marriages, or of denying free choice, or of wounding innocence; which had they had, they would not have confirmed your teaching, but would have lost the honor of their office.

*Augustine.* O clear and kindly reasoning of defense! Ambrose, namely, said more simply that, from the mingling of male and female, no one is free of fault; and, contemplating something else, and not having the necessity of meeting questions arising at his side, negligently and incautiously poured the Manichean—as you say—poison into his writings and disputations. Most loquacious man, fearing men, you spare a man; you do not defend those sayings. For if these sayings are defended by a true reasoning, without doubt they are truly defended, and are true; and if this is so, original sin is rightly asserted, and your teaching is destroyed. But if these sayings are defended by a false reasoning, that clear and kindly reasoning of yours is not a reasoning, but a deception. But indeed *Ambrose often praised marriages*; this we too do. *No affection*, you say, *did he suppose inserted in bodies by the devil*; if a good affection, neither do we; if an evil one, as he, so we too. *Nor did he subject*, you say, *divine works, that is, the members of nature, to the kingdom of the devil*; as though the members of adulterers are not divine works and members of nature, which nonetheless it is established are by their fault, and through this by the devil, subjected. *They*, you say, *unfolded both marriages instituted by God and endowed with blessing, and free choice, as opportunity brought it*. So we too. *It is human*, you say, *that they not be judged to stand in the company of your crime*; nay, it is insane that we be judged not to stand in the company of theirs by your crime. You say: *It does not prejudge the holy Scriptures that all heresies conceal themselves under some of their testimonies and strive to defend themselves*; and so you wish: *that it not prejudge the Catholic disputants, that we object to you certain things which were, as you suppose, more incautiously poured out by them*. What is this, but to say that even in the holy Scriptures the things which heretics usurp for themselves were more incautiously poured out, and therefore are not true? Than which what more wicked thing can be said? Or if those things are true, but are not understood by the heretics as they are; this comparison has nothing similar; since the sayings either of Ambrose, or of Cyprian, or of other Catholics, which we set against you—if you grant them to be true—you will confirm original sin. Accordingly, just as we with them both praise marriages, and confess free choice, and defend innocence; so you with them say that the little ones are not free of fault; otherwise we are with them, you against them. For you do not defend their sayings, as you had promised, but censure them. For by your teaching, since you are compelled to blame them and to contend that they are false, you are in no way permitted to exhibit, by their authorities, the defense which you had promised; because both in accusing you try to be a flatterer, and in flattering you are caught an accuser.

**IV. 114.** *Julian.* Freely, then, I conjecture and pronounce that, if any of them survived to these days, and saw the glory of Christian discipline extinguished, free will at leisure in all things, and affecting to impute to necessity whatever it commits of its own accord, and the disparagement of the works of God, and the overthrow of his law under the name of an ineffectual grace instilled into the ears of the peoples; he would be moved with all his gall against you; weighing too that natural sin cannot be discerned from the Manichean impiety, he would defend the Catholic faith—with you either corrected or condemned—both more plainly and more cautiously.

*Augustine.* Why, then, with us corrected, and not also with themselves? Where is what you had promised, that you would freely conjecture and pronounce? Behold, neither is your vanity free, and your liberty false; for you feared to say that, if Ambrose survived these days, having heard you, he would correct first himself, then us; but, as though a free man dreading to mutter this, you nonetheless wished it understood. Behold to what these days have come, that in them Ambrose, if he were living here, would learn that he had been a Manichean, and, having heard Julian, or Caelestius, or Pelagius himself, ought to be one no longer, but to be healed of that nefarious pestilence with you commanding and curing. What a spectacle presents itself to the mind of one thinking? What it would be, to see Ambrose standing, or, if he permitted it, sitting before Pelagius, and learning a new paradise full of the calamities of this age which we see the little ones suffer, where it would be necessary, even if no one had sinned, that the flesh have desires opposed to the spirit; and, lest it drag to unlawful and disgraceful things, that the spirit too have desires opposed to the flesh; which dissension Ambrose was wont to say turned into the nature of human beings through the transgression of the first man[^155]; but, under you now as masters, would not dare to say. It would be necessary too in such a paradise that the pregnant grow pale, endure long loathings, that those giving birth groan and howl; while human beings are born with diverse vices of wits and bodies, and a few of sound heart learn their letters with a lesser, yet not without, labor, the rest somewhat slow or slow, and the slower each one the more, are either cut up by the rods of teachers, or remain unlearned and unskilled; but the foolish are not even given to masters, but are reared to be grieved over or laughed at; while infants, before they could will or do anything evil, are agitated by diseases, tortured by pains, treated with torturing medicaments, vexed by demons, expire under overcoming disasters. But if Ambrose, abhorring this, were unwilling to believe it, and answered that all these things, if no one had sinned, would in no way have been going to be in the place of so great blessedness, where not even after sin could they be, those being cast out thence by whose transgression these evils followed; and through this that they come from the misery of mortals, which would be none, unless human nature, vitiated and changed by the gravest sin of the first man, had merited to propagate this age full of so many and so great calamities; where not even the redeemed, having now received the pledge of everlasting salvation, lack such evils; but when they have departed from this age, then they will lack them: if, then, Ambrose answered such things, by your splendid syllogisms he would be forbidden to say these things—lest, namely, the concupiscence of the flesh being disparaged, and original sin being believed, marriages be condemned, free choice be taken away, the works of God be disparaged, and under the name of grace the overthrow of the law follow. Not so, plainly, not so. Blush, or rather be aghast, to think such things. Nay rather, if that man survived, much more vehemently than we and more authoritatively would he resist you on behalf of the Catholic faith and on behalf of the grace or equity of God, showing how the things you think consequent are not consequent; because both one can live rightly, with evil concupiscence not driven out nor praised, but reined in; nor is the Founder of nature blamed, when that is shown by him to be healed which could be vitiated by the enemy, not created; nor are marriages condemned, which use shameful disordered desire well; nor is free choice taken away, but it is shown by whose benefit it is free in good; nor is the law made void through grace, but is fulfilled. These things that excellent teacher would dispute excellently, and would dash, against your shameless faces, those things which we said above about your paradise, and which truly follow from your error, and are either laughed at or abhorred by all human beings as insane and furious.

**IV. 115.** *Julian.* For it is not in anyone's right to take up definite things and exclude what follows. Whoever, then, says that the little ones pertain to the right of the devil for this reason, because they are generated of the mingling of the sex, without doubt condemns the mingling itself, in which is the work of nature, together with the institution of that very nature.

*Augustine.* To you indeed this seems so; but that it is not true (which you do not see) Ambrose saw, who said that those born of man and woman, that is, through that mingling of bodies, are not free of fault[^156]; nor yet does he condemn the mingling itself together with the institution of that very nature. For the mingling of spouses for the sake of procreating is the good of marriage; but so do some good works come about not without the evil of vices, just as evil works come about not without the good of the members. But with however many vices any nature whatever is befouled, its institution is always good. For just as the institution of the body is good, even when it is born diseased; and the institution of the mind is good, even when it is born foolish; so the institution of the man himself is good, when he is born liable to the contagion of original sin.

**IV. 116.** *Julian.* Which is either both denied at once, or both at once taken up; and although there is in the power of each a rejection, there is nonetheless not in the choice of one the election of the other; except that it can more easily, by an unlawful mingling, be burdened with the vice of will—the fruitfulness of the seeds being nonetheless defended—than that, with its fruits accused, the business of the sexes be vindicated. Your fluctuation, then, which you suffer between fear and impiety, will not be able to establish new rules of disputing, so that, of two connected things, you take up one and exclude what clings to it. *Sooner shall the light-footed stags feed in the air*[^157] than the effecting be denied after the prejudgment is taken up. This kind the apostle Paul exercises, when he says: *If the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen. If Christ has not risen, your faith is vain*[^158]. But now Christ has risen; it is established, then, that the resurrection of the dead will be. And, to drive home this very thing by a briefer example for the reader's sake: let it be doubted whether the just is good; I ask, do you confess that every just thing is honorable? Which being granted, I add another: do you pronounce that every honorable thing is also good? This too being given, willing or unwilling, I conclude: If every just thing is honorable, every honorable thing good; therefore every just thing is good. Whoever, after the first things have been taken, wishes to struggle against this effected conclusion, does not shake the edifice of reason, but exposes himself to derision. That the comparison may recur to the cause, then, it is asked whether there is sin in nature; I asked, would you concede that there is no sin without will; you assented, as your words attest. I added, would you suppose there is a will in a little one; this too you denied. What follows as third, if there is no sin without will? This is so certain that it could not come into doubt even among the Academics, whose riches are to hold nothing certain. You, then, when, after the two first things have been taken, you deny the third, in which is the effect of the two, do not overturn the foundations of reason, but betray your fury.

*Augustine.* Are you so foolish as to think that there is no sin in nature, when sin can be at all only in a nature? For it is either in an angel or in a man, which without doubt are natures; if, then, sin were in the nature of none of these, surely it would be nowhere. And through this, the question being proposed whether there is sin in nature, you had intended to teach that there is no sin in nature; that your intention, then, was vain, and that you set forth this question in vain, you understand, if you are not too vain. Behold, I overturn the foundations of your reasoning, since the reasoning is not true; nor yet, as you revile, do I betray my fury, but your error. For, the two things being granted which you take, therefore I deny the third, because there is not in it, as you suppose, the effect of those two. For I therefore concede that there is no sin without will, because without will it cannot come to be. But by another reasoning, again, it is rightly said that there is sin without will, because it remains, as long as it is not remitted, even with the will ceasing by which it was done. Likewise I concede that there is no sin without will, because original sin too was not made without the will of him from whom that very origin took its beginning. Accordingly, when I concede this too, that the will to sin is not in a little one, the third is not effected from these two—that there is no sin in a little one. But it would be effected, if, just as I concede that there is no sin without will, so I conceded that there is not anyone's sin without his own will. Accordingly the little one indeed does not have a will to sin, but would not have sin, unless that one, from whom he drew it, had sinned by will. For of the very nativity of man too something such can be said; for if you said: No one is born except from will; I would not without reason concede it; but if you said: No one is born except from his own will, I would not concede it. In this way, then, since we are dealing with the sin of a little one, his original sin, just as his very origin, could not be without will, but not his own.

**IV. 117.** *Julian.* Therefore, if those priests, whose sayings we are now washing, should hear it doubted whether the conjugal mingling were good, and I should ask of them whether bodies were formed by God, they would confess it. And this being granted, I would add whether they would grant that marriages were ordained by God. Which being likewise confirmed, I would ask again whether there could be offspring without mingling. And this being denied, what followed? Namely, that if there is body only through God, mingling only through body, offspring only through mingling, both offspring and mingling pertain to the same author to whom bodies pertain.

*Augustine.* Is this question turned between us, whether the conjugal mingling is good, when we both call it good? What is it, then, that you think so badly of those priests—whose sayings you do not, as you lie, wash, but pollute—as though you wished to persuade them, as though doubting, of what you convict neither them nor us to doubt? The conjugal mingling, which comes about for the sake of procreating, is good. But Ambrose, who said that no one is free of fault from the mingling of either sex, did not refute the conjugal mingling; but he saw the evil, by whose good use that comes about which no Catholic doubts to be a good work. You speak empty things, you spend time on superfluous ones; you desert what is at issue, and you strive to teach, as though it were doubted, or even denied, what is not doubted. But why do I marvel that you make so many and so empty books?

**IV. 118.** *Julian.* To this effected conclusion the prudent Catholic priests would without doubt assent, and, seeing that nothing in the senses of the flesh, nothing in the offspring of the sexes, did any other than the true God make; but that God made nothing that was evil; and that evil is nothing other than an evil will, sinning without any compulsion of natural things; they would pronounce, surely, that the Manicheans and the Traducianists had burst apart at the gleaming thunderbolts of Catholic reasoning.

*Augustine.* Why, then, do you lie without any shame that you defend and wash the sayings of those whom you call prudent men and Catholic priests, if they too burst apart at your thunderbolts? But if they are defended and washed, and for this reason remain entire, you rather are thunderstruck by them. For what Ambrose says—that one conceived from the mingling of either sex is free of fault—is either false or true. If you say it is false, then you assail the sayings of prudent men and Catholic, as you confess, priests, you do not defend and wash them. But if, that they may deservedly be defended and washed, you grant this to be true, you rather are thunderstruck by the sayings of Catholic bishops. What is it, then, that you boast and say that those most blessed and most learned men, if they had heard your syllogisms, would have pronounced that we, whom you call *Manicheans and Traducianists, burst apart at the gleaming thunderbolts of Catholic reasoning*? They would pronounce, then, against themselves, and would show that they themselves, together with us, burst apart with you thundering. Why do you not dare to say directly what you are convicted of saying obliquely? With Ambrose we confess original sin, and you hurl thunderbolts with so great power, that on account of these sayings common to us and to him, we burst apart at them, and he is washed? You are vain: you do not distinguish him from us; and you accuse both us utterly and him; nor yet do you thunder at either us or him, if you do not wish to violate the testimony of Pelagius your teacher, who said that the faith of Ambrose and his most pure sense in the Scriptures not even an enemy dared to censure[^159]. Whence you too, since you are understood to be enemy enough to his faith and most pure sense in the Scriptures, do not nonetheless dare to censure him; but you think to show, by censuring me, what evil he says. Man pierced through, and not professing, you are by a great force compelled to breathe out cloudy fallacies; in vain you feign the assaults of one thundering, when you rather breathe out the smoke of one thunderstruck. These are certainly the things by which you try to teach that Ambrose, and the other teachers his companions, do not hold that there is original sin: *that God formed bodies, and ordained marriages, and that there would be no offspring without mingling*. These things are granted; granted also those you add: namely, *that offspring and mingling pertain to the same author to whom bodies pertain*; if at least you wished the conjugal mingling to be understood, when you said this. Although that, by itself, is known to be true, it is not consequent upon your reasoning; otherwise, with you saying: *If body only through God, mingling only through body*, and wishing to conclude this, *that mingling pertains to the same author to whom bodies pertain*, another can say: If body only through God, adultery only through body, adultery pertains to the same author to whom body pertains; which you see how great an injury is said to God, and how much evil your syllogisms teach. As, then, it does not follow that adultery be attributed to God for this reason, because it comes about only through body, of which God is the author; so it does not follow that mingling be attributed to God for this reason, because it comes about only through body, of which God is the author. But nonetheless we concede that the conjugal mingling, surely—that which comes about for the cause of procreating—must be attributed to God as author; not because this follows from your premises, but because, considered by another reasoning, it is perceived to be true. But what you seek to effect from this, neither follows from those things above, nor is true. For neither, because God made the body, God instituted marriages, of whose mingling offspring would arise, does God create the very offspring of living things, is it therefore true what you add: *That nothing in the senses of the flesh, nothing in the offspring of the sexes, did any other than the true God make*. For where did the devil do the evil he did with the first human beings, except in the senses of their flesh too? For the sense was corrupted by an evil persuasion, when an assent to sinning was made. And where did he do whatever evil he afterward did to the human race, except in the offspring of the sexes, that is, in the sons of men? But how do you say: *That God made nothing that is evil*? Is not Gehenna an evil to the damned? But now, whoever believes you saying *that evil is nothing other than an evil will, sinning without any compulsion of natural things*—to be silent about the innumerable evils which evil angels and men suffer unwilling—the very eternal punishment will not be feared, which is both the greatest evil, and is not an evil will, but the punishment of an evil will. These are your reasonings, by which you think your word is a thunderbolt, when your heart is ash.

**IV. 119.** *Julian.* Cease, then, to accuse men of sound head and the bishops of the Churches; let their more negligent comments not be subjected to judgment; for not a brief hesitation, but a persistent intention, deserves wrath. Let us, surely, imitate that zeal of theirs, by which they built up the peoples by exhorting, beseeching, refuting; of which would they have done anything, if, after your manner, they had believed sins to be not voluntary, but natural?

*Augustine.* We too, according to our measure, build up the peoples by exhorting, beseeching, accusing, which Ambrose did; and yet of original sin we feel and say this, which Ambrose felt and said—nor he alone, but with his other great companions. Whom, since you call *bishops of the Churches* and *men of sound head*, with what head do you, atrociously, censure those things which they, by a most well-known consensus, learned and taught, and fallaciously defend them; and you accuse me, saying that I am their slanderer, when you see me the defender of them against your crimes, which you obliquely object to them? You say *their more negligent comments should not be subjected to judgment*; do you so defend and wash their sayings? Or do you rather, under the name of negligence, reprove and accuse, and, with no one doubting, condemn what was negligently poured out, and therefore pronounce to be false? We ask you, if you assert that the sayings you defend are false, assert that the things you censure are true. *Not a brief hesitation*, you say, *but a persistent intention deserves wrath*; as though you bring forth something whence you can show that they, I do not say after a brief, but at least after a long intention, or at the end of their life, changed their sentence about original sin. You speak empty things, you speak insane things, you speak perverse things and things adverse to your own salvation; be quiet, I beg, why do you speak so much?

**IV. 120.** *Julian.* But let us proceed to the rest. It avails nothing at all, what you say you reckon to the devil, not bodies but faults; you wholly deny, as we have often shown, with no other zeal than to avoid the odium of the Manichean, whose poison you breathe in. For bodies, plainly bodies, you reckon to the prince of darkness, whose mingling you profess to be diabolical, whose genitals, whose movements, whose fruit you accuse. You openly accuse, from his first teaching office, the members, not the vices; for you call that diabolical which even the Manichean, as I taught in his writings, is indignant flourishes in the sexes. And, that our reader may receive by a brief repetition what he can hold: either show that there is a will in the little ones, or remove the crime from them. Which since you do not do, but assert that they are possessed by the devil for this reason, because they are generated of the mingling of bodies, you show that you reckon not the sins, which are not without will, but the bodies themselves, to the adverse power. Just as, then, that disordered desire which is found both in human beings and in cattle is natural and instituted by God, so this disordered desire which you suffer in the variety of your disputation, by which you turn amid diverse and contrary teachings, was conceived no less from folly than from profanity. Not unjustly, then, do we distinguish Ambrose from your cohort; nor do we call him, as you ask, a Manichean.

*Augustine.* You labored in vain, to arrive at that false and ridiculous conclusion, by long and twisting, fallacious and fleeting windings, where you should say: *Not unjustly do you distinguish Ambrose from our cohort, nor call him a Manichean*. Surely, if you do not call him this, neither ought you to call me this; but if you think me to be called this, you are compelled to call him too, and all those great and illustrious teachers of the Church, who concerning original sin—on account of which you call me a Manichean—say, without any obscurity or ambiguity, the things I say, as in the first and second of my six books, which I published against your four, I have shown evidently enough. But namely Ambrose would be terrified by your dialectic, if he survived these days, so that, finding from the false consequences that what he had thought was false, he would not dare to say any longer that the little ones born of the mingling of the sexes are not free of fault, lest through this he should establish them under the power of the devil; then, therefore, with you as teacher, he would cease to be a Manichean. O what has he lost, who could not hear you! Accordingly, because on account of this sense you say I am a Manichean, surely, persevering in this sense, Ambrose, with you as author, departed from this life a Manichean. You ought, then, not to defend him—which you can in no way do—but to grieve, because you can no longer teach him; which if you could do, that man, rebuked and corrected by your teaching office, would surely forbid, in the Church he ruled, the little ones to be baptized to be exorcized and exsufflated; lest, in so many innocents, and by no reasoning, as you assert, established under the power of the devil—images of God—so grave and signal an injury be done to God himself; which nonetheless, if Ambrose forbade to be done, he would be driven, with you, from the Catholic Church. For far be it that this be called correction rather than deception. Far be it, then, that that man should stand with you against mother Catholic; but, unconquered, he would resist on her behalf against you. What is it, then, by which you think I must be separated from him in this cause? Through the mingling of the bodies of male and female, I say that no one is born free of fault, as he; nor yet do I attribute bodies to the devil as founder, because neither does he; just as we both blame the vice of nature, so we both venerate the author of nature. If, because I say that the concupiscence of the flesh, by which the flesh has desires opposed to the spirit, turned into nature through the transgression of the first man, I therefore *accuse the members, not the vices*: this he too did; but if the origin of vices is from one source, of members from another; neither I, nor he. That a proper will is in those being born, neither Ambrose says, nor I; but that, through the transgressing will of the first man, the vice of concupiscence was made, whence those born through the mingling of the sexes draw original sin, both Ambrose says, and I. Both of us, then, reckon human beings born, before they are reborn—not on account of their substance, of which God is the creator, but on account of the sin which through one entered, and passed through all human beings, of which sin the devil is the author—to the adverse power. What is it that you lie, with a most shameless brow, that you defend and wash the sayings of Ambrose and of others like him? Who is so blind as not to see that you censure them, I defend; you pollute, I wash? The sayings of Ambrose, speaking of Christ, are: *And therefore*, he says, *as man he was tempted through all things, and in the likeness of men sustained all things; but as born of the Spirit, he abstained from sin; for every man is a liar, and no one is without sin except one God. It has been observed, then*, he says, *that from man and woman, that is, through that mingling of bodies, no one is seen to be free of fault; but he who is free of fault is free also of this kind of conception*[^160]. These sayings you say are most false, and that in them is contained the nefarious teaching of the Manichean; and through this you censure and pollute the sayings of Ambrose. But I both proclaim them most true, and show that they are not only not friendly but even hostile to the Manichean, as I have already done; I, therefore, rather defend them, and wash them from your nefarious slanders. Whether, then, that man is ours or yours, you too see; but because you fear the men by whom he is loved, surely him whom you are convicted of accusing atrociously, you try to excuse fallaciously.

**IV. 121.** *Julian.* As for the insult you say was made to him by Jovinian[^161]—in which, however, I think you lie; but let us grant that you can be a true accuser even of Jovinian, and that Ambrose was called a Manichean by him; yet it is established that this was insane. For he could not rightly be called a Manichean who preached a good nature, voluntary sins, marriages instituted by God, little ones made by God. If, then, Jovinian thought the preferring of virginity to be an accusation of marriages, he knew not at all what he was saying. For one thing is contrary, another ordered. The praise of a good is a step toward better things; but the defamation of nature is a road to the Manichean. Since, then, Ambrose did not condemn marriages, nor say that the mingling of spouses was either a work of the devil or a necessity of sin; Jovinian acted wickedly, in comparing him to a Manichean, and in supposing there was no difference between an accuser and a praiser of marriages. For if Ambrose said that those generated of the legitimate and God-instituted union of bodies, after they began to have the use of reason, lied of their own accord by the imitation of the good; he nonetheless did not want this union to seem a necessity of lying, but an indication of universality. For such is what he said: *Those born of parents lie*, as if he had said: Every man, at least one having command of his own choice, has at some time lied; for he knew that no one without the mingling of parents, except Christ, had existed. Therefore by the conjugal work the prudent man wished not iniquity, but generality, to be indicated. But Christ he pronounced to have guarded against all lying, whom he showed by the miracle of his bearer; which indeed strongly rages against Jerome, of whom you are a follower, who most expressly tried to fasten a lie upon Christ. Not rightly, then, was he called a Manichean (if at least he was called one), who against your error frequented the praise of creatures.

*Augustine.* We too frequent the praise of creatures; what is it, then, that you say Ambrose does this against our error, when he does it according to our faith? But how he set down those words which, objected by me to you, you feared to record, lest your darkness be refuted by their most clear light, Ambrose shows in other places too of his writings, in such a way that this sentence does not seem to have crept upon him once, as upon one more negligent, as you speak, and more incautious; but he declares, by a sufficiently clear manifestation, his teaching about original sin, since it is a Catholic teaching. Why, then, and by what reasoning he said: *It has been observed, then, that from man and woman, that is, through that mingling of bodies, no one is seen to be free of fault; but he who is free of fault is free also of this kind of conception*—because here he wanted not the sins of human beings after they began to have the use of reason understood, as you alienate it, but original sin—attend to what he says elsewhere: *The Jordan*, he says, *turned backward, signified the future mysteries of the saving bath, through which the little ones who have been baptized are reformed from malice into the first state of their nature*[^162]. Say, Julian, from what malice, if they do not draw original sin. Hear yet in another place: *For not a man's coupling*, he says, *unbarred the secrets of the virginal womb, but the Holy Spirit poured an immaculate seed into the inviolable womb. For alone through all things, of those born of woman, holy is the Lord Jesus, who, by the newness of an immaculate birth, did not feel the contagions of earthly corruption, and by his heavenly majesty drove them off*[^163]. Answer, Julian, what are the contagions of earthly corruption which alone of those born of woman the Lord Jesus did not feel by the newness of his immaculate birth. Hear still: *Before we are born*, he says, *we are stained by contagion*. And a little after: *And if not even the infant of one day is without sin, much less are those days of the maternal conception without sin*[^164]. Many such things of this man, whom you have confessed to have been of sound head, I can record; but to whom these do not suffice, what suffices? From these, then, understand how you are not permitted, even those, as you attempted, to wrench into another sense and pervert, by which Ambrose said of those who are born through the mingling of bodies—not accusing the creature of God, but original sin. Why do you hold out to me Jerome, whose follower you slander me to be, of whose words there is now no question? Which words, however, if you had set down, I would either show that they have nothing that ought to displease, or would leave them to be expounded by the more understanding; or if they were without doubt contrary to truth, I would, with the liberty that is worthy, disapprove them. Look at Ambrose, whom you do not dare to call a Manichean; when you call me one for this reason, because of original sin I say the things he says. For if you would not for that reason call Ambrose a Manichean, because he preached a good nature, voluntary sins, marriages instituted by God, and little ones made by God; neither would you call me one, who faithfully preach the same things. But if you think I must be called a Manichean for this reason, because I confess original sin; this he too confesses; why do you not think both? But me you say petulantly, him you opine secretly—not with truth distinguished, but rather with liberty extinguished. For this you do not dare to say of him, which you dare to believe; or if you do not believe these things of him, surely neither of me; because, even if you reckon us to err, it is easy for you to see that they are not Manicheans, who do not assert a proper substance of the sinner, which God did not create; but affirm that original sin was propagated from a voluntary vice of the nature which God founded good. It is easy, then, for you to see this, and at once to see that we are contrary to the Manicheans; but you drive away the name of Manichean from Ambrose by flattering, and object it to me by slandering.

**IV. 122.** *Julian.* But just as Jovinian, hostile to him, is refuted, so compared to you he is absolved. For when will the censure of prudent men grant you so much as to place you on a par with the merit of Jovinian? For he said that there is a necessity of good; you, of evil; he said that human beings are restrained from error through the mysteries; you, that they are not even set free through grace; he dissolved the virginity of Mary by the condition of childbearing; you transcribe Mary herself to the devil by the condition of being born; he equals better things to good things, that is, integrity to wedlock; you call the mingling of marriage diseased, and depreciate chastity by comparison with a most foul thing; nor do you add a step between these things; but you change the whole genus, preferring virginity not, surely, to a good, but to an evil. But it is of the utmost vileness not to be able to please, unless compared to a most foul thing. But now what such thing did he say of the injuries of God as you? He wished to confound the vigor of his judgment on the side of kindness; you, on the side of malignity; he says that with him good and best men will enjoy equal honor; you, that good men and the impious, that is, innocents and the devil, are to be tortured with one punishment; he, then, wants God to seem most clement; you, most unjust; he says that men imbued with his mysteries cannot sin; you contend that God himself sins, both in the mysteries by weakness, and in the precepts by immoderation, and in the judgments by monstrousness. Since, then, there is so great a disparity between you and Jovinian, as great a conjunction between you and the Manichean; you are proved so much more bearable than Jovinian, as the Manichean is more horrid than Jovinian.

*Augustine.* How handsome you seem to yourself, when, comparing me to Jovinian, you try to show me worse! But this most mendacious insult too I rejoice to receive with Ambrose from you; but I am saddened that you so rave. For by that cause you say I am worse than Jovinian, by which you say I am also a Manichean. And what is this? That original sin, namely, which you deny with Pelagius, but we confess with Ambrose. With him, then, according to you, we are both Manicheans, and worse than Jovinian, and whatever else you shall have said with a saucy mouth, and not, surely, truthful, but slanderous; the Lord has taught us to rejoice and exult, when we hear curses of any kind, not from truth, but because we contend for truth[^165]. Behold, I do not say that there is a necessity of evil, because neither does Ambrose; and yet I say that the little ones are reformed from malice, which Ambrose too; and therefore there is no necessity of evil, because that too is curable by God which nativity draws; how much more what the will adds? I do not say that human beings are not even set free through grace; which far be it that Ambrose should say; but, what you do not want, we say that they are set free only through grace, not only that their debts be remitted to them, but even that they be not brought into temptation. We do not transcribe Mary to the devil by the condition of being born; but for this reason, because that very condition is dissolved by the grace of being reborn. Not as though a good to an evil, but as though to a good a better, do we prefer virginity to marriage. Not, as you slander, good men and the impious with one punishment; but the good with none, but the impious not with one, but, according to the diversity of the very impiety, with diverse punishments, do we say must be tortured. We do not say that God sins in the mysteries by weakness, in the precepts by immoderation, in the judgments by monstrousness—God, both whose grace the mysteries are useful to the regenerated, and whose grace the precepts are wholesome to the set free, and whose judgments are fittingly distributed to the good and the evil. Behold, we remove from us those things in which you say we are worse than Jovinian; you remove from yourselves, if you can, those in which I will show you to be worse than that same Jovinian. He said that there is a necessity of good; you say that there is a good disordered desire of evil; he said that human beings are restrained from error through the mysteries; you, that the disordered desire of walking the right way is not inspired by God, but acquired by free choice; he dissolved the virginity of Mary by the condition of childbearing; you equal the very holy flesh procreated from the Virgin to the rest of the flesh of human beings, not distinguishing the likeness of the flesh of sin from the flesh of sin; he equals better things to good things, that is, integrity to wedlock; you, evil things to good things; for the discord of flesh and spirit you say to be a good, as the concord of marriage; he says that with God good and best men will have an equal honor; but you say that some good men not only will obtain no honor in the kingdom of God, but will not even see that kingdom; he says that human beings imbued with the mysteries of God cannot sin; you say that more easily indeed through the grace of God, but even without it, through free choice, human beings can not sin; resisting God with a giant's boldness, when he speaks of good fruits and says: *Without me you can do nothing*[^166]. Since, then, you differ from the error of Jovinian by so much for the worse, you nonetheless subject us to him, and rather equate us to the Manichean; fortified, surely, by a great defense, as though for this reason you founded a new heresy, that when we refute you, we might be able to equate you to no heretics. I, however, in this cause, in which I much seem to you, on account of original sin, detestable, and rather to be equated to the Manichean, am with Ambrose, like it or not, whom Jovinian called a Manichean, as you call me; but he freely, you slyly. Finally, once he is conquered, when Ambrose is shown not to be a Manichean; but you, because you wished to have a double heart, are twice conquered. You argue that Ambrose is a Manichean: I show that he is not; you deny that you argue this: I show that you do argue it; but both will be clear to him who will read above what he himself said.

**IV. 123.** *Julian.* But let us look at the rest too. Concerning the things we have done, I think the reader is sufficiently and more than sufficiently instructed, with what strength he may either assail any of my words, or defend his own opinion; and therefore it will not be necessary to show it by a repetition of all the writings. Since, taking scarcely individual and scattered little verses from my book and setting them before himself, and frequently praising them, sometimes too he tickles them with a most brief suggillation; though nonetheless what he thinks must be bitten was not brought forth by me as he suspects. Wherefore I direct the reader to that work of mine; he will see the assertion harmonize with truth. This man, surely, who complained that it was objected to him that he was accusing nature and the seeds, could not keep a continual patience of pretense; but after he calmed his hearing with the legerdemain of his patron, like a tortoise he opens his head. For when he had said that, unless Adam had sinned, human beings could have generated in the way we are accustomed either to move our joints or to cut our little nails; he added: *But if the seed itself has no curse, what is it that is written in the book of Wisdom: Not being ignorant that their nation was wicked, and their malice natural, and that their thought could not be changed forever; for their seed was cursed from the beginning*[^167]? And the very testimony which he brought forth he pursues with a subjoined argumentation: *Surely, of whomsoever it says these things, it says them of human beings*, he says. Behold one who professed that he had desisted from the pursuits of the Manicheans, who, under the occasion of a sentence which he does not understand, pronounces a cursed seed, a natural malice, and an unconvertible thought of the wicked. There is a report that, near the cataracts of the Nile, the inhabitants are deprived of the use of their ears by the roar of the rushing waters. Opinion may have feigned that, which scatters greater things about great matters; yet the example avails to chastise the deafness of the foolish, who, by the crash of mutual terror, like deaf asps, struggle against the voices of things. Augustine cries out: The nation of human beings is wicked, the malice natural, the thought immutable forever, the seed cursed from the beginning. And are there still found those who would suppose him not everywhere struck with the mark of the Manichean? Let any who are public followers of those same filths be questioned today; if they should say otherwise, let us be thought to have lied. If, then, there is a natural malice, how do you feign that you do not pronounce nature evil? If there is a seed cursed from the beginning, how do you say that you accuse the vices of wills, not of seeds? If there is an immutable thought of the malignant, how do you forswear yourself confessing free choice? Unless it remains for you to say that those Hebrews, Sirach, or Philo, who by an uncertain opinion are believed to be the authors of that book which is called *Wisdom*, were Manicheans.

*Augustine.* Whoever was the author of this book, it is well that you do not spurn its authority. It is therefore suitable, that from it we may bring forth, against you, what testimonies we can find. For Pelagius too, your teacher, in the book which he published of *Testimonies* or of *Chapters*, set down from that book the things he believed suited his intention[^168]. The author of which book, the more because he was not a Manichean, sufficiently and openly shows that even by those who are not Manicheans, and who have merited to be read and received in the Church of Christ, a natural malice could be spoken of, without any censure of the institution and condition of God, the most wise and best creator of all natures. Whence the Apostle too is understood to have said in no other sense: *For we too were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^169]. Which some have interpreted not according to the word, but according to the meaning, saying: For we too were once naturally children of wrath. But in that he says: *like the rest*, he shows that all are by nature children of wrath, unless divine grace discern certain of them from the mass of perdition. Of those alien from this grace, then, the apostle Peter too, speaking: *But these*, he says, *like dumb animals, naturally procreated for capture and destruction*[^170]. For they were not putting off the old man. But unless every old man were born, no little one would be renewed by being reborn. Far be it, then, that, when the injury of the Creator be said, human beings are naturally children of wrath; just as without any injury of his anyone is said to be naturally deaf; or naturally blind, or naturally diseased; and likewise another naturally foolish, another naturally forgetful, another naturally irascible; and other innumerable vices, whether of bodies, or—what is graver—of minds created by the handiwork of God, and by the hidden but just judgment of that same God vitiated. For he himself is the same creator of the whole man: and yet, although with his praise man be a nature, in no way nonetheless is man with his censure a vicious nature. We know, then, that not vices but natures are to be attributed to God the founder; but whence the vices are, he ought to say who wishes to resist the Manichean. And concerning the vices indeed of other things, which we confess were created by God, but, by his most wise ordination, subjected either to good or even to evil angels, it is most easy to answer, that by those to whom they are subject, their seeds too can be vitiated, so that they not only come to be, but even are conceived and born, vicious. It is about man that we are dealing, about a rational living being, about the image of God, whose nature would in no way become the plaything of the devil—whom we rightly believe to be the author of vices—except through the just judgment of God on account of original sin. Finally, not even you yourselves, as I think, dare to say—although your teaching is followed by this so dire absurdity—that in paradise so many and so great natural vices would have been going to arise, if, with no one sinning, the happiness in which it was founded had remained for human nature. But you, denying original sin, surely cause a nature which God did not create—from whose mingling are the vices of human beings with which they are born—to be introduced. O perverse heretics, helpers of the Manicheans, slanderers concerning the Manicheans, and contentious accusers of some Catholics saying the same true things concordantly against you, sly flatterers of others!

**IV. 124.** *Julian.* Now perhaps the reader awaits how this testimony ought to be understood; which surely that it does not pertain to the traduction of sin, nor to the opinion of the Manicheans, this very man who had been its usurper shows; for he says: *Of whomsoever it says this, it says it of human beings*; when surely, if it spoke of natural sin, it would speak not of some, but of all. For the opinion of the Manicheans defames generally the nature of all mortals; but the sentence about which we ask receives even from its own plagiarist the testimony that it was uttered not against all, but against some. From which it is established that he had no business with traduction, which by no means strikes all, but very many. Therefore, it being shown that nothing of it pertains to the profane opinion, by the suffrage of the book itself let the soundness of its writer be asserted. He speaks to God: *You have mercy on all, because you can do all things, and you overlook the sins of men for the sake of penance; for you love all things that are, and hate nothing of those things you have made. For how could anything endure, unless you had willed it? But you spare all, because they are yours, O Lord, who love souls. For your good spirit is in all things; for which reason those who err you correct in parts, and concerning the things in which they sin you admonish and address them, that, with malice abandoned, they may believe in you, O Lord*[^171].

*Augustine.* How does God have mercy on all, when another Scripture says: *You will not have mercy on all who work iniquity*[^172]; unless because even in those on whom he does not have mercy, all human beings are in such a way that all kinds of human beings are understood—just as it is said: *You tithe every herb*[^173], that is, every kind of herb? But what does it help you, that the scripture of the book of *Wisdom* was not speaking of all human beings, where it said that their malice was natural? For not because it was dealing with some, not all human beings, did it therefore make it understood that there are no others such; since the Apostle says: *We were once by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^174]. But the Israelites at that time—not indeed all of them either, but the devout—from those of whom it was said, *their malice is natural*, not nature, but grace distinguished; on account of which they were also called sons of God. It must be seen too how it was said: *You love all things that are*; since there are also the wicked; and elsewhere it is written: *You hate all who work iniquity*[^175]. He loves, then, even those very ones, insofar as they are human beings; he hates them, insofar as they are wicked; and he condemns those who are wicked, and makes them be because they are human beings: *For nothing*, it says, *you hate of those things you have made*. God loves human beings, then, to such a degree that even when they are wicked, he loves them as they are human beings, although he hates that they are wicked. And through this the wicked, whom God hates, are both human beings, since God loves his handiwork; and miserable, since God loves judgment.

**IV. 125.** *Julian.* Do you see how hostile to your opinion is the gratification of God's praiser? He says that he founds souls, and loves them; which is denied by you, who swear that the souls of innocents are under the devil, and hateful to God; when they have nothing else than what they have been allotted by their author.

*Augustine.* Therefore, according to you, whoever are born foolish are allotted foolishness too from God their author—whom the sacred Scripture says must be lamented more than the dead[^176]. But the souls of these too, according to a certain measure—that is, that they be, that they live, that they have sense, that, though with a dulled edge of mind, they nonetheless surpass the cattle—God surely loves. But that love is another, of which it is written: *God loves no one except him who dwells with wisdom*[^177]. But you, say whence God loves more the souls of those little ones for whom he provides the bath of regeneration, that he may send them into the kingdom; and whence to others he does not bestow this benefit; when no merits of wills distinguish them, nor is there with God, what you most insipidly are wont to object to us, a respecting of persons. Although where it is said: *You love souls*, it is not said: All souls, there is no question. For perhaps it is so set down that God founds all souls; nor yet loves all, but those whom, not by their own merits, but by the largess of his own grace, he distinguishes from the rest, so that they dwell with wisdom; because *God loves no one except him who dwells with wisdom; but the Lord gives wisdom*[^178].

**IV. 126.** *Julian.* The penance too, which he affirms that God gives, your teaching does not receive, in which a natural evil and a malice that knows not how to be changed is contained.

*Augustine.* Nay rather, we ourselves rather say against you that God gives the very penance too; because, although each one does penance by will, yet the will too is prepared by the Lord[^179], *and this is the change of the right hand of the Most High*[^180], which the sacred Psalm sounds; because, that Peter might weep, the Lord looked upon him[^181]; whence his fellow Apostle says of certain people: *Lest perhaps God give them penance*[^182]. And what is called an immutable malice is immutable to the man, who cannot change it, not to God, who can do all things.

**IV. 127.** *Julian.* But what he commended—that God admonishes, so that, with malice abandoned, sinners may believe in him—wholly overturns the opinion of natural evil; because it is certain that congenital things cannot be abandoned.

*Augustine.* Even congenital things can be abandoned; but when the Almighty works this in a man; for corruption too is congenital to the body, which nonetheless will be incorruptible.

**IV. 128.** *Julian.* Hence, the patience of God being unfolded, and his most mild dispensation, by which he does not will the death of the dying, but that he turn back and live[^183], he persists, by examples too, to approve what he had said, and he makes mention of those who, living most wickedly in the land of Canaan, had kindled the wrath of the Almighty, that it might be expended in the most just vengeance of the triumphs of the Israelites, now living under the law of God. *For those ancient inhabitants of your holy land, hating because they did to you hateful works through medicaments and unjust sacrifices, and slayers of children without mercy, you willed to destroy through the hands of our parents, that the land, which of all is dearest to you, might receive a worthy colony of the children of God*[^184]. The seven nations, for the most part destroyed, to the Israelites the land of promise was given; on account of what God willed them to perish, it attests. For lest, in one nature of human beings, there should seem to be a discrimination with God through some respecting of persons, on account of what the Canaanites merited to be slain, it commends. *Those ancient inhabitants of your holy land*, then, he says, *you held in hatred*. Wherefore? Surely, according to you, he ought to subjoin: because they were generated of a diabolical mingling, because they were possessed by the prince of darkness, because Adam infected with disease all who would come from his stock. But nothing of these; but what cause does he render for the hatreds? The deeds alone committed through free will. *You hated them*, he says, *because they did to you hateful works*. But lest we should not know what the works were, and you should call these deeds of the nations genital concupiscence, it pursues the very kinds of the working. *Through medicaments*, he says, *and unjust sacrifices, and they were slayers of children without mercy*; that is, they served sorceries and sacrifices, which were unjust, since they were offered to idols, with the worship of the Creator cast off; to which sacrileges they reckoned even their own kinships; placating the demons, namely, even by the monstrousness of parricides. See, then, how it does not speak of that crime which the Manichean contrives—that is, clinging by nature to all mortals—who records that they offended him chiefly by this, that they did not even spare from the destructions of their own little ones. Whose death surely would not recur to the odium of the slayer, if for one sin both the parricide displeased God, and the son.

*Augustine.* You speak these things as though all human beings were punished on account of some one kind of sin, or as though we said that those who already pass through grown ages are children of wrath on account of original sin alone. *Whoever does not believe in the Son*, as the Son himself says, *will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon him*[^185]; but some are worse than others, upon whom surely a greater wrath of God remains; nor yet are all so overthrown and destroyed as these of whom we are speaking, who possessed the land that was given to the Israelites. What is it, then, that you say: *See, then, how it does not speak of that crime which the Manichean contrives*? Where you wish original sin to be understood; which the Manichean does not contrive, but, as the other Catholics, both against you and against the Manichean, Ambrose confesses; because it is not the mingling of an alien nature, which the Manichean introduces, but the depravation of our own, from the sin which through one man entered into the world and passed through all human beings[^186], which against the Catholic faith you deny. Are you so blind as not to see that, if for this reason original sin is not, because it is here not mentioned, neither will the other sins be sins, which are here not mentioned, whether lesser than these, or greater? Were the Sodomites, then, not destroyed, because it was not said of them that they did sorceries and parricidal sacrifices? Or were these Canaanites for this reason not destroyed, because it was not said of them that males wrought shamefulness upon males? Yet it was not passed over in silence that their malice was natural; which indeed is of all human beings, but in some less, in others greater; just as bodies are corruptible of all, but they weigh down some souls less, others more, according to the diversity of the judgments of God, hidden indeed, but without any doubt just. What wonder, then, if, when the cause was set forth why such vengeance proceeded against them, not only their voluntary malice was mentioned, but also the natural—to which, besides the common contagion of the human race, something more had been added from a prophetic curse (for holy Noah cursed his grandson Canaan, the progenitor of those people[^187]); not unjustly, surely, since to it he was liable, by the obligation of succession, in his offspring? Which appears also in their little ones, who, with their parents, and this with God commanding, without exception of any age, were destroyed[^188]; since the elders for this reason chiefly displeased God, because they were wont to pour out the blood of their own little ones to demons. Nor did God command those same little ones to be spared; nay, he commanded that they not be spared at all. From this understand the *seed cursed from the beginning*[^189]; for you will not dare to call him who commanded this unjust. Which if it had come into your mind, you would not separate the innocence of the sons from the impious parents for this reason, that *the parents chiefly offended God by this, that they did not even spare from the destructions of their own little ones. Whose death*, you say, *would not recur to the odium of the slayer, if for one sin both the parricide displeased God, and the son*. Nor do you see that the slayers of children so displeased God that he nonetheless commanded those same sons too to be killed with them. For not because they most impiously immolated even their sons to demons, ought therefore those same sons too not, by no human crime, but by a divine judgment, surely just, although hidden, as a cursed seed, just as it was done, likewise to perish. These things you do not attend, while you are swept away by eloquence without wisdom, and, by a river of vanity, wish to be eloquent, deserted by the light of truth.

**IV. 128.** *Julian.* *But even these*, he says, *as men you spared, and you sent before your army hornets; not because you were powerless in battle to subject the impious to the just, but, judging by parts, you gave place to penance*[^190]. By the stings of flies, he says, you goaded even those very sacrilegious ones, that they might turn from the sport of their own affliction to the power of the avenger. But after, by the counsel of God and by the kind of vengeance, voluntary sins were laid open, whose rejection was awaited and demanded; then at last against the obstinate profanity of the punished the writer's invective rises, and he says that they had entered into so great a familiarity with crimes that they seemed in some way inborn. *Not being ignorant*, he says, *that their nation was most wicked, and their malice natural, and that their thought could not be changed forever; for their seed was cursed from the beginning; nor fearing anyone, you gave pardon to their sins*[^191]. When, he says, you have so kindly and so patiently both indulged a time of penance and fixed a watch-tower of admonition, that you might assert your justice and mercy free of all infamy, of all suspicion of cruelty; yet they spurned your admonitions, who had despised the benefits sent before, like those who would wish to prove that they were of the seed of that Ham, against whom blessed Noah, his nakedness derided, had hurled with paternal censure a curse[^192]. What wonder, then, if, by the merit of their greater wantonness, the writer, struck by the severity of the sentence, recalled, to the stain of posterity not desisting from emulation, the curse—since frequently in the law the imitation of posterity is reproached even by the very terms of kinship? For thus the Lord in the Gospel to the Jews: *You*, he says, *are from your father the devil*[^193]. And Daniel against the foul elders, coming nonetheless from the stock of Israel, is read to have inveighed by the same kind: *Seed of Canaan*, he says, *and not of Judah*[^194]. The prophet Ezekiel too reproaches the people of Jerusalem: *Your mother*, he says, *was a Hittite, and your father an Amorite*[^195]. There is, then, here a usage by which to the odium of the will is added the defamation of the stock, and crime seems to cling too much, by whose merit even the seeds suffer reproach. But this same custom is preserved also on the side of the good, so that when a man seems upright in all things, he is said to flourish with congenital virtues. Whence blessed Job too confirms that the affection of mercy by which he aided the needy he drew from his very nursing breasts, nay, that it came forth with him from the womb of his mother[^196]. Nothing, then, can prejudge perspicuous matters, whether a likeness, or an exaggeration, or an ambiguity; since, as it is certain that the correction of natural things is never demanded of mortals, but God commands that human beings desist from evil; so it is more truly than anything approved that natural sin cannot be.

*Augustine.* You thought indeed that you had expounded the words of the book of Wisdom; but you are not permitted to elude them with words of folly. For it appears sufficiently, and it is clear, in what way that nation was said to be most wicked, and its malice natural; in what way too it was called a seed cursed from the beginning. For if, as you suppose, it had been said on account of emulation, that is, imitation, by which they imitated their forefather Ham, whom by the merit of his own sin his father Noah cursed; surely God, when he brought forth most just vengeance upon that nation, would command its little ones to be spared, whom you cannot say merited punishment by imitation of their forefathers. Since, then, he not only did not command mercy to be shown them, but he himself moreover commanded that they take an equal vengeance with their parents—he who surely could not command anything unjust—it shines clearly enough that their malice was said to be natural, and they themselves a seed cursed from the beginning, not on account of exaggeration, nor on account of imitation, but on account of generation. For against your error, in this very seed of which we are speaking, prophetic authority kept watch, so that the just Noah cursed his sinning son Ham in his son, that is, in Canaan; that hence we might understand that sons are bound by the merits of their fathers, unless this bond, drawn by generation, be loosed by regeneration. From this Canaan, then, the Canaanites draw their origin, who were called a seed cursed from the beginning; whose little ones too, because they themselves too were this, not by imitation, but by stock, were divinely commanded to be killed with their parents. To these Canaanites the prophet Daniel wished the unchaste elders to be understood to be like, when he said to them: *Seed of Canaan, and not of Judah*[^197], as though he said: Like the sons of Canaan, and not the sons of Judah; just as it was said: *Brood of vipers*[^198], on account of a certain likeness of viperine malice. But where the Lord says: *You are from your father the devil*[^199], he truly wished imitation, not seed, to be understood; but neither did he himself say: You are the seed of the devil. And where it is written: *Your father an Amorite, and your mother a Hittite*[^200], it appears to have been said for this reason, that they had imitated those to whom this was said; finally, neither there was it said: Seed of the Amorites, or of the Hittites. Not, then, as you said: *On account of the merit of a will clinging too much to crimes, the seeds too suffer reproach*. For far be it that holy Scripture should, like you, revile undeserving seeds. But where holy Job, as you record, *confirms that the affection of mercy came forth with him from the womb of his mother*; and you think that he said this, in order to commend that same affection much, not because it was so; why, I beg you, do we not grant that some are by nature merciful, if we do not deny that some are by nature senseless? For there are certain congenital things which, at the age when the use of reason begins to be, just as reason itself, begin to appear. Accordingly, to human beings by nature children of wrath, precepts of living are for this reason given, because grace too is given, that he who commands may help; and thus the evils, not only those that are added by will, but also those that are congenital, may be conquered. For what is impossible for human beings is easy for God[^201]. But those to whom the grace of God is not given—on account of which it was said: *For who distinguishes you? But what have you that you have not received?*[^202]—become transgressors, the law being added, not just. But these too live for the usefulness of the sons of mercy, so that, seeing them, and understanding what is given them not by merit but freely, they may not be exalted, but he who glories may glory in the Lord[^203].

**IV. 130.** *Julian.* *Wisdom* then follows: *Who will reproach you, if the nations perish whom you made? Since there is no other God than you, who have care of all things. But since you are just, you order all things justly; even him who ought not to be punished, to condemn, you reckon foreign to your power; for your power is the beginning of justice*[^204]. It supports, surely, the common reasoning of the prudent, that the greatest sign in God is justice, which the Manichean and the Traducianist, by every effort of their fables, deny.

*Augustine.* In reviling, to whom shall I say you are like, except to yourself? But if you were wise that God is just, you would never attribute the congenital vices, either of the human body or of the mind, which you cannot deny, to his handiwork, but to his judgment; and hence you would understand original sin, or sins, not deny them.

**IV. 131.** *Julian.* Finally, a little after, it follows: *For chastising them, you made your sons of good hope, since, in judging, you give place to penance in their sins. For if you freed with so great a torment of admonition the enemies of your servants and those owed to death, giving time and place through which malice could be changed; with how great diligence do you judge your sons, to whose parents you gave both oaths and pacts of good promises? When, then, you give us discipline, you scourge our enemies in manifold ways, that we may think on your goodness when we judge*[^205].

*Augustine.* Why God—since he is foreknowing of all things future—gave time and place through which malice could be changed, even to those of whom it had been said that their thought could not be changed forever, he shows sufficiently, where he says: *You made your sons of good hope, since, in judging, you give place to penance in sins*[^206]. For this end, then, time and place of penance are given even to the sons of wrath given over to death, and who will not do penance, because there are among them, or will arise from them, sons of mercy, to whom this may profit, which to those it did not profit. And so even over the sons of perdition the patience of God is neither empty nor fruitless; for it is necessary that it profit those whom, from the mass of perdition, not human merits, but divine grace distinguishes; when they either give thanks that they are distinguished from them by the mercy of God, or, out of those who will perish, those who will not perish are born by the disposition of God.

**IV. 132.** *Julian.* You note, then, how great a distinction the surface of the words has made concerning the natures of the two peoples: it calls the Israelites sons of God, and the Canaanites a cursed seed. If what it sounds were referred to generations and births, it would have to be said that the generation of the religious is one thing, of the profane another.

*Augustine.* Is it so, that because, when we hear sons of God, we understand grace; therefore, when we hear sons of men, we ought not to acknowledge and confess nature? What is it, then, that you say, not finding what to say, contentious man? Recognize what is true, attend to what is clear. The Canaanites were called a cursed seed on account of so evil a progeny that, with God most just avenging and commanding, not even their little ones were spared, who followed their parents by no imitation of will. But the Israelites were called sons of God, not on account of the progeny of nature, but on account of the adoption of grace. Whether, then, they be called sons and sons, or seed and seed, what does this consonance of names help you, where there is so great a difference of things?

**IV. 133.** *Julian.* While it would be established, nonetheless, even according to this vanity, that there is no traduction of the first sin; which the seeds, interrupted, of many nations at once thoroughly taught.

*Augustine.* One thing is the seed of that one man, which all human beings are; another the diverse seeds of diverse nations, which do not interrupt that one, because from it all descend; nor do they bring it about, by their variety, that that sin of the first man, by which human nature merited to be changed, should be either harmful, or rather less harmless, to any of his posterity born long after; but more or less harmless. For just as certain parents aggravate original sin, so certain relieve it; but no one takes it away, except he of whom it was said: *Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sins of the world*[^207]; to whom no good of a man is impossible, no evil incurable.

**IV. 134.** *Julian.* Further, just as that which is said in praise of the Jews does not avail to this, that anyone be believed to shine with a congenital sanctity; so too what is called natural malice does not avail to this, that it commend vicious seeds; but, without prejudice to nature, whose reasoning is imperturbable—which, whatever it has, it is allotted from God its author—that variety of words signifies either the impetus of invective or the office of laudation.

*Augustine.* If no one's sanctity is congenital, how was it said to Jeremiah: *Before you came forth from the womb, I sanctified you*[^208]? How too was it said of John the Baptist: *He will be filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother's womb*[^209]? Which his exultation too shows, when the pregnant Virgin Mary greeted the pregnant married Elizabeth[^210]. Or are these sayings too not sayings of the proclamation of a property, but of the office of laudation? So act, so vanish away; this it remains for your madness to say. For why do I set Jeremiah, why John, against the swelling of your brow; when you do not distinguish Christ himself from the flesh of sin, and, saying that no flesh is originally of sin, so equal him to the rest that you are compelled to deny that even he had a congenital sanctity—he who was born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, utterly free of fault, because free of that conception which comes about by the mingling of the sexes? For both Jeremiah and John, although sanctified in the wombs of their mothers, nonetheless drew original sin. For by what other merit would their souls perish from their people, unless on the eighth day they were circumcised[^211]; that is, unless they came to the grace of Christ, whom, about to rise on the eighth day, that is, after the seventh of the Sabbath, for the sake of our justification, that circumcision of the flesh prefigured? They were, then, both by nature children of wrath from the wombs of their mothers, and by grace children of mercy from the wombs of their mothers; because that sanctity was not yet in them which would loose the bond of the liable succession, which in its own time it behooved to be loosed; and yet that was in them which designated the herald of Christ from his maternal inmost parts. But, new heretic, you wish to be thought a religious natural philosopher, when you say: *natural malice and a cursed seed are said without prejudice to nature, whose*, you say, *imperturbable reasoning, whatever it has, it is allotted from God its author*. Do they not admonish you to have a heart, at least those who are born foolish? Nor yet do even the foolish themselves dare to call God the author of foolishness. Nor surely, as the Manichean is out of his senses or raves, did the mingling of an alien nature, but the depravation of our own, bring in this vice. The merit, then, of this vice, and of any other natural vices whatever, those who are sound in faith do not find, except original sin.

**IV. 135.** *Julian.* But just as here a cursed seed, so elsewhere indeed—where the authority of the reading is greater—the seed is called blessed. For Isaiah the prophet, speaking of the Israelites: *They will build houses*, he says, *and themselves dwell in them; they will plant vineyards, and themselves eat their fruits; they will not build, and others dwell; and they will not plant, and others eat. For according to the days of the tree of life shall be the days of my people; the works of their hands shall endure; my elect shall not labor in vain, nor shall they procreate children unto a curse, because it is a seed blessed by God*[^212].

*Augustine.* If you understood this prophecy of Isaiah, you would set it against, not us, that you might slip away, but rather yourself, that you might be corrected. For you would see another seed, not mortal, but immortal; not carnal, but spiritual; which John the Evangelist saw, when he said: *Everyone who is born of God does not sin; because his seed remains in him*[^213]. According to this seed each one does not sin; because, even if he sins as a man, he has nonetheless another seed, according to which the seed cannot sin, because he is born of God. According to this seed children are not procreated unto a curse. In which words of the Prophet you ought to have awakened, and to have noticed that this would not be promised to the people of God as a great gift, unless because children are procreated unto a curse according to the other seed, which is from Adam; but they are not procreated unto a curse according to Christ, which is the seed blessed from the beginning. For he is also the Wisdom of God, of which it is said: *It is a tree of life to all who embrace it*[^214]; whence too this Prophet, or rather God through the Prophet: *According to the days of the tree of life*, he says, *shall be the days of my people*. Eternal and immortal life was promised by these words, not to carnal, but to spiritual Israelites. There the spiritual vineyards and houses will not be possessed by others, with their planters and builders dying at some time, but by those very persons living without end. Acknowledge, then, two kinds of seeds, one of generation, and the other of regeneration; and do not be unbelieving, but faithful.

**IV. 136.** *Julian.* Let the little boys, then, suffer a question from these contrarieties of words, so that, capable of nothing greater than the sounds, they may battle for the cups that mock them; but the Catholic faith neither believes the law of God to quarrel against itself, nor admits any authority to the destruction of reason; nor does it listen to any opinion or flattery to the stain of divine equity; but, as it not only believes but even knows God to be the Creator of all natures, so it imputes sin there to no other than a free will; through all which it does not doubt that the traduction of sin is false.

*Augustine.* The Catholic faith rather does not doubt that there is original sin; which faith not little boys, but grave and constant men, learned in the Church, and teaching the Church, have defended even to the day of their death. But you do not believe that the law of God, as you say, quarrels against itself, and you yourselves quarrel against it with a blind impiety or an impious blindness. For you boast that you admit no authority to the destruction of reason; so that to your reasonings, which are not reasonings but deceptions, even the divine authority be deposed rather than expounded. Although no one ought to be so grave of heart as to be deceived by the reasoning of Pelagius, which, as though expounding the Apostle, he brought forth, and said: *The body dead because of sin was for this reason said, because the body dies to sins, when it turns away from sins*. Against which vanity one must not dispute, but the Apostle himself must be read, saying: *But if Christ is in you, the body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit is life because of righteousness; but if the spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he will give life even to your mortal bodies through his spirit dwelling in you*[^215]. What plainer? What clearer? Who, I ask, against this manifestation, except a heretical madness, would deny original sin? On account of which sin even now still the body is dead; although now because of righteousness the spirit is life. But *God will give life*, he says, *even to your mortal bodies*. Who would bark against this truth, except the rabies of vanity, which boasts that *it listens to no opinion or flattery to the stain of divine equity*; when rather divine equity is compelled to deny, whoever has been deceived by you? Since so many vices either of bodies or of wits, with which human beings are born, if they are said to draw the merit of no sin, without doubt the just judgment of God is denied. And so, since you so impute sins to the will that you are unwilling to impute original sin to the will of the first man, all the evils which infants draw or suffer, you compel those who believe you to impute to an unjust judgment of God.
