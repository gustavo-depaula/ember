# Book II: To the priest Peter

To the most beloved lord and brother and fellow priest Peter, Augustine the bishop sends greeting in the Lord.

## How you received those books I do not know.

**1. 1.** There have come to me two books of Vincent Victor, which he wrote to your Holiness, our brother Renatus having sent them to me—a man indeed a layman, but, for his faith and that of those he loves, prudently and religiously solicitous. When I had read them, I saw a man who flows in speech not only to sufficiency but even to overflowing, but in the matters of which he wished to speak, not yet instructed as he ought to be: which, if it be granted him by the Lord's gift, he will be able to be useful to many. For he has no small faculty by which he can unfold and adorn what he thinks, if he first takes care to think rightly. For things eloquently said yet wrong are very harmful, because to less learned men, in that they are eloquently said, they seem also true.

But how you yourself received those books I do not know; yet if what I heard is true, you are said, when they were recited, to have leapt up with such joy that you, an old man, kissed the head of that young man, you, a priest, the head of a layman, and gave thanks that you had learned what you did not know. In which indeed I do not disapprove your humility; rather, I even praise that you honored your teacher—not the man, but the very truth that deigned to speak to you through him; provided, however, that you can show what truth you received through him. I would wish, therefore, that by your reply you would teach me what he taught you. For far be it that I should blush to learn from a priest, if you did not blush to learn from a layman, with a humility to be praised and imitated, if you learned true things.

## I desire to know what you are said to have learned from him.

**2. 2.** Accordingly, most beloved brother, I desire to know what you learned from him, so that, if I already knew it, I may congratulate you; but if I did not know it, I may learn through you. Were you, then, unaware that soul and spirit are two distinct things, according to what is written: *You have freed my soul from my spirit*[^1]? And that both belong to the nature of man, so that the whole man is spirit and soul and body, but that sometimes these two together are named by the word "soul," as is that: *And man became a living soul*[^2]?

For there the spirit too is understood. And likewise that sometimes both are called by the word "spirit," as is: *And bowing his head, he gave up the spirit*[^3]? where the soul too must necessarily be understood. And that both are of one substance? I think you already knew these things. But if you did not know them, know that you have not learned something which it is of great peril not to know.

And if anything is to be disputed about this more subtly, it is better dealt with with him, whose eloquence too we now know: whether, when "soul" is said in such a way that the spirit too is understood along with it, both are soul, but the spirit is something of the soul; or whether, as it seemed to him, the whole is named by this word from a part; or whether also both are spirit, but a part of it is what is properly called soul; or whether this too is named the whole from a part, when "spirit" is said in such a way that the soul too is understood along with it: for so it pleases this man. But these things, as I said, are both subtly discussed and are not known without any, or at least without great, peril.

## That there are some senses of the body, others of the soul, I am surprised if this man taught you.

**2. 3.** And likewise that there are some senses of the body, others of the soul, I am surprised if this man taught you; and that you, a man of such age and honor, before you heard this man, thought it to be one and the same thing by which white and black are distinguished, which even the sparrows see along with us, and by which just and unjust are discerned, which Tobit saw[^4] even when the lights of his flesh were lost. If this is so, then surely when you heard or read, *Lighten my eyes, lest I ever sleep in death*[^5], you thought only of the eyes of the flesh. Or if this is obscure, certainly when you recalled that saying of the Apostle, *the eyes of your heart enlightened*[^6], you believed that we have a heart beneath the forehead and above the cheeks. Far be it that I should think this of you. Neither, then, did this man teach you this.

## I fear that perhaps this man taught you what is rather contrary to the faith you held.

**2. 4.** Or if perhaps, before this man's teaching, which you now rejoice to have found, you thought the nature of the soul to be a portion of God, you plainly were unaware that this, with dreadful peril, is false. And if you have learned from this man that the soul is not a portion of God, give God as much thanks as you can that you did not depart from the body before you learned this. For you would have departed a great heretic and a dreadful blasphemer. In no way, however, would I think even this of you, that you, a Catholic man and not a contemptible priest, held the nature of the soul to be a portion of God. Hence I confess to your Charity, I fear that perhaps this man taught you what is rather contrary to the faith you held.

## I fear that perhaps you agreed with this man that God did not make the soul from nothing.

**3. 5.** For just as I do not think that you ever believed in the Catholic Church that the soul is a portion of God, or that in any way the soul and God are of the same nature, so I fear that perhaps you agreed with this man, *that God did not make the soul from nothing, but that it is so from him that it emanated from him.* For this word too this man set down among the rest, by which in this question he strayed to a monstrous precipice. But if he taught you this, I do not want you to teach me; rather, I even want you yourself to unlearn what you had learned.

For it is not enough not to believe or say that the soul is a part of God. For neither do we say that the Son or the Holy Spirit is a part of God; and yet we say that Father and Son and Holy Spirit are of one and the same nature. It is not enough, then, that we should not say the soul is a part of God; but this too is needful, that we should say it and God are not of one and the same nature. Hence he says rightly indeed that *souls are of the kind of God, by gift, not by nature,* and through this not of all, but of the faithful; but again he has slipped back into that from which he had turned aside, and has said that God and the soul are of the same nature—not indeed in these words, but in a clear and manifest sense. For when he says that the soul is so from God that he created it neither from another nature, nor from nothing, but from his very self, what is he trying to persuade us of, except that which in other words he denies—namely, that the soul is of the same nature as God is?

For every nature is either God, who has no author, or is from God, because it has him as its author. But of what has God as its author, from whom it is, something was not made, something was made. Moreover, what was not made, and yet is from him, was either begotten by him, or proceeds from him: what was begotten is the only Son; what proceeds, the Holy Spirit; and this Trinity is of one and the same nature. For these three are one, and each single one God, and all together one God, immutable, everlasting, without any beginning or end of time. But that nature which was made is called a creature; and the Creator is God, namely, that Trinity.

A creature, then, is said to be from God in such a way that it was not made from his nature. For it is said to be from him for this reason, that it has him as its author, so that it exists—not in such a way that it was born of him, or proceeded; but that it was created, founded, made by him, partly from no other thing, that is, altogether from nothing, like heaven and earth, or rather the universal matter created together with the world, of the whole worldly mass;

but partly from another nature already created and existing, as man from clay, woman from man, a man from his parents: yet every creature is from God, but by his creating it either from nothing or from something, not by his begetting or producing it from his very self.

## If you think God makes souls from his very self, you have strayed far from the Catholic faith.

**3. 6.** I speak these things with a Catholic, admonishing rather than teaching. For I do not think they are new to you, or even heard before, yet not believed; but, as I judge, you so read my letter that here you recognize also your own faith, which, by the Lord's gift, is common to us in the Catholic Church. If, then, I speak these things, as I had begun to say, with a Catholic, from where, I beseech you, do you believe the soul to be—I do not say of each of us, but the first soul given to that first man? If from nothing, and yet made and breathed in by God: you believe what I do. But if from some other creature, which lay before God the maker as it were a material from which the soul would be made—as the dust from which Adam would be made, or his rib from which Eve, or as the waters from which fish and birds, or as the earth from which all the land animals[^7]—it is not Catholic, it is not true. But if you think that God made, or makes, souls neither from nothing, nor from any other creature whatever, but from his very self, that is, from his nature—which God forbid—this indeed you learned from this man; but I do not congratulate you, nor flatter you; you have strayed, along with him, far from the Catholic faith.

For more tolerably—what is indeed false, yet, as I said, more tolerably—would you believe the soul fashioned from some other creature, which God had already made, than from the nature of God; so that what is mutable, what sins, what becomes impious, what, if it has persisted impious to the end, will be damned without end, might not be referred, with horrible blasphemy, to the nature of God. Cast away, brother, cast away, I beseech you, that error—not, plainly, a faith, but an error of execrable impiety—lest you, a serious man seduced by a young man, and a priest by a layman, while you judge this to be the Catholic faith, be removed from the number of the faithful, which may the Lord avert from you.

For one must not deal with you as with him; nor is this so dreadful an error of yours worthy of the same pardon as that young man's, although it passed from him to you. He recently came to the Catholic sheepfold to be healed; you are counted among the Catholic shepherds. We do not want her who came from error to the Lord's flock to be so treated that the ulcerous sheep first destroys the shepherd by a pestilent contagion.

## Let an empty rumor be refuted by your letter.

**3. 7.** But if you say, This man did not teach me that, nor did I in any way consent to this error of his, however enticed by the sweetness of his eloquent and ornate speech: I give immense thanks to God. But I ask, why did you, having kissed his head, as is said, give thanks that you had learned what, until you heard his disputation, you did not know? Or if it is false that you did and said this, this very thing I ask you to deign to make known to us, so that an empty rumor may be refuted by your letter. But if it is true that you gave thanks to the man with that humility, I rejoice indeed if he did not teach you that which above I showed to be how detestable and to be guarded against;

**4. 7.** And I do not blame your having been grateful with such great humility to a teacher, if, in his disputing, you learned something else true and useful; but I inquire what this is—whether, perhaps, that the soul is not a spirit, but a body? I do not indeed judge it a great loss to Christian doctrine to be ignorant of these things; and if one disputes subtly about the kinds of bodies, this is learned thoroughly with more difficulty than profit. But if the Lord wills that I write to that young man himself, as I desire, there perhaps your Charity will know even how he did not teach you this—if, however, you rejoice that you learned it from him. But lest perhaps there be something else which is agreed to be useful and to pertain to necessary faith, I ask that you not be reluctant to write back.

## The parable of the rich man and the poor man.

**4. 8.** For that which he most rightly and very wholesomely believes—that souls are judged when they have departed from their bodies, before they come to that judgment by which they must be judged with their bodies now restored, and be tormented or glorified in the very flesh in which they lived here—was this, after all, what you yourself did not know? Who has grown so deaf, with such obstinacy of mind, against the Gospel that, in that poor man who after death was carried into Abraham's bosom, and in that rich man whose torment in hell is set forth[^8], he does not hear these things, or, hearing them, does not believe them?

But did he teach you how a soul without a body could desire a drop of water from the poor man's finger, when he himself confessed that the soul seeks bodily nourishment only for the sake of propping up the falling-down of its corruptible body? These are his words: *Do we believe,* he says, *that because the soul seeks either food or drink, the nourishment passes over to it?* And a little after: *From this it is understood,* he says, *and proved, that the supports of food belong not to the soul but to the body, for which also, besides food, clothing is procured by like reasoning, so that the supply of pasture seems necessary to that to which it also belongs to have the clothing itself.* This opinion of his, set forth clearly enough, he added to, illustrating it also by a certain comparison, and said: *But what do we think a tenant provides for his dwelling? Does he not, if he has felt it either tremble in its roof, or sway in its wall, or totter in its foundation, seek props and pile up heaps by which he may carefully and diligently shore up the impending ruin, lest the safety of the dweller seem to hang under the peril of the dwelling? So, then, recognize,* he says, *that the soul too desires food for its flesh, from which without doubt it conceives the very desire.* These things, indeed, that young man unfolded with most lucid and most sufficient words, asserting that nourishment is required not for the soul but for the body, by the care of the soul indeed, but as one dwelling in a house and, by provident refreshment, shoring up the impending ruins of the dying flesh.

Let him, then, explain to you also this: what that soul of the rich man, which surely no longer had a mortal body, and yet thirsted and sought a drop of water from the poor man's finger, desired to prop up against falling. This teacher of old men has where to exercise himself: let him seek and find, if he can, for what purpose that soul among the dead would beg for moist nourishment, even so scanty, when it now lacked its ruinous dwelling.

## God, who is incorporeal, exhales a corporeal breath from his very self!

**5. 9.** That he believes God to be incorporeal, indeed, I am glad that in this at least he is set apart from the ravings of Tertullian. For Tertullian contends that, as the soul, so also God is corporeal[^9]. Dissenting from him in this, this man undertakes to persuade us of more astonishing things: that God, who is incorporeal, makes a breath not from nothing, but exhales a corporeal breath from his very self.

O the doctrine at which every age should prick up its ears, which deserves to have men advanced in years, and even priests, as disciples! Let him read—let him read in assembly what he has written; let him invite the known and the unknown, the learned and the unlearned, to hear him recite it. Elders, come together with the younger; learn what you did not know; hear what you had never heard.

Behold, by this man's teaching, God creates a breath not from something else that in some way exists, nor from that which altogether does not exist; but from that which he himself is, since he is incorporeal, he breathes out a body. His own nature, then, before it is changed in the body of sin, he himself changes into a body.

Or does he say that he does not change anything from his nature, when he makes a breath? Then he does not make it from his very self: for he himself is not one thing, and his nature another. Who, most insane, would think this? But if he says that God so makes a breath from his nature that he himself remains entire, the question is not from there; but whether that which is not from something else, nor from nothing, but from him, is not the same as he is—that is, of the same nature and essence.

For with the Son begotten too he remains entire; but because he begot him from his very self, he begot nothing other than that which he himself is. For, except that he assumed manhood, and *the Word was made flesh*[^10], the Word, the Son of God, is indeed another person, but is not another thing—that is, he is another person, but not a different nature. And from where is this, except that he was not created from another, or from nothing, but born from him—not so as to be better than he was, but altogether so as to be, and to be that which is he from whom he was born; that is, of one and the same nature, equal, coeternal, in every way like, equally immutable, equally invisible, equally incorporeal, equally God; altogether that which the Father is, except that the Son is himself the Son, not the Father?

But if he himself indeed remains God entire, and yet creates something different and worse, not from nothing, nor from another, but from his very self, and a body emanates from the incorporeal God—God forbid that a Catholic mind should drink this in: for it is not a stream from the divine fountain, but a figment of the human heart.

## It is he who must sweat to give an account of his sayings.

**6. 10.** Now, how ineptly he labors to vindicate the soul, which he thinks to be corporeal, from the passions of the body, disputing about the soul's infancy, about the paralyzed and the oppressed senses of the soul, about the limbs of the body amputated without the cutting of the soul—I ought to deal not with you, but rather with him: for it is he who must sweat to give an account of his sayings; lest we seem to wish to weary the seriousness of an old man over the work of a young man. But as to his disputing that the likenesses of character found in sons do not come from the seed of the soul: it is indeed a consequence that whoever does away with the propagation of the soul should hold this; but not even those who establish it place the weight of their assertion there. For they see sons unlike their parents in character too; which they think comes about because one and the same man often has, in his own character, other characters unlike it—not, of course, another soul received, but his life changed for the better or for the worse. So they say it is not impossible that a soul does not have the character which that one has from which it was propagated;

since one and the same soul can now have one character, at another time another. Wherefore, if you believe you learned this from him, that the soul is not from transmission: would that you had truly learned it; I would most gladly hand you over to be taught. But it is one thing to learn, another to seem to oneself to have learned. If, then, you think you have learned what you still do not know, you have plainly not learned, but rashly believed what you gladly heard, and a false speech crept upon you through a sweet speech.

I do not say this because I am now certain that it is false that souls are rather breathed in new than drawn from the origin of the parents; for this I think must still be inquired of those who can teach it: but because this man has discussed this matter in such a way that he not only does not resolve the question, which must still be examined, but even says such things as have no doubt of their falsity. For when he wished to prove doubtful things, he dared to say things to be rejected without doubt.

## See in these words how greatly this teacher of yours has erred.

**7. 11.** Or will you doubt to reject this, that, when he was speaking of the soul, he said: *You do not want the soul to contract ill health from the flesh of sin, to which in turn you may see sanctification pass through the flesh, so that through it it may recover the state through which it had lost its merit? Or is it that, because in Baptism the body is washed, what is believed to be conferred through Baptism does not pass over to the soul or the spirit? Rightly, then, through the flesh it recovers its former condition, which it had seemed for a little while to have lost through the flesh, so that through it it may begin to be reborn, through which it had deserved to be defiled.*

See in these words how greatly this teacher of yours has erred. He said that *the soul recovers through the flesh the state through which it had lost its merit.* The soul, then, had some state and some good merit before the flesh, which he wants it to recover through the flesh, when the flesh is washed by the bath of regeneration. It had therefore lived somewhere before the flesh in a good state and merit, which state and which merit it lost when it had come into the flesh. He said that *it recovers through the flesh its former condition, which it had seemed for a little while to have lost through the flesh.* It had, then, before the flesh an ancient condition—for this is what "former" means—and what could that condition be but a blessed and praiseworthy one? Which he asserts is recovered through the sacrament of Baptism; though he does not want it to draw its origin by propagation from that soul which is agreed once to have been happy in paradise.

How, then, does he say in another place that *he steadfastly asserts the soul to be not from transmission, nor from nothing, nor through its very self, nor before the body?* Behold, in this place he wants souls to live somewhere before the body so blessedly that the same blessedness is restored to them through Baptism. And, as though again forgetful of himself, he adds and says: *so that through it,* that is, through the flesh, *it may begin to be reborn, through which it had deserved to be defiled.* Above he had signified that a good merit had been lost through the flesh; but now he signifies an evil merit, by which it came about that it came or was sent into the flesh, by saying, *through which it had deserved to be defiled.* For if it deserves to be defiled, it is surely not a good merit. Let him say what it sinned before it was defiled through the flesh, so that it deserved to be defiled through the flesh. Let him say it, if he can—which he can in no way; because to find here what he may say that is true, he is altogether unable.

## What you praised in the reciter, or why after the recitation you gave thanks, did you consider?

**8. 12.** Likewise somewhat after he says: *And so the soul, if it deserved to be sinful, which could not be sinful, yet did not remain in sin, because, prefigured in Christ, it ought not to be in sin, just as it could not be.* I ask you, brother, do you think—did you at least afterward read and consider these things, and did you consider what you praised in the reciter, or why after the recitation you gave thanks—what is it, I beseech you: *And so the soul, if it deserved to be sinful, which could not be sinful?* What is *it deserved* and *it could not,* when it could not have deserved this unless it had been sinful, and would not have been unless it could have been—so that, sinning before all evil merit, it made for itself thence a merit by which it might come to other sins, the Lord forsaking it?

Or did he say *which could not be sinful* because, unless it came into the flesh, it could not be sinful? What, then, did it deserve, so as to be sent where it could be sinful, and where, had it not come, it could not be sinful elsewhere? Let him say what it deserved. For if it deserved to be sinful, it had already sinned something, whereby it deserved again to be sinful. But these things perhaps seem obscure, or are boasted to be obscure, though they are most plain. For he ought not to have said that the soul deserved to be sinful through the flesh, of which he can find neither a good nor an evil merit before the flesh.

## He first tried to fortify his opinion by the foreknowledge of God.

**9. 13.** But let us come to clearer matters. For when he was hemmed in by great straits as to how souls are held bound by the bond of original sin, if they do not draw their origin from that one which first sinned, but the Creator breathes them, pure from all contagion and propagation of sin, into the sinful flesh—lest it be said to him that by so breathing them in God makes them guilty—he first tried to fortify this opinion by the foreknowledge of God, *that he had prepared redemption for them.* In the sacrament of which redemption little ones are baptized, that original sin may be washed away, which they drew from the flesh; as though God were amending his own deeds, in that he had made them, though innocent, to be defiled.

But after it came to his speaking of those who are not aided by such help, and breathe out before they are baptized, he says: *In this place I do not pledge myself as an author, but conjecture something from an example. We say that an account of this kind is to be held concerning infants who, predestined to Baptism, are overtaken by the death of the present life before they are reborn in Christ. For we read,* he says, *written of such: He was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding, or lest deceit beguile his soul. For this cause he hastened to lead him out of the midst of iniquity: for his soul was pleasing to God*[^11]; and: *Being made perfect in a short while, he fulfilled long times*[^12].

Who would disdain to have this man as a teacher? So, then, little ones, whom men often want baptized, and who, while there is a rush to do it, die before, if they were delayed in this life a little while, so that, baptized, they would die immediately, wickedness would change their understanding, and deceit would beguile their souls, and, lest this befall them, it was provided for them that they should be caught up before they were baptized? In the very Baptism, then, they would be changed for the worse and beguiled by deceit, if after Baptism they were caught up. O doctrine to be admired and followed—nay rather, to be detested and execrated! But this man presumed this from your prudence, you who were present

when he recited it, and most of all from yours, to whom he wrote and to whom he handed over the recited books, that he might be confident you would believe to be written of unbaptized infants what is written of the immature ages of all the saints—those with whom fools judge it to have gone ill if they have been quickly caught up from this life, and could not reach the years that men wish for themselves as a great divine gift.

But what is it to say that *infants predestined to Baptism are overtaken by the death of the present life before they are reborn in Christ,* as though some force of fortune or fate or of any thing whatever did not permit God to fulfill what he had predestined? And how does he himself catch them up, because they pleased him? Or does he both predestine them to be baptized, and himself not allow what he predestined to come to pass?

## To the unbaptized the Lord granted not the kingdom of heaven, but paradise.

**10. 14.** But attend to what he still dares, he to whom, in so great a depth of this question, our hesitation, more cautious than learned, is displeasing. *I would dare to say,* he says, *that these can attain to the pardon of original sins, yet not so as to be led into the heavenly kingdom: just as to the thief who confessed indeed, but was not baptized, the Lord granted not the kingdom of heaven, but paradise*[^13]; *although surely that saying still stood: Whoever has not been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^14]. *Especially since the Lord professes that there are many mansions with his Father*[^15], *in which are designated the many and diverse merits of the dwellers: so that here the unbaptized is led to pardon, the baptized to the palm, which is prepared through grace.*

You see the man separating paradise and the mansions that are with the Father from the kingdom of heaven, so that even to the unbaptized places of everlasting happiness abound. Nor does he see, when he says these things, that in so unwilling to separate the mansion of any baptized little one from the kingdom of heaven, he does not fear to separate from it the very house of God the Father, or some parts of it. For the Lord Jesus did not say, "In the universe of creation," or "in any part of the universe," but: *In my Father's house,* he said, *are many mansions*[^16]. How, then, will the unbaptized be in the house of God the Father, when he cannot have God as Father unless reborn? Let him not be ungrateful to God, who deigned to free him from the division of the Donatists or Rogatists, so as to seek to divide the very house of God the Father, and to place some part of it outside the kingdom of heaven, where the unbaptized may dwell. And by what reasoning does he presume that he himself will enter the kingdom of heaven, from which kingdom he excludes the house of the very king in as great a part as he wishes?

But from that thief, who, crucified beside the Lord, hoped in the Lord though crucified, and from Dinocrates, the brother of holy Perpetua, he argues that pardon of sins and some seat of the blessed can be given even to the unbaptized: as though anyone whom it were impious not to believe had indicated to him that they had not been baptized. About whom, however, in that book which I wrote to our brother Renatus, I set forth more fully what seemed to me. Which your Charity will be able to know, if you do not scorn to read it; for he will not be able to deny it to one who asks.

## I judge that the sacrifices of priests should be offered for them.

**11. 15.** Yet this man seethes, and is suffocated by dreadful straits. For perhaps he attends more closely than you to the evil he says—namely, that original sin is loosed in little ones apart from the Baptism of Christ. Finally, that in this cause he may to some extent, even if late, take refuge in the sacraments of the Church, *For these, certainly,* he says, *I judge that constant offerings, and the sacrifices of the holy priests, should be offered continually.*

Have this man, if you please, even as a censor, if it was too little to have him as a teacher, so that you offer the sacrifice of the body of Christ even for those who are not incorporated into Christ. For when he dared to insert into his books a thing so new, and so foreign to ecclesiastical discipline and to the rule of truth, he did not say "I think," he did not say "I suppose," he did not say "I judge"; he did not say even "I suggest" or "I say," but "I rule," so that, namely, if we were offended by the novelty or perversity of the opinion, we might be terrified by the authority of his ruling.

You must see, brother, how you can endure this man teaching: yet the Catholic priests who are soundly wise, among whom you too ought to be joined, far be it that they should consent to hear this man ruling—one whom they rather wish to be coming to his senses and grieving, and repenting, with a most wholesome correction, that he thought, nay rather even wrote, these things. *But this,* he says, *I maintain must be done by the example of the Maccabees falling in battle, who, when they secretly carried off forbidden things and fell in the very contest, from the priests,* he says, *we find that this counsel was taken: that the offering of sacrifices should restore those whose souls guilt from a forbidden thing had bound*[^17]. So he says this, as though he had read those sacrifices offered for the uncircumcised, just as he ruled these of ours to be offered for the unbaptized. For circumcision was the sacrament of that time, which prefigured the Baptism of our time.

## Although that principal saying stands in the way.

**12. 16.** Nevertheless this man, in comparison with what he afterward appeared, still errs more tolerably. For, as though he repented—not of what he ought to have repented, that is, of having dared to assert that original sin is relaxed for the unbaptized, and pardon of all sins given, so that they are sent into paradise, that is, a place of such great happiness, and deserve to have blessed mansions in the Father's house; but rather of having granted them seats of lesser blessedness outside the kingdom of heaven—he added and said: *Or if perhaps anyone struggles against it—that paradise was conferred for the present, temporarily, on the soul of the thief or of Dinocrates (for there still remains for them, in the resurrection, the reward of the kingdom of heaven, although that principal saying stands in the way, that whoever has not been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^18])*—let him nonetheless hold also my assent in this part, as one not envying, provided he both enlarge the effect of divine mercy and foreknowledge, and its affection.* These words I copied as read in his second book. Could anyone, in this cause, have greater audacity, rashness, presumption of error? He himself recalls the Lord's saying, he himself mentions it, he himself inserts it into his writings, he himself says: *although that principal saying stands in the way, that whoever has not been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit will not enter the kingdom of heaven*; and yet he dares to raise the neck of his ruling against the principal saying! *Let him hold,* he says, *also my assent, as one not envying:* he who says that the souls of the unbaptized deserve paradise temporarily—for on their account, the thief and Dinocrates, he makes mention, as though prescribing, or rather prejudging—but in the resurrection are transferred to better things and receive the reward of the kingdom of heaven: *although,* he says, *the principal saying stands in the way.* Now, then, consider yourself, I beg you, brother: whoever gives his assent to anyone against the authority of the principal saying, what saying will he deserve from the prince?

## Vincent Victor dared more than the Pelagians.

**12. 17.** The novel Pelagian heretics the authority of the Catholic councils and of the Apostolic See most justly condemned, because they dared to give to unbaptized little ones a place of rest and salvation, even outside the kingdom of heaven. Which they would not have dared, unless they denied that they have original sin, which would need to be loosed through the sacrament of Baptism. But this man, like a Catholic, says that little ones are bound by original sin, and yet he absolves them from such a bond without the bath of regeneration, and mercifully sends them after death into paradise; but after the resurrection, more mercifully, even leads them into the kingdom of heaven. Such a merciful man Saul seemed to himself when he spared the king whom God had commanded to be killed[^19]: but his disobedient mercy, or merciful disobedience, was deservedly reproved and condemned; that a man may beware lest he deserve mercy from him, against the saying of him by whom man was made.

The Truth thunders through the mouth of his own body: *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^20]. And, that he may make the martyrs exempt from this sentence—those to whom it has fallen to be killed for the name of Christ before being washed by the Baptism of Christ—he says in another place: *Whoever loses his soul for my sake will find it*[^21]. And, lest to anyone who has not been reborn by the bath of the Christian faith the abolition of original sin be promised, the Apostle cries out: *Through the offense of one upon all men unto condemnation*[^22]. Against which condemnation the Lord, showing that there is one help of salvation, says: *Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved; but whoever does not believe will be condemned*[^23]. The mystery of which believing is fulfilled in little ones through the response of those by whom they are carried; lest, if it be not done, all go from one into condemnation. And yet, against such manifest voices, which the truth sings in harmony, there comes forth into the midst a vanity more witless than merciful, and says: Not only do little ones not go into damnation, even if no bath of the Christian faith absolves them from the bond of original sin; but they even enjoy meanwhile, after death, the happiness of paradise, and after the resurrection will possess also the happiness of the kingdom of heaven. Would this man dare to say these things against the most firmly founded Catholic faith, if he did not dare to take up the question of the soul's origin, which is to be resolved—a question exceeding his powers?

## Our aged hesitation seemed cheap to the young man.

**13. 18.** For he was hemmed in by dreadful straits by those who say: *Why did God punish the soul with so unjust a chastisement as to will to banish it into a body of sin, since it begins to be sinful through fellowship with the sinful flesh, when it could not be sinful?* For surely they say: The soul could not be sinful unless God had mingled it with the fellowship of sinful flesh. By what justice, then, God did this, since this man could not find—especially because of the eternal damnation of dying little ones, for whom, unbaptized, original sin would not have been expiated: why, then, the just and good God, by sending into the body which is drawn from Adam the souls of little ones, for whom he foreknew the sacrament of Christian grace would not come to aid, souls free from all guilt of propagation, has bound them with the bond of original sin, and in this way made them guilty of eternal damnation,

since he could not find this, and would not say that these too draw their sinful origin from that one: he preferred to depart by a miserable shipwreck rather than, with sails lowered and the oars of his disputation held back, to curb his rash course by provident deliberation. For our aged hesitation seemed cheap to the young man, as though for this most troublesome and most dangerous question an impulse of eloquence were more necessary than the counsel of prudence. And he himself foresaw this too, but in vain. For, about to set before himself these objections as though from adversaries, he says: *Hence other reproaches are heaped up by the querulous murmurings of those who bark against us, and, shaken as if by some whirlwind, again and again we are dashed among monstrous rocks.* Having foretold these things, he set before himself the most rocky question mentioned above, where he was shipwrecked from the Catholic faith, unless by repenting he repairs what he broke.

Avoiding that whirlwind and those rocks, I was unwilling to commit my ship to them; and I wrote of this matter in such a way as to show the reasoning of my hesitation rather than the rashness of presumption. When he had found this little work of mine in your possession, he laughed at it, and committed himself to those crags with an impulse more spirited than considered. But where this overconfidence has brought him, I think you now see; and I give the more abundant thanks to God if you saw it even before. For when he would not restrain his headlong course because of the doubtful outcome, he found a miserable onset, asserting that God confers on little ones who have died without Christian regeneration both paradise now and afterward the kingdom of heaven.

## The testimonies of the Scriptures are uncertain and ambiguous, as far as this question is concerned.

**14. 19.** But whatever testimonies of the Scriptures he set down, by which he tried, as it were, to prove that God does not draw souls from the propagation of that first one, but breathes their own into each individual, as he did that first one, they are, as far as this question is concerned, so uncertain and ambiguous that they can most easily be taken otherwise than he wishes. Which I have already, as I judge, sufficiently shown in that book which I wrote to our friend, whom I mentioned above. For the very testimonies that he adduced, where it is read that God either gives, or makes, or forms souls, do not show from where he gives them, or from where he makes or forms them—whether from the propagation of that first one, or by breathing in, as he did that first one. But this man, from the very fact that it is read that God gives souls, whether he makes or forms them, already thinks that the propagation of souls is denied: although God, the same Scripture witnessing, also gives, or makes, or forms bodies, which nonetheless no one doubts are given, made, formed by him from the propagation of seed.

## Other testimonies must be sought, which are not found ambiguous.

**14. 20.** Likewise, that it is written that God made *every race of men from one blood*[^24]; or that Adam said, *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^25]: because neither there was it said "from one soul," nor here, "soul of my soul,"

he thinks the souls of sons are denied to be from the parents, or that of that woman from the man: as though, indeed, if it were said not *from one blood,* but "from one soul," anything other than the whole man would be understood, and the propagation of the body would not be denied. So too, even if it were said, "soul of my soul," the flesh would surely not be denied, which it was agreed had been taken from him.

For the Scripture often signifies the whole by a part, as also the part by the whole. For certainly, if it were written in that place from which this man adduced his testimony, not *from one blood,* but "from one man," that the human race was established, it would not prejudge those who deny the propagation of souls; although man is not soul alone, nor flesh alone, but both. For they would answer that the Scripture could have signified the part by the whole—that is, by "man" the flesh of man alone. So, then, those too who defend the propagation of souls contend that what was said, *from one blood,*—by "blood," namely, "man"—that is, the whole was signified by a part.

For just as it seems to help them that it was said *from one blood,* and it was not said "from one man," so it seems also to help these others that it was said: *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed upon all men, in whom all sinned*[^26]; and it was not said, "in whom the flesh of all sinned." And likewise, just as it seems to help them that it was said, *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^27], because a part was named, not the whole, so again it helps these others what immediately follows there: *She shall be called woman, because she was taken from her man*[^28]. For it ought to have been said, they say, "because her flesh was taken from her man," if not the whole woman, that is, with the soul, but the flesh alone were taken from the man[^29].

Moreover, when both sides are heard, one who judges without partisan zeal surely sees that neither are those testimonies, where a part is named, to be brought forward against those who defend the propagation of souls—because the Scripture could there signify the whole by a part, just as, when we read *the Word was made flesh*[^30], we surely understand not flesh alone, but the whole man—nor are those to be brought forward against those who do away with the propagation of souls, where not a part of man but the whole is mentioned—because the Scripture could there signify the part by the whole, just as we confess Christ buried, though his flesh alone was buried. And through this we say that the propagation of souls is neither rashly to be established nor rashly to be done away with; but we admonish that other testimonies must be sought, which are not found ambiguous.

## What this man taught you, and why you gave thanks, I do not yet know.

**15. 21.** Wherefore, what this man taught you, and why you gave thanks, I do not yet know. For that question remains as it was, in which it is asked about the origin of souls, whether God gives, makes, forms them for men from the propagation of that one which he first breathed into man, or from his breath, as he did for the first man—souls which the Christian faith does not doubt that he gives, makes, forms.

When this man tried to resolve this question, and did not regard his own powers, doing away with the propagation of souls, and asserting that the Creator breathes them, pure from all contagion of sin, not from nothing but from his very self, he defamed the nature of God with the reproach of mutability, which had not been necessary;

and while he wishes to give an account, lest God be believed unjust, if he binds with the bond of original sin souls pure from all sin, even those whom he does not redeem by Christian regeneration, he said things which I do not want him to have taught you. For he attributed to unbaptized little ones as much of salvation and happiness as not even the Pelagian heresy could. And yet, concerning so many thousands of little ones who are born of the impious and die among the impious—not those whom men, by Baptism, when they wish, cannot aid, but those about whose baptizing no one could or will be able to think; nor has anyone offered, or will offer, the sacrifice for them, which this man ruled to be offered even for the unbaptized—he found nothing to say.

About whom, if he be asked what their souls deserved, that God should insert them—neither to be washed by Baptism, nor to be expiated by the sacrifice of the body and blood of Christ, and to be damned forever—into sinful flesh, either he will be altogether stuck, and our hesitation will please him, even if late; or, for all little ones together who die throughout the whole world without Christian Baptism, even with their names passed over in silence, since they are not known in the Church of Christ, not incorporated into the body of Christ, he will rule that the body of Christ is to be offered.

## Vincent Victor laid upon your shoulders the religious burden of his correction and amendment.

**16. 22.** Far be it from you, brother, that these things should please you; far be it that you should rejoice either to have learned them, or presume to teach them: otherwise he himself will be found far better than you. For in the opening of his first book he spoke modestly and humbly, saying: *While I desire to obey you, I have incurred the mark of presumption;* and a little after: *Since I am not myself so credulous as to think that what I shall say can be proved; and I am eager always not to defend even my own opinion, if it be detected as improbable; and it is at heart with me, my own judgment being condemned, rather to pursue better things and those that are truer.*

*For as it is the mark of an excellent purpose and praiseworthy counsel to be easily led across to truer things, so it is the mark of a wicked and obstinate judgment to be unwilling to be bent the sooner to the path of reason.* For if he said these things truthfully, and feels as he spoke, he surely bears a mind of great hope. Likewise at the end of the second book: *Nor think that it must perhaps be recalled to your displeasure, since I place the judgment of my sayings in your power. And lest perhaps the gaze of some curious reader be disturbed and offended by the traces of the letters remaining among the smeared fibers, tear apart the woven sequence of the page with a severe thumb; and, with me free from this censure, punish the ink that has marked unworthy utterances; lest on this occasion both your judgment toward me, by which you greatly indulge me, and my own ineptitudes that lay hidden, be laughed at.*

## Do not act against love or against truth.

**17. 23.** Since, then, he both fortified his books at the beginning and secured them at the end with this, and laid upon your shoulders the religious burden of his correction and amendment, let him find with you what he asked: that, just in mercy, you amend him and reprove him; but the oil of the sinner, with which his head may be made fat[^31], far be it from your hands and eyes—that is, the unbecoming flattery of one who fawns, and the deceptive softness of one who soothes. But if you neglect to amend him, when you see that he is to be amended, you act against love; but if he does not seem to you to be amended for this reason, because you think he thought rightly about these things, you are wise against truth. And therefore he is better, being more ready to be amended, if an amender is not lacking, than you, if either knowingly you scornfully despise one erring, or unknowingly you alike pursue error.

And so, in the same books written to you and handed over to you, consider all things soberly and watchfully, and you will perhaps find more to blame than I. And whatever things there are there to be approved and praised, if perhaps in them there was indeed something you did not know, and you learned it from this man's discoursing, plainly profess what it is; so that all who either heard along with you when he recited, or afterward read those same books, may know that for this you gave thanks, not for those many things there that are to be disapproved:

lest in his ornate eloquence, as in a precious cup, with you inviting, even if not drinking, they drink poison, if they do not know what you drank from it and what you did not drink, and, on account of your praise, judge that everything there is to be drunk wholesomely. Although both to hear, and to read, and to draw into the memory what has been said—what is it but to drink?

But the Lord foretold of his faithful that *even if they drink anything deadly, it will not harm them*[^32]. And through this, those who read with judgment, and according to the rule of faith approve what is to be approved and disapprove what is to be disapproved, even if they commit to memory the things said that are to be disapproved, are harmed by no poisoned perversity of the opinions. That I have admonished or warned your Seriousness and Religion of these things, whether by mutual or by anticipating love, I shall by no means repent, the Lord being merciful, however you take what I thought should be paid forward to you. But I shall give abundant thanks to him, in whose mercy it is most wholesome to trust, if this letter either finds your faith free and whole from these perversities and errors, which I have been able to show from this man's books in this letter, or makes it so.
