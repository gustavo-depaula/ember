# Book IV: To Vincent Victor

## You reproved in me things which humility does not compel me to correct, but which truth compels me partly to confess, partly to defend.

**1. 1.** Receive now also concerning myself the things I desire to say to you, if I can—that is, if he grants it, in whose hand are both we and our words. For you reproved me twice over, expressing even my name: and although at the beginning of your book you called yourself quite conscious of your own inexperience and destitute of the support of learning, but called me, where you named me, most learned and most skilled, nevertheless, in those matters in which you seemed to yourself to know what I either confess that I do not know, or, though I do not know it, presume to know, with the liberty that was fitting, you did not hesitate to reprove—not only an old man as a young man, and a bishop as a layman, but even a man, by your own judgment, most learned and most skilled.

But I both do not know myself to be most learned and most skilled—nay rather, I know most certainly that I am not—and I do not doubt that it can come to pass that something falls to the knowledge of some inexperienced and unlearned person which some learned and skilled person is ignorant of: and in this I plainly praise you, that you preferred truth, even if not such as you grasped, at least such as you thought, to a man; rashly, indeed, because you supposed you knew what you do not know, but freely, because, not revering a person, you chose to open what you think. Hence you ought to understand how much greater care ought to be ours to recall the Lord's sheep from errors, if even for sheep it is shameful to conceal from the shepherds themselves the vices of the shepherds, if they have known any.

Oh, if only you reproved those things of mine which are worthy of just reproof! For I ought not to deny that, as in my very character, so there are many things in my so many little works which could be blamed by right judgment and with no rashness. Of which, if you yourself reproved some, there perhaps I would show you what kind of person I would wish you to be, in those things in which you were not wrongly reproved;

and I, your elder, to you the younger, and the one set over to the subordinate, would exhibit an example of correction, the more wholesome the more humble. But you reproved in me things which humility does not compel me to correct, but truth compels me partly to confess, partly to defend.

## You reproved, first, that I did not dare to determine anything about the origin of souls after the first man.

**2. 2.** But these are the things: one, that about the origin of souls which were given, or are given, to men after the first man, I did not dare to determine anything, because I confess that I do not know; the other, that I said I knew the soul to be a spirit, not a body. But in this latter you reproved two things: one, that I did not believe it to be a body; the other, that I believed it to be a spirit. For it seems to you that the soul both is a body, and is not a spirit.

Hear, then, my defense against your reproof, and from this occasion, by which I clear myself to you, learn in yourself what you should purge. Recall the words of your book, where you first named me. *I know,* you say, *that very many, and easily most skilled men, and moreover prudent ones, have kept silence, or said nothing more express, when they withdrew a definition from their disputations with the exposition begun—as I lately read repeatedly in your possession, comprised in the letters of Augustine, a most learned man and a celebrated bishop—more modestly indeed, as I think, and more bashfully, searching out the secrets of this matter, having swallowed within themselves the judgment of their own treatise, and having professed that they could determine nothing further about this. But to me, believe, it seems absurd enough and more, and incongruous with reason, that man himself should be without knowledge of himself, or that he who is believed to have attained the knowledge of all things should be held unknown to himself.*

*But how does man differ from a beast, if he does not know how to inquire and discourse about his own quality and nature; so that there deservedly applies to him what is written: Man, when he was in honor, did not understand; he was likened to the beasts, and compared to them*[^1]? *For since the good God founded nothing without reason, and procreated man himself a rational animal, capable of understanding, possessed of reason, and lively in sense, who distributes all things devoid of reason by a prudent ordering, what can be said so unfittingly as that he should have defrauded him of the knowledge of himself alone? And since the wisdom of the world, which extends itself, with a superfluous investigation indeed, even to the knowledge of the true—because it cannot know through whom it is permitted to know the things that are true—has nonetheless attempted to discern, concerning the nature of the soul, some things near, nay rather akin, to truth: how unbecoming and shameful it is that any religious man should about this very thing either know nothing, or have utterly forbidden himself to know!*[^2]

## However far we advance in this life, we are ignorant of some things pertaining to nature.

**2. 3.** This most eloquent and most lucid chastisement of yours, upon our ignorance, compels you so to know all things that pertain to the nature of man, that, if you were ignorant of any of them, you would be compared to the beasts, not by my judgment but by your own. For although you seem to touch us the more pointedly, in that you said, *Man, when he was in honor, did not understand*[^3]—because we are in that honor of the Church in which you are not—nevertheless you too are in that honor of nature, so that you are preferred to the beasts, to which, by your own judgment, you are to be compared, if you are ignorant of any of those things which are agreed to pertain to your nature. For you did not sprinkle this reproof only on those who do not know what I do not know—that is, the origin of the human soul—which indeed I do not utterly not know; for I know that God breathed into the face of the first man, and man became a living soul; which, however, unless I had read it, I could not know of myself—but you said: *But how does man differ from a beast, if he does not know how to inquire and discourse about his own quality and nature?*

Which you seem so to have meant, as though man ought so to inquire and discourse about his whole quality and nature that nothing of himself should lie hidden from him. But if this is so, I will now compare you to the beasts, if you do not answer me the number of your hairs. But if you grant that, however far we advance in this life, we are ignorant of some things pertaining to our nature, I ask how far and to what extent you grant it; lest perhaps this too be among them, that we do not in every way know the origin of our soul: although, as far as pertains to the salvation of faith, we have known, all evasions removed, that the soul was divinely given, and that it is not of that nature of which God is.

Or do you perhaps think that each one's nature is to be unknown only so far as you do not know it, and is to be known only so far as you have been able to know it: so that if anyone knew it a little more than you, you would compare him to the beasts, because you could have been more knowing than he; and so, if anyone knew it a little more than you, by the same justice he will compare you to the beasts? Say, then, how far you grant us to be ignorant of our nature, that our distance from the beasts may be safe: and consider, nevertheless, lest he be more distant from the beasts who knows that he does not know something of it, than he who thinks he knows what he does not know.

Surely the whole nature of man is spirit, soul, and body: whoever, then, wants to alienate the body from human nature is a fool. Yet the physicians who are called anatomists searched, by dissecting through the members, through the veins, through the nerves, through the bones, through the marrow, through the inner vital parts, even living men, so long as they could live among the hands of those probing them, in order to know the nature of the body: nor yet did they, because we do not know these things, compare us to the beasts.

Unless perhaps you are going to say that those are to be compared to the beasts who do not know the nature of the soul, not those who do not know that of the body. Then you ought not to have spoken at the outset in that way. For you did not say, "How does man differ from a beast, if he does not know about the quality and nature of his soul"; but you said, *if he does not know how to inquire and discourse about his own quality and nature.* Surely our quality and our nature are reckoned with the body, although the things of which we consist are discoursed of each one singly. But how many things I could most knowingly dispute about the nature of man, if I wished to unfold them, I would fill many volumes: yet I confess that I am ignorant of many things.

## You yourself were ignorant about the breath of man.

**3. 4.** But to what do you wish to pertain what we disputed in the earlier book about the breath of man—whether to the nature of the soul, because it itself does this in man; or to that of the body, which is moved by it to do this; or to that of this air, by whose to-and-fro motion it is shown to do this; or rather to all three, namely, to the soul which moves the body, and to the body which by motion receives and gives back the breath, and to this air poured around on every side, which by entering nourishes, by going out relieves? And yet this you, a literate and eloquent man, surely did not know, when you believed, and said, and wrote, and read in the assembly of a gathered crowd, that from our nature we inflate a skin, and in our nature we have nothing the less:

whereas you could most easily, not by searching through the divine and human pages, but by attending to yourself, know, when you wished, from what we do this. How, then, shall I commit it to you to teach me about the origin of souls, which I confess I do not know—you who were ignorant from where you do what you do, with your nostrils and mouth, without interruption? And may the Lord grant that, admonished by me, you yield rather than resist a truth so ready at hand and most open: and that you not so question your lungs about inflating a skin that you prefer to have them inflated against me, rather than to acquiesce in them as they teach you, and give you a true answer, not by speaking and wrangling, but by breathing in and breathing out. Accordingly, I would not take it ill that you rebuke and chide my ignorance about the origin of souls—nay rather, I would even give great thanks—if you would not only strike it with hard insults, but also shake it out with true sayings. For if you could teach me what I do not know, I ought most patiently to endure you striking me not only with words but even with fists.

## I doubt whether it pertains to us to know anything certain about the origin of souls while we live here.

**4. 5.** For I confess to your Charity, as far as concerns this question, that I greatly desire to know one of two things, if I can: either, about the origin of souls, what I do not know, or whether it pertains to us to know this while we live here. For what if it is among those things of which it is said to us: *Seek not the things that are too high for you, and search not the things that are too mighty for you; but think always on the things that the Lord has commanded you*[^4]? But I desire to know this either from God himself, who knows what he creates, or even from some learned person who knows what he says, not from a man who does not know what he pants out.

No one recalls his own infancy, and do you think that a man can know, unless God teaches him, from where he began to live in his mother's womb; especially if human nature still lies so hidden from him that he is ignorant not only of what it has within, but even of what comes to it from outside? And so, most beloved, will you teach me, or anyone, from where men, being born, are given a soul—you who did not yet know from where men, living, are so nourished that, that nourishment being a little withdrawn, they immediately die?

Will you teach me, or anyone, from where men are given a soul—you who did not yet know from where skins, when they are inflated, are filled? Would that, just as you do not know from where is the origin of souls, so I at least knew whether it must be known by me in this life. For if it is among those higher things which we are forbidden to inquire into and search out, it is to be feared lest we sin in this, not by being ignorant, but by inquiring. For we ought not on that account to think it is not among those higher things, because it pertains not to the nature of God, but to ours.

## Some things in the works of God are known with more difficulty than God himself.

**5. 6.** What of the fact that some things in the works of God are known with more difficulty than God himself, in so far as he can be known? For we have learned that God is a Trinity; but how many kinds of animals he created—at least of the land animals, which could enter into the ark of Noah—we still do not know. Unless perhaps you have already learned this. In the book of Wisdom too it is written: *For if they were able to know so much as to be able to estimate the world, how did they not more easily find its Lord?*[^5] Or is it, because this is within us, that it is therefore not higher than we?

For the nature of our soul is more inward than the body. As though, indeed, the soul could not more easily know the body itself from outside, through the eyes of the body itself, than from within, through itself. For what is there in the inward parts of the body, where it itself is not? And yet even the various internal and vital parts it has inquired into with the eyes of the body, and whatever it could learn of them, it learned through the eyes of the body. And surely it was there, even when it did not know those things. And since our inward parts cannot live within without the soul, the soul could more easily give life to them than know them.

Or is its body perhaps higher, for its knowing, than itself; and therefore, if it should wish to inquire and discourse when the seed of man is turned into blood, when into solid flesh, when the bones begin to harden, when to be filled with marrow; how many kinds of veins and nerves there are, by what courses and windings those irrigate the whole body, these bind it; whether the skin is to be reckoned among the nerves, whether the teeth among the bones—for they differ in that they lack marrow—and how the nails differ from both,

since they are like these in hardness, but to be cut and to grow is common to them with the hairs; what is the use of those veins, not of blood but of air, which they call arteries: if the soul should desire to know these things and things of this kind about the nature of its own body, is it then to be said to a man, *Seek not the things that are too high for you, and search not the things that are too mighty for you*[^6]? But if it inquires about its own origin, what it does not know, is it not higher nor mightier than that it can apprehend it?

And do you think it absurd and incongruous with reason that the soul does not know whether it was breathed in new by God, or drawn from the parents, since it does not now remember this as past, and reckons it among those things which, irrevocably—like its infancy and the rest of the age recently from the womb—it has forgotten, if indeed it was done with any perception of its own when it was done: yet do you not think it absurd and incongruous that the soul does not know the body subject to it, and is utterly ignorant of what is not among its past things but among the present—whether it moves the veins in order to live in the body, but the nerves in order to act with the members of the body; and, if it is so, why it does not move the nerves unless it wills, but the pulse of the veins it carries on without interruption even if it does not will;

from what part of the body it rules the rest—what they call the ἡγεμονικόν—whether from the heart, or from the brain, or, distributed, from the heart for movements, from the brain for sensations, or from the brain for both sensations and voluntary movements, but from the heart for the involuntary pulses of the veins; and if it does those two from the brain, why it senses even if it does not will, but does not move the members unless it wills? Since, then, it does these things in the body, and none but itself does them, why does it not know what it does, or from where it does it?

And it is not shameful for it not to know these things; and do you think it shameful if it does not know from where or how it was made, since it did not make itself? Or, because some know how and from where it acts these things in the body, do you therefore not think that this pertains to those higher and mightier things?

## Why must I learn from another man from where is done by me what is done in me?

**6. 7.** But from this I raise for you a greater question: why very few know from where they do what all do. Perhaps you are going to say: Because those learned the anatomical or empirical art, which the discipline of medicine contains, which few attain; but the rest were unwilling to learn these things, though they could have if they had wished. Here I omit to say why many try to learn these things and cannot, because—what is very wonderful—they are hindered by a slow talent from learning from others the things that are done by themselves, and in themselves.

But this very thing is the greatest question: why I need no art to know that the sun, and the moon, and the other stars are in the heaven; and I need art to know, when I move a finger, from where I begin—from the heart, or from the brain, or from both, or from neither; and I need no teacher to know what is so far above me, but must wait to learn from another man from where is done by me what is done in me. For although we are said to think in the heart, and we know what we think, with no other man knowing it; yet the heart itself, where we think, we do not know in what part of the body we have, unless we learn from another, who does not know what we think.

Nor am I ignorant that, when we hear that we should love God with our whole heart, this is not said of that little piece of our flesh which lies hidden beneath the ribs, but of that power by which thoughts are made: which is deservedly called by this name, because, just as motion does not cease in the heart, from where the pulse diffuses itself everywhere through the veins, so we do not rest from turning something over in thought. Nevertheless, since all sense is present, from the soul, to the body too, why even in darkness, and with eyes closed, by the sense of the body which is called touch, we count our members from outside; but, by the inner presence of the soul itself, by which it is present to all the things it gives life to and animates, we know none of our inward parts within—I think that no one knows, neither the empiric physicians, nor the anatomists, nor the dogmatists, nor the methodists, but no man.

## It is we who are not able to comprehend ourselves.

**6. 8.** And whoever has tried to learn these things, it is not in vain said to him: *Seek not the things that are too high for you, and search not the things that are too mighty for you.* For they are not higher than our stature can reach, but than our conjecture can comprehend; and mightier than the force of human talent can penetrate: and yet it is not the heaven of heaven, not the dimension of the stars, not the measure of the sea and the lands, not the lower hell: it is we who are not able to comprehend ourselves; we exceed the measure of our own knowledge as things higher and mightier; we cannot grasp ourselves, and surely we are not outside ourselves. Nor for that reason are we to be compared to the beasts, because we do not utterly find out that which we are: and do you think we are to be compared to the beasts, if we have forgotten what we were, if indeed we ever knew it? For my soul is not now drawn from my parents, or breathed in by God: whichever of these he did, he did then, when he created me; he does not even now do it from me or in me: that was done and finished, nor is it present to me, nor recent. I do not even know this: whether I knew it, and have forgotten; or whether even then, when it was done, I could perceive and know it.

## We are altogether ignorant of what our memory can do.

**7. 9.** Behold, now, now while we are, while we live, while we know that we live, while we are most certain that we remember and understand and will—we who boast ourselves to be great knowers of our own nature—we are altogether ignorant of what our memory, or understanding, or will can do. A certain friend of mine from my youth, Simplicius by name, a man of excellent and wonderful memory, when he was asked by us what verses Virgil had said above the last ones in all his books, answered at once, quickly and from memory. We asked him also to say the verses above those; he said them. And we believed that he could recite Virgil backward. From whatever passage we wished, we asked him to do it; he did it. Even in prose, from whatever oration of Cicero which he had committed to memory, we wished him to do this; as far as we wished, he followed upward, verse by verse. When we marveled, he called God to witness that he had not known he could do this before that experiment. So, as far as pertains to memory, then his mind learned itself; and whenever it would learn, it could not except by trying and experiencing. And surely, before he tried, he was the very same: why, then, did he not know himself?

## The powers of my understanding are not known to me.

**7. 10.** Often we presume that we will retain something in memory, and when we think so, we do not write it down; nor does it afterward come to mind when we wish, and we repent of having believed it would come, or of not having bound it in letters lest it flee; and suddenly again, when we are not seeking it, it occurs to us. Were we not ourselves, when we were thinking it? Nor yet are we this which we were, when we cannot think it. What is it, then, that, I know not how, we are withdrawn and denied to ourselves; and likewise, I know not how, we are brought forth to ourselves and restored to ourselves? As though we were other, and were elsewhere, when we seek and do not find what we placed in our memory; and as though we ourselves could not reach ourselves, as if placed elsewhere, and then reach ourselves when we find it.

For where do we seek but in ourselves? And what do we seek but ourselves? As though we were not in ourselves, and had withdrawn somewhere from ourselves. Do you not attend, and shudder at so great a depth? And what is this other than our nature—not such as it was, but such as it now is? And behold, it is more sought than comprehended. Often I have thought I would understand a question set before me, if I thought about it; I thought, and could not: often I did not think I would, and yet I could. The powers of my understanding, then, are surely not known to me, and I believe that neither are they to you.

## The apostle Peter did not know how great powers his will had.

**7. 11.** But perhaps you despise me confessing this, and for this too will compare me to the beasts. But I do not cease to advise, or, if you do not deign to accept it, at least to admonish, that you rather recognize the common weakness, in which strength is made perfect[^7]: lest, by presuming things unknown as known, you be unable to reach the truth. For I think there is something which you too seek to understand and cannot, nor yet would you seek it unless you hoped you could. And through this you too do not know the powers of your understanding, you who profess the knowledge of your nature, and do not confess ignorance with me.

What shall I say of the will, where surely free choice is proclaimed to be ours? Surely the most blessed apostle Peter wished to lay down his life for the Lord[^8]: he plainly wished it; for he did not deceive God by promising it: but how great powers it had, the will itself did not know. Accordingly, so great a man, who had recognized that Jesus is the Son of God[^9], was hidden from himself. We know, then, that we will or do not will something: but our will, even when it is good, how much it avails, how great powers it has, to what temptations it yields, or to which it does not yield, if we do not deceive ourselves, dear son, we do not know.

## Paul did not know what pertained to his own nature.

**8. 12.** See, then, how many things, not past but present, about our nature—not only what pertains to the body, but even what pertains to the inner man—we are ignorant of, and yet are not compared to the beasts. And you, because I am not altogether ignorant of the past origin of my soul, but do not fully know it—for I know that it was given to me by God, and yet is not of God—judged me worthy of so great a reproach. And when can I recount all the things we do not know about the nature of our spirit and soul? Where we ought rather to cry out to God what he cried out in the Psalm: *Your knowledge has become wonderful from me; it has grown strong, I shall not be able to reach it*[^10]. For why did he add, *from me,* except because from himself he conjectured how incomprehensible the knowledge of God is, since he was not able to comprehend himself?

The Apostle was caught up into the third heaven, and heard ineffable words, which it is not lawful for a man to speak, and whether this happened to him in the body or outside the body, he says he does not know[^11], nor does he fear to be compared by you to the beasts. He knew that his spirit was in the third heaven, was in paradise, and whether it was in the body he did not know. And surely the third heaven and paradise were not the apostle Paul himself; but his body and soul and spirit were himself. Behold, he knew great, high, and divine things, which he himself was not; and this he did not know, which pertained to his own nature. Who would not marvel, in so great a knowledge of hidden things, at so great an ignorance of himself?

Who, finally, would believe it, unless he who does not deceive said it: *We do not know what we should pray for as we ought*[^12]? Where our attention ought most to be, that we may extend ourselves to the things that are before: and do you compare me to the beasts, if, among the things that are behind, I have forgotten something about my origin, when you hear the same Apostle saying: *Forgetting the things behind, stretched out to the things that are before, according to my intent I follow on to the palm of the heavenly calling of God in Christ Jesus*[^13]!

## Paul, against the usefulness and perfection of his own salvation, ignorantly prayed that the thorn of the flesh might depart from him.

**9. 13.** Or do you perhaps think that I should be laughed at for this too, because I said: *We do not know what we should pray for as we ought*[^14], and judge me like the irrational living things? And perhaps more tolerably. For since by right and sound judgment we prefer our future things to our past, and prayer is necessary to us not for what we were but for what we shall be, it is surely much more troublesome not to know what we should pray for than how we arose. But let it come to your mind where you read this, or recall it by rereading: and do not throw at me the stone of this reproach, lest it reach one whom you do not want.

For that very Apostle, the teacher of the gentiles, said: *For we do not know what we should pray for as we ought.* Which he taught not only by word, but demonstrated also by his own example. For against the usefulness and the perfection of his own salvation, he ignorantly prayed that the thorn of the flesh might depart from him, which he said was given to him lest he be exalted by the greatness of his revelations. And because the Lord loved him, he did not do what he asked ignorantly[^15].

But yet, where he says, *For we do not know what we should pray for as we ought,* he soon added: *But the Spirit himself intercedes for us with unutterable groanings. And he who searches the hearts knows what the Spirit desires, because he intercedes for the saints according to God*[^16]; that is, he makes the saints intercede. That Spirit, surely, whom *God sent into our hearts, crying, Abba, Father*[^17]; and *in whom we cry, Abba, Father*[^18]: for both were said, both that we received the Spirit *crying, Abba, Father,* and *in whom we cry, Abba, Father;* that it might be explained how he said *crying,* that is, "making us cry," so that, with him doing it, we cry.

Let him, then, teach me this too when he wills, if he knows it is expedient for me, that I may know from where I draw my origin according to the soul. But let that Spirit teach me this, who searches the depths of God[^19]; not a man who does not know the breath from where a skin is inflated. Yet far be it that I should on this account compare you to the beasts. For you did not know this, not because you could not, but because you had not attended.

## Why do you bring forward testimonies of the Scriptures about the origin of souls?

**10. 14.** But you perhaps, although the things sought about the origin of souls are higher than from where we draw and give back our breath, nevertheless trust that you have learned these same higher things from the holy Scriptures, from which we have learned by faith things that no human talents could trace out. For it is surely far more excellent to know that the flesh will rise again and live without end, than whatever the physicians could learn about it by searching—which the soul perceives by no sense, while by its presence it quickens all the things it does not know: and it is far better to know that the soul, which has been reborn and renewed in Christ, will be forever blessed, than whatever we do not know about its memory, understanding, will. But these things which I have called more excellent and better, we could in no way know, unless we believed the divine eloquence.

These eloquences, then, you perhaps think you trust, so that you do not hesitate to bring forward a determined opinion about the origin of souls. First, if it were so, you ought not to have attributed to human nature itself that man knows how to inquire and discourse about his own quality and nature, but to the gift of God. For you said: *How does man differ from a beast, if he does not know this?* What need, then, is there to read anything, that we may know this, if by that very thing by which we differ from a beast we ought already to know this? For just as you read me nothing in order that I may know that I live—for my nature has it that I cannot not know this—so, if to know that too is of nature, why do you bring forward to me, on this matter, testimonies of the Scriptures for me to believe? Do only those who read them differ from a beast?

Were we not created so as to differ from the beasts, even before we could reach any letters? What is it, I ask, that you arrogate so much to our nature, that by that very thing by which it differs from a beast it already knows how to inquire and discourse about the origin of souls; and again you make it so devoid of this knowledge that it cannot know this in a human way, unless it believes divine witnesses?

## Moreover, the divine testimonies do not resolve this question.

**11. 15.** Next, in this too you are deceived. For the divine testimonies, which you wished to bring to bear for resolving this question, do not open it. It is another thing that they show, without which we truly cannot live piously—namely, that we have God as the giver, creator, former of our souls. But how he does this, whether by breathing them in new, or by drawing them from the parents, they do not express, except concerning that one which he gave to the first man. Read diligently what I wrote to our brother Renatus, the servant of God; for there, because I showed this, it was not necessary to write it here too.

But you would want me to determine what you yourself determined, so that I might be thrust into such straits as those into which you yourself, thrust, spoke so many and so great things against the Catholic faith that, if you faithfully and humbly recall and consider them, you will surely see how much it would have profited you, had you known how not to know what you do not know; and how much it profits you, if even now you know it.

For if understanding pleases you in the nature of man—since indeed, if it did not have it, we would, as far as souls are concerned, differ in nothing from the beasts—understand what you do not understand, lest you understand nothing at all; and do not despise a man who, in order truly to understand what he does not understand, understands that he does not understand this. But from where it was said in the sacred Psalm, *Man, when he was in honor, did not understand; he was compared to the senseless beasts, and made like them*[^20], read and understand, that you may rather humbly avoid this reproach yourself, than proudly cast it at another.

For this was said of those who reckon this life alone to be life, living according to the flesh, and hoping for nothing after death, like the beasts: not of those who neither deny that they know what they know, and confess that they do not know what they do not know, and more safely understand their own weakness than trust in their own strength.

## Let not my aged fear displease your youthful presumption.

**11. 16.** Let not, then, my aged fear displease your youthful presumption, son. For I, if I can know this which we ask about the origin of souls, with neither God nor any spiritual man teaching me, am more ready to defend that God rightly willed that we should not know this too, as many other things, than rashly to say

something that either is so obscure that I can not only not bring it to the understanding of others, but not even understand it myself; or that certainly even helps the heretics, who for this reason try to persuade us that the souls of little ones are pure from all guilt—namely, lest that same guilt run back and return to God their author—because, by giving innocent souls, for whom he foreknew that the bath of regeneration too would not come to aid, to sinful flesh, he forced them to be sinful, with no grace of Baptism to come to aid, by which they might be freed from perpetual damnation: since innumerable souls of infants depart from their bodies before they are baptized.

For far be it that, wishing to wash this away, I should say what you yourself said, *that the soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh, and to be a sinner,* having no sin before, by which it could rightly be said to have deserved this, and *that even without Baptism original sins are loosed,* and *that the kingdom of heaven too is granted to the unbaptized in the end.* Unless I feared to say these and such poisons of the faith, perhaps I would not fear to determine something about this matter. How much better, then, that I do not separately dispute about the soul and affirm what I do not know; but what I see the Apostle most plainly to have taught, I simply hold. That from one man all men who are born of Adam go into condemnation[^21], unless they are so reborn in Christ, as he appointed that they should be reborn[^22], before they die in the body—those whom the most merciful bestower of grace predestined to eternal life: who is also, to those whom he predestined to eternal death, the most just repayer of punishment; not only on account of those things which they willingly add, but even if infants add nothing, on account of original sin. This is, in this question, my determination, that the hidden works of God may have their secret, my faith being safe.

## Let us first dispute whether the soul is incorporeal.

**12. 17.** Now, as far as the Lord deigns to grant, I ought also to answer that place where, speaking of the soul, you repeated my name and said: *For we do not allow, as Augustine, a most skilled bishop, professes, that it is incorporeal and the same as spirit.* First, then, let us dispute whether the soul is to be thought incorporeal, as I said, or corporeal, as you do. Then whether it too, according to our Scriptures, is called spirit; although properly the spirit is named not the whole of it, but something of it. And first I would like to know what you define a body to be. For if there is no body except what consists of fleshly members, then neither will the earth be a body, nor the heaven, nor a stone, nor water, nor the stars, nor anything of this kind. But if a body is whatever consists of its greater and lesser parts occupying greater and lesser spaces of place, then these too which I have mentioned are bodies: air is a body, this visible light is a body, and all, as the Apostle says, are *heavenly bodies and earthly bodies*[^23].

## What lacks a body is not on that account an empty substance.

**12. 18.** But whether the soul is anything such is most scrupulously and most subtly asked. Nevertheless you—for which I most congratulate you—affirm that God is not a body. But you make me anxious again, where you say: *If the soul lacks a body, so as to be, as it pleases some, a substance of empty void, of airy and futile being.* For by these words of yours you seem to believe that everything which lacks a body is of an empty substance. But if this is so, how do you dare to say that God lacks a body, and not fear lest it follow that he is of an empty substance? Moreover, if God both lacks a body, which you have already confessed, and that he is of an empty substance—far be it that you should say so: therefore whatever lacks a body is not of an empty substance. And therefore one who says the soul is incorporeal does not consequently want it to seem of an empty and futile substance, because he confesses God too, who is not anything empty, to be at the same time incorporeal.

But see how great a difference there is between what I say and what you suppose me to say. For I do not say that the soul is of an airy substance; otherwise I confess it to be a body. For air is a body, according to all who, when they speak about bodies, know what they speak. But you, because I said the soul is incorporeal, thought I had said it to be a substance not only of empty void, but, on this account, airy: whereas I both said that it is not a body, which air is; and what is filled with air cannot be empty. Which not even your skins could admonish you of. For what else but air is compressed into them when they are inflated? They are so far from being empty that by that same fullness they even support weights. But if perhaps it seems to you that breath is one thing and air another—since air, when moved, is itself breath, which can be taught even by a fan when waved—certainly, that you may know that any hollow vessels which you think empty are full, plunge them into water on that side by which they are filled, and see that no moisture can enter them, the air repelling it, with which they are full. But when they are placed with the mouth upward, or on the side, then they receive liquid, if any is poured in or enters, the air going out and escaping by the side where an exit lies open. This could be demonstrated more easily by a present deed than by writing.

But there is no need to delay here longer, since, whether you understand the nature of air to be corporeal or do not understand it, you nevertheless ought not to think that I said the soul to be even airy, but altogether incorporeal: which you too confess God to be, whom you do not dare to say is anything empty; but you cannot deny that he is of the almighty and immutable substance. Why, then, do we fear lest the soul be of empty void, if it is incorporeal, since we confess that God is incorporeal, and do not say that he is of empty void? So, then, the incorporeal could create an incorporeal soul, just as the living the living, although the immutable the mutable, and the almighty one far unequal.

## Why you would want the soul to be a body, I do not see.

**13. 19.** But why you would not want the soul to be a spirit, but would want it to be a body, I do not see. For if it is not a spirit on the ground that the Apostle distinctly named the spirit, saying, *And may your whole spirit, and soul, and body*[^24], the same reason holds why it is not a body, because he distinctly named the body too. But if you affirm that the soul too is a body, although the body was distinctly named, allow that it is also a spirit, although the spirit was distinctly named. For the soul ought to seem to you much more to be a spirit than a body; because you confess that spirit and soul are of one substance, but you do not say that soul and body are of one substance. By what reasoning, then, is the soul a body, since its nature and the body's are diverse; and the soul is not a spirit, since its nature and the spirit's are one and the same? What of the fact that by this reasoning of yours you are forced to say that the spirit too is a body? Otherwise, if the spirit is not a body, and the soul is a body, then spirit and soul are not of one and the same substance. But you confess that both, although you think them two distinct things, have one substance. Therefore the spirit too is a body, if the soul is a body: for otherwise they cannot be of one and the same nature.

Accordingly, according to you, that which the Apostle says, *your spirit, and soul, and body*[^25], are three bodies; but of these two, soul and spirit, are bodies of one nature; but that body, which is also called flesh, is of a diverse nature. And of these three bodies, as you suppose—of which one is of a diverse, but two are of one and the same substance—the whole man consists, one certain thing and one substance. Although you assert these things, you nevertheless do not want two things of one and the same substance—that is, soul and spirit—to have the one name of spirit: whereas two things not of one and the same, but of an unequal and diverse substance—that is, soul and body—have, as you think, the one name of body.

## Let us see which is the inner man: whether the soul, or the spirit, or both.

**14. 20.** But this I omit, lest there be among us a controversy rather about names than about things. Let us see which is the inner man: whether the soul, or the spirit, or both. But, as I see you have written, you say the inner man is the soul. For of this you were speaking, when you said: *And the substance which could not be grasped, congealing, made another body within the body, massed together by the force and breathing of its own nature, and thence the inner man began to appear, whom, enclosed as it were in the form of a corporeal sheath, the condition of the outer man delineated to its own likeness.* Then you infer: *The breath of God, therefore, made the mind—nay rather, the breath from God was made soul, a fashioned, substantial being, and according to its nature corporeal, and like its own body, and conformed to its image.* After this, beginning to speak of the spirit: *This soul,* you say, *which had its origin from the breath of God, could not be without its own sense and inmost understanding, which is the spirit.*

As, then, I see, you want the inner man to be the soul; the inmost, the spirit: as though it too were more inward than the soul, as that is than the body. So it comes about that, just as the body, through its inner hollows, receives another body, which is the soul, as you suppose, so the soul too is to be believed to have inner empty places, by which it receives a third body, the spirit: and so the whole man consists of three—the outer, the inner, the inmost. Do you not yet, then, regard how many most absurd things follow you, when you try to assert that the soul is corporeal?

Then tell me: which of them will be renewed in the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him[^26]? The inner, or the inmost? The Apostle indeed, besides the inner and the outer, I do not see that he knows another, an inner one of the inner—that is, an inmost one of the whole man. But choose whichever you wish, which is to be renewed according to the image of God: how will it receive this, which has already taken the image of the outer? For if the inner ran through the members of the outer and congealed (for you used even this word, as though a molded figure were made from a clay form, which was made from dust), how, the same form remaining, which was impressed on it or expressed from the body, can it be reformed to the image of God? Or will it have two images, from the top indeed of God, but from the bottom of the body; as on a coin is said to be "head and ship"?

Or do you perhaps say that the soul took the image of the body, and the spirit takes the image of God, as though the one were contiguous to the body, and the other to God; and so that inmost man, not this inner one, is reformed to the image of God? But you say this in vain. For if that inmost one too is so diffused through all the members of the soul, as the soul is through those of the body, then it too has already, through the soul, taken the image of the body, as that form formed it:

and through this it has nowhere to take the image of God, that image of the body remaining in it; unless, just as a coin, as I said, it is formed otherwise from the lower, otherwise from the upper part.

To these absurdities, when you think about the soul, the carnal thinking of bodies, whether you will or not, compels you. But God, as you yourself too most rightly confess, is not a body: how, then, can a body take his image? I beseech you, brother, that you be not conformed to this age, but be reformed in the newness of your mind[^27], and be not wise according to the flesh, since it is death[^28].

## You think that the recognition of a man does not come about without the form of the body.

**15. 21.** *But,* you say, *if the soul lacks a body, what is it that the rich man recognizes among the dead? Surely,* you say, *he already knew Lazarus; he did not know Abraham. From where came to him the recognition of Abraham, who died so long before?*[^29] Saying this, if you think the recognition of a man does not come about without the form of the body, that you may know yourself, I believe you constantly look at a mirror, lest, if you have forgotten your face, you cannot recognize yourself. I ask you, what man does a man know more than himself, and whose face can he less see than his own? But who can know God, whom you too do not doubt to be incorporeal, if, apart from the form of the body, as you think, knowledge cannot come about—that is, if bodies alone can be known? But what Christian, disputing about such great and most difficult matters, would bend his mind so negligently upon the divine words as to say: *If the soul is incorporeal, it must necessarily lack form?* Have you forgotten that you read of a form of doctrine[^30]? Then the form of doctrine is corporeal. Have you forgotten that it is written of Christ Jesus, before he had put on man, that he was in the form of God[^31]? How, then, do you say: *If the soul is incorporeal, it must necessarily lack form,* when you hear of the form of God, whom you confess not to be corporeal; and you so speak as though form could not be except in bodies?

## Even where there is no body, there is form.

**15. 22.** You say also *that names cease there, where form is not distinguished; and that the appellation of names does nothing there, where there is no designation of persons.* Hence wishing to prove that Abraham's soul was corporeal, because it could be said, *Father Abraham.* We have already said that even where there is no body, there is form. But if you think the appellation of names does nothing where there are no bodies, count these names, I ask you: *But the fruit of the spirit is love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control*[^32]: and tell me whether you do not recognize the things themselves of which these are the names, or whether you so recognize them that you see some outlines of bodies. Behold—to be silent about the rest—tell me what figure, what members, what color love has; love which surely, if you yourself are not empty, cannot seem to you anything empty. *He whose help was implored,* you say, *was surely seen as corporeal and formed.* Let men hear you, and let no one implore the help of God, because no one can see him as corporeal.

## Why do these names of members not make a body for you in God, but make one in the soul?

**16. 23.** *Finally,* you say, *members of the soul are there described, so that it is truly a body,* and you want *the whole head to be understood by the eye, because he is said to have lifted up his eyes; the throat by the tongue, the hand by the finger, because it was said: Send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, that he may cool my tongue*[^33]. Yet, lest by the names of members a corporeal God be prescribed to you, you say *that by these incorporeal powers are to be understood,* because you most rightly defend that God is not corporeal. What reason, then, is there why these names of members do not make a body for you in God, but make one in the soul? Or are these things, when said of a creature, to be taken properly; but when of the Creator, figuratively and by transference?

Are you, then, going to give us corporeal wings, since it is not the Creator, but a creature, that is, a man, who says: *If I take my wings like a dove*[^34]? Moreover, if that rich man had a corporeal tongue for this reason, because he said, *that he may cool my tongue:* then in us too, still living in the flesh, the tongue itself has corporeal hands, because it is written: *Death and life are in the hands of the tongue*[^35]. I think too that it does not seem to you that sin is either a creature or a body: why, then, does it have a face? Or do you not hear in the Psalm: *There is no peace in my bones, from the face of my sins*[^36]?

## Understand the bosom of Abraham to be a remote seat of rest.

**16. 24.** But as for your supposing *that the bosom of Abraham is corporeal, and asserting that through it his whole body is recognized,* I fear lest in so great a matter you be believed to act jestingly and mockingly, not seriously and gravely. For you would not be so foolish as to judge that the corporeal bosom of one man bears so many souls, or rather—to speak according to you—bears so many bodies of the well-deserving, whom the angels carry there as they did Lazarus. Unless perhaps you suppose that that one soul alone deserved to reach that bosom. If you are not jesting, and do not wish to err childishly, understand the bosom of Abraham to be a remote and secret seat of rest, where Abraham is.

And therefore it was said to be Abraham's, not because it is his alone, but because he is set as the father of many nations, to whom he is set forth, by the primacy of faith, for imitation[^37]: just as God willed to be called the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob[^38], though he is the God of innumerable ones.

## The soul, whether of one dead or of one sleeping, perceives good and evil things in the likeness of its body.

**17. 25.** Nor think that I discourse thus as though I deny that it can come to pass that the soul of one dead, like that of one sleeping, perceives, in the likeness of its body, things either good or evil. For in dreams too, when we endure some hard and troublesome things, it is surely we; and unless those things passed away when we awoke, we would pay the heaviest penalties. But to believe that those are bodies, by which we are as it were borne and fly here and there in dreams, is the mark of a man who has thought too little vigilantly about such matters: for from these images of things seen, the soul is most of all proved not to be corporeal—unless you would say that those too are bodies, which, besides ourselves, we see so many of in dreams: heaven, earth, sea, sun, moon, stars, rivers, mountains, trees, animals[^39]. Whoever believes these to be bodies is incredibly foolish: yet they are altogether most like bodies. Of this kind are also those things which are divinely shown as signifying something, whether in dreams or in ecstasy. From where these come, that is, what their material as it were is, who could trace out or say? Yet without doubt it is spiritual, not corporeal. For such forms as it were of bodies, yet not bodies, are both formed by the thinking of those awake, and contained in the depth of the memory; and from its most hidden recesses, in I know not what wonderful and ineffable way, when we remember, they come forth and are turned over as it were before the eyes. So many, then, and so great images of bodies the soul could not, if it were a body, take in by thinking or contain in memory. For, according to your definition, *a corporeal substance does not exceed this outer body.* By what magnitude, then—which is none to it—does it take in images of such great bodies and spaces and regions?

What wonder, then, if it appears to itself too in the likeness of its body, even when it appears without a body? For it does not appear to itself with its body in dreams, and yet in that very likeness of its body it runs as it were through known and unknown places, and perceives many glad or sad things. But I think that not even you would dare to say that that figure of the body and members, which it seems to itself to have in dreams, is a true body. For in that way the mountain it seems to itself to climb will be a true mountain; and the house it seems to itself to enter, a corporeal one; and a true tree and true wood having a body, under which it seems to itself to lie; and true water, which it seems to itself to draw; and all the things among which it moves as among bodies will be bodies, if it too is a body, which moves among all those by a like image.

## Holy Perpetua saw herself in dreams turned into a man, wrestling with a certain Egyptian.

**18. 26.** Something must be said to you about the recorded visions of the martyrs, since you thought a testimony was to be adduced even from there[^40]. Surely holy Perpetua seemed to herself in dreams to wrestle with a certain Egyptian, turned into a man. But who would doubt that in that likeness of a body it was her soul, not her body, which surely, remaining in its female sex, lay with its senses lulled on the bed, when her soul was wrestling in that likeness of a male body? What do you say here? Was that likeness of a man a true body, or was it not a body, although it had the likeness of a body? Choose what you will. If it was a body, why did it not keep the form of its sheath? For it had not found in the flesh of that woman male genitals, from which it could be so formed by contracting itself and, as you say, "congealing."

Then, I beseech you, since the body of the sleeping woman still lived, when her soul was wrestling, it was in its sheath, surely enclosed in all the members of the living body, and kept in her body its own form, from which it had been formed—for it had not yet left those joints, as happens in death; it had not yet, by the compelling force of death, drawn out the members formed from the members forming them: from where, then, was the male body of the soul, in which she seemed to herself to wrestle with her adversary? But if it was not a body, and yet was something like a body, in which surely a true labor or true joy was perceived; do you now at last see how it can come to pass that there is in the soul a certain likeness of a body, and it is not itself a body?

## Another vision of holy Perpetua.

**18. 27.** What if something of this kind takes place among the dead, and in them souls recognize themselves, not as bodies, but as likenesses of bodies? For when we suffer sad things, although in dreams, even if there is that likeness of corporeal members, not corporeal members, yet it is not a likeness of punishment, but punishment: so too where glad things are perceived. But because holy Perpetua was not yet dead, you do not perhaps want a prejudgment to be made for you from here: although it greatly pertains to the matter, of what nature you think those likenesses of bodies are which we have in dreams; and this whole case is finished, if you confess them both to be like bodies, and not to be bodies.

Nevertheless Dinocrates, her brother, was dead: she saw him with that wound which he had while alive, and from which he was brought to death. Where is what you labored at with such great efforts, when you were treating of the cutting off of members, lest the soul be thought to be cut along with them? Behold, there was a wound in the soul of Dinocrates, which by its force, when it was in his body, drove it out of the body. How, then, according to your opinion, *when the members of the body are cut off, does it withdraw itself from the blow, and gather itself by condensing into other parts, lest any part of it be amputated by the wound of the body, even if something of the members be cut off from one sleeping and unaware?* For you attribute to it so great a vigilance that, even occupied with the visions of dreams, if a blow rush upon it unawares, by which the flesh is struck, it providently and swiftly withdraws itself, lest it be struck and vexed, or cut: nor do you attend, prudent man, that if the soul withdrew itself from there, that blow too would not be felt.

But find what you can answer to this, how the soul snatches away its parts, and stores them within, lest, where a member of the body is cut off or struck, it too be amputated and vexed. Look at Dinocrates, and say why his soul did not withdraw itself from that place of the body which was being laid waste by the mortal wound, lest there come to be in it what would appear even after death in the face of his body.

Or does it now please you rather that we believe these to be likenesses of bodies than bodies; so that, just as a wound appears as it were, which is not a wound, so what is not a body may appear as it were a body? For if the soul can be wounded by those who wound the body, is nothing to be feared, lest it be able to be killed by those who kill the body? Which the Lord most plainly testifies cannot come to pass[^41]. And yet the soul of Dinocrates could not die, from where his body died: and it seemed as it were wounded, as the body had been wounded, since it was not a body, but had, in the likeness of a body, even a likeness of a wound: moreover, in a not-true body there was a true misery of the soul, which was signified by the shadowed wound of the body, from which it deserved to be freed by the prayers of its holy sister.

## The soul would often turn out monstrous, if it took its form from the body.

**18. 28.** Now, what kind of thing is it to say *that the soul takes its form from the body, and is extended and grows with the increase of the body,* and not to attend how monstrous the soul of a young man or an old man turns out, if his arm was cut off as an infant? *For it contracts itself,* as you say; *the hand of the soul, lest it too be amputated along with the hand of the body, and it encloses itself by condensing into other parts of the body.* And through this that arm of the soul, however short was the body's from which it had taken its form, so short it will be kept wherever it is kept; because it has lost the form by whose increase it could grow likewise. There departs, then, the soul of a young man or an old man, who lost his hand when he was small, having indeed two hands, because the one fleeing was not amputated with the body, but the other youthful or aged, but the other as it first was, infantile. Such souls, believe me, the form of the body does not make, but the deformity of error fashions.

You do not seem to me to be able to be rescued from this error, unless, by God's help, you diligently consider the visions of dreamers, and from there recognize that there are certain things which are not bodies, but likenesses of bodies. For although those things too which we think as like bodies are of that kind, nevertheless, as far as concerns the dead, a more apt conjecture is drawn from those sleeping. For not in vain does holy Scripture call those who are dead "sleeping"[^42], except because sleep is in a manner the kindred of death[^43].

## The soul bears not the truth of a body, but a likeness.

**19. 29.** Accordingly, if the soul were a body, and the corporeal figure in which it sees itself in dreams were corporeal, because it had been expressed from its body, no man, a member of his body having been amputated, would, just as he lacks it, so see himself without it in dreams, but rather always whole, because nothing of the soul itself had been amputated. But since sometimes they see themselves whole, but sometimes, as they are, maimed in some part, what else does this fact teach but that the soul, as of the other things which it perceived in dreams, so also of the body, bears now in this way, now in that, not the truth, but a likeness? But its joy or sadness, delight or offense, whether it be in bodies or in likenesses of bodies, is true.

Did you not yourself say, and truly say, *that food and clothing are not necessary to the soul, but to the body?* Why, then, did the rich man desire a drop of water among the dead? Why did holy Samuel after death, as you yourself also mentioned, appear clothed in his accustomed garment[^44]? Did he desire to repair the ruins of the soul, as of the flesh, by the nourishment of moisture? Had he departed from the body clothed? But in the one there was a true trouble, by which the soul was tormented; yet not a true body, for which it would seek nourishment. And the other could appear clothed in such a way that it was not a body, but both the soul and the garb had a likeness of a body. For the soul does not extend and contract itself into garments, as into the members of the body, so as to be formed from them too.

## What power of knowing souls receive after death, who could trace out?

**19. 30.** But after death, what power of knowing souls, even those not good, receive, unburdened of their corruptible bodies, so that they can behold and recognize, by their inner senses, either souls likewise evil, or even good ones, whether in those very things which are not bodies but likenesses of bodies, or in the good or evil affections of the mind, in which there are no outlines as it were of members, who could trace out? From this comes also that the rich man, when he was in torments, recognized father Abraham, whose bodily figure was not known to him, but whose likeness of body, though incorporeal, the soul could retain.

But who would rightly say that he had known any man, except in so far as he could know his life and will, which surely have no mass or colors? For so we know even ourselves more certainly than others, because our conscience and will are known to us: which we plainly see, and yet in it we do not see any likeness of a body. This we do not discern in another, though present—even one whose face, when absent, we know, recall, think of. But our own face we cannot in that way know, recall, think of, and yet we most truly say that we ourselves are known to us more than that other: so clear is it where the better and truer knowledge of a man lies.

## The diverse knowing faculties in us.

**20. 31.** Since, then, one thing in the soul is that by which we perceive true bodies, which we do by the five senses of the body; another that by which, besides these, we discern things like bodies that are not bodies, where we also behold ourselves not otherwise than as like bodies; another that by which we perceive neither bodies nor likenesses of bodies, such as faith, hope, love, without any colors and masses and their likenesses—and surely more certainly and more firmly: where ought we more to be, and in a manner more familiarly to dwell; where to be renewed in the knowledge of God according to the image of him who created us[^45]? Is it not in that which I placed in the third place? For there surely we bear neither any sex, nor any likeness of sex.

## Of this monstrosity which you imagine, you bring forward no example in the nature of things.

**20. 32.** For that form of a male or female soul, distinguished by male and female members—if it is not a likeness of a body, but a body, then, like it or not, it is a male; like it or not, it is a female, whatever either appears as male or as female. But yet, if according to your opinion the soul is both a body, and a living body, and has swelling and pendulous breasts, and has no beard, and has a womb and the genitals which the members of a female body have, and is not a female: shall not I more constantly say true things—And it has an eye, and has a tongue, and has a finger, and has the other like members of a body, and this whole is a likeness of a body, not a body: since each one proves what I say in himself, when he imagines the bodies of the absent; let him prove it surely, when he recalls the figures both of his own and of others' dreams: but from you, of this monstrosity, where there is both a true and a living and a female body, and the sex is not female, no example is brought forward in the nature of things?

## If you do not want there to be sex in the soul, let the soul not be a body.

**20. 33.** For what you say about the phoenix[^46] pertains not at all to the matter of which we are treating. For it signifies the resurrection of bodies, it does not destroy the sex of souls: if indeed, as is believed, it is reborn from its own death. But I judge that you thought your discourse would be too little plausible, if you did not declaim much about the phoenix, after the manner of young men. For are there in its body male genitals, and it is not a male, or female ones, and it is not a female? But attend to what you say, what you try to establish, what to persuade. *You say that the soul, diffused through all the members, congealing, grew rigid, and from the crown to the lowest soles, from the inmost marrow to the surface of the skin, took the whole form of the whole body;* and through this it took, in a female body, whatever females have of female inward parts, and this body is true, and these members are true, and yet it is not a female.

Why, I beseech you, in a true and living body are all the members female, and it is not a female? In a true and living body are all the members male, and it is not a male? Who would presume to believe, say, teach these things? Or is it because souls do not beget? Then neither are mules, male and female, male and female. Or is it because souls cannot lie together without fleshly bodies? But this is taken away too from those who are castrated: and yet, although both the function and the motion are taken from them, the sex is not taken, the figure of the male members, however small, remaining. No one ever denied that a eunuch is a male.

What of the fact that with you the souls even of eunuchs have their testicles whole, and if from anyone the genitals are wholly and entirely taken away, whole in his soul, according to your opinion, and altogether entire, they will remain? *For it knows how to withdraw itself,* as you say, *when that part of the flesh begins to be cut away; so that the form which was taken from there, when that from which it was taken is amputated, does not perish; but, although diffused it has congealed, yet by a most swift motion it is snatched away, and stored within, that it may be kept safe:* and yet he is not a male among the dead, carrying with himself the whole of his male genitals, who, when he did not have them in the body, was a male only on account of their place. These things are false, son: if you do not want there to be sex in the soul, let it not be a body either.

## A likeness of a body and a body.

**21. 34.** Not every likeness of a body is a body. Sleep, and you will see: but when you have awakened, vigilantly discern what you have seen. For in dreams you will appear to yourself as it were corporeal: yet it will be not your body, but your soul; not a true body, but a likeness of a body. For your body will lie, that will walk: the tongue of your body will be silent, that will speak: your eyes will be closed, that will see: and surely the members of your body will lie living, not dead. And through this there has not yet been drawn out as it were from its sheath that congealed form, as you think, of your soul, and yet in it is discerned, whole and entire, the likeness of your flesh. Of this kind of corporeal likenesses, which are not bodies though they appear as bodies, are all the things which, reading the holy Books, you do not understand even in the prophetic visions: by which are signified the things that are done in time, whether present, or past, or future. But you are deceived in them, not because they themselves are deceptive, but because you do not take them as they are to be taken.

For where the souls of the martyrs were seen[^47], in the same revelation was seen also the lamb as it were slain, having seven horns[^48]: there horses, and other animals, figured as was fitting: there, finally, both the stars fell, and the heaven was folded like a book; nor yet did the world then fall[^49]. All these things, then, if we take them wisely, although we say the visions are truthful, yet we do not say the bodies are true.

## How angels appear.

**21. 35.** But it is a matter of longer discourse, the most diligent disputation about this kind of corporeal likenesses: whether the angels too, whether good or evil, so appear, when they appear in the form of men, or of any bodies; or whether they have some true bodies, and are seen rather in the truth of those; or whether, indeed, in dreams or in ecstasy, they are discerned in these, not bodies, but likenesses of bodies; but to those awake they present true bodies to be discerned, and, if need be, even to be touched. But I do not think these things are to be inquired into and handled in this book. For now let enough have been said about the incorporeal soul: which, how you prefer to believe corporeal, you must first define what a body is; lest perhaps, when about the thing itself it is agreed between us, we labor in vain about the name. Yet how many absurdities have followed you in thinking such a body in the soul, as are those which by all the learned are called bodies—that is, those which, through the distance of length, breadth, height, occupy spaces of place, lesser with their lesser parts, and greater with the greater—I think you now prudently observe.

## The whole soul too is called spirit.

**22. 36.** It remains to show how, although the spirit is also properly so called—not the whole soul, but something of it (as the Apostle says: *And may your whole spirit, and soul, and body*[^50]; or that much more expressly in the book of Job: *You have freed my soul from my spirit*[^51])—nevertheless the whole soul too is called by this name; although this seems much more to be a question of names, not of things. For since it is agreed that there is something in the soul which is properly named spirit, with which excepted the soul too is properly named, now about the things themselves there is no contention; especially since I too say that what is properly called spirit is that which you also say, that is, that by which we reason and understand, when these things are said as distinctly as the Apostle says: *And may your whole spirit, and soul, and body*[^52]. But this spirit he seems also to call the mind, when he says: *With the mind I serve the law of God, but with the flesh the law of sin*[^53]. For it is the same statement: *And the flesh lusts against the spirit, the spirit against the flesh*[^54]: so that what he there said, "mind," is understood here to be called "spirit": not, as you suppose, that *the whole mind is called that which consists of soul and spirit,* which where you read it, I do not know. For our mind we are not accustomed to call anything but our rational and intellectual part: and through this, what the same Apostle says, *Be renewed in the spirit of your mind*[^55], what else did he say than, Be renewed in your mind? For so the spirit of the mind is nothing else than the mind, just as the body of the flesh can be nothing else than the flesh: for this too is written: *In the despoiling of the body of the flesh*[^56]; where he calls the flesh the body of the flesh. He says, indeed, in another way too the spirit of man, which he plainly distinguishes from the mind: *For if I pray in a tongue,* he says, *my spirit prays, but my mind is unfruitful*[^57].

But now we are not speaking of that spirit which is distinct from the mind. That one has its own and equally difficult question: for in many ways and in diverse significations the divine Scriptures name the spirit: but of that of which we now treat, by which we reason, understand, are wise, it is agreed between us that it too is so properly named spirit that it is not the whole soul, but something of it. Nevertheless, that the soul too is a spirit, if you deny it for this reason, because its understanding is distinctly called spirit, you will be able to deny that the whole seed of Jacob is called Israel, since, except Judah, Israel was also distinctly the name of the ten tribes, which were then in Samaria[^58]. But what need is there to delay here longer?

## Examples in the Scriptures.

**23. 37.** Now then, that we may more easily show it, attend: that which is the soul is also called spirit, when you hear or read, at the Lord's dying, what is written, *And bowing his head, he gave up the spirit*[^59]: you so want it understood, as though he signified the whole by a part, not that what is the soul can also be named spirit. But that I may be able more readily to prove what I say, I will adduce you yourself as a witness more quickly and conveniently. For you so defined the spirit that the beasts appear to have not spirit, but soul. For they are called irrational for this reason, that they do not have the force of understanding and reason. Hence, when you admonished man himself to know his own nature, you spoke thus: *For since the good God founded nothing without reason, and procreated man himself a rational animal, capable of understanding, possessed of reason, and lively in sense, who distributes all things devoid of reason by a prudent ordering.* By these words of yours you asserted enough, what is altogether most true, that man is possessed of reason and capable of understanding, which surely the animals devoid of reason are not. Hence by the divine testimony too you compared to the beasts those who do not understand, the beasts surely not having understanding[^60]. Which is also written in another place: *Do not be like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding*[^61].

Since these things are so, attend also to the words by which you defined and described the spirit, when you tried to distinguish it from the soul: *This soul,* you say, *which has its origin from the breath of God, could not be without its own sense and inmost understanding, which is the spirit;* and a little after: *And although the soul,* you say, *gives life to the body, yet that which perceives, which is wise, which is vigorous, must necessarily be the spirit;* likewise a little after: *The soul will be one thing,* you say, *and the spirit another, the wisdom and the sense of the soul.* By these words you indicate enough what you think the spirit of man to be, that is, our rational part, by which the soul perceives and understands; not as it is perceived by the senses of the body, but as is that inmost sense, from which the word "opinion" (sententia) is named.

But from this we are without doubt set before the beasts, in that they are devoid of reason. The beasts, then, do not have spirit, that is, understanding and the sense of reason and wisdom, but soul only. For of them too it was said: *Let the waters bring forth creeping things of living souls,* and: *Let the earth bring forth the living soul*[^62].

That, then, you may most fully and most plainly know that what is the soul is, in the manner of the divine eloquence, also called spirit, the spirit of a beast is so named. And surely the beasts do not have that spirit which your Charity, distinguishing it from the soul, defined. Hence it is manifest that by the general word the soul of a beast could rightly be so called, as is read in the book of Ecclesiastes: *Who knows whether the spirit of the sons of man goes upward, and whether the spirit of the beast goes downward into the earth*[^63]? And likewise in the devastation of the flood it is written: *And all flesh died that moved upon the earth, of fowls, of cattle, and of beasts, and of wild beasts, and every creeping thing that moves upon the earth, and every man, and all things whatsoever that have the spirit of life*[^64]. Where, all the windings of doubt removed, we understand that "spirit" is a general name of the soul. The signification of which name lies open so widely that even God is called Spirit[^65]. And this airy breath, although it is corporeal, is called in the Psalm the spirit of the storm[^66]. Wherefore I think that which is the soul is also named spirit, according to that: *You will take away their spirit, and they will fail*[^67], and again: *His spirit will go forth, and it will return to its own earth*[^68]—that is, the body to its own dust. The name "spirit," then, belongs to the soul from the fact that it is spiritual; the name "soul" from the fact that it gives life to the body, that is, quickens it. I judge that, admonished by these testimonies of the divine pages which I have mentioned, where even the soul of a beast, which has no understanding, is read called spirit, you will no longer deny it. Wherefore, if you both grasp and are wise concerning those things that have been disputed about the incorporeal soul, there is no reason for me to displease you, because I said I know it to be not a body, but a spirit: since it is both shown not to be a body, and is named by the general name of spirit.

## Beware especially those eleven things, of which I warned you in the earlier book.

**24. 38.** Wherefore, if you receive and read these books written to you with lavish love, with love repaid; if, at the beginning of your first book, you hear yourself too, and are eager, as you said, *not to defend your opinion, if it is detected as improbable,* beware especially those eleven things, of which I warned you in the earlier book: lest you so say the soul is from God that he created it neither from nothing, nor from another, but from his own nature; or that through infinite time, and so always, he gives souls, just as he himself who gives always is; or that the soul lost some merit through the flesh, which it had before the flesh; or that the soul recovers through the flesh its former condition, and is reborn through that same flesh through which it had deserved to be defiled; or that the soul, before all sin, deserved to be a sinner; or that infants dead without the baptism of regeneration attain to the pardon of original sins; or that those whom the Lord predestined to baptism can die before, and what the Almighty predestined not be fulfilled in them; or that of those who breathe out before they are baptized it was said what is written: *He was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding*[^69]; and the rest pertaining to this sense; or that some of the mansions which the Lord said are many in his Father's house are outside the kingdom of God[^70];

or that the sacrifice of the body and blood of Christ is to be offered for those who have departed from the body unbaptized; or that some of those who die without the baptism of Christ are received meanwhile into paradise, and afterward attain also the blessedness of the kingdom of heaven. Beware especially these things, son, and do not delight to be surnamed Vincent, if you wish to be a victor over error. Nor, when you do not know something, think that you know it: but, that you may know, learn to not know.

For one does not sin by being ignorant of something in the hidden works of God, but by rashly establishing things unknown as known, and by bringing forward and defending false things as true.

But my ignorance—whether the souls of men are made new, or from the parents (which, however, it is not lawful to doubt are made by God the Creator, not from his own substance)—either ought not to be reproved, or ought to be reproved by him by whom, teaching, it can also be taken away: and that souls have in themselves incorporeal likenesses of bodies, but are not themselves bodies: and that, the distinction of soul and spirit being kept safe, the soul too is universally named spirit—I think I have persuaded your Charity.

But if I could not persuade you, whether nevertheless I said the things I ought to have said to persuade you, those who read will rather judge.

## Let it not be burdensome to you to come to me.

**24. 39.** If indeed you perhaps desire to know any other things—of which very many seem to me to be amended in your books—let it not be burdensome to you to come, not as a disciple to a master, but as a young man to an aged one, as a strong man to a weak one. For although you ought not to have published them, yet with greater and truer glory is each one, corrected, reproved by his own confession, than praised by the mouth of any erring person. Although, in the recitation of those same books, I would not believe that all your hearers and praisers either thought beforehand these things which sound doctrine disapproves, or consented with you to them: but that, the edge of their mind being grazed by the very impulse and course of your recitation, they could attend too little to these things; or certainly that even those who could attend praised in you not the most limpid truth of the matters, but the affluence of words and the faculty and disposition of talent. For very often eloquence is praised, proclaimed, and loved in the hope of a young man; even if he does not yet have the maturity and trustworthiness of a teacher. Wherefore, that you both may be rightly wise, and that what you speak may not only be able to delight others, but even to build them up, you ought to take care about your discourses, putting aside others' applause.
