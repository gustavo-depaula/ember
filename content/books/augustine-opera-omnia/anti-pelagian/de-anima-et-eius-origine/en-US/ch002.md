# Book I: To Renatus

## You have done what my friend ought to have done.

**1. 1.** Your sincerity toward us, Renatus, my dearest brother, along with your brotherly goodwill and the affection of mutual love, we held to be proven even before; but now you have shown it to us as still more proven by your loving diligence, in that you sent me, this past summer, two books by a man whom I did not know at all—though he was not on that account to be despised—Vincent Victor (for that is the name I found written there at the head). They were delivered to me, however, only at the end of autumn, since I had been away. For how could you, my dearest friend, be able or obliged not to bring to my notice writings of any sort, by whatever man, that had come into your hands—even though written to another—where nonetheless my name was mentioned and read, in such a way that my words, which I had published in certain little works, were being contradicted? And so you have done what my most sincere and beloved friend ought to have done.

## Perhaps he did it out of love for me.

**2. 2.** But on this point I am a little distressed: that I am still less known to your Holiness than I would wish, since you supposed that I would take it as though you had done me an injury by making known what another did. But see how far this is from my mind, so that I do not even complain that I have suffered an injury from him. For when he thought differently about certain matters than I do, ought he to have kept silent? Hence I should be grateful that he did not keep silent, but did so in such a way that I too can read what he wrote. Someone may say that he ought rather to have written to me than to another about me; but because I was unknown to him, he did not dare to thrust himself upon me in refuting what I had said. Nor did he think he should consult me where it seems to him there is no room for doubt at all, but that he holds a clearly known and certain opinion.

And he complied with his friend, by whom he says he was compelled to write. And if, in the course of his argument, he let fall anything that redounded to my reproach, I would not believe that he did it out of a wish to insult, but out of the necessity of one who thinks differently. For where a man's mind toward me is unknown and uncertain to me, I judge it better to think the better things than to blame what I have not examined. For perhaps he did it out of love for me, knowing that what he wrote could reach me, while he does not want me to err in those matters in which he thinks it is rather he himself who is not erring. And so I ought to hold welcome even the goodwill of one whose opinion I must reject; and through this, in those matters in which he does not think rightly, he seems to me still to be corrected gently, not detested harshly—especially since, as I hear, he has recently become a Catholic, on which he is to be congratulated. For he was freed from the division and error of the Donatists, or rather of the Rogatists, by which he was formerly held—provided, however, that he understands the Catholic truth as he ought, so that we may truly rejoice at his conversion.

## A deadly draught must not be drunk from a precious cup.

**3. 3.** For he has eloquence by which he can unfold what he thinks. Hence we must deal with him and wish for him that he may think rightly, lest he make delightful things that are useless, and lest, because he has said things eloquently, he seem to have said true things. And yet even in his very eloquence he has much to amend and much that must be restrained from its excessive overflow. This displeased you too, as a serious man, as your writings show. But this is either easily corrected, or, without harm to the faith, is loved by light minds and tolerated by serious ones. For we already have certain men who are foamy in speech but sound in faith[^1].

And so we must not despair that even this in him (although it is tolerable, should it persist) can nonetheless be purged and tempered, and either brought or recalled to a whole and sound measure—especially since he is said to be young, so that what he lacks in skill diligence may supply, and what the rawness of his garrulity belches out the maturity of age may digest. What is troublesome and dangerous, or even pernicious, is if, when eloquence is praised, foolishness is recommended, and a deadly draught is drunk from a precious cup.

## The human soul, if it was not made out of any other thing, was without doubt made out of nothing.

**4. 4.** To begin now to show what is chiefly to be avoided in his argument: he says that the soul was indeed made by God, and is not a part or the nature of God[^2]—which is altogether true. But since he will not admit that it was made out of nothing, and mentions no other creation out of which it was made, and so assigns God as its author in such a way that it is believed God made it neither out of nothing existing, that is, out of nothing, nor out of anything that is not what God is, but out of his very self, he does not realize that he is rolling back into the very thing he thought he had avoided—namely, that the soul is nothing other than the nature of God;

and so it follows that something is made out of the nature of God by this same God, for whose making the matter out of which he makes is the very one who makes; and through this God's nature would be both mutable and, when changed for the worse, would be condemned as the nature of this same God by this same God himself. By your faithful understanding you see how this is not to be thought, and how it is to be shut out from a Catholic heart and fled far away. For the soul was made either out of the breath, or as the very breath of God, in such a way that it is not out of him, but created by him out of nothing.

For just as a man, when he blows, cannot make a breath out of nothing, but gives back the breath he drew from this air—so it must not be thought that for God there were already certain breezes spread about, from which he drew some tiny particle in breathing and poured it back in exhaling, when he breathed into the face of man and in that way made him a soul[^3]. And even were it so, then what he breathed could not be out of him, but out of some underlying breathable thing.

But God forbid that we should deny that the Almighty was able to make out of nothing the breath of life, by which man became a living soul, and that we should be driven into such straits that we either suppose there already was something, which he himself was not, out of which he made the breath, or believe that he made out of his very self what we see was made mutable. For what is out of him must necessarily be of the same nature as he is, and through this also immutable. But the soul, as all confess, is mutable. It is not, then, out of him, since it is not immutable as he is. But if it was not made out of any other thing, it was without doubt made out of nothing, yet by him.

## Vincent Victor holds that the soul is a body.

**5. 5.** But as for his contention that the soul is not a spirit but a body, what else does he aim to bring about than that we are made up not of soul and body, but of two or even three bodies? For when he says that we are made up of spirit, soul, and body, and asserts that all these three are bodies, he plainly thinks we are compacted of three bodies. How great an absurdity follows him in this opinion, I think should be shown to him rather than to you. But this is a tolerable error in a man who has not yet recognized that there is something which, though it is not a body, can nonetheless bear a certain likeness of a body.

## The soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh.

**6. 6.** But who could bear this, that in the second book, when he was trying to solve a most difficult question about original sin—how far it pertains to body and soul, if the soul is not drawn from the parents but breathed in newly by God—when, then, he was straining to unravel so troublesome and so deep a question, he says: *Rightly through the flesh it recovers its former condition, which it had seemed for a little while to have lost through the flesh, so that through the flesh it may begin to be reborn, through which it had deserved to be defiled.* You see indeed that the man, having dared to take up what exceeds his powers, has fallen into so monstrous a precipice as to say that the soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh, when he cannot in any way say from where it drew this desert before the flesh. For if its desert of sin begins from the flesh, let him say, if he can, from where, before its own sin, it deserved to be defiled by the flesh.

For this desert, by which it was sent into sinful flesh so as to be defiled by it, it surely had either from its own self or—what is far more abhorrent—from God. From the flesh it could not have had, before the flesh, the desert by which, deserving to be defiled, it was sent into the flesh. If, then, it had this desert from its own self, how did it have it, since before the flesh it had done nothing evil? But if it is said to have had this desert from God, who could listen to this? Who could bear it? Who could allow it to be said with impunity? For the question here is not what it deserved, so as to be judged worthy of condemnation after the flesh, but what it deserved so as to be condemned, before the flesh, to be sent into the flesh to be defiled. Let him explain this, if he can, who dared to say that the soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh.

## He tries to free himself by the foreknowledge of God, but in vain.

**7. 7.** Likewise in another place, when he set forth, as if to be explained, the same question in which he had entangled himself, he speaks as though in the person of his adversaries: *Why,* they say, *did God punish the soul with so unjust a chastisement as to will to banish it into a body of sin, since it begins to be sinful through fellowship with the sinful flesh, when it could not be sinful?* In the rocky whirlpool, as it were, of this question, he ought surely to have guarded against shipwreck, and not to have committed himself to it where he could not extricate himself by passing through, but only perhaps by turning back—that is, by repenting. For he strives to free himself by the foreknowledge of God, but in vain. For the foreknowledge of God foreknows as sinners those whom he is going to heal; it does not make them sinners.

For if he frees from sin those souls which, innocent and clean, he himself entangled in sin, he heals a wound that he inflicted on us, not one that he found in us. But may God avert it, and may it be altogether far from us, that we should say that when, by the bath of regeneration, God cleanses the souls of little ones, he is then correcting his own evils, which he himself did to them, when he mingled them, having no sin, with sinful flesh, by whose original sin they were to be contaminated. Yet this man, accusing these souls, says that they deserved to be defiled through the flesh, and he cannot say from where they deserved so great an evil before the flesh.

## He entangles himself still further.

**8. 8.** Thinking, then, that he could resolve this question in vain by the foreknowledge of God, he entangles himself still further, and says: *If the soul deserved to be sinful, which could not be sinful, yet it did not remain in sin, because, prefigured in Christ, it ought not to be in sin, just as it could not be.* What does he mean by saying it could not be sinful, or could not be in sin, except, I believe, if it did not come into the flesh? For it could not be sinful with original sin, or be in any way in original sin, except through the flesh, if it is not drawn from a parent. We see, then, that it is freed from sin by grace; but we do not see from where it deserved to cling to sin. What, then, does he mean by saying: if it deserved to be sinful, yet it did not remain in sin? For if I ask him why it did not remain in sin, he will answer most rightly that the grace of Christ freed it. As, then, he says from where the soul of a little one was freed as a sinner, so let him also say from where it deserved to be a sinner.

## Vincent Victor does not free himself at all from his errors unless he repents of having said them.

**8. 9.** But what does he say, to whom what he prefaced has happened? For, in order to set this question before himself, he said: *Other reproaches are heaped up by the querulous murmurings of those who bark against us, and, shaken as if by some whirlwind, again and again we are dashed among monstrous rocks.* If I were saying this about him, perhaps he would be angry. They are his own words; and having set them down, he proposed the question in which he might show the very rocks against which he was dashed and shipwrecked. For he was brought to this point, and carried, driven, and fixed upon such dreadful crags, that he cannot extricate himself at all except by amending what he said—being unable to show by what desert the soul was made sinful, which he did not fear to say deserved, before all its own sin, to be made a sinner. Who deserves so monstrous a punishment without sin, that, conceived in the iniquity of another, before it comes forth from its mother's womb, it is already not without sin? But from this punishment gratuitous grace frees the souls of little ones, who are regenerated in Christ, with no preceding merits of their own: *otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^4].

Accordingly, let this very intelligent man, to whom our hesitation in so great a depth—a hesitation that, if not learned, is at least cautious—is displeasing, say, if he can, by what desert the soul came into this punishment, from which punishment grace frees it without desert. Let him say it, so that, by some reasoning, if he can prevail, he may defend what he said. For I would not demand this, had he not himself said that the soul deserved to be a sinner. Let him say what its desert was, whether it was good or evil. If good, by what good desert did it come into evil? If evil, from where any evil desert before all sin? Again I say: if good, then grace frees it not gratuitously but according to what is owed, since a good merit preceded; and so grace will no longer be grace[^5]. But if evil, I ask what it is: is it that it came into the flesh, into which it would not have come unless he, with whom there is no iniquity, had himself sent it[^6]? Never, then, except by hurling himself into worse things, will he contrive to establish this opinion of his, by which he said that the soul deserved to be a sinner.

And concerning those little ones whose original sin is washed away in Baptism, he found something or other to say, since the foreknowledge of God could have done no harm to those predestined to eternal life if they clung for a little while to another's sin. This would be tolerably said, were this man not entangled by his own words, saying that the soul deserved to be a sinner—from which he does not free himself at all unless he repents of having said it.

## Concerning little ones who are overtaken by death before they are baptized.

**9. 10.** But concerning those little ones who are overtaken by death before they are baptized in Christ, when he wished to give an answer, he dared to promise them not only paradise but even the kingdom of heaven—finding no way out by which to avoid being forced to say that God condemns to eternal death innocent souls, which, with no preceding merit of sin, he inserts into sinful flesh. But somehow sensing what an evil he had said—that the souls of little ones are redeemed without any grace of Christ into eternal life and the kingdom of heaven, and that in them original sin can be loosed without the Baptism of Christ, in which the remission of sins is made—seeing, then, into what a depth of a shipwrecking whirlpool he had cast himself, he says: *Certainly I judge that for them constant offerings and the sacrifices of the holy priests should be offered continually.* Behold another point from which he will never come out unless he repents of having said it. For who would offer the body of Christ except for those who are members of Christ? But since it was said by him, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^7]; and in another place, *Whoever loses his soul for my sake will find it*[^8]: no one becomes a member of Christ except either by Baptism in Christ or by death for Christ.

## A prejudgment against the sacrament of Baptism is sought from the case of the thief.

**9. 11.** Hence even that thief, who was a follower of the Lord not before the cross but a confessor on the cross—from whose case a prejudgment is sometimes sought, or attempted, against the sacrament of Baptism—is counted by holy Cyprian among the martyrs, who are baptized by their own blood[^9], as happens to very many of the unbaptized when persecution rages. For so great a weight was attached, and so much did it avail with him who knows how to weigh these things, that he confessed the Lord crucified, as much as if he had been crucified for the Lord. For then his faith flowered from the wood, when that of the disciples had withered; unless, by whose death's terror it had withered, by his resurrection it should grow green again.

For they despaired of the dying one, but he hoped in one dying with him; they fled from the author of life, but he begged of his companion in punishment; they grieved over the death as of a man, but he believed he would reign after death; they deserted the surety of salvation, but he honored his fellow on the cross. The measure of a martyr was found in him, who then believed in Christ when those who were to be martyrs failed. And this indeed was clear to the eyes of the Lord, who at once conferred so great a happiness on one not baptized, as though washed by the blood of martyrdom[^10]. But let any of us also consider with how great faith, how great hope, how great love he was able to take up death for the living Christ, who sought life in the dying one.

To this is added that it is not incredibly said that the thief who then believed, beside the crucified Lord, was sprinkled, as with a most sacred baptism, by that water which gushed from the wound of his side[^11]. To say nothing of the fact that no one of us knows, and no one proves, that he had not been baptized before he was condemned. But let everyone take these things as he will, provided that no prejudgment is made about Baptism contrary to the Savior's precept, on the example of this thief; and that no one promise to unbaptized little ones, between damnation and the kingdom of heaven, as it were a middle place of rest or of any sort of happiness anywhere whatever. For this the Pelagian heresy too promised them: since it neither fears damnation for little ones, whom it thinks to have no original sin, nor hopes for them the kingdom of heaven, if they do not come to the sacrament of Baptism. But this man, while he confesses that little ones are bound by original sin, has dared to promise them, even unbaptized, the kingdom of heaven as well—which not even those dared to do who assert that they are altogether without any sin. Behold with what snares of presumption he entangles himself, unless he repents of having written such things.

## A prejudgment against the sacrament of Baptism is sought from the case of Dinocrates.

**10. 12.** But concerning Dinocrates, the brother of holy Perpetua, the writing itself is not canonical, nor did she, or whoever wrote it, so write that she said that boy, who died at seven years of age, had departed without Baptism: for whom she is believed to have been heard, when martyrdom was imminent, so that he was transferred from punishments to rest. For boys of that age can already both lie and speak the truth, both confess and deny. And therefore, when they are baptized, they already both recite the creed and themselves answer for themselves to the questions. Who, then, knows whether that boy, after Baptism, had in the time of persecution been estranged from Christ by an impious father through idolatry, on account of which he went into the damnation of death, and came out of it only by the prayers of his sister, who was to die for Christ?

## What will he answer about so many thousands of infants who are born of the impious and depart from this life without the bath of regeneration?

**11. 13.** But even if this be granted to him—which by no reasoning is granted, the Catholic faith and the ecclesiastical rule being kept safe—that the sacrifice of the body and blood of Christ may be offered for unbaptized human beings of any age, as though through such piety of their kin they were helped to reach the kingdom of heaven: what will he answer about so many thousands of infants who are born of the impious, and do not come into the hands of the pious by any compassion, whether divine or human, and depart from this life, in that most tender age, without the bath of regeneration?

Let him say, if he can, from where these souls deserved to be made sinners in such a way that they are not freed from sin even afterward. For if I ask why they deserve to be damned if they are not baptized, I am rightly answered: on account of original sin. Again, if I ask from where they drew original sin, this man will answer: from the sinful flesh, of course. If, then, I ask from where they deserved to be condemned into sinful flesh, having done nothing evil before the flesh, let him find here what to answer—and how they are condemned to undergo the contagions of others' sins, so that neither does Baptism regenerate the ill-begotten, nor do the sacrifices expiate the defiled. For there too these little ones were born of such parents, or are still being born, so that they can be aided by no such help.

Here surely all argumentation fails. For we are not asking from where souls deserved to be damned after fellowship with sinful flesh, but we are asking from where souls deserved to be damned so as to undergo fellowship with sinful flesh, having no sin before fellowship with sinful flesh. It cannot be said, *The contagion of another's sin, shared for a little while, did them no harm, for whom redemption had been prepared in God's foreknowledge.* For we are now speaking of those for whom, departing from the body before Baptism, no redemption comes to aid. It cannot be said, *Those whom Baptism does not wash, frequent sacrifices for them will cleanse; and God, foreknowing this, willed that they should cling for a little to others' sins, with no destruction of eternal damnation, and with the hope of eternal happiness.* For we are now speaking of those whose birth among the impious and from the impious could find no such safeguards. And even if such safeguards could be applied, without doubt they could be of no profit to the unbaptized—just as not even the sacrifices that he recalled from *the book of Maccabees,* made for the sinful dead[^12], would have profited them anything, had they not been circumcised.

## Are souls that are wholly innocent absolved from the bond of original sin, or are they punished with eternal damnation?

**11. 14.** Let this man, then, find, if he can, what he is to say when he is asked what a soul deserved, without any sin, whether original or its own, so to be condemned to undergo another's original sin that it cannot be freed from it; and let him see which of two things he chooses—whether he will say that even those souls of dying little ones, who depart from here without the bath of regeneration, and for whom no sacrifice of the Lord's body is offered, are absolved from the bond of original sin, although the Apostle teaches that through one all go into condemnation[^13], those, namely, whom grace does not help to be rescued, through one, into redemption; or whether he will say that souls having no sin, whether their own or original, and in every way innocent, simple, and pure, are punished with eternal damnation by a just God, when he himself inserts them, not to be freed, into sinful flesh.

## Those souls did not sin before the flesh, nor do they deserve damnation for sins foreseen by God but not committed by them.

**12. 15.** I maintain that neither of these two things is to be said; nor that third thing, that souls sinned elsewhere before the flesh, so as to deserve to be condemned into the flesh. For the Apostle most plainly determined that those not yet born in the flesh had done nothing good or evil[^14]. From which it is clear that little ones, in order to need the remission of sins, have contracted nothing but original sin. Nor that fourth thing, that those souls of little ones, who are to die without Baptism, are banished and condemned into sinful flesh by a just God because he foreknew that, had they reached the age at which they would use free choice, they would have lived badly.

For not even this man dared to say this, placed in such straits; indeed he even spoke against this vanity already clearly and briefly enough, where he says that *God would be unjust if, before the works of one's own will were accomplished, he willed to judge a man who had not been born.* For he gave this answer when he was treating the question against those who say: Why was God making a man whom, as foreknowing, he knew would not be good? For he would be judging one not born, if he had on that account refused to create him, because he foreknew that he would not be good.

And surely, as it seemed even to this man, he ought to judge a man by his accomplished works, not by those foreknown and never permitted to come to pass. For if sins which, if a man lived, he would commit, are condemned in him though dead though not committed, no benefit was conferred on him who *was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding*[^15]—since he will be judged according to the wickedness that was going to be in him, not according to the innocence that was found in him;

and there could be security about no baptized dead person, since even after Baptism men can not only sin in any way whatever but even apostatize. What, then, if one who was baptized and caught up from here was going to be an apostate, had he lived? Shall we think that no benefit was conferred on him in that he *was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding,* and shall we judge that, on account of God's foreknowledge, he is to be judged not as a faithful member of Christ but as an apostate? For how much better, if sins not yet done, not yet even thought, but foreknown and future, are punished, that those two should have been cast out of paradise before sin, lest a sin be committed in so holy and beatifying a place!

And further, foreknowledge itself is altogether emptied out, if what is foreknown will not be. For how is it rightly said that something future is foreknown, which will not be future? How, then, are sins punished which are nothing—that is, which were neither committed before the flesh, since this life had not yet begun, nor after the flesh, since death intervened?

## Let him not dare to affirm what he neither grasps by human reason nor defends by divine authority.

**13. 16.** This interval, then, from when the soul was sent into the flesh until it would be released from the flesh—since it is the soul of a little one and has not lived the age of free choice—he finds nothing on account of which it would be condemned, Baptism not having been received, except original sin. We do not deny that the soul is justly condemned for this sin, since a just law has appointed punishment for sin. But we are asking why it was condemned to undergo this sin, if it is not drawn from that one soul which sinned in the first father of the human race.

Wherefore, if God does not condemn the innocent, nor make guilty those whom he perceives to be innocent; and if the Baptism of Christ in the Church of Christ does not free souls except from sins, whether original or their own; and if souls had absolutely no sin before the flesh; and if sins before they are committed—and much more those that have never been committed—cannot be condemned by a just law: let this man say none of these four things, and let him explain, if he can, by what desert the souls of little ones, who depart without Baptism and are sent into damnation, were sent into sinful flesh—they who committed no sin—so that they might there find a sin on account of which they would be deservedly condemned.

Moreover, if, avoiding these four things which sound doctrine condemns—that is, if not daring to say either that souls existing without any sin are made sinners by God, or that original sin is loosed in them without the sacrament of Christ, or that they sinned somewhere before they were sent into the flesh, or that sins which they never had are condemned in them—if, unwilling to say these things, since they are by no means to be said, he should say that little ones do not draw original sin, and have nothing for which they may be condemned if they depart from here without receiving the sacrament of regeneration, he will fall, without doubt, into the damnable Pelagian heresy, deserving to be damned. That this may not befall him, how much better he holds to my hesitation about the origin of the soul, lest he dare to affirm what he neither grasps by human reason nor defends by divine authority—lest he be forced to profess foolishness while he is afraid to confess ignorance.

## The testimonies of the Scriptures that Vincent Victor brought forward on the question of the soul's origin are shown to be ambiguous.

**14. 17.** Here perhaps he may say that his opinion is defended by divine authority, since he thinks he can prove by the testimonies of the holy Scriptures that souls are made by God, not from propagation, but breathed in newly to individuals. Let him prove it, if he can, and I will confess that I have learned from him what I was seeking with great earnestness. But let him seek other testimonies, lest perhaps he find them: for he has not proved this by the testimonies that he has already set down.

For all that he brought forward here are certain as to something; but as to that which is asked about the origin of the soul, they are shown to be ambiguous.

For it is certain that God gave men breath and spirit, as the prophet says: *Thus says the Lord, who made heaven and founded the earth and the things that are in it, who gives breath to the people upon it, and spirit to those who tread it*[^16]. This man wants this testimony to be taken in the sense he defends, so that when it says, *he gives breath to the people,* it should be believed that he makes souls for the people not from propagation but by breathing them in newly. Let him dare, then, to say that he did not give us our flesh, because the origin of the flesh is drawn from the parents.

And where the Apostle says of a grain of wheat, *God gives it a body as he has willed*[^17], let him deny, if he dares, that wheat is born from wheat, and its blade from the seed according to its kind. But if he does not dare to deny it, how then does he know in what sense it was said, *he gives breath to the people:* whether drawing it from the parents, or breathing it in new?

## The testimony of Isaiah.

**14. 18.** How, too, does he know whether it is a repetition of the statement, *who gives breath to the people upon it, and spirit to those who tread it*[^18]—so that both are understood as said of one thing, and he meant to signify not the soul or the spirit by which human nature lives, but the Holy Spirit? For if the Holy Spirit could not be signified by breath, the Lord would not, after the resurrection, have breathed on the disciples and said, *Receive the Holy Spirit*[^19]. Nor would it be written in the *Acts of the Apostles, There came suddenly a sound from heaven, as of a violent breath being borne along, and there appeared to them divided tongues as of fire, which also settled upon each one of them, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit*[^20]. What if the Prophet foretold this, saying, *who gives breath to the people upon it?* and, as if explaining what he meant by breath, repeated it and said, *and spirit to those who tread it?*

For then it most evidently came to pass, when *they were all filled with the Holy Spirit.* Or if the hundred and twenty men who were then present in one place are not yet to be called a people, certainly when at once four or five thousand believed, and being baptized received the Holy Spirit[^21], who would doubt that a people received the Holy Spirit at once, and a multitude that walked on the earth—that is, men treading the earth? For that spirit which is given as belonging to the nature of man, whether it be given from propagation or breathed in new (of which I say neither is to be affirmed, until one or the other becomes clear without any doubt), is not given to those treading the earth, but to those still enclosed in the maternal womb.

He gave breath, then, to the people upon the earth, and spirit to those treading it, when many believing at once were filled with the Holy Spirit. And he himself gives it to his people, though not to all at once, but to each in his own time, until, by departing from this life and succeeding into this life, the whole number of that same people is completed—so that in this passage of holy Scripture breath is not one thing and spirit another, but a repetition of the same statement.

Just as *he who dwells in the heavens* is not one and *the Lord* another, nor is *to deride* one thing and *to mock* another, but the same statement is repeated, where we read: *He who dwells in the heavens will deride them, and the Lord will mock them*[^22]. Or when it was said: *I will give you the nations for your inheritance, and the ends of the earth for your possession*[^23]. He did not mean one thing by *inheritance,* another by *possession;* nor one thing by *nations,* another by *the ends of the earth:* but it is a repetition of the same statement. And one will find countless expressions of this kind in the divine eloquence, if one attends to what one reads.

## It must still be asked whether God gives the soul from the origin of a preceding generation, or breathes in a new one.

**14. 19.** But what the Greek says, πνοήν, the Latins have variously rendered: sometimes "breath," sometimes "spirit," sometimes "inspiration." For the Greek codices have this word in that prophetic testimony with which we are now dealing, where it was said, *who gives breath to the people upon it*[^24]—that is, πνοήν. It is the same word too where man was given life: *And God breathed into his face the breath of life*[^25]. But it is the same word also in the Psalm where it is sung: *Let every spirit praise the Lord*[^26]. It is the same too in the book of Job, where it is written: *But it is the inspiration of the Almighty that teaches*[^27].

He did not wish to say "breath," but *inspiration,* when in the Greek it is πνοή—which is also in those words of the Prophet about which we are now disputing. And certainly in this place I do not know whether it ought to be doubted that the Holy Spirit was signified. For the discussion was about wisdom, from where it is in men: *For it is not from the number of years,* he says, *but the Spirit that is in men; and the inspiration of the Almighty is what teaches*[^28]—so that by this repetition it might be understood that he did not say of the spirit of man what he said, *the Spirit is in men.* For he wished to show from where they have wisdom, namely, not from themselves; and by repeating it he explained this, saying, *It is the inspiration of the Almighty that teaches.*

Likewise in another place in the same book: *The understanding of my lips,* he says, *understands pure things: the divine Spirit that made me, and the inspiration of the Almighty that teaches me*[^29]. And here too what he says, *inspiration* or breathing-in, is in the Greek πνοήν, which in those words of the prophet was rendered "breath." Wherefore, since it is rash to deny that it was said of the soul of man or of the spirit of man, *who gives breath to the people upon it, and spirit to those who tread it*—although there the Holy Spirit too can far more credibly be understood—by what reasoning, then, will anyone dare to determine that the Prophet meant in that place to signify the soul or the spirit by which our nature lives? Since surely, even if he said most plainly, "who gives the soul to the people upon the earth," it would still have to be asked whether God himself gives it from the origin of a preceding generation—as from the origin of a preceding generation he himself nonetheless gives the body, not only to a man or a beast, but even to a grain of wheat, or of any of the rest, as he has willed[^30]—or whether he breathes in a new one, as the first man received it.

## We wish the origin of the human soul to be defended not ambiguously, as this man does, but by some most certain authority of the divine eloquence.

**14. 20.** There are also those who so understand these prophetic words that, where it says, *He gave breath to the people upon it,* that is, upon the earth, they want breath to be taken as nothing but the soul; but what he added, *and spirit to those who tread it*[^31], they judge to signify the Holy Spirit—in that order, namely, in which the Apostle too says: *Not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animal; afterward, the spiritual*[^32]. For from this prophetic statement that elegant sense too is carved out, that he said *those who tread it* in such a way as to wish it understood, "those who despise it."

For those who receive the Holy Spirit, by the love of heavenly things, despise earthly ones. All these opinions are not against the faith—whether one understands both, that is, both the breath and the spirit belonging to human nature; or takes both as said of the Holy Spirit; or refers the breath to the soul and the spirit to the Holy Spirit. But if the soul and the spirit of man are to be understood here too, then, as it is not to be doubted that God gives it, so it must still be asked from where he gives it—whether from propagation, as indeed he himself gives, but nonetheless gives from propagation, the members of the body; or whether he distributes it new, and not propagated, by breathing it into individuals: which we wish to be defended not ambiguously, as this man does, but by some most certain authority of the divine eloquence.

## Concerning the soul, it must be inquired from where God makes it: whether from propagation, like the body, or by breathing it in, as he made the first one.

**14. 21.** By the same reasoning, what God also says—*For the Spirit will go forth from me, and I have made every breath*[^33]—what he says, *The Spirit will go forth from me,* is indeed to be taken of the Holy Spirit, of whom the Savior too says, *He proceeds from the Father*[^34]; but what was said, *I have made every breath,* cannot be denied to have been said of every soul. But he also makes every body; and that he makes the human body from propagation no one doubts. And through this, concerning the soul, since it is agreed that it is made by him, it must still surely be inquired from where he makes it—whether from propagation, like the body, or by breathing it in, as he made the first one.

## The testimony of Zechariah.

**14. 22.** He added also a third testimony, that it is written: *Who forms the spirit of man within him*[^35]. As if this were denied: but from where he forms it, this is what is asked. For who but God forms even the bodily eye of a man? And, I think, not outside, but within him; and yet, as is certain, from propagation. Since, then, he also forms the spirit of man within him, it must be asked whether by a new breathing-in, or drawn from propagation.

## The testimony of the second book of Maccabees.

**14. 23.** We know too that the mother of the young Maccabees, more fruitful in virtues when her sons suffered than in offspring when they were born, exhorted them thus, saying: *My sons, I do not know how you appeared in my womb. For it was not I who gave you spirit and soul, nor was it I who formed the features and limbs of each of you; but God, who made the world and all that is in it, and made the human race, and searches out the deeds of all, will himself give back to you spirit and soul with great mercy*[^36]. We know these things indeed; but how they support this man in what he asserts, we do not see. For what Christian would deny that God gives men soul and spirit? But in the same way I think this man cannot deny that God gives men tongue, ear, hand, foot, and all the senses of the body, and the form and nature of all the members. For how is he going to deny that these are gifts of God, unless he forgets that he is a Christian? But just as it is agreed that these are made and given by him from propagation, so it must also be asked from where the spirit and soul of man is produced by him, by whom, as their producer, it is given—whether from the parents, or from nothing, or—what this man affirms, but which is in every way to be guarded against—from some existing nature of his breath, not created from nothing, but from himself.

## We demand or seek sure testimonies as to whether it is not from transmission.

**15. 24.** Since, then, the testimonies of the Scriptures that he recalls by no means teach what he tries to persuade us of (for, as concerns this question, they do not express it at all), what is it that he says: *We steadfastly assert that the soul is from the breath of God, not from transmission, because it is given from God?* As though the body were given from anything other than from him by whom it is created, from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things[^37]—although not from his nature, but from his workmanship. *Nor from nothing,* he says, *because it proceeds from God.*

We advise that it is not this that must still be asked, whether it is so; but rather that what he says is altogether untrue—that is, that the soul is neither from transmission nor from nothing; this, I say, we affirm without doubt to be untrue. For it is one of two things: if it is not from transmission, it is from nothing—lest it be so from God as to be of the nature of God, which it is altogether sacrilegious to believe. But as to whether it is not from transmission, we still demand or seek sure testimonies—not such as this man set down, by which what we are asking is not shown.

## Would that he imitated the mother of the Maccabees.

**15. 25.** Would that, in so great a depth of the question, so long as he does not know what he is saying, he imitated the mother of the Maccabees—who, although she knew that she had conceived her sons of a husband, and that they had been created for her by the Creator of all, whether according to the body or according to soul and spirit, nevertheless said: *I do not know how you appeared in my womb*[^38]. I would have this man say what she did not know. For these things which I have mentioned she surely knew: how, according to the body, they had come into her womb, since she could not doubt that she had conceived them of a husband. She confessed also—for she surely knew this too—that God had given them soul and spirit, that he himself had formed for them their features and limbs. What, then, did she not know?

Was it, perhaps, what we too do not know—whether God drew from the parents the soul and spirit which he without doubt gave them, or breathed it in new, as for the first man? But whether it was this or something else about the constitution of human nature that she did not know, she said that she did not know it; she did not rashly defend what she did not know. Nor yet would this man say to her what he was not ashamed to say to us: *Man, placed in honor, did not understand; he has been compared to the senseless beasts, and is made like them*[^39].

Behold, this woman said of her sons: *I do not know how you appeared in my womb;* and yet she is not compared to the senseless beasts. *I do not know,* she said; and, as though they asked her why she did not know, she added: *For it was not I who gave you spirit and soul.* He, then, who gave it knows from where he made what he gave—whether he drew it from propagation, or breathed it in new;

*which I,* she says, *do not know. Nor was it I who formed the features and limbs of each of you*[^40]: he who formed them knows whether he formed them together with the soul, or gave the soul to them already formed. In what way, then—whether this way or that—her sons came into her womb, she did not know; and yet she knew this, that he who gave the whole would give it back. But let this man choose, in so deep and hidden a secret of human nature, what this woman did not know—only let him not judge her a liar, nor compare to the senseless beasts one who does not know. Whatever it was that she did not know, it surely pertained to the nature of man; yet a man could not know it without fault. Wherefore I too say of my own soul: I do not know how it came into my body; for I did not give it to myself. He who gave it knows whether he drew it from my parent, or created it new, as for the first man. I too shall know, if he himself teaches me, whenever he wills. But now I do not know; nor am I ashamed, like this man, to confess that I do not know what I do not know.

## The testimony of the Apostle Paul.

**16. 26.** *Learn,* he says, *behold, the Apostle teaches.* I will plainly learn, if the Apostle teaches: for it is none but God who teaches through the Apostle. But what, after all, is it that the Apostle teaches? *Behold,* he says, *when he was speaking with the Athenians, he steadfastly set this forth, saying: Since he himself gives to all life and spirit*[^41]. For who denies this? *But understand,* he says, *what the Apostle says:* "gives," he says, not "gave"; recalling it to an infinite and perpetual time, not pronouncing of the past and completed. And what he gives without ceasing, he always gives—just as he himself who gives always is. I have set down his words as I found them in the second book of those that you sent.

First, see here how far he has gone while he strives to affirm what he does not know. For he dared to say that God gives souls to those being born not only now and in this present age only, but through infinite time without ceasing, and altogether always. *He always gives,* he says, *just as he himself who gives always is.* That I understand what the Apostle said, since it is plain enough, God forbid that I should deny; but what this man says he himself ought to understand to be against the Christian faith, and to guard hereafter against saying it. For when the dead have risen, no one will be born any longer; and therefore he will not then give souls to those being born, but will judge those whom he gives in this age together with their bodies. He does not, then, always give, although he himself always is who now gives.

Nor yet, because the blessed Apostle did not say "gave" but *gives,* does there follow from this what this man wants to conclude, that God does not give souls from propagation. For he himself gives, even if he gives from propagation. For the members of the body, and the senses of the body, and the form of the body, and altogether the substance of the body, he himself gives to men, although he gives from propagation. For, because the Lord says, *If God so clothes the grass of the field, which is today and tomorrow is cast into the oven*[^42], and does not say "clothed," as at first when he established it, but says *clothes,* which he does even now—shall we for that reason deny that lilies are brought forth from the origin of their kind?

What, then, if the soul and spirit of man is so given by God too, so long as it is given, and yet is given from the propagation of its kind? This I neither defend nor refute. But if it is to be defended or refuted, I advise that it must be done by clear, not ambiguous, testimonies. Nor am I on that account to be compared to the senseless beasts, because I declare that I do not yet know this; but rather to cautious men, because I do not dare to teach what I do not know. This man, however, I do not in turn, as if repaying a curse for a curse, compare to the beasts; but I admonish him as a son, that he should confess that he does not know what he does not know, and not undertake to teach what he has not yet learned—lest he be compared, not to the beasts, but to those men of whom the Apostle says, *wishing to be teachers of the law, understanding neither what they say nor the things about which they make assertions*[^43].

## Men are from God according to the body too.

**17. 27.** For how is it that he so fails to attend to the Scriptures of which he speaks that, when he reads that men are from God, he contends that they are from God not according to the body too, but only according to soul and spirit? For what the Apostle says, *We are from him*[^44], this man wants referred not to the body, but only to soul and spirit. If, then, bodies are not from God, what is written is false: *From whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things*[^45].

Next, where the same Apostle says, *For as woman is from man, so also man is through woman*[^46], let this man explain to us what propagation he meant to signify—of the soul, or of the body, or of both. But he does not want souls to be from propagation. It remains, then, that, according to him and all who do away with the propagation of souls, the Apostle signified only the male and female body, saying, *For as woman is from man, so also man is through woman:* because the woman was made from the man, so that the man too might afterward be born through woman. If, then, the Apostle in saying these things wished to be understood not also souls and spirits, but only the bodies of both sexes, why did he immediately add, *but all things are from God,* unless because bodies too are from God? For so he says: *For as woman is from man, so also man is through woman; but all things are from God.*

Let this man, then, choose of what it was said. If of bodies, then surely bodies too are from God. What is it, then, that wherever this man reads in the Scriptures *from God,* when it is a matter of men, he wants understood not bodies too, but only souls and spirits?

But if what was said, *but all things are from God,* was said both of the body of either sex and of soul and spirit, then woman is from man according to all things. *For woman is from man, man is through woman; all things are from God.* What *all things,* except those of which he was speaking—that is, both that man from whom the woman is, and that woman who is from man, and that man who is through woman?

For it is not that man who is through woman, from which man the woman is; but the man who was afterward born from man through woman, just as men are born today too. And through this, if the Apostle in saying these things was speaking of bodies, then without doubt the bodies of either sex are from God. Moreover, if he wants only the souls and spirits of men to be from God, then surely woman is from man according to soul and spirit too, and nothing will any longer be left to those who dispute against the propagation of souls. But if he so divides as to say that the woman is from the man according to the body, but from God according to soul and spirit, how will what the Apostle says be true—*but all things are from God*—if the woman's body is so from the man that it is not from God?

Wherefore, that the Apostle may rather speak the truth than this man be preferred to the Apostle, woman is from man, whether according to the body alone or according to the whole of which human nature consists (for we affirm none of these as certain, but are still asking which of them is true); and man is through woman, whether the whole nature of man, which is born through woman, is drawn from the father, or only the flesh—on which there is still a question; yet all things are from God, on which there is no question—that is, both body and soul and spirit, both of man and of woman. For even if they were not born or drawn from God, or did not flow from him, so as to be of his nature, nevertheless they are from God. For from him by whom they were created, founded, and made, they have it that they exist.

## The manner of speaking of the Scriptures.

**17. 28.** *But,* he says, *by saying, And he himself gives to all life and spirit, and then adding, And he made from one blood every race of men*[^47], *the Apostle referred the soul and spirit originally to their author, the body to transmission.* On the contrary, one who does not wish rashly to deny the propagation of souls, before it becomes clearly evident whether it is so or not so, has something in these words of the Apostle that he may understand: that by *from one* with *blood* he meant "from one man," signifying the whole by a part in the manner of speech.

For if it is permitted to him to understand the whole by a part in what is written, *And man became a living soul*[^48]—so that there the spirit too is understood, of which the Scripture there was silent—why is it not permitted to others so to take what was said, *from one blood,* so that there too both soul and spirit can be understood, since the man signified by the name of blood consists not of body alone, but also of soul and spirit?

For just as one who defends the propagation of souls ought not to overwhelm this man on the ground that it was written of the first man, *In whom all sinned*[^49]—for it was not said, "in whom the flesh of all sinned," but *all* was said, that is, all men, though man is not flesh alone—just, then, as this man ought not to be overwhelmed on this ground, because perhaps it was said "all men" in such a way that they were understood according to the flesh alone, so this man ought not on this ground to press the defenders of the propagation of souls, because it was said, *every race of men from one blood*[^50], as though for that reason only the flesh belonged to propagation. For if this is true which they assert, that the soul is not from the soul, but only the flesh from the flesh, then it was said *from one blood* in such a way that not the whole man was signified by a part, but only the flesh of one man; and that which was said, *In whom all sinned,* the flesh alone of all men is to be understood, which was transfused from there, the Scripture signifying the part by the whole. But if that is true, that the whole man is propagated from the whole man—that is, body, soul, and spirit—there it was said properly, "in whom all men sinned"; but here, figuratively, *from one blood,* the whole is signified by a part—that is, the whole man, who consists of soul and flesh, or rather, as this man likes to speak, of soul and spirit and flesh.

For the divine eloquence is accustomed to signify both the whole by a part and the part by the whole. For the whole is signified by a part where we read: *To you all flesh will come*[^51], since by flesh the whole man is understood. But the part by the whole, when Christ is said to be buried, though his flesh alone was buried. As for that which is set down in this testimony of the Apostle, that *he himself gives to all life and spirit*[^52], I think that, according to the earlier discussion, it moves no one. For he himself gives; but we are still asking from where he gives—whether from a new breathing-in, or from propagation. For he is most rightly said to give even the substance of the flesh, which nevertheless it is not denied that he gives from propagation.

## The testimony of Genesis.

**18. 29.** Now let us look at that testimony from Genesis, where the woman, made from the side of the man, was brought to him, and he said: *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^53]. For this man thinks that Adam ought to have said, "soul of my soul," or, "spirit of my spirit," if this too had been drawn from him. But those who assert the propagation of souls think they thereby fortify their opinion more invincibly, because, although it is written that God took a rib from the man's side and built it into a woman, it is not added that he breathed the breath of life into her face[^54]: therefore, they say, because she had already been given a soul from the man. For if she had not, they say, holy Scripture would by no means have defrauded us of the knowledge of this matter[^55]. But as to what Adam said, *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh,* and did not say, "spirit or soul, of my spirit or of my soul," it can be answered by them, just as was shown above, that this, *bone and my flesh,* was said with the whole understood by a part; but that what was drawn out had been given a soul, not dead. For it is not to be denied that the Almighty could do this, merely because no man can cut off anything with a soul from human flesh.

For as to what Adam went on to add, *She shall be called woman, because she was taken from her man*[^56], why did he not rather say, in a way that would confirm the opinion of these men, "because her flesh was taken from her man"? And so here those who think otherwise can say that, because it was not written that the flesh of the woman, but the woman, was taken from her man, she must be taken as the whole, with soul and spirit. For although the soul lacks sex, nevertheless, when women are spoken of, it is not necessary to understand them apart from the soul. Otherwise they would not be admonished to adorn themselves thus: *Not with braided hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly attire, but,* he says, *as becomes women, professing piety through good conduct*[^57]. Surely piety is within, in the soul or in the spirit, and yet they were called women, even to adorn themselves within, where there is no sex.

## I admonish both sides not to trust in things unknown and not rashly to dare to affirm what they do not know.

**18. 30.** Since, then, these men have so contended among themselves in alternating discourse, I judge between them in such a way as to admonish both sides not to trust in things unknown, and not rashly to dare to affirm what they do not know. For if it were written, "He breathed the breath of life into the face of the woman, and she became a living soul," not even so would it yet follow that the soul is not propagated from the parents, unless this too were likewise read written of their son. For it could be that a member not given a soul, drawn out from the body, needed to be given a soul, while the soul of the son was drawn from the father through the mother by the transfusion of propagation. But where it is passed over in silence, it is concealed, not denied; yet neither is it affirmed.

And through this, if anywhere perhaps it is not passed over in silence, it must be established by clearer documents. Hence neither are those who defend the propagation of souls helped at all by the fact that God did not breathe into the face of the woman; nor ought those who deny it to persuade themselves of what they do not know, because Adam did not say, "soul of my soul."

For just as, the question being not resolved but remaining, the Scripture could be silent that the woman, God breathing, received a soul as her man did, so, the question being not resolved but remaining, the Scripture could be silent so that Adam did not say, "soul of my soul." And through this, if the soul of the first woman is from the man, the whole was signified by a part where we read, *This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^58], since the whole was taken from the man, not the flesh alone. But if it is not from the man, but God breathed it into her as into the man, the part was signified by the whole where we read, *You were taken from your man*[^59], since not her whole flesh was taken.

## New, altogether certain testimonies are needed.

**18. 31.** Wherefore, since by these testimonies—which, as far as concerns this matter, are surely ambiguous—this question is not resolved, this nonetheless I know: that men argue in this way, who from this think that the soul of the woman is not from the soul of the man, because it was not said, "soul of my soul," but *flesh of my flesh*[^60], just as the Apollinarists argue, or whoever else there are, against the soul of the Lord, which they deny on the ground that they read it written, *The Word was made flesh*[^61].

For if a soul too were there, they say, it ought to have been said, "the Word was made man." But to these it is said that the Scripture is accustomed to name the whole man by the word "flesh," as it is: *And all flesh will see the salvation of God*[^62]—for flesh without a soul can see nothing—since in very many other places of the holy Scriptures it is shown, without any ambiguity, that not flesh alone, but also a human, that is, rational, soul is in the man Christ. Hence these men too, by whom the propagation of souls is defended, could take it that *bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh*[^63] was said with the whole understood by a part, so that there the soul too was understood, just as we take the Word made flesh to be not without a soul:

if only, as other testimonies teach that Christ has a human soul, so these men too would establish the propagation of souls by some testimonies that are not ambiguous. By like turn, therefore, we admonish also those who do away with the propagation of souls, to assert by sure documents that new souls are breathed in by God; and then to defend that what was said, *bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh,* was said not figuratively, the whole by a part, so that the soul too is understood along with it, but properly, of the flesh alone.

## Let them not agree with this man that souls are from the breath of God in such a way that they are not from nothing.

**19. 32.** Since these things are so, I see that this book must now be closed. For it contains all that seemed to me most necessary, by which those who read it may know that they ought to beware lest they agree with this man, whose two books you sent me, on this point: namely, lest they believe that souls are so from the breath of God that they are not from nothing. For whoever believes this, even if he denies it in words, by the thing itself cries out that souls have the substance of God[^64], and are of his kind, not by gift but by nature. For from whom anyone draws the origin of his nature, that from him he draws the kind of his nature can in no way be soberly denied. But this man is so contrary to himself that he says *souls are of the kind of God, not by nature but by gift, and yet not made from nothing, but draw their origin from him;* and so he does not hesitate to recall them—what he had first denied—to the nature of God.

## Many absurdities of Vincent Victor.

**19. 33.** But the breathing-in of new souls without propagation we by no means forbid to be defended—but by those who can find something, whether in the canonical Books that is not ambiguous, for resolving this most knotty question, or in their own reasonings that is not contrary to Catholic truth; not by such men as this one has shown himself, who, finding nothing to say, and unwilling to suspend his deliberation, not measuring his powers at all, lest he keep silent, dared to say that *the soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh, and the soul deserved to be a sinner*—of which he could find no desert, whether good or evil, before the flesh. And that *original sin can be loosed in little ones departing from the body without Baptism, and that the sacrifice of the body of Christ is to be offered for them,* who are not incorporated into Christ through his sacraments in his Church; and that *they, departing from this life without the bath of regeneration, do not go only to rest, but can even reach the kingdom of heaven.* And many other absurdities, all of which it seemed too long to gather and arrange in this book. Far be it, then, that the propagation of souls, if it is false, should be refuted by such men; and that the breathing-in of new souls, if it is true, should be defended by such men.

## Four things to be guarded against in every way.

**19. 34.** Wherefore, whoever wishes to defend that, as is said, new souls are breathed into those being born, and are not drawn from the parents, let them guard in every way against any of those four things which I mentioned above: that is, let them not say that sinful souls are made by God through another's original sin; let them not say that little ones who depart without Baptism can reach eternal life and the kingdom of heaven, original sin being loosed by any other means whatever; let them not say that souls sinned somewhere before the flesh, and on that desert were thrust down into sinful flesh; let them not say that sins which were not found in them, because they were foreknown, were deservedly punished, when they were not permitted to reach that life in which they would commit them. Saying, then, none of these four things, since any of them is false and impious, let them also find most certain testimonies of the Scriptures on this matter, and let them defend this opinion of theirs—not only with no forbidding on my part, but even with my favor and thanks.

But if they do not find a most certain authority of the divine eloquence on this matter, and are compelled by their poverty to say any of those four things, let them restrain themselves, lest through that very poverty they be compelled to say also that the souls of little ones do not have original sin, according to the Pelagian heresy, long ago damnable and most recently damned. For it is better for a man to confess that he does not know what he does not know, than to fall into a heresy either already damned or to found a new one, while he rashly dares to defend what he does not know. The other false and absurd things of this man, in which he strays from the path of truth not so dangerously, but nonetheless does stray, since they are many, and since, if the Lord wills, I want to write something to him too about his books—there perhaps I will show all of them, or, if I cannot all, very many.

## I want him not condemned but corrected.

**20. 35.** But this book, which I thought should be written to you rather than to anyone else—you who, as a true Catholic and a good friend, have faithfully and kindly taken care both of our faith and of my reputation—you will give to be read, or to be copied, to whomever you can, or to whomever you judge it should be given. In it I thought the presumption of this young man should be so restrained and refuted that nonetheless I love him, and would have him not condemned but corrected, and so advance in the great house, which is the Catholic Church, to which divine mercy has brought him, that he may be in it *a vessel sanctified unto honor, useful to the Lord, prepared* always *for every good work*[^65], both by living well and by speaking soundly. Moreover, if I ought to love him, as I do, how much more you, brother, whose goodwill toward me, and whose Catholic faith, cautious and sober, I know most well. Hence it came about that you took care to have those books copied and sent to me, which displeased you, and in which you found my name set down otherwise than you would wish, with a truly brotherly and plainly sincere love. So far, then, am I from being angry at your love because you did it, that rather, had you not done it, I ought by the full right of friendship to be angry. I give, therefore, abundant thanks. But how I have received your deed, I have indicated more plainly by this, that I composed this book to you, as soon as I read those, without any delay.
