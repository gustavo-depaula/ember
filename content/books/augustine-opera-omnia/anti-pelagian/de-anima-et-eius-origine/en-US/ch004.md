# Book III: To Vincent Victor

## I love you not to be followed, but to be corrected.

**1. 1.** What I thought I ought to write to you, this I want you to consider first, most beloved son Victor: that if I despised you, I would by no means have done it. Nor yet, for that reason, should you so abuse our humility as to think yourself approved because you see that you were not despised. For I love you not to be followed, but to be corrected; and since I do not despair that you can be corrected, I do not want you to wonder that I cannot despise one whom I love.

For if I ought to love you before you were in communion with us, that you might be a Catholic, how much more ought I to love you now in communion, lest you be a new heretic, and that you may be such a Catholic that no heretic can resist you?

For as far as appears from the gifts of talent which God has already bestowed on you, you will surely be wise if you believe that you are not so; and if, that you may be so, you ask of him who makes men wise, piously, suppliantly, and insistently; and if you prefer not to be deceived by error than to be honored by the praises of those who err.

## We do not want you to have such a surname, as though you were the monument of a dead heretic.

**2. 2.** First, in your books, the title of your name made me anxious for you. For when I had asked who Vincent Victor was, from those who knew you and happened to be present, I heard that you had been a Donatist, or rather a Rogatist, but had recently come into communion with the Catholic Church. And when I rejoiced as much as we are accustomed to rejoice over those whom we know to have been freed from that error—nay rather, even much more, because your talent, in which I delighted in your writings, I saw had not remained with the adversaries of truth—

it was added by those who reported it, what saddened me amid those joys, that you had wished to be surnamed Vincent because you still hold in your mind, as a great and holy man, the successor of Rogatus, who was called by this name; and on that account you wished his name to be your surname. Nor were there lacking those who said that you had even boasted of this, that he himself had appeared to you in some vision, and so helped you to compose these books, about which I have undertaken to deal with you in this little work of ours, that, as far as concerns the matters themselves and their reasonings, he himself dictated to you what you should write. But if this is true, I no longer wonder that you could say those things which, if you listen patiently to my admonition, and consider and handle those books with a Catholic mind, you will without doubt repent of having said. For he who, as the Apostle reveals, *transfigures himself into an angel of light*[^1], was transfigured for you into him whom you believe to have been, or to be, as it were an angel of light. And in that way, indeed, he avails less to deceive Catholics, when he transfigures himself not into angels of light, but into heretics; but I would not now want you, a Catholic, to be deceived by him. Let it, then, torment you to have learned what is true, by as much as he had rejoiced to have persuaded you of what is false.

But, that you may not love a dead man, whose love can harm you but cannot profit him, I admonish you to consider this briefly: that he surely is not holy and just, if you have escaped the snares of the Donatist or Rogatist heretics; but if you judge him holy and just, you, by being in communion with Catholics, have perished. For surely you feign yourself a Catholic, if in mind you are that which he was whom you love. And you know how terribly it is written, that *the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee the feigned*[^2]. But if, being truly in communion, you do not feign yourself a Catholic, why do you still so love a dead heretic that you wish to boast yourself still by the name of one whose error you no longer hold? We do not want you to have such a surname, as though you were the monument of a dead heretic. We do not want your book to have such a title as we would say to be false if we read it on his tomb. For we do not know Vincent to be a victor, but vanquished; and would that it were fruitfully, as we want you to be vanquished by truth.

But you are thought to have wished, shrewdly and cunningly, when you call your books, which you desire to be believed dictated to you by his revealing, "Vincent Victor's," not so much that you should be called Vincent as that he should be called Victor, as though, by revealing to you what you should write, he had vanquished error. To what end these things for you, son?

Be rather a true, not a feigned, Catholic, lest the Holy Spirit flee you, and Vincent be able to profit you nothing—Vincent, into whom the most malignant spirit transfigured himself to deceive you: for those things are his, by whatever fraud persuaded to you.

Which things, if, being admonished, you correct them with pious humility and Catholic peace, they will be judged the errors of a most studious young man, desiring rather to be amended than to remain in them. But if for them he should persuade you even of obstinate contention—which God forbid—then it will be necessary that they be condemned, as heretical dogmas, together with their author, with pastoral and medicinal care, namely, before through the incautious crowd the dire contagions creep, when, not by the truth but by the name of love, wholesome discipline is neglected.

**3. 3.** If you ask what those things are, you can indeed read my writings to our brothers, Renatus the servant of God, and the priest Peter, to whom you thought these very matters of which we are treating should be written, *obeying, as you assert, the will of him who asked.* For they will without doubt give them to you to read, if you wish, and will press them on you even unasked. Nevertheless, here too I will not be silent about what I most desire to be amended in those books of yours and in your faith.

## You press in a comparison by which we are to understand how God makes the soul from himself without detriment to his nature, a soul which is not what he is.

The first is, that *you do not want the soul to have been so made by God that he made it from nothing, but from his very self.* In which you do not think it a consequence that it is of the nature of God: because surely how impious that is, you yourself recognize. That you may be free from this impiety, you must so say that God is the author of the soul, that it was made by him, not from him. For what is from him, like the only-begotten Son, is of the same nature of which he too is. But that the soul may not be of the same nature of which he is, it was indeed made by him, but not from him. Either, then, say from where, or confess from nothing.

What is it that you say, that *it is a certain particle of the breath of the nature of God?* Do you deny that the very breath of the nature of God, of which this is a particle, is of the same nature of which God is? If you deny it, then God made from nothing this breath too, of whose breath you want the soul to be a particle. Or if not from nothing, say from where God made it. If from his very self, then he himself is—God forbid—the material of his own work. But you say, *when he makes a breath or wind from his very self, he himself remains entire:* as though the fire of a lamp too did not remain entire, when another is kindled from it, and yet is of the same, not another, nature.

**4. 4.** *But,* you say, *when a wineskin is inflated by us, no portion of our nature or quality is infused; since this very breath, by which, drawn in, the skin is filled and stretched, is given out without any diminution of ourselves.* To these words of yours you still add and dwell, and press in a comparison as though necessary, by which we are to understand how God, without any detriment to his nature, both makes the soul from his very self, and the soul, made from him, is not what he is. For you say: *Is the inflation of the skin a portion of our soul, or do we fashion men when we inflate skins, or do we suffer detriment of ourselves in anything, when we divide our breaths into different things? But we suffer no detriment, when we transmit breath from ourselves to something; and, the full quality and entire quantity of our own breath remaining in us, we do not remember to feel any loss from the inflation of the skin.*

In this comparison, which seems to you elegant and fitting enough, attend to how greatly you are deceived. For you say that God is incorporeal, and breathes the soul, made not from nothing by himself, but from his very self, as corporeal: whereas we emit a breath, although corporeal, yet more subtle than our bodies; and we exhale it not from our soul, but from this air, through the inward parts of the body. For the soul, at whose nod the other members of the body too are moved, moves the lungs for drawing in and giving back this airy breath, as it moves bellows. For besides solid and liquid nourishments, from which come food and drink, God has poured around us this third nourishment of air, which we so take that we can be a long time without food and drink, but without this third nourishment, which the air surrounding us on every side furnishes to us as we breathe in and breathe out, we cannot live even for a brief space of time. But just as food and drink must not only be taken in, but also given out through the passages established for this, lest by either they harm us, either by not entering or by not going out, so this third breathable nourishment, because it is not allowed to remain in us, nor is corrupted by lingering, but is given out as soon as it is taken in, received not other passages, but the same—that is, the mouth, or the nostrils, or both—both by which it would enter and by which it would go out.

## In this comparison attend to how greatly you are deceived.

**4. 5.** In yourself prove for yourself what I say: emit the breath by blowing, and see whether you last, if you do not take it back; take it back by breathing in, and see what straits you suffer, if you do not again emit it. This, then, we do when we inflate a skin, as you say, which we do in order to live: except that then we draw a little more, so as to emit a little more, so that we may force the breathable breath—that is, the wind—into the skin to be filled and stretched, not by the quiet of breathing in and breathing out, but by the impulse of panting. How, then, do you say, *we suffer no detriment, when we transmit breath from ourselves to something; and, the full quality and entire quantity of our own breath remaining in us, we do not remember to feel any loss from the inflation of the skin?* It appears, son, that if you ever inflated a skin, you did not notice what you did. For what you lose by blowing, you do not feel, taking it back at once. But you can learn this most easily, if you would rather do this than defend your sayings, because they have already been said—not by inflating a skin, but, inflated yourself, and by inflating your hearers, whom you ought to build up with true things, with the empty noise of windy speech.

In this matter I send you not to a teacher, but to yourself. Emit a breath into a skin, and close your mouth at once, and hold your nostrils, and so at least feel that what I say is true. For when you begin to suffer intolerable straits, what will you desire to take back with open mouth and nostrils, if, whenever you have blown, you think you have lost nothing? See in what an evil you are, unless by drawing in you take back what by pouring out you had given. See what losses and detriments that breathing-in would have caused, unless the breathing-out had repaired them. For unless what you spent on filling the skin returns to you likewise, the entrance being opened, to nourish you, what will remain to you, not only from which to inflate it, but from which you yourself can live?

## God can make from nothing a breath that lives, perceives, is mutable.

**4. 6.** These things you ought to have considered when you wrote; and not, by this comparison of skins inflated or to be inflated, to introduce to us a God who either, from another nature that already existed—as we make a breath from this air poured around us—breathes souls; or certainly, what both is abhorrent to that comparison and abounds in impiety, a God who, without any detriment indeed to himself, yet from his nature, either brings forth something mutable, or, what is worse, as though he himself were the material of his own work, makes it.

In order, then, that we may apply some comparison from our own breath to this matter, this rather must be believed: that, just as we make a breath when we blow—not from our nature, but, because we are not almighty, from this air poured around us, which we draw in and give back when we breathe in and breathe out—a breath neither living nor perceiving, although we live and perceive; so God can make a breath living and perceiving, not from his nature, but because he is so almighty that he can create what he wills even from that which altogether does not exist, that is, from nothing—but, plainly, since he himself is immutable, a mutable one.

## The example of Elisha.

**5. 7.** But what does it mean, that you thought you should add to this comparison the example of blessed Elisha, that by blowing into the boy's face he raised the dead one[^3]? Do you, then, think that the breath of Elisha became the soul of the boy? I would not believe that you stray so far from the truth.

If, then, that soul which had been taken from the living boy so that he died was the very same one restored to him so that he revived, what is it to the matter that you said *nothing was diminished from Elisha,* as though something were believed to have passed from him, from which the boy might live? But if it was said for this reason, because he blew and remained entire, what need was there for you to say this of Elisha raising the dead, which you could no less say of anyone blowing and raising no one?

You spoke incautiously, indeed (since far be it that you should believe the breath of Elisha became the reviving soul of the boy), in that you wished to distinguish that first deed of God from this deed of the prophet by this, that the one blew once, the other thrice.

For you said that Elisha breathed into the face of the deceased son of that Shunammite, after the likeness of the primeval origin. *And when,* you say, *the divine power kindled the dead members, reanimated to their former vigor, through the breath of the prophet, nothing was diminished from Elisha, through whose breath the dead body received again a living soul and spirit: except that the Lord once breathed into the face of man, and he lived; Elisha three times breathed upon the face of the dead, and he revived.* Thus these words of yours sound, as though only the number of blowings

made the difference, so that the prophet too is believed to have done what God did. And this, then, must be amended. For so great a difference was there between that work of God and this of Elisha, that the one blew the breath of life, by which man became a living soul[^4]; but the other blew a breath neither perceiving nor living, but figuring something for the sake of signifying.

Finally, that this boy might revive, the prophet did not bring it about by giving him a soul, but by loving so that God might do this, he obtained it. But as to your saying that he blew thrice, either your memory, as is wont to happen, or a fault of the codex deceived you. What more? You need not seek some examples and arguments to establish this, but rather your opinion must be amended and changed. Do not, then, believe, do not say, do not teach *that God made the soul not from nothing, but from his nature,* if you wish to be a Catholic.

## The second error: that God will always give souls.

**6. 8.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that through infinite time, and so always, God gives souls, just as he himself who gives always is,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For there will be a time when God will not give souls, while he himself nonetheless does not cease to be. What you said, *he always gives,* could indeed have been taken so as to be understood as giving without ceasing, so long as men beget and are begotten[^5], just as it was said of certain ones: *Always learning, and never coming to the knowledge of the truth*[^6]—for what is set down here, "always," is not so taken as though they never cease to learn, since without doubt they do not learn when they cease to live in this body, or when they begin to burn in the punishment of the fire of Gehenna; but you did not allow your word to be so taken, when you said *he always gives,* since indeed you thought it should be recalled to infinite time.

And this was too little: but, as though it were asked of you that you should explain more openly how you said *he always gives,* you added and said *just as he himself who gives always is.* This the sound and Catholic faith altogether condemns. For far be it that we should believe that God always gives souls, just as he himself who gives always is. For he himself so always is that he never ceases to be; but he will not always give souls, but, the age of generation being finished[^7], there being no longer any being born to whom they are to be given, he will without doubt cease to give.

## The third error: that the soul lost some merit through the flesh.

**7. 9.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that the soul lost some merit through the flesh, as though it had a good merit before the flesh,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For the Apostle says that those not yet born did nothing good or evil[^8]. From where, then, could the soul have a good merit before the flesh, where it had done nothing good? Or will you perhaps dare to say that it lived well before the flesh, which you cannot show even to have been? How, then, do you say: *You do not want the soul to contract ill health from the flesh of sin, to which in turn you may see sanctification pass through the flesh, so that through it it may recover the state through which it had lost its merit?* These dogmas, by which the soul is thought to have had some good state and good merit before the flesh, if perhaps you do not know it, the Catholic Church has condemned—apart from the ancient heretics—even more recently in the Priscillianists.

## The fourth error: that the soul deserved to be defiled through the flesh.

**7. 10.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that the soul recovers through the flesh a former condition, and is reborn through that through which it had deserved to be defiled,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For—to pass over the fact that, in what you said, *Rightly, then, through the flesh it recovers its former condition, which it had seemed for a little while to have lost through the flesh, so that through it it may begin to be reborn, through which it had deserved to be defiled,* you proved yourself, so close at hand, contrary to your very self; so that you, a man who a little before had said that the soul recovers through the flesh the state through which it had lost its merit—where in no way can anything but a good merit be understood, which you surely want recovered through the flesh in Baptism—again said that it deserved to be defiled through the flesh, where now not a good but an evil merit can be understood—to pass over this, then: to believe at all that the soul had either a good or an evil merit before the flesh is not Catholic.

## The fifth error: that the soul deserved to be a sinner before all sin.

**8. 11.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that the soul deserved to be a sinner before all sin,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For it is a very evil merit to have deserved to become a sinner. And surely so evil a merit it could in no way have had before all sin, especially before it came into the flesh, when it could have neither an evil merit nor a good one. How, then, do you say: *And so the soul, if it deserved to be a sinner, which could not be a sinner, yet did not remain in sin, because, prefigured in Christ, it ought not to be in sin*[^9], *just as it could not be?* Attend to what you say, and now cease to say it. For how did it deserve, and how could it not be a sinner? How, I ask you, did it deserve to be a sinner, which did not live badly? How, I ask you, was it made a sinner, which could not be a sinner? Or if you say *it could not* for this reason, because apart from the flesh it could not: how, then, did it deserve to be a sinner, by which merit it would be sent into the flesh, since before the flesh it could not be a sinner, whence it might deserve something evil?

## The sixth error: that infants can be freed from original sin without Baptism.

**9. 12.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that infants, overtaken by death before they are baptized, can attain to the pardon of original sins,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For the examples that deceive you, whether about the thief who confessed the Lord on the cross[^10], or about Dinocrates, the brother of holy Perpetua, support you nothing toward the opinion of this error. For that thief, although he could by divine judgment be counted among those who are purified by the confession of martyrdom, yet you are also ignorant whether he was not baptized. For—to pass over what is believed, that, the water gushing out together with the blood from the Lord's side, he, fixed beside him, could have been sprinkled, and washed by a baptism of this most holy kind—what if he had been baptized in prison, which some too were afterward able to obtain secretly in time of persecution? What if even before he was seized? For the public laws could not on that account spare him, as far as concerns the death of the body, because he had divinely received the remission of sins. What if, already baptized, he had fallen into the crime and offense of robbery, and, not lacking Baptism, but as a penitent, received pardon of the crimes which, baptized, he committed?—since so faithful a piety appeared both to the Lord in his mind, and to us in his words.

For if we contend that those of whom it is not written whether they were baptized departed from this life without Baptism, we slander the Apostles themselves, of whom—except the apostle Paul[^11]—we do not know when they were baptized. But if it could become known to us that they were baptized through this, that the Lord said to blessed Peter, *He who has been washed has no need to wash*[^12], what of the others, of whom we read no such thing said—of Barnabas, of Timothy, of Titus, of Silas, of Philemon, of the very evangelists Mark and Luke, of the countless others, whom far be it that we should doubt to have been baptized, although we do not read it?

But Dinocrates, a boy of seven years—at which years boys, when they are baptized, already recite the creed and answer for themselves to the questions—why he did not seem to you possibly to have been baptized, and recalled by an impious father to the sacrileges of the gentiles, and on that account to have been in the punishments from which, his sister praying, he was freed, I do not know. For did you read that he either was never a Christian, or died a catechumen? Although that very reading is not in that canon of the Scriptures from which in questions of this kind testimonies are to be brought forward.

## The seventh error: that divine predestination can be frustrated.

**10. 13.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that those whom the Lord predestined to Baptism can be snatched from his predestination, and die before what the Almighty predestined has been fulfilled in them,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For I do not know what power here is given to chances against the power of God, by whose rushing in what he predestined is not allowed to come to pass.

How great a whirlpool of impiety this error swallows up the one erring, there is no need to amplify, since it suffices to have briefly admonished a prudent man, ready to be corrected. For these are your words: *We say,* you say, *that an account of this kind is to be held concerning infants who, predestined to Baptism, are overtaken by the death of the present life before they are reborn in Christ.* If, then, predestined to Baptism, they are overtaken by death before they come to it, either God predestined what he foreknew would not be, or he did not know this would not be—so that either his predestination was frustrated, or his foreknowledge deceived? You see how many things could be said here, did I not hold to what I said a little before, so as briefly to admonish you of this.

## The eighth error: that premature death snatches unbaptized infants from wickedness and from deceit.

**10. 14.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that of infants who, before they are reborn in Christ, are overtaken by death, it is written: He was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding, or lest deceit beguile his soul. For this cause he hastened to lead him out of the midst of iniquity; for his soul was pleasing to God; and: Being made perfect in a short while, he fulfilled long times*[^13], if you wish to be a Catholic.

For this does not pertain to them at all, but rather to those who, baptized and living piously, are not permitted to live here long, made perfect not in years but in the grace of wisdom. But this error, by which it is thought that this was said of little ones dying before they are baptized, does an intolerable injury to the most holy bath itself, if a little one who could be snatched away baptized is for this reason snatched away first, lest wickedness change his understanding, or lest deceit beguile his soul: as though in that same Baptism there were believed to be this wickedness and deceit by which he would be changed for the worse and beguiled, if he were not snatched away first.

Then, since his soul was pleasing to God, he hastened to lead him out of the midst of iniquity, in such a way as not to delay even a little, so that he might fulfill what he had predestined in him: but he preferred to act against his own predestination, as though hastening, lest what had pleased him in the unbaptized be destroyed in Baptism—as though an infant about to die should perish there to which one must run with him lest he perish. Who, then, would believe, say, write, recite that these words written in the book of *Wisdom* were said of little ones dead without Baptism, if he considered them, as he ought, thoughtfully?

## The ninth error: that some mansions of the Father's house are outside the kingdom of God.

**11. 15.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that some of the mansions which the Lord said are in his Father's house are outside the kingdom of God,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For he did not say, as you yourself set down this testimony, "There are many mansions with my Father"; for if he had said so, they would have to be understood nowhere else than in his Father's house: but he plainly says, *In my Father's house are many mansions*[^14]. Who, then, would dare to separate some parts of the house of God from the kingdom of God; so that, whereas the kings of the earth are found to reign not only in their own house, nor only in their own homeland, but far and wide even across the sea, the king who made heaven and earth should be said not to reign even in his whole house?

## The kingdom of God and the kingdom of heaven.

**11. 16.** But perhaps you say that all things indeed pertain to the kingdom of God, because he reigns in the heavens, reigns on the earth, in the depths, in paradise, in hell (for where does he not reign, whose supreme power is everywhere?);

but that the kingdom of heaven is one thing, to which it is not lawful to approach unless washed by the bath of regeneration, on account of the true and fixed saying of the Lord[^15]; but the kingdom of the lands, or of other parts of creation, another,

where there can be some mansions of the house of God, although pertaining to the kingdom of God, yet not to the kingdom of heaven, where the kingdom of God is more excellent and more blessed:

so that it comes about that neither are some parts and mansions of the house of God wickedly separated from the kingdom of God; and yet not all the mansions are prepared in the kingdom of heaven; and in those which are not in the kingdom of heaven, there can dwell happily those to whom God has willed to give them, even unbaptized; so that they are in the kingdom of God, although they cannot be in the kingdom of heaven, because they are not baptized.

## The kingdom of God is said to be where his faithful household, the whole of it, will reign blessedly and everlastingly with him.

**11. 17.** Those who say this seem indeed to themselves to say something, because they attend negligently to the Scriptures, and do not understand in what way the kingdom of God is spoken of, for which we pray, saying, *Your kingdom come*[^16]. The kingdom of God is said to be where his faithful household, the whole of it, will reign blessedly and everlastingly with him. For according to the power which is his over all things, even now he surely reigns. What, then, is it that we pray may come, except that we may deserve to reign with him? But under his power will be also those who will burn in the punishment of eternal fire: are we, on that account, going to say that they too will be in the kingdom of God? For it is one thing to be honored by the gifts of the kingdom of God, another to be restrained by the laws of the kingdom of God.

But that it may appear to you most plainly that the kingdom of heaven is not to be distributed to the baptized, and other parts of the kingdom of God given to the unbaptized, as it seemed to you, hear the Lord himself, who does not say, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit,* he cannot enter the kingdom of heaven; but, *he cannot,* he says, *enter the kingdom of God*[^17]. For his words on this matter to Nicodemus are these: *Amen, amen I say to you, unless one is born anew, he cannot see the kingdom of God*[^18]. Behold, here he did not say "the kingdom of heaven," but "of God." And when Nicodemus had answered and said, *How can a man be born, when he is old? Can he enter again into his mother's womb, and be born?*[^19] the Lord, repeating the same saying more plainly and openly, says: *Amen, amen I say to you, unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^20]. Behold, neither here did he say "the kingdom of heaven," but "the kingdom of God." For what he had said, *Unless one is born anew,* he explained what it was, saying, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit.* And what he had said, *he cannot see,* this he explained by saying, *he cannot enter.* Yet what he had said, "the kingdom of God," he did not repeat by another name.

Nor is there need now to ask and discuss whether the kingdom of God and the kingdom of heaven are to be understood with some difference, or whether it is one thing called by two names: it suffices that he cannot enter the kingdom of God who has not been washed by the bath of regeneration. But to separate some mansions established in the house of God from the kingdom of God—how far that strays from the truth, I think you now understand. And therefore what you thought, that in some of the mansions which the Lord said are many in his Father's house there would dwell certain ones not even reborn of water and the Spirit, I admonish you—that you may hold the Catholic faith—not to delay, if you permit, to amend.

## The tenth error: that the sacrifice of Christians is to be offered for those who have departed from the body without Baptism.

**12. 18.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that the sacrifice of Christians is to be offered for those who have departed from the body unbaptized,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For neither do you show that the sacrifice of the Jews which you mentioned *from the books of the Maccabees*[^21] was offered for those who had departed from the body uncircumcised. In which opinion of yours, so new, and brought forward against the authority and discipline of the whole Church, you used also a most insolent word, saying: *For these, certainly, I judge that constant offerings, and the sacrifices of the holy priests, should be offered continually*—so that you, a layman, neither subjected yourself to the priests of God by learning, nor at least joined yourself by inquiring together, but set yourself above them by ruling. Take these things from yourself, son: it is not thus that one walks on the way which Christ, in his humility, taught that he himself is; no one enters through his narrow gate with this swelling[^22].

## The eleventh error: that infants can be saved without Baptism.

**13. 19.** Do not believe, nor say, nor teach *that some of those who have departed from this life without the Baptism of Christ do not, meanwhile, go into the kingdom of heaven, but into paradise; but afterward, in the resurrection of the dead, attain even to the blessedness of the kingdom of heaven,* if you wish to be a Catholic. For not even the Pelagian heresy dared to give them this, which holds that little ones do not draw original sin: little ones whom you, although, like a Catholic, you confess that they are born with sin, nevertheless, by I know not what perversity of a more perverse novelty of opinion, assert to be absolved, without the bath of salvation, both from this sin with which they are born, and to be led into the kingdom of heaven; nor do you consider how, in this cause, you are worse wise than Pelagius. For he, fearing the Lord's saying, by which the unbaptized are not permitted to enter the kingdom of heaven, although he believes them free from all sin, does not dare to send little ones there: but you so despise what was said, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^23], that—besides the error by which you dare to separate paradise from the kingdom of God—to certain ones whom, like a Catholic, you believe to be born guilty, dead without Baptism, you do not hesitate, over and above, to promise both the absolution of that guilt and the kingdom of heaven:

as though against Pelagius, in establishing original sin, you could be a true Catholic then, if against the Lord, in destroying his saying about Baptism, you were a new heretic. We do not, most beloved, want you so to be a victor over heretics that error vanquishes error, and, what is worse, the greater the lesser.

For you say: *Or if perhaps anyone struggles against it—that paradise was conferred for the present, temporarily, on the soul of the thief or of Dinocrates (for there still remains for them, in the resurrection, the reward of the kingdom of heaven, although that principal saying stands in the way, that whoever has not been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit will not enter the kingdom)—let him nonetheless hold also my assent in this part, as one not envying, provided he both enlarge the effect of divine mercy and foreknowledge, and its affection.*

These are your words, where you confess that you agree with one who says that paradise was so conferred temporarily on certain unbaptized ones that there remains for them, in the resurrection, the reward of the kingdom of heaven, against the principal saying, by which it is established that he will not enter that kingdom who has not been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit. Which principal saying Pelagius, fearing to violate, did not believe that even those whom he did not believe guilty would enter the kingdom of heaven without Baptism: but you both confess that little ones are guilty of original sin, and yet absolve them without the bath of regeneration, and send them into paradise, and afterward also permit them to enter the kingdom of heaven.

## Correct all these errors without any delay, if you spoke truthfully at the outset.

**14. 20.** These things, and things of this kind—if perhaps, more attentive and at more leisure, you can find others too in your books—correct now without any delay, if you bear a Catholic mind; that is, if you spoke truthfully at the outset, saying *that you are not so credulous toward yourself as to think that what you said can be proved; and that you are always eager not to defend even your own opinion, if it is detected as improbable; and that it is at heart with you, your own judgment being condemned, rather to pursue better things and those that are truer.* Now prove, dearest one, that you did not say these things deceitfully, so that the Catholic Church may rejoice over your disposition, not only talented but also cautious, pious, modest, and not blaze up over a contentious heretical madness of obstinacy.

Now is the time for you to show with how great a sincerity of heart you said, after these good words of yours which I mentioned, what you immediately added: *For as it is the mark of an excellent purpose and praiseworthy counsel to be easily led across to truer things, so it is the mark of a wicked and obstinate judgment to be unwilling to be bent the sooner to the path of reason.*

Be, then, of an excellent purpose and praiseworthy counsel, and easily led across to truer things; and do not be of a wicked and obstinate judgment, so as to be unwilling to be bent the sooner to the path of reason. For if you spoke these things freely, if you did not sound them on your lips, but felt them within and genuinely in your heart, then in so great a good as your correction you hate even delays.

For it was too little for you to say that *it is the mark of a wicked and obstinate judgment to be unwilling to be bent to the path of reason,* unless you added *the sooner,* so as thereby to show how execrable is he who never does this good; since one who does it more slowly seems to you to be blamed with such severity that he is deservedly called of a wicked and obstinate judgment.

Hear, then, yourself, and use you most of all and above all the fruits of your own eloquence, so that you may the sooner bend yourself to the path of reason by seriousness of mind, than you had turned aside from it, less learnedly and less prudently, by the slipperiness of your age.

## Would that I could read your writings with you, and by conversing rather than by writing show what is to be amended.

**14. 21.** It is too long to handle and discuss everything in your books, or rather in yourself, that I want amended, and at least to give you a brief account of each thing to be corrected. Nor yet, for that reason, should you despise yourself, and judge that your talent and eloquence are to be valued little. Nor have I found the memory of the holy Scriptures in you to be small; but your erudition is less than befitted so great a disposition and labor. And so I do not want you either to grow vain by attributing to yourself more than you ought, or, again, to grow cold by casting yourself down and despairing. Would that I could read your writings with you, and by conversing rather than by writing show what is to be amended. This business would be more easily accomplished by our conversation between us than by letters: which, if it had to be written, would need many volumes. But these capital matters, which I wished even to comprehend in a fixed number, I urgently admonish you not to delay to correct, and to make them altogether foreign to your faith and your preaching: so that what faculty of disputing you have, by the gift of God, you may use usefully for the building up, not for the destruction, of sound and wholesome doctrine.

## I will run briefly through the eleven errors by recapitulating them.

**15. 22.** These, now, are the things of which, as I could, I have already discoursed; but I will run briefly through them by recapitulating them. One is, that you said God made the soul not from nothing, but from his very self; the second, that through infinite time, and so always, God gives souls, just as he himself who gives always is; the third, that the soul lost some merit through the flesh, which it had before the flesh; the fourth, that the soul recovers through the flesh its former condition, and is reborn through that same flesh through which it had deserved to be defiled; the fifth, that the soul deserved to be a sinner before all sin; the sixth, that infants overtaken by death before they are baptized can attain to the pardon of original sins; the seventh, that those whom the Lord predestined to Baptism can be snatched from his predestination, and die before what the Almighty predestined has been fulfilled in them; the eighth, that of infants who, before they are reborn in Christ, are overtaken by death, it is written: *He was caught up lest wickedness change his understanding,* and the rest that are read to this purpose in the book of *Wisdom*[^24]; the ninth, that some mansions, of those which the Lord said are in his Father's house, are outside the kingdom of God; the tenth is, that the sacrifice of Christians is to be offered for those who have departed from the body unbaptized; the eleventh, that some of those who have departed from this life without the Baptism of Christ do not, meanwhile, go into the kingdom, but into paradise; but afterward, in the resurrection of the dead, attain even to the blessedness of the kingdom of heaven.

## As many heresies as opinions.

**15. 23.** These eleven, meanwhile, much and openly perverse, and adverse to the Catholic faith, now at once do not hesitate to root out and cast away from your mind, from your word, from your pen, if you want us to rejoice that you have not only crossed over to the Catholic altars, but are truly a Catholic. For these, if obstinately defended one by one, can make as many heresies as there are opinions reckoned to be. Wherefore consider how dreadful it is that all should be in one man, which would be damnable each in each one. But if you fight for them with no contention—nay rather, if you storm them with faithful words and writings—you will be a more praiseworthy censor upon yourself than if you reproved anyone else with right reason; and a more admirable amender of them than if you had never thought them.

May the Lord be present to your mind, and pour into your spirit, by his Spirit, so great an ease of humility, light of truth, sweetness of love, peace of piety, that you would rather be a victor over your own mind in true things than over anyone who contradicts you in false ones.

But far be it that you should judge that, by holding these opinions, you have withdrawn from the Catholic faith, although they are adverse to the Catholic faith—if, before God, whose eye is deceived in no one's heart, you regard that you truly said *that you are not credulous toward yourself, that what you said can be proved; and that you are eager always not to defend even your own opinion, if it is detected as improbable, because it is at heart with you, your own judgment being condemned, rather to pursue better things and those that are truer.*

For this mind, even in things said, through ignorance, that are not Catholic, is itself, by the very forethought and preparation of correction, Catholic. But let this be the end of this volume, where the reader may rest a little, so that for what follows his attention may be renewed by another beginning.
