# Book 4

**1. 1.** Now, beginning with your second volume, let us examine the remaining arguments by which you strive to refute my book. As we have undertaken to do, we omit what creates no knotty questions requiring our care to unravel, lest by lingering over superfluities we discourage the reader from this laborious task through the work's very length. In the preceding book, therefore, we said enough to make it apparent to those who judge rightly that, although the true and good God is the creator of human beings, and marriage is good, instituted by him through the creation and union of both sexes and blessed with the gift of fertility, nevertheless the concupiscence of the flesh that desires against the spirit is evil [^1]. Marital chastity uses this evil well, while a holier continence does better by not using it. This evil has not been mingled with us from another substance that God did not create, as Mani madly imagines; rather, it arose and was transmitted through one man's disobedience and must be expiated and healed through one man's obedience. Its obligation binds the newborn through deserved punishment and is loosed in the reborn through undeserved grace. By praising this evil against me you appear my adversary; but by fighting it within yourself you are my witness. If, however, you do not fight it, you are your own enemy. Therefore, because I think I have answered your first book sufficiently, although the whole case has been settled there, nevertheless, lest we seem unable to answer the other three, observe how vainly you have spoken in them as well.

**1. 2.** After quoting my words from my book, you exult because I said that, “on the Apostle's testimony, *marital chastity is a gift of God*” [^2], as though the Apostle had thereby praised the evil you praise, by which *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^3], an evil marital chastity uses well, as I have already shown in the preceding book. It is therefore no small gift of God that this evil is so bridled that it is given no rein for anything unlawful but serves only to generate children who are to be regenerated. For its impulse is not moderate of its own accord: it abstains from nothing unlawful if the members serve its desire. Hence its movement is praised not in the thing whose movement is restless, but in the one who restrains it and uses it well.

**1. 3.** Thus, when married believers make good use of this evil, from whose guilt they have been freed by the Savior's gift, certainly all who are born from it by the gift of that same creator are not, as you accuse us, “prepared for the devil's kingdom,” but rather to be rescued from it and transferred into the kingdom of the Only Begotten. For this is and ought to be the intention of devout spouses: that generation be prepared for regeneration. But if this evil which parents feel within themselves, against which, to use your words, “the legion of the Apostles fought” [^4], did not pertain to the children, they would surely be born without it. Since, however, they are born with it, why do you wonder that they are reborn on its account, to be freed from its obligation and either taken from this life delivered from it, or freely to fight against it in this life and be rewarded as victors at the end?

**1. 4.** Which of us, however, ever supposed that “the use of marriage was invented by the devil”? Who believed that “the mingling of bodies arose through the evil of transgression,” when without these things marriage could not exist at all? But there would be no evil for them to use well if no one had sinned. Charge me with what I said, so that I may clear myself; for if you also charge me with what I did not say, when shall we finish?

**1. 5.** You think “it follows that God's gift does harm if a human being is born with an evil, for no one is born except by God's gift.” Listen, then, and understand: God's gift, by which each person exists and lives, harms no one; but the evil of concupiscence can exist only in what exists and lives. Therefore an evil can exist in God's gift, to be healed by another gift of God. Thus in a human being who exists and lives by God's gift, there can be an evil contracted through generation and to be healed through regeneration. For certainly no infant would be born with a demon if it were not born; yet birth is not therefore itself the cause of this evil. It is born, then, by God's gift, but with a demon by God's judgment—certainly hidden, but surely not unjust?

**2. 6.** As for my saying, “Even the good of marriage, if one does not have it, must be asked of the Lord” [^5]—by whom, of course, except those who need it?—I seemed to you to say that “one must pray for strength for intercourse.” But I said that God must be asked for marital chastity itself, in which there is not unrestricted use of intercourse but a lawful measure. For if a man cannot have intercourse, he should rather not seek a wife, since the apostolic judgment, where it says, *if he does not contain himself, let him marry*[^6], intended marriage as a remedy, as you yourself confessed, against the disease of concupiscence—which you refuse to confess, though you confess the remedy [^7]. This remedy, however, is not to bring concupiscence into being where it does not exist, but to prevent it, whose movement is unbridled, from dragging us into unlawful things. For what we say in the Lord's Prayer also pertains to this kind of petition: *do not bring us into temptation*[^8], since *each person is tempted by his own concupiscence*[^9], as the Apostle James says. What we say also accords with this: *deliver us from evil*[^10]. For spouses say this regarding this matter, so that, meanwhile free from evil in mind, they may use well the evil they have in the flesh—for they know that good does not dwell in their flesh [^11]—so that afterward, with all corruptibility healed, no evil may remain in them to use. Why do you triumph as over a vanquished enemy? Conquer instead the internal enemy you praise. For my victory over you is secure while that evil fights against you. You will not dare to say that one who speaks truth is conquered by one who speaks falsehood. I say that the concupiscence against which you fight is evil; you say it is good. But your struggle confesses the evil which your tongue falsely calls good, adding another lie when you assert that I too called it good. For I would not call good the concupiscence of the flesh which the Apostle John said *is not from the Father*[^12]. Rather, I called marital chastity good: it resists the evil of concupiscence, lest, when stirred, it drag us into unlawful things.

**2. 7.** But knowing how vainly you argued, you “strike,” as you say, “the other side of my definition,” and say: “If generative heat, the servant of marital honor, is held back from immoderate advances both by believers' effort and by the power of the gift, and is not extinguished by grace but bridled, it is commendable in its kind and in its measure, and accused only for its excesses.” In saying this, you fail to notice that the union of spouses for the sake of generation is a praiseworthy good precisely because through it a lawful limit is fixed for the evil of concupiscence. Why should what you confess must be bridled not rather be called evil? Why, I ask, must it be bridled, except to prevent harm, except to prevent its being released toward the unlawful things it desires? Surely the desire for evil is evil, even if one does not consent to it, until we reach the state in which we do not even have it. We must therefore consider not what good is made from the concupiscence of the flesh, but what evil it itself does. For marital chastity, finding concupiscence eager to obtain pleasure from either unlawful or lawful things, restrains it from the unlawful and permits it the lawful. This is good, yet not its good, but the good of the one who uses it well. What concupiscence itself does, however—burning indiscriminately for lawful or unlawful things—is surely evil. Marital chastity therefore uses this evil well; virginal continence does better by not using it.

**2. 8.** “If generative heat could be naturally evil,” you say, “it should be uprooted, not regulated.” See how you refused to say what you said above, “bridled,” and preferred “regulated.” For you perceived that no one would bridle it unless resisting it, and thus, by changing the word, in your very fear you confessed that what resists the good is evil. You also call it generative heat because you are ashamed to call it lust, or, as divine speech customarily calls it, *the concupiscence of the flesh.* Speak, then, in this way, and say: if the concupiscence of the flesh could be naturally evil, it should be uprooted, not regulated. Thus even slower people who know Latin can understand what you say. But you speak as though all those who marry because they cannot bear the labor of continence by which this evil is resisted, and therefore prefer to use it well rather than do better by not using it at all, would not prefer to uproot it if they could. But if in the body of this death this evil is necessary to the married because without it the good of generation cannot occur, let the continent uproot the concupiscence of the flesh. You yourself, you who speak without attending to what you say, uproot lust from your members. For it is not necessary to you too, nor are its desires good: if you consent or yield to them, you will perish.

**2. 9.** Moreover, if there is an evil in you which you resist, attack, and defeat when you conquer it, you do better by not using this evil which those in whom you contend it is good use well. In this you certainly lie or are mistaken. For you will not say that lust is good in spouses but evil in holy virgins or the continent. We already possess your judgment where you said: “One who keeps the measure of natural concupiscence uses a good well; one who does not keep the measure uses a good badly; but one who, out of love for holy virginity, despises even the measure itself does better by not using a good, for,” you say, “confident in his health and strength, he has despised remedies so that he might engage in glorious contests” [^13]. By these words you declare unambiguously that the concupiscence of the flesh is the same thing in both, that is, in married and continent people. For what the married use well and the continent do better not to use, you call good and I evil. But in holy virgins and the continent, the concupiscence of the flesh shows itself evil, since you confess that against it they “engage in glorious contests.” Beyond doubt, then, they do better by not using an evil, not a good. Therefore spouses who use it also use not a good but an evil well. Thus the whole controversy, if any remains, is reduced to whether this concupiscence of the flesh about which we dispute is good or evil in those who have vowed continence to God. Whichever of these two it is found to be in them will also appear in spouses, since these use well what those do better not to use. Gather, then, all the powers of your acute mind and unabashed brow, and dare to answer, if you can, that what, as you confessed in the preceding book, “the legion of the Apostles resisted” is good. You were reproaching me, of course, for allegedly saying “that lust's powers are so great that not even the legion of the Apostles resisted it” [^14], although it favors my case instead that a legion not of just any saints but of the Apostles themselves resisted the evil you call good. Who would believe this evil could have such power that it found itself a praiser even among its assailants? Certainly not any of the ancient Apostles or saints, far from it; but it is remarkable that it found even one of the new heretics who professes in inexplicable ways to be both things at once, an assailant and defender of lust. Remaining in the Pelagian heresy, he tries to show that he sincerely praises what kills his very mind unless defeated, and sincerely defeats what destroys his doctrine unless praised.

**2. 10.** I ask, if you have any human sense, can sin be evil and desiring sin be good? For what does this concupiscence produce in the flesh of the holy continent except desires to sin, by not consenting to which they “engage,” as you confess, “in glorious contests”? For in that profession of continence even the desire for marriage cannot be anything but evil. What, then, does it do there, where whatever it does is evil, and where, if consent is given, it also completes the deed? What does this concupiscence do where nothing good is desired from it? What does lust do where nothing good will please through it? Its presence among the married may be called opportune; for if they attain the highest summit of marital chastity, they do something good through it, although they do nothing for its sake. But what does it do in holy virgins and the continent, I beg you? What does this thing you protect when foolish, and oppose when wise, do where it itself does no good and no good is made from it? What does it do in those in whom whatever is desired according to it is evil? What does it do in those whom it forces to watch and fight against it, and, if it sometimes steals any consent from them even in sleep, to groan on waking and amid their groans say: *how my soul is filled with illusions!*[^15] For when dreams deceive sleeping senses [^16], somehow even chaste souls fall into shameful consents; if the Most High imputed these, who would live chaste?

**2. 11.** This evil, then, which you will not call good unless you have become so deaf to every trumpet of truth that you cry that desiring evil is good—something you would not dare say even among the deaf—why, I say, is this evil not uprooted from the flesh of the holy continent? Why is it not “entirely removed by the work of the mind”? For you say that “this ought to happen if it were evil.” And because it does not happen in the married, where a measure of it is necessary, you therefore think it good, although you see it does not happen even where no measure of it is necessary. As far as it exists it harms, even if not by causing loss of the saints' inheritance when no consent is given, yet by diminishing the spiritual delight of holy minds: that delight of which the Apostle says, *for I delight in God's law according to the inner man*[^17]. This delight certainly diminishes when the warrior's mind is occupied, if not in fulfilling, at least in fighting the concupiscence of carnal pleasure, and engages in glorious contests in such a way that these very contests draw it away from delight in intelligible beauty. But because in this human misery pride must be guarded against as a worse enemy, this concupiscence is doubtless not completely extinguished in the flesh of the holy continent, so that, while fighting against it, the mind may be reminded of its dangers lest it grow puffed up in security, until human frailty reaches such perfect health that no rottenness of wantonness or swelling of pride need be feared. Thus *strength is perfected in weakness*[^18], because fighting itself belongs to weakness. For the more easily someone conquers, the less he fights. But who would fight within himself if nothing of himself resisted him? And what in us resists us except what is still being treated in us to be healed? Within ourselves, then, weakness alone is the cause of fighting, and weakness in turn admonishes us not to be proud. Thus the strength by which we are not proud here, where pride is possible, is perfected in weakness.

**2. 12.** Therefore spouses use well what the continent do better not to use. Thus the evil spouses use well is present in spouses for them to use well, and present also in the continent for them to do better by not using it; for it is present precisely so that they may not exalt themselves. It is therefore only the one who does not restrain lust who “is accused solely for its excesses”; lust itself, however, is in itself most rightly accused for its very movements, which are resisted lest it exceed its bounds. It is not, then, as you say, that “the moderation of something harmful by its very kind does nothing to advance innocence.” Not consenting to evil does advance innocence, and what is not consented to is not therefore other than evil. Rather, it is undoubtedly evil precisely because not consenting to it is good. For what evil would someone do who consented to good concupiscence, when even someone who by the marital act, not without its evil, sows a human being, God's good work, does no evil? Nor should you call lust “the maker of seed.” For he creates human seed who creates a human being from seed; but the source matters. For the contagions of this evil are hidden and dreadful, and God does not create either seed or human beings from people free of this evil, even if some have been freed from its guilt through regeneration, as those born from them must be freed.

**2. 13.** I certainly said, and do not regret saying, of marital chastity, in the words you quoted from me, that “when these things are shown to be gifts of God, we learn from whom they must be asked if we do not have them, and to whom thanks must be given if we do.” For thanks are given “not for the origin,” as you say, “of concupiscence,” whose origin is humanity's first evil, “but for its government,” which you say truly. For you say both, “either its government or its origin.” Thanks are given for the government of concupiscence because it is conquered when it resists. But what resists a good will is not good; indeed, who could deny that it is evil except someone who does not possess the good of the will against which he would confess that what resists it is evil?

**3. 14.** You insert other passages from my book where, after saying that marital chastity is God's gift and teaching this on the Apostle's testimony, I refused to pass silently over the question that then arose: what should be said when even some impious people seem to live chastely with their spouses [^19]? For “you customarily deny that the virtues by which one lives rightly are God's gifts, attributing them to human nature and will rather than God's grace, and use the argument that unbelievers sometimes possess them.” Thus you try to nullify our statement that no one lives rightly except by faith through Jesus Christ our Lord, the one mediator of God and human beings, and in this you most openly profess yourselves his adversaries. Indeed, let us not go far: if perhaps I am mistaken, answer this. I said that “one is not truly called chaste who does not keep marital fidelity to his wife for the sake of the true God.” A little later, to show this, I brought forward what seemed to me a great proof: “For since chastity is a virtue,” I said, “whose contrary vice is unchastity, and all virtues, even those that act through the body, dwell in the mind, how is a body asserted to be chaste with true reason when the mind itself commits fornication away from the true God?” [^20] Then, lest any of you deny that the mind of unbelievers commits fornication, I brought forward the testimony of holy Scripture, where it is read: *for behold, those who make themselves far from you will perish; you have destroyed everyone who commits fornication away from you*[^21]. But you, who “pursue,” as you say, “the arguments of mine that seem acute to me,” passed over all this as though it had seemed dull to me. See, then, which of these things you think should be denied. You most readily confess that marital chastity is a virtue; you do not deny that all virtues, even those acting through the body, dwell in the mind. Moreover, only one who openly professes himself an adversary of the holy Scriptures can deny that the unbeliever's mind commits fornication away from God. From all these things this conclusion follows: either true chastity can exist in a mind committing fornication, which you see is absurd, or true chastity cannot exist in an unbeliever's mind, although you pretended to be deaf when I asserted this. It is not therefore, as you slanderously say, “out of zeal for disparaging substance that I praise gifts.” For human substance could not receive divine gifts unless it were good; even its vices bear witness to its natural goodness. For what rightly displeases in a vice except that it removes or diminishes what pleases in nature?

**3. 15.** When a human being is helped by God, then, he is not helped only “to attain perfection,” as you wrote, plainly wishing it understood that he begins by himself without grace what grace perfects. Rather, it is as the Apostle says: may *he who began a good work in you perfect it to the end*[^22]. For insofar as you wish “a human being,” as you put it, “to be spurred toward something praiseworthy by the impulses of a noble heart,” you wish him to glory not in the Lord but in free choice, and thus to give first so that repayment may be made to him. In this way grace would no longer be grace [^23], because it would not be gratuitous. You say that “human nature is good, deserving the assistance of such grace.” I would hear this gladly if you said it because it is a rational nature; for God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord is not bestowed on stones, wood, or cattle. Rather, because it is God's image it deserves this grace, yet not so that its good will can precede without grace, lest it first give even that, to receive repayment, and thus grace no longer be grace, since it is not given freely but paid as a debt. Why, then, did you believe I had, in your fashion, “called heavenly gifts the accomplishment of human will,” as though human will moved toward the good without God's grace, so that God might repay it with accomplishment as something owed? Had you thus forgotten that with Scripture we say against you: *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^24], or that *God works in us even the willing?* O ungrateful to God's grace! O enemies of Christ's grace, Christians in name alone! Does not the Church pray for her enemies? What does she ask, I beg you? If she asks that the payment due their will be rendered them, what does she ask for them but great punishment? That would be against them, not for them; but she prays for them, not therefore because they possess a good will, but that their evil will may be turned into a good one. For *the will is prepared by the Lord*, and *it is God*, as the Apostle says, *who works in you even the willing* [^25].

**3. 16.** But as most bitter enemies of this grace, you oppose to us examples of impious people who, you say, “though strangers to faith, abound in virtues, in whom there is only the good of nature without grace's assistance, although enslaved to superstitions, and who by the powers of innate freedom alone are often found merciful, modest, chaste, and sober.” In saying this, see, you have now taken away even what you attributed to God's grace, namely the accomplishment of the will. For you did not say that they wish to be merciful, modest, chaste, and sober, but are not so because they have not yet attained through grace the accomplishment of this good will. If they both wished to be so and are so, we already find in them both the will and its accomplishment. What remains to reserve for grace in such evident virtues, with which you said they abound? If you take such delight in praising the impious that, not hearing Scripture say, *one who says the impious man is righteous will be cursed among the people and hated among the nations*[^26], you even proclaim them to abound in true virtues, how much better, I say, to confess that these very things in them are God's gifts! Under his hidden yet not unjust judgment, some are born foolish, others exceedingly slow of understanding and, in a manner, leaden in comprehension; some forgetful, others acute or retentive; others endowed with both gifts, understanding acutely and storing what they know in the treasury of a most tenacious memory. Some are naturally mild, others flare up in anger most readily at the slightest causes, and others fall between the two in their desire for revenge. Some are eunuchs, others so cold toward intercourse that they scarcely ever stir, others so lustful that they can scarcely be restrained, and others between the two, easily aroused and easily restrained. Some are exceedingly timid, others exceedingly bold, others neither; some cheerful, others sad, others inclined to neither. None of what I have mentioned comes from training and purpose, but from nature, which is why physicians venture to attribute these things to bodily temperaments. Even if this could be proved with no question arising or with every question settled, did each person make his own body? Should it be attributed to his will that he suffers these natural evils to a greater or lesser degree? For while living here no one can in any way or by any means avoid suffering them altogether. Yet whether pressed by the greatest or the smallest, he may not lawfully say to him who fashioned him, though almighty, just, and good: *why did you make me thus?*[^27] And no one delivers from the heavy yoke upon the children of the first Adam [^28] except the second Adam. How much more tolerable, then, to attribute those virtues you say exist in the impious to a divine gift rather than to their will alone, although they themselves do not know this until, if they belong to that number of the predestined, they receive the spirit that is from God, so as to know what God has given them [^29]!

**3. 17.** But far be it that true virtue should exist in anyone unless he is righteous. And far be it that he should be truly righteous unless he lives by faith, for *the righteous man* *lives by faith*[^30]. Now, who among those wishing to be considered Christians, except the Pelagians alone, or perhaps even you alone among them, would call an unbeliever righteous, an impious person righteous, one enslaved to the devil righteous? Let him be Fabricius, Fabius, Scipio, or Regulus, whose names you thought would frighten me, as though we were speaking in the ancient Roman senate. Yet even if in this cause you appeal to the school of Pythagoras or Plato, where most learned and scholarly men, renowned for a philosophy far more excellent than the rest, said that no virtues are true except those somehow impressed upon the mind by the form of that eternal and immutable substance which is God, even there, as much as he who called us grants, I shall cry with piety's freedom against you: neither in these men is true righteousness. *The righteous man lives by faith. Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ; Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes*[^31]. How are they truly righteous who despise the humility of the truly righteous one? For from the point they approached by understanding they withdrew through pride: *because, knowing God, they did not glorify him as God or give thanks, but became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened. For saying they were wise, they became fools*[^32]. How is true righteousness in those in whom true wisdom is absent? If we attribute it to them, there will be no reason not to say that they reach that kingdom of which it is written: *the desire for wisdom leads to a kingdom*[^33]. Hence Christ died in vain if, without faith in Christ, human beings attain true faith, true virtue, true righteousness, and true wisdom by any other thing or any reasoning. Indeed, just as the Apostle says most truly about the law, *if righteousness comes through the law, then Christ died in vain*[^34], so it is most truly said: if righteousness comes through nature and will, then Christ died in vain. If any righteousness comes through human teachings, then Christ died in vain. For that through which true righteousness comes is also that through which God's kingdom comes. For God himself would be unjust—which is unthinkable—if a truly righteous person were not admitted to his kingdom, since his kingdom itself is righteousness, as it is written: *God's kingdom is not food and drink, but righteousness, peace, and joy* [^35]. Moreover, if the impious do not have true righteousness, then neither do they truly have the other virtues, its companions and associates, if they have any [because when God's gifts are not referred to their author, those who use them badly thereby become unrighteous]. Hence neither continence nor chastity is a true virtue of the impious.

**3. 18.** But you understand so badly the Apostle's saying, *for one who competes in the contest is continent in all things* [^36], that you contend that even flute players and other such shameful and infamous persons possess continence, so *great a virtue, of which it is written that no one can be continent unless God grants it* [^37]. For when they compete in a contest they abstain from all things to receive a corruptible crown, yet do not abstain from their vain desire for it. This vain, and therefore perverse, desire conquers and bridles other perverse desires in them, and for this reason they are called continent. But, to inflict the gravest insult on the Scipios, you have given also to actors the continence you praised in them with such eloquence, not knowing that the Apostle, in exhorting people to virtue, drew his example from a vicious human affection, just as elsewhere Scripture, exhorting people to love wisdom, said it should be sought like money [^38]. Should holy Scripture therefore be said to have praised greed? Rather, because it is known how patiently lovers of money submit to great labors and pains, from how many pleasures they restrain themselves either from desire to increase their money or fear of diminishing it, with what shrewdness they pursue profit and prudently avoid losses, how often they fear seizing others' goods and sometimes disregard their own goods taken from them lest by demanding them back and litigating they lose more, we have rightly been admonished to love wisdom so as to desire most eagerly to treasure it up for ourselves. And so that we may acquire it more and more and not diminish it in any part, we should endure troubles, bridle lusts, look ahead to the future, and maintain innocence and beneficence. When we do this we possess true virtues because that for which we do it is true, that is, it accords with our nature for salvation and true happiness.

**3. 19.** For those who said, “Virtue is a disposition of the mind in accord with the measure of nature and with reason,” did not define virtue absurdly [^39]. They spoke truth, but did not know what accords with the liberation and beatification of mortal nature. For we would not all naturally wish to be immortal and blessed if we could not be so. But this highest good cannot be bestowed on human beings except through Christ, and him crucified, by whose death death is conquered and by whose wounds our nature is healed. Therefore *the righteous man* *lives by faith* in Christ [^40]. For by this faith he lives prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly, and thus through all these true virtues he lives rightly and wisely because he lives faithfully. Therefore, if virtues do nothing to help a human being attain the true happiness which true faith in Christ promises us as immortal, they cannot possibly be true virtues. Or do you wish us to call the virtues of the greedy true, when they prudently devise ways of profit, bravely endure many cruel and harsh things to acquire money, temperately and soberly chastise the various desires that make life expensive, and refrain from others' goods and often disregard losses of their own—which seems to belong to justice—lest they consume more in quarrels and lawsuits? For when something is done prudently, bravely, temperately, and justly, it is done by all four virtues, which according to your argument are true virtues if, to know whether they are true, we need only examine what is done and not ask why it is done. Lest I seem to slander you, I shall now set down your very words. “The origin of all virtues,” you say, “lies in the rational mind, and all the affections through which we are good fruitfully or fruitlessly have their subject in our mind: prudence, justice, temperance, and fortitude. Although the force of these affections is naturally in everyone,” you say, “it does not hasten toward one end in everyone; rather, according to the judgment of the will, whose bidding they serve, they are directed either toward eternal or temporal things. When this happens they differ neither in what they are nor in what they do, but only in what they merit. Therefore,” you say, “they can suffer loss neither of their name nor of their kind, but are either enriched by the greatness or frustrated by the smallness solely of the reward they sought.” Where you learned these things I do not know, yet now you see, I think, that it follows that the prudence of the greedy, by which they devise kinds of petty profits, is true virtue; so is their justice, by which, fearing grave losses, they sometimes more readily disregard their own goods than usurp anything of another's; so is their temperance, by which they restrain the appetite for luxury because it is costly and are content with only necessary food and clothing; and so is their fortitude, by which, as Horace says, *they flee poverty through sea, through rocks, through fires* [^41], and by which, indeed, we have known some who, during a barbarian invasion, could not be compelled by any enemy tortures to reveal what they possessed. Thus these virtues, shameful and deformed by such an end, and therefore in no way genuine and true virtues, nevertheless seem so true and beautiful to you “that they can suffer loss neither of their name nor of their kind, but are frustrated only by the smallness of the reward they sought,” that is, the yield of earthly advantages rather than heavenly rewards. Nor will it be anything but true justice in Catiline to embrace many in friendship, protect them by his service, and share what he possessed with all; and it will be his true fortitude that he could endure cold, hunger, and thirst, and true patience that he could endure starvation, cold, and sleeplessness beyond anyone's belief [^42]. Who could think this wisely without being foolish?

**3. 20.** But evidently, learned man that you are, you are deceived by the semblance of truth in vices that seem neighboring and close to virtues, although as far removed from them as vices are from virtues. For just as constancy is a virtue opposed to inconstancy, obstinacy is nevertheless a vice that seems its neighbor and appears to imitate constancy. May you be free of this vice when you recognize that what I say is true, lest, as though loving constancy, you think you must obstinately remain in error. Thus all virtues have not only vices contrary to them by a manifest distinction, as rashness is to prudence, but also vices in a manner neighboring and similar, not in truth but through a deceptive appearance: thus, for prudence itself, not rashness or imprudence but cunning. This is nevertheless a vice, although in holy Scripture it is used in a good sense in the saying, *cunning as serpents*[^43], and in a bad sense when in paradise *the serpent was the most prudent of all beasts*[^44]. Nor can names easily be found for all these vices we have called neighbors of virtues; yet even if what to call them cannot be found, they must be guarded against.

**3. 21.** Know, therefore, that virtues must be distinguished from vices by their ends, not their duties. A duty is what must be done; an end is that for which it must be done. Thus, when a person does something in which he does not seem to sin, if he does not do it for the purpose for which he ought, he is proved to sin. Not attending to this, you separated ends from duties and said that duties without their ends should be called true virtues. From this follows so great an absurdity that you are forced to call true justice even that whose mistress you discover to be greed. For refraining one's hands from another's goods, if you consider the duty, may appear an act of justice; but when it is asked why this is done and the answer is, “So that more money may not perish in litigation,” how can this deed belong to true justice when it serves greed? Such were the virtues Epicurus introduced as pleasure's handmaids, doing absolutely everything they did for the sake of acquiring or retaining it. But far be it that true virtues serve anyone except him, or for his sake, to whom we say: *God of hosts, turn us back*[^45]. Thus virtues that serve carnal delights or any temporal advantages and profits cannot possibly be true. Nor are those true which wish to serve nothing. For true virtues serve God in human beings, to whom he gives them; they serve God in angels, to whom he gives them also. Whatever good a human being does without doing it for the purpose for which true wisdom commands it, although it seems good as a duty, is sin because its end is not right.

**3. 22.** Some good things, therefore, can be done by people who do not do them well. For it is good to help a person in danger, especially an innocent person. But if one does this from loving human glory more than God's, he does not do the good well, because he does not do as a good person what he does without a good will. For far be it that a will which glories in others or itself, not in the Lord, should be or be called good. Hence neither should this be called its fruit, for a bad tree does not bear good fruits; rather, it is the good work of him who does good even through the bad. Therefore one cannot say how greatly this opinion deceives you, in which you said that “all virtues are affections through which we are good fruitfully or fruitlessly.” For we cannot be good fruitlessly; rather, whatever we are fruitlessly, we are not good. For *a good tree* *bears good fruits*[^46]. Far be it that the good God, who prepares an axe for trees not bearing good fruit, should cut down good trees and throw them into the fire [^47]. Thus people are in no way good fruitlessly; but those who are not good can be, some less, others more evil.

**3. 23.** Hence I do not see at all how those you wished to mention help you, of whom the Apostle says: *gentiles who do not have the law are a law to themselves, who have the work of the law written in their hearts*[^48]. For through them you tried to prove that even those strangers to faith in Christ can have true righteousness, because, on the Apostle's testimony, they *naturally do the things of the law*[^49]. Here you expressed more evidently your doctrine, by which you are enemies of God's grace given through Jesus Christ our Lord, *who takes away the sin of the world*[^50], introducing a kind of human being who can please God without faith in Christ through the law of nature. This is why the Christian Church chiefly detests you. But what do you wish these people to be? Do they have true virtues and are they good fruitlessly because not for God's sake? Or do they also please God by these things and receive eternal life as his reward? If you call them fruitless, what benefit is it to them that, according to the Apostle, their thoughts will defend them *on the day when God judges the hidden things of human beings* [^51]? But if those defended by their thoughts because they naturally performed the works of the law are not good fruitlessly and therefore find an eternal reward with God, they are undoubtedly *righteous* because they *live by faith*[^52].

**3. 24.** For the testimony I quoted from the Apostle, *everything that is not from faith is sin*[^53], you took as you pleased and explained not according to its meaning but according to yours. The Apostle was speaking about foods. Yet after saying, *one who distinguishes, if he eats, is condemned, because it is not from faith* [^54], he wished to prove the particular kind of sin he was discussing by a general statement, immediately adding: *for everything that is not from faith is sin.* But even if I grant you that this should be understood only of foods, what will you say of the other testimony I also quoted, which you did not discuss at all because you could not find how to twist it to your opinion, written to the Hebrews: *for without faith it is impossible to please*[^55]? Surely, when this was said, the whole of human life was in question, in which the righteous man lives by faith. Yet although without faith it is impossible to please God, virtues without faith so please you that you proclaim them true and people good through them; and then, as though regretting your praise of them, you do not hesitate to pronounce them fruitless.

**3. 25.** Therefore these people righteous by natural law either please God and please him by faith, because without faith it is impossible to please him—and by what faith do they please him except faith in Christ? For, as we read in *the Acts of the Apostles*, *in him God established faith for all, raising him from the dead* [^56]. Hence they are said naturally to do the things of the law without the law, because they came to the Gospel from the gentiles, not from the circumcision to which the law was given, and naturally because nature itself was corrected in them through God's grace so that they might believe. Nor can you prove through them what you wish, that unbelievers too can have true virtues, for these are believers. Or, if they do not have faith in Christ, they are certainly neither righteous nor pleasing to God, whom it is impossible to please without faith. But their thoughts will defend them on the day of judgment to this extent, that they will be punished more tolerably because they naturally did the things of the law in some measure, having the work of the law written in their hearts so far as not to do to others what they would not wish to suffer. Yet they sinned in this, that, being without faith, they did not refer these works to the end to which they ought. For Fabricius will be punished less than Catiline, not because the former is good but because the latter is more evil; Fabricius is less impious than Catiline not by possessing true virtues but by not straying as far from true virtues.

**3. 26.** Or will you perhaps provide some place between condemnation and the kingdom of heaven for these too, who displayed a Babylonian love for their earthly homeland and served demons or human glory with civic virtue, not true but resembling truth—namely the Fabriciuses, Reguluses, Fabiuses, Scipios, Camilluses, and others like them—as you do for infants who die without baptism? A place where those who did not please God, whom it is impossible to please without faith, which they possessed neither in works nor in hearts, will be not in misery but in everlasting blessedness? I do not think your ruin can leap as far as such impudence. “Will they therefore be in everlasting condemnation,” you say, “in whom there was true righteousness?” O utterance headlong with greater impudence! There was, I say, no true righteousness in them, because duties are weighed not by their acts but by their ends.

**3. 27.** But with wit and charm, most elegant and urbane man, you say: “If it is said that the chastity of unbelievers is not chastity, with the same impudence it will be said that pagans' bodies are not bodies, pagans' eyes have no sense of sight, and grain growing in pagans' fields is not grain, and many other things,” you say, “so absurd that they can move intelligent people to laughter.” Certainly your laughter moves intelligent people not to laughter but rather to tears, as the laughter of the delirious moves their sane friends' minds to tears. Do you thus either deny, against holy Scripture, that an unbeliever's mind commits fornication, or say that true chastity exists in a mind committing fornication, and laugh, and are sane? From where, how, by what reasoning can that be? Certainly neither is that true chastity nor true sanity. Certainly, I say, the chastity of a fornicating mind is not true, and the madness of a man speaking this disgrace and laughing is true. But far be it from us to say that pagans' bodies are not bodies, or other such things. For it does not follow that, if the virtue of which an impious person boasts is not true, the body God makes is not true. But we can certainly say that heretics have no forehead, if by “forehead” we are understood to mean not the bodily member God made but a sense of shame. What if, in that very book of mine which you boast of answering, I had not already laid the groundwork lest in the sentence, *everything that is not from faith is sin*[^57], even those things in unbelievers which are God's gifts, whether goods of mind or body, should be included? For among them are also the things you chatter about in vain: the body, eyes, and other members. To that category belongs also the grain growing in pagans' fields, whose creator is God, not the pagans. Did you not yourself quote among other things these words of mine: “For the soul, the body, and whatever goods of soul and body are naturally implanted, are God's gifts even in sinners, since God, not they themselves, made them. But of what they do it is said: *everything that is not from faith is sin*”? If you had retained this brief but certainly clear statement of mine in your mind, I think you would not have been so unscrupulous as to assert that we could say that “pagans' bodies are not bodies, pagans' eyes have no sense of sight, and grain growing in pagans' fields is not grain.” For, to repeat to you as though waking you from sleep the same words of mine which perhaps slipped from your memory so that you said this: “These are God's gifts even in sinners, since God, not they themselves, made them; but of what they do it is said: *everything that is not from faith is sin*.” But when you say insane things and laugh, you resemble someone delirious; when, however, you so disregard and forget the truths I say, which you yourself quoted shortly before and inserted in this very work in which you seem to answer me, you are compared no longer to the delirious but to the lethargic.

**3. 28.** You say that “you marvel that so outstanding an intellect”—mine, of course, as you mockingly put it—“did not see how much I helped you in this too, by saying that some sins are conquered by other sins.” For immediately you add and conclude: “Therefore a human being can very easily be without sins through the pursuit of holiness, which God helps. For if sins are conquered by sins,” you say, “how much more can sins be overcome by virtues?” As though we deny that God's help is so powerful that, if he wished, we could today have no evil concupiscences against which we might fight even most unconquerably. Yet you too do not deny that this does not happen. Why it does not happen, who has known the mind of the Lord [^58]? Yet I know no small thing when I know that, whatever the reason, it is neither injustice in the just God nor weakness in the almighty. There is therefore something in his hidden and profound counsel why, as long as we live in this mortal flesh, there is something in us against which our mind must fight, and also something for which we say: *forgive us our debts*[^59]. But to speak to you as a human being to a human being, and as one whose earthly dwelling weighs down his mind with its many thoughts [^60], nothing among creatures, as regards the worth of natures divinely established, surpasses the rational mind. Hence it follows that a good mind pleases itself more and delights in itself more than in any other creature. How dangerously, indeed destructively, it pleases itself when thereby it swells with conceit and is exalted by the disease of inflation, while it does not yet see as it will at the end that supreme and immutable good—in comparison with which it may scorn itself and become cheap in its own eyes through love of that good, and be so filled with its spirit that it prefers it to itself not only by reason but also by eternal love—would take much argument to show. One understands this who, exhausted by hunger, returns to himself and says: *I will arise and go to my father*[^61]. How, then, do we know whether perhaps, because this evil of pride will be unable to tempt the mind and there will be nothing at all against which to contend when it is so satisfied by the vision and so inflamed with love of the higher good that it cannot fall away from love of that good into self-pleasing, it has therefore been arranged that here, in this place of weakness, we should live under daily forgiveness of sins so that we should not live proudly? Because of this evil of pride, not even the Apostle Paul was entrusted to his own choice; but because he had not yet come to such participation in that higher good that he could no longer be exalted there, here an angel of Satan was set beside him to buffet him, lest he be exalted here where he could be [^62].

**3. 29.** But whether this is the reason or some other far more hidden from me, I cannot doubt that, however much we advance under this burden of the corruptible body, *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^63]. Therefore the holy Church, even in those members of hers in whom she has neither the stain of crime nor the wrinkle of falsehood [^64], despite your pride's contradiction, does not cease saying to God: *forgive us our debts*[^65]. But the arrogance and presumption in your own strength with which you said, “Therefore a human being can very easily be without sins through the pursuit of holiness, which God helps,” is not recognized by one who does not know your doctrines. For you wish the pursuit of holiness to precede in the human will without God's help, which God ought then to help by merit, not freely; and you believe that a person can be without sins in this miserable life so as to have in himself no reason to say: *forgive us our debts.* Yet you seem to have put this somewhat more timidly, because you did not say, “He can be without all sins.” But neither did you wish to say, “Without some.” Rather, as though you yourself blushed at your excessive presumption, you so moderated the statement that it could be defended by both your voice and ours. For if it is discussed among the Pelagians, the answer will be that “some” was not said because you said a human being can be without all sins. If, however, among us, the answer will be that “all” was not said because you intended any person whatever to be understood as necessarily asking pardon for some sins. But we who know what you think cannot fail to know how you say these things.

**3. 30.** “If a gentile clothes the naked,” you say, “is it a sin because it is not from faith?” Certainly, insofar as it is not from faith, it is sin; not because the deed itself, clothing the naked, is sin, but because only the impious deny that failing to glory in the Lord over such a work is sin. To understand this, although it has already been discussed sufficiently, listen a little longer, for it is an important matter. Look, I repeat the very things you set down. “If a gentile” who does not live by faith “clothes the naked, rescues someone in danger, tends a sick person's wounds, spends wealth on honorable friendship, and cannot be driven even by torture to false testimony,” I ask you whether he does these good works well or badly. For if, although good, he nevertheless does them badly, you cannot deny that one who does anything badly sins. But because you do not wish him to sin when doing these things, you will certainly say: “He does good things and does them well.” A bad tree therefore bears good fruits, which the Truth says cannot happen. Do not rush your judgment; consider carefully what you should answer. Or do you call an unbeliever a good tree? Then he pleases God, for what is good cannot fail to please the good. And where will the Scripture stand: *without faith it is impossible to please God*[^66]? Or will you answer: “He is a good tree not insofar as he is an unbeliever, but insofar as he is human”? Of whom, then, does the Lord say: *a bad tree cannot bear good fruits*[^67]? For whoever it is will be either a human or an angel. But if a human is a good tree insofar as human, an angel too is surely a good tree insofar as angel. For these are works of God, the creator of good natures. Thus there will be no bad tree of which it was said that it cannot bear good fruits. What unbeliever could think so unbelievingly? Therefore each person is a bad tree unable to bear good fruits not insofar as he is human, which is God's work, but insofar as he has an evil will. See, then, whether you dare call an unbelieving will a good will.

**3. 31.** But perhaps you will say: “A merciful will is good.” This would be rightly said if mercy were always good, just as faith in Christ, that is, *faith* *working through love*[^68], is always good. But if an evil mercy also exists, by which partiality is shown to a poor person in judgment [^69], and because of which King Saul finally deserved condemnation from the Lord, who is surely merciful, because against his command he spared a captive king out of human affection [^70], consider more attentively whether mercy is good only if it belongs to this good faith. Rather, answer, to perceive this beyond doubt, whether you judge an unbelieving mercy to be good. Moreover, if showing mercy badly is a vice, showing mercy without faith is undoubtedly a vice. Even if mercy in itself through natural compassion is a good work, one who uses it without faith uses even this good badly, and one who does it without faith does this good badly. But one who does something badly surely sins.

**3. 32.** From this it follows that even the good works unbelievers do are not theirs but his who uses the evil well. Their sins, however, are theirs, by which they do even good things badly, because they do them with an unbelieving rather than faithful will, that is, a foolish and harmful will. Such a will, as no Christian doubts, is a bad tree which can bear only bad fruits, that is, sins alone. For *everything*, whether you like it or not, *that is not from faith is sin*[^71]. Hence God cannot love these trees, and if they remain such he intends to cut them down, because *without faith it is impossible to please*[^72]. But I linger here as though you had not yourself already pronounced these trees fruitless. How, then, I ask, are you not either joking or raving in these arguments, when you praise the fruits of fruitless trees? These fruits either do not exist, or, if evil, should not be praised; or, if the fruits are good, the trees certainly are not fruitless. Indeed, those whose fruits are good are themselves good and ought to please God, whom good trees cannot fail to please, and the Scripture will be false: *without faith it is impossible to please*[^73].

**3. 33.** But what will you answer except vanities? “I called fruitlessly good,” you say, “people who, because they do not do the good things they do for God's sake, do not obtain eternal life from him.” Is the just and good God, then, going to send good people into eternal death? I am weary of saying how many absurdities follow for you when you think, say, and write such things and censoriously reproach me for not being equally foolish about them. But hear briefly, lest, when you err about the very matters as greatly as one can err, I seem to be disputing with you about words. Either understand what the Lord says: *if your eye is evil, your whole body will be dark; but if your eye is single, your whole body will be light*[^74], and recognize this eye as the intention with which each person does what he does. Thus learn that one who does not do good works with the intention of good faith, that is, faith working through love, has his whole body, as it were, composed of those works as its members, dark, that is, filled with the blackness of sins. Or at least, since you concede that the works of unbelievers which seem good to you nevertheless do not lead them to everlasting salvation and the kingdom, know that we say that the human good, the good will, the good work by which alone a person can be led to God's eternal gift and kingdom, can be bestowed on no one without God's grace, given through the one mediator of God and human beings. Therefore let everything else which seems to have some praise among human beings seem to you true virtues and good works done without any sin. As for me, I know this: a good will does not perform them, for an unbelieving and impious will is not good. Let such wills be called good trees according to you; it suffices that before God they are fruitless and thus not good. Let them be fruitful among human beings, among whom they are also good, with you as their authority and praiser, and, if you wish, even planter, provided I establish this, whether you like it or not: the love of the world by which anyone is this world's friend is not from God; the love of enjoying any creatures without love of the creator is not from God; but the love of God by which we come to God is only from God the Father through Jesus Christ with the Holy Spirit. Through this love of the creator each person uses even creatures well. Without this love of the creator no one uses any creatures well. This love is therefore necessary for marital chastity too to be a good that brings blessedness, so that its intention, when using the spouse's flesh, is not in the pleasure of lust but in the will for offspring. If, however, pleasure too has conquered and forced something to be done for its own sake, not to propagate children, let the sin be venial because of Christian marriage.

**4. 34.** But I did not say what you said I said after inserting other words from my book: “Children are under the devil's power because they are born from the mingling of bodies.” For it is one thing to say “who are born from the mingling of bodies,” another “because they are born from the mingling of bodies.” Their being born from the mingling of bodies is not the cause of the evil; for even if human nature had not been corrupted by the first humans' sin, children could only be begotten from the mingling of bodies. But those born from the mingling of bodies are under the devil before being reborn through the spirit because they are born through that concupiscence by which the flesh desires against the spirit and compels the spirit to desire against itself [^75]. This struggle of good and evil would not exist if no one had sinned. And just as it did not exist before human iniquity, so it will not exist after human weakness.

**5. 35.** But again you quote other words of mine and argue at great length: “Because we are composed of unequal goods, the mind ought to rule the body.” You recall that we have the one in common with gods, the other with beasts. “Therefore,” you say, “what is better, that is, the mind endowed with virtue, rules well both the body's members and its desires” [^76]. You do not consider that desires are not ruled as members are. For the desires we bridle by reason and fight with our mind are evil, while the members we move by the choice of our will are good, excepting the genitals, although they too are good as God's work. They are called shameful parts because lust has more power than reason over moving them. Yet we do not allow them to carry out what their arousal provokes, since we hold the other members under easy command. But when does a human being use good members badly, except when consenting to the evil desires dwelling in us? Among these lust is shameful above the rest, and unless resisted commits horrifying uncleanness. Only marital chastity uses this evil well. But this lust is not evil in beasts because it does not resist reason, which they lack. Why do you not believe that the human beings placed in paradise before sin could have been divinely granted the power to procreate children through the tranquil movement and union or mingling of their members without any lust, or at least to have such lust that its movement neither preceded nor exceeded the will? Is it not enough for you that lust pleases, unless you are pleased also with a lust that troubles the unwilling and even the resisting? The Pelagians indeed boast of it even in disputes as though it were something good, but the saints confess it with groans so that they may be delivered from evil.

**6. 36.** You slanderously say that “with laughable contradiction I said that some become guilty through a good work, others holy through an evil work,” because I said that “unbelieving people, by using the good of marriage without faith, turn it into evil and sin, and in the same way believers' marriages turn the evil of concupiscence to a use of righteousness” [^77]. Hence I did not say that some are guilty through a good work, but through an evil work by which they use good things badly; nor that others are holy through an evil work, but through a good work by which they use evil things well. If you do not wish to understand, or pretend not to understand, do not drown out those willing and able to understand.

**7. 37.** “If anyone could be created evil,” you say, “he would never pass into good through washing.” In the same way you would say that a body created mortal cannot become immortal, yet you would speak falsely in both cases. For God did not create an evil when he created a human being; rather, the good he himself created derives evil from sin, which he did not create, and he heals this evil which he did not create within the good he created.

**7. 38.** Since we do not say that “demons instituted marriage,” or “the seminal mingling of both sexes,” or that “spouses' intercourse for generation is the devil's work,” because God instituted all these things and all could exist without the evil of concupiscence if the devil had not inflicted the wound of transgression from which discord of flesh and spirit followed, do you not look at yourself and blush at the garrulous loquacity with which you spun empty tales: namely, that “demons catch married people in intercourse and, as though caught in the demons' own activity, do not allow them to generate children to be freed through regeneration”? As though the devil, if he did whatever he wished, would not immediately strangle the adult impious themselves, still placed under that same devil's power, when he learned that they were planning to become Christians! It does not therefore follow that demons threaten and terribly oppose parents begetting children for regeneration and having intercourse for that purpose, as you invent for yourself, because through the wound inflicted by the devil, in which the human race limps, something is born by God's creation to be transferred from Adam to Christ. For a legion of demons could not even gain power over pigs unless Christ granted their request [^78], and he knows how to raise crowns for martyrs even from the persecutions he allows the devil to inflict, using every kind of evil well for the benefit of the good. But even in spouses who either do not think of their children's regeneration or even detest it, the lawful mingling of the sexes for generation is a good work of marriage, whose fruit is the orderly reception of children, although they use that good badly and sin in glorying over the impious offspring to be propagated or already propagated. For human beings, polluted by any contagion or commission of sin, are good insofar as they are human; therefore it is good that they be born, because they are good.

**7. 39.** Yet it does not follow that “adulteries or any debaucheries should be committed so that they may be begotten,” the absurdity you think we are compelled into because we say marriage produces the good of offspring from the evil of lust. But that perverse and false conclusion in no way follows from our true and right judgment. For because the Lord says, *make friends for yourselves from the mammon of iniquity*[^79], we should not therefore add further iniquity and commit thefts and robberies so that we can feed more holy poor people with more generous mercy. Just as, then, friends who may receive us into eternal dwellings must be made from the mammon of iniquity, so spouses must beget children from the wound of original sin to be regenerated into eternal life. And just as thefts, frauds, and plunder must not be added so that, by money increased through iniquities, more righteous poor friends may be acquired, so adulteries, debaucheries, and fornications must not be added to the evil with which we were born in order that children may be born in greater numbers. For using well an evil already present is one thing, and committing an evil not yet present another. The former is to do a voluntary good from the evil derived from parents; the latter is to pile other evils, now our own and voluntary, upon the evil derived from parents. There is, however, a difference: the mammon of iniquity is praiseworthily distributed to the needy, whereas carnal concupiscence is more praiseworthily bridled by the virtue of continence than expended on the fruits of marriage. For its evil is so great that not using it is better than using it well.

**8. 40.** Next you quote other words of mine and say much against them that amounts to nothing, repeating things already exhausted in the preceding discussion. If I too wished to repeat them, where would it end? Among these you also say that familiar claim of yours against Christ's grace, often vainly uttered by you: that “under the name of grace we say people become good by fatal necessity,” although those who cannot yet speak stop your mouths and silence your tongues. For although you labor with great verbosity to establish and persuade people of what Pelagius condemned at the examination by the Palestinian bishops, “that God's grace is given according to our merits,” you can nevertheless assert no merits in infants by which those adopted as God's children may be distinguished from those who die without obtaining this grace.

**8. 41.** You slanderously say that I teach that “no effort should be expected from the human will, contrary to the Gospel saying in which the Lord says: *ask and you will receive; seek and you will find; knock and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and one who seeks finds, and to one who knocks it will be opened* [^80].” For there, as I see, you have now begun to place merits preceding grace, namely asking, seeking, and knocking, so that grace may be repaid as owed to these merits and thus be called grace in vain. As though no grace had preceded and touched the heart so that the good that brings blessedness might be asked of God, God might be sought, and one might knock at God's door; as though it were vainly written, *his mercy will go before me*[^81], and he vainly commanded us to pray for our enemies [^82] if turning awayward and hostile hearts were not his work.

**8. 42.** But you quote the Apostle's testimony and say that the one *who wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth*[^83] opens to those who knock, so that, with you as teachers, we may understand that not all are saved and come to knowledge of the truth because they themselves refuse to ask while God wishes to give, refuse to seek while God wishes to show, and refuse to knock while God wishes to open. But those infants who neither ask, seek, nor knock refute your understanding by their very silence. Indeed, even when baptized they cry out, reject it, and resist; yet they receive and find, it is opened to them, and they enter God's kingdom, where they may have eternal salvation and knowledge of the truth, although far more infants are not adopted into this grace by him *who wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth.* To these he cannot say: *I willed, and you were unwilling*[^84], for if he had willed it, which of those who do not yet have the choice of their own will would have resisted his almighty will? Why, then, do we not understand the saying, *who wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth*[^85], as we understand the same Apostle's saying, *through the justification of one, upon all human beings unto justification of life*[^86]? For God wills all these to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth: all upon whom grace comes through the justification of one unto justification of life. Otherwise someone may say to us: if God wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth, but they do not come because they themselves are unwilling, why do so many thousands of infants who die without receiving baptism not come into God's kingdom, where knowledge of the truth is certain? Are they not human beings, so that they do not belong to the phrase *all human beings*? Or can anyone say, “God wills it, but they are unwilling,” of those who do not yet know how to will or refuse such things? For even infants who are baptized and die, and through that grace come to the most certain knowledge of the truth in God's kingdom, do not come because they themselves wished to be renewed by Christ's baptism. Since, therefore, the former are not unbaptized because they are unwilling, nor the latter baptized because they are willing, why does God, who wills *all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth*, allow so many who do not resist him by any choice of will not to come into his kingdom, where knowledge of the truth is certain?

**8. 43.** Unless perhaps you will say that infants should not be counted among all those whom God wills to be saved because they are already saved with the salvation intended here, contracting no sin at all. Then a still more intolerable absurdity will follow you. For in this way you make God more benevolent toward all the most impious and criminal people than toward the most innocent and pure, free from every stain of sin. For since he wills all the former to be saved, he also wills them to enter his kingdom, which follows if they are saved; but those unwilling harm themselves. Yet he does not will the immense number of infants dying without baptism to be added to his kingdom, although no sins hinder them, as you think, and, as no one doubts, they cannot resist his will by a contrary will. Thus he wills all those, many of whom are unwilling, to become Christians, but does not will all those, none of whom is unwilling. This is alien to truth. *The Lord knows those who are his* [^87], and his will is certain concerning their salvation and admission into his kingdom. Therefore the saying, *he wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth*[^88], must be understood as the saying is understood: *through the justification of one, upon all human beings unto justification of life*[^89].

**8. 44.** If you think this apostolic testimony should be understood so that “all” stands for the many justified in Christ—for many others are not made alive in Christ—you will be answered that likewise in the passage, *he wills all human beings to be saved and come to knowledge of the truth, all* stands for the many whom he wills to come to this grace. It is understood far more fittingly as said for this reason: no one comes unless he himself wills that person to come. *No one can come to me*, says the Son, *unless the Father who sent me draws him*[^90], and: *no one can come to me unless it has been given him by my Father*[^91]. All, then, who are saved and come to knowledge of the truth are saved by his will and come by his will. For those who, like infants, do not yet exercise the choice of their will are regenerated by his will, by whose creation they are generated; and those who already exercise the choice of their will cannot will unless he wills and helps, by whom the will is prepared [^92].

**8. 45.** If here you say to me, “Why, then, does he not convert the wills of all the unwilling?” I shall answer: why does he not adopt through the washing of regeneration all infants who are about to die, in whom he finds as yet no wills, and therefore no contrary wills? If you perceive that this is deeper than you can discover, let both questions be deep for both of us: why, among both older and younger people, God wills to help one and not another. Yet let us hold certain and immovable that there is no injustice with God [^93] by which he condemns anyone without evil merits, and that there is goodness with God by which he delivers many without good merits. In those he condemns he shows what is owed to all, so that those he delivers may learn from this what deserved punishment is remitted them and what undeserved grace is granted them.

**8. 46.** You do not know how to think of these matters as befits Christian hearts, and rather, according to your own opinion, you say they happen by fate. For the judgment is yours, not ours: “What does not happen by merit happens by fate.” And lest, according to this definition, whatever happens to human beings happens by fate if not by merits, you therefore assert good or evil merits as far as you can, lest fate follow if you deny merits. Hence we say to you: if whatever is bestowed on human beings without any merits must now happen by fate, and merits must therefore be established lest fate follow if there are no merits, then infants with no good merits are baptized by fate and enter God's kingdom by fate, and again infants with no evil merits go unbaptized by fate and do not enter God's kingdom by fate. See: little ones unable to speak prove that you rather are the advocates of fate. But since we say there are evil merits from corrupted origin, we say an infant enters God's kingdom by grace because God is good, and another deservedly does not enter because God is just, and in neither case is there fate, because God does what he wills. But though we know that by him to whom we sing mercy and judgment with faithful voice [^94], one is condemned according to judgment and another freed according to mercy, as to why he condemns or frees this one rather than that, who are we to answer back to God? *Does the thing fashioned say to him who fashioned it: why did you make me thus? Does not the potter have power over the clay, from the same lump* of corrupted and condemned origin, *to make one vessel for honor* according to mercy, *and another for dishonor* according to judgment [^95]? He does not make both for honor, lest nature think it has deserved this as though blameless; he does not make both for dishonor, so that mercy may triumph over judgment [^96]. Hence the one deservedly condemned does not justly complain of punishment, nor does the one freely delivered proudly boast of merit; rather, he humbly gives thanks, recognizing in the one from whom the debt is exacted what is forgiven him in the same condition.

**8. 47.** You assert that in another book of mine I said: “Free choice is denied if grace is commended, and in turn grace is denied if free choice is commended.” You slander me. That was not said by me; rather, I said that because of the difficulty of the question itself this could seem to be said and thought. It is no great burden to quote my own words, so that readers may see how you lay snares for my writings, and with what conscience you abuse slow or ignorant minds, so that they think you are answering because you refuse to remain silent. For near the end of my first book to Saint Pinianus, entitled *On Grace against Pelagius*, I said: “This question concerning the choice of the will and God's grace is so difficult to distinguish that, when free choice is defended, God's grace seems to be denied; but when God's grace is asserted, free choice is thought to be taken away,” and so on [^97]. But you, an honorable and truthful man, removed the words I said and said what you yourself invented. For I said that this question is difficult to distinguish; I did not say it cannot be distinguished. Much less, therefore, would I say what you falsely claim I said: “Free choice is denied if grace is commended, and grace is denied if free choice is commended.” Restore my words, and your slander will vanish. Put back in their places where I said “seems” and where I said “is thought,” so that it may be apparent with what frauds you discuss so great a matter. I did not say, “Grace is denied,” but, “so that grace seems to be denied.” I did not say, “Free choice is denied or taken away,” but, “so that it is thought to be taken away.” And you promise that “when those books begin to be sifted, the impiety of my statements will be laid bare and pierced by you.” Who would not await the wisdom of your exposition, knowing your fidelity in lying?

**8. 48.** What do you mean by saying: “It adds nothing to the praise of grace if it bestows on its own what sins bestow on the impious”? You mean marital chastity, which the impious also seem to you to possess. Contentious man, grace gives true virtue, not something called virtue that is not. Why do you join chastity and virginity as though of the same kind? Chastity belongs to the mind, virginity to the body. Indeed, while the former remains intact in the mind, the latter can be forcibly taken from the body; and while the latter remains intact in the body, the former can be corrupted in the mind by a wanton will. Therefore I did not say, “True marriage, widowhood, or virginity,” but: “Chastity, whether marital, widowed, or virginal, should not be called true unless devoted to true faith” [^98]. For women can be spouses, widows, or virgins and not be chaste, if with a contaminated will they commit adultery, if with an unclean spirit they resolve to commit debaucheries. Yet you say that true chastity exists in them even if they fornicate in mind, which divine words attest that all the impious do.

**8. 49.** Which of us says that “the joining of marital members is evil,” in which marriage uses the evil of concupiscence well to propagate children? This concupiscence would not be evil if it were moved to lawful intercourse solely for generation. But now, when marital chastity resists it, chastity is a limit upon evil and is therefore good. Hence “its crime,” as you slanderously say, “does not go unpunished through religion,” because it is guilty of no crime when through the good of faith it uses the evil of lust well. Nor can it be said here, as you think: *let us do evil so that good may come*[^99], because marriage is evil in no part of itself. For what it did not cause but found in the human beings whom parents beget is not marriage's evil. In the first spouses, however, who were born of no parents, the discordant evil of carnal concupiscence, which marriage would use well, arose through sin, not through marriage itself, so that marriage would deserve condemnation for it. Why, then, do you ask “whether I call the pleasure of intercourse in Christian spouses chastity or unchastity”? Here is my answer: it itself is not called chastity, but the good use of that evil is; and through this good use that evil cannot be called unchastity either. For unchastity is the shameful use of that same evil, just as virginal chastity is no use of it. Therefore, with marital chastity preserved, an evil is derived from evil through birth to be purified through rebirth.

**8. 50.** “But if because of this evil of lust,” you say, “guilty offspring are brought forth even by Christian spouses, it follows that virginal chastity brings happiness; and because it is found in the impious, unbelievers exalted by the virtue of chastity surpass Christians polluted by lust's corruption.” It is not as you say; you are greatly mistaken. For those who use lust well are not polluted by its corruption, although they beget those polluted by its corruption and therefore needing regeneration. Nor is virginal chastity found in the impious, although bodily virginity is, because true chastity cannot dwell in a fornicating mind. Hence the virginal good of the impious is not preferred to the marital good of believers; rather, spouses using an evil well are preferred to virgins using a good badly. Therefore, when married believers use the evil of lust well, it is not that “among them,” as you slanderously say, “crime enjoys impunity because of faith,” but that in them because of faith there is the true, not false, virtue of chastity.

**8. 51.** What has it to do with us that you affirm the Manichaeans say: “If someone commits homicide with a fearful conscience, he is guilty because he feared; but if he perpetrates some crime with exultant audacity, as though believing he does from faith what he does evilly, he has escaped guilt”? Indeed, I never heard the Manichaeans say this. But what has it to do with us whether they say it or you slander even them, since the Catholic faith we hold and by whose weight we press you does not say this? For we say that what seem good works are not truly good without faith, since truly good works must please God [^100], and because without faith it is impossible to please him, surely what is a truly good work cannot exist without faith. Yet those works which are most openly evil are not done by *faith working through love*[^101], because *love of one's neighbor does not work evil*[^102].

**8. 52.** “Natural concupiscence is therefore good,” you say—for you are ashamed to say “carnal”—“and when it is kept within its own measure,” you say, “it is defiled by no taint of evil.” How is it kept within its own measure, I ask you? How is it kept there except by resistance? And why is it resisted except lest it fulfill evil desires? How, then, is it good?

**9. 53.** You also come to those words of my book where I said: “Were not those first spouses, whose marriage God blessed by saying, *increase and multiply*[^103], naked and unashamed [^104]? Why, then, shame over those members after sin, unless an indecent movement arose there which marriage undoubtedly would not have had if human beings had not sinned?” [^105] Seeing these words of mine so grounded in Scripture that anyone who reads or has read that passage of Genesis approves without doubt what I said, you sweated much in a lengthy contrary argument, but did not become sincere. For you persisted in your perverse opinion, though by the very experiment you proved that you could not overthrow my right one. In this contradiction of yours I pass over your gestures and boastings, like those of a panting man unable to reach what he strives for, yet pretending to have arrived amid the fog he spreads in his confusion. But, as far as the Lord helps me, I seize and break the necessary joints of your discourse, so that whoever reads through both your work and mine may see the whole body lying defeated, especially because the things you repeat in many ways have often been demolished by us too.

**9. 54.** Among other things you say here: “In the fact that after sin the first human beings, ashamed, covered those members in which lust is present, I found a means to persuade people that God instituted airy marriages.” If, then, marriages without lust would be airy, according to you bodies will be airy when lust no longer exists. Or do you love this lust so much that, just as you place it in paradise before sin, you impose it even on resurrected bodies? But I do not say, as you affirm I do, that “what nature cannot exist without is not natural.” Rather, I say that the vice without which human nature is now not born is called natural, although human nature was not established thus at the beginning. Therefore this evil too derives its origin not from nature's first establishment but from the first man's evil will; nor will it remain, but it will either be condemned or healed.

**9. 55.** You call “my opinion a bedbug, which causes annoyance when alive and a stench when crushed,” as though you “were ashamed,” as you say, “to crush me by victory,” or “shrank from pursuing and destroying me because, as though defeated, I fled to muddy places.” For, you claim, “your modesty, like a temple guardian, has barred from freedom of speech those parts of your argument by which I must be crushed or destroyed, because you are forced to speak of the shameful parts.” Why do you not rather speak freely of the good things you praise? Why is there no freedom of speech about God's work, if its dignity remains unviolated and sin has done nothing there to produce shame and suppress freedom of speech?

**10. 56.** “If marriage does not exist without lust,” you say, “and you condemn lust generally, you condemn marriage too.” You could say that, because death will be condemned, all mortals must be condemned. But if lust came from marriage, it would certainly not exist before or outside marriage. “What marriage cannot exist without cannot be called a disease,” you say, “because marriage can exist apart from sin, whereas a disease is called sin by the Apostle.” But the answer is: not every disease is called sin. This disease is the punishment of sin, without which human nature, not yet healed in every part, cannot exist. But if lust is not evil because without it the good of marriage does not occur, conversely neither is the body good because without it the evil of adultery does not occur. This is false; therefore so is that. Who does not know that the Apostle commanded the married that each should know how to possess his vessel, that is, his wife, not in the disease of desire like the gentiles who do not know God [^106]? One who reads the Apostle on this matter therefore disregards you. And are you not ashamed to introduce this disease, or what you admit to exist although blushing, this lust, into paradise and attribute it to the spouses before sin? Is it for this reason you are not lurking hidden in mud, because you crown yourself with the lust of flesh and blood like a rosy flower of paradise and, as though gladly suffused with such color, both blush and praise?

**11. 57.** What of the fact that you so delight in talking that you try with superfluous length of speech to prove what we confess and teach, as though we denied it? For who denies that intercourse would have occurred even if sin had not preceded? But it would have occurred with the genital members, like the others, moved by will, not aroused by lust; or at least with lust itself—to avoid saddening you too much over it—not as it now is, but serving the will's bidding. Yet you labor so faithfully for your protégé that you feel injured unless you place it in paradise too just as it is now, saying not that sin made it such there but that it would have remained such had no one sinned. In that peace it would have had to be fought, or, to avoid fighting, satisfied whenever it arose. O holy delights of paradise! O the impudence of bishops of whatever sort! O the faith of the chaste of whatever sort!

**12. 58.** But wishing to show that not everything covered should be thought shameful because of sin, you very vainly mention many things naturally covered in our body, as though they had been covered after sin, like the things those human beings, about whom our question concerns, covered as shameful after sin although before sin they were neither shameful nor covered. “Balbus,” you say, “in Cicero discussed truly and carefully with Cotta” [^107]. From this you quote a few things, you say, “to burden me with shame that under the teaching of sacred law I did not understand what the gentiles could come to know under reason's guidance alone.” And you quote Balbus's words from Cicero's book to teach us what the Stoics thought about male and female differences in mute animals, the body's genital parts, and the wonderful lusts for mingling bodies. Yet before inserting those words, whether Cicero's or anyone else's, you most cautiously prefaced them by saying that “he therefore touches on the function of the sexes under the occasion of beasts, because for propriety's sake he passed over this in his description of humanity.” What does “for propriety's sake” mean? Is propriety embarrassed in the human sex, where God works more worthily as in a more excellent nature? But the Stoics taught you to discuss hidden things and did not teach you to blush at shameful things. You then add “how he depicts the human being himself in his discourse,” as you say, “and discusses how the nature of the belly lies beneath the stomach, being a receptacle for food and drink; how the lungs and heart draw breath from outside; how many things in the belly, made of sinews, are wonderfully formed; how it is manifold and winding and restrains and contains whatever it receives, whether dry or moist,” and other things of this kind, down to “how the remnants of food are expelled, as the intestines alternately contract and relax.” Since he could describe these things in beasts too, why did he turn from them to the human being, except because these things are not shameful, just as in beasts the matters of the genitals and sexual organs are not shameful, though in humans they are? For precisely the reason they were first covered with fig leaves after sin. For when in describing the human body he came to those extremities through which remnants of food are expelled, he said: “How it happens is certainly not difficult to say, but it must be passed over lest the discourse contain anything unpleasant.” He did not say “embarrassing,” or “lest the discourse contain anything shameful,” but “anything unpleasant.” For some things bring horror to the senses because they are ugly, others shame to the mind even if beautiful: the former offend delight, while the latter stir lust or are stirred by lust.

**12. 59.** How, then, do these things help you? “Because our maker himself,” you say, “acknowledged no fault in his art when he hid our vital organs so carefully.” Certainly, far be it that so great an artist acknowledged any fault in his art. But you yourself said a little above why he covered these things: “Lest, exposed, they perish or cause horror.” Yet what the first spouses covered with fig leaves [^108] certainly neither perished nor caused horror when they were naked and unashamed [^109]. And now the caution of chastity avoids the sight of those same members more lest their appearance arouse desire than lest it cause horror. Vainly, then, you thought your protégé could be aided by the Stoics' testimony, though it is certainly no friend of theirs, since they placed no part of human good in bodily pleasure. Indeed, unlike you, they chose to praise lusts in beasts rather than human beings. In keeping with their view Cicero says somewhere that he does not think the good of a ram and of Publius Africanus is the same. By this statement you should rather have been warned what to think of human lust.

**12. 60.** But if you wish us to proceed with such literature, since in it too some traces of truth are found, I think you must now confess that what you yourself quoted has no force against us. See now whether what I shall say does not overthrow your assertion. In the third book *On the Republic*, the same Cicero says that the human being is “brought forth into life by nature not as by a mother but as by a stepmother, with a naked, fragile, and weak body, and a mind anxious in troubles, abject in fears, soft before labors, and prone to lusts; in which, however, a certain divine fire of intelligence and mind lay as though buried” [^110]. What do you say to this? This author did not say that this was brought about by the habits of those living badly, but rather accused nature. He saw the fact, but did not know its cause. For he did not know why there was *a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day they leave their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all*[^111], because, not instructed in sacred letters, he was ignorant of original sin. But if he thought well of the lust you defend, a mind prone to lusts would not have displeased him.

**12. 61.** But if you defend these as lesser goods toward which the mind should not incline from higher things, not because lust is a vice but because it is the lowest good, hear the clearer things he says in that same third book *On the Republic*, discussing the reason for ruling. “Do we not see,” he says, “that nature itself has granted dominion to whatever is best for the greatest benefit of the lowest? Why, then, does God rule the human being, the mind the body, and reason lust, anger, and the other vicious parts of the same mind?” [^112] Do you now at least, taught by him, confess that these parts of the mind you defend as good are vicious? Hear further. A little later he says: “But distinctions in ruling and serving must also be understood. For just as the mind is said to rule the body, it is also said to rule lust, but the body as a king rules his citizens or a parent his children, while lust as a master rules slaves, since it restrains and breaks it. Thus the commands of kings, generals, magistrates, fathers, and peoples preside over citizens and allies as the mind over bodies; masters, however, constrain slaves as the best part of the mind, wisdom, constrains the vicious and weak parts of the same mind, such as lusts, angers, and other disturbances.” Have you still something to chatter against us from the authors of secular literature? But if, for this error—which may God turn away from you—you intend to seek something to say against bishops, most illustrious expositors of divine words, and a way to resist their holiness, what will Cicero mean to you that you would not contend that he was foolish and raving in this opinion? Be silent, then, about books of that kind, and do not try insultingly to teach us from them, lest you be crushed by the testimonies of those by whom you vainly believe you are supported.

**13. 62.** Why did you think you should argue vainly about the woman's movement, of which she too was ashamed? Certainly the woman did not cover a visible movement, but when she felt more secretly in the same members something like what the man had felt, both covered what both had felt when, seeing one another, they were stirred toward one another, and they were ashamed either each before himself or one before the other. But, speaking vanities, you “ask modest ears to forgive and to groan with you rather than resent your necessity.” Why are you embarrassed to discuss God's works? Why do you ask to be forgiven? Is not your very request for pardon an accusation of lust? “But if the male member could move before,” you say, “then the offense introduced nothing new.” Certainly it could move before too, but it was not indecent so as to cause shame, because the will's command moved it, not flesh desiring against the spirit. For in this arose the shameful novelty which your novelty shamelessly defends. Yet I never blamed the movement of the genitals generally, what you call “categorically”; I blamed that movement produced by the concupiscence through which *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^113]. Since your error defends it as good, I do not know how your spirit desires against it as against an evil.

**13. 63.** “If this lust was in the fruit of that tree,” you say, “it is shown to have been made by God, and therefore it too is defended as good.” But the answer is: lust was not in the tree's fruit, and therefore the tree was good; rather, evil is the disobedience of lust which, when God abandoned humanity, arose against the human disobedience committed concerning the tree. For far be it that God should at any time have bestowed on those human beings at any age, through a good tree, such a benefit that they would have in their members an adversary against which chastity must fight.

**13. 64.** We know that “the Apostle John did not blame this world, that is, heaven and earth and all things substantially within it, which world the Father made through the Son, when he said: *all that is in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh, the concupiscence of the eyes, and worldly ambition, which is not from the Father but is from the world*[^114].” We know this; I do not want you to teach us. But wishing to explain what “concupiscence of the flesh he said was not from the Father,” you said that “luxury should be understood.” Yet when I ask you what one consents to so that there is luxury, or resists so that there is not, your protégé confronts you to your face. Whether you should still praise it is for you to see: consenting to it is luxury; resisting it is continence. I wonder whether you deliberate whether to blame it rather with luxury, which occurs through consent to it, or to praise it with continence, which wages war on it. In this war chastity is continence's victory, luxury concupiscence's victory. But you, an incorruptible and upright judge, while praising continence and blaming luxury, nevertheless show such partiality to concupiscence—you must see why you fear offending it—that you are not ashamed to praise it with its adversary and dare not blame it with its victory. But far be it that any man of God should so hear you as a censurer of luxury as to approve you as a praiser of concupiscence and judge good from your discourse what he experiences as evil within himself. Indeed, one who conquers through conflict the concupiscence you wrongly praise will not possess the luxury you rightly blame. But how do we obey the Apostle John if we love the concupiscence of the flesh? You will answer: “That is not what I praise.” And what is it that John says is not from the Father? “Luxury,” you will say. But we will not be luxurious unless we love this concupiscence you praise. Hence when he said, *do not love the concupiscence of the flesh*, he did not want us to be luxurious. We are therefore forbidden to love this concupiscence of the flesh you praise when we are forbidden luxury; but this which we are forbidden to love is not from the Father. Therefore the concupiscence you praise is not from the Father. For two goods from the Father cannot fight each other, but continence and concupiscence fight each other. Answer which of these you would rather have be from the Father. I see your straits: you favor concupiscence but feel shame before continence. Let your shame conquer and thereby your error be conquered. Since, then, continence, which defeats the concupiscence of the flesh, is from the Father, receive from the Father the continence before which you rightly blushed, and conquer the concupiscence you praised with a perverse mouth.

**14. 65.** Indeed, you thought the pleasure of all the senses should be summoned to help, as though genital pleasure, even with so great an advocate, could not suffice alone unless a troop of companions came to its aid. For you think “it follows that we confess the senses of sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch were bestowed on us not by God but by the devil, if we grant that the concupiscence of the flesh which we resist through continence was not in paradise before sin, and arose from the first sin which the devil persuaded the first man to commit.” Thus you do not know, or pretend not to know, that in any bodily sense keenness, usefulness, or necessity of sensation is one thing, and lust for sensation another. Keenness of sensation is that by which one perceives more and another less what is true in bodily things themselves according to their measure and nature, and distinguishes it more or less from the false. Usefulness of sensation is that through which we provide for our body and the life we lead by approving or disapproving, taking or rejecting, seeking or avoiding something. Necessity of sensation is when even things we do not wish are thrust upon our senses. But lust for sensation, of which we now speak, is what impels us to sensation by an appetite for carnal pleasure, whether we consent in mind or resist. This is contrary to love of wisdom, hostile to virtues. As regards the part of this evil by which the sexes mingle, marriage uses it well when spouses procreate children through it and do nothing for its sake. If you had wished or been able to distinguish it from keenness, usefulness, and necessity of sensation, you would see how superfluously you said so much. For the Lord did not say, “One who looks at a woman,” but: *one who looks to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart*[^115]. See how briefly and clearly, if you are not obstinate, he distinguished the sense of sight from lust for sensation. The former God created in equipping the human body; the latter the devil sowed by persuading to sin.

**14. 66.** Let devout people therefore praise heaven, earth, and everything in them, and from all these praise God by contemplating beauty, not by the heat of lust. Thus the religious and the greedy praise gold's splendor differently: the former with piety in venerating the creator, the latter with lust to possess the creature. Certainly the mind is moved to a devout affection when hearing a divine song; yet even there, if the lust for hearing desires the sound, not the sense, it is disapproved. How much more if it delights in trivial or even shameful songs! The other three senses are more corporeal and in a manner coarser, not extending themselves outward but inwardly performing their proper function. Smell is discerned by smelling, flavor by tasting, and many things by touching. For hot and cold are not the same as smooth and rough, nor these the same as soft and hard; and what is called light or heavy differs from them all. In all these, when troublesome things are avoided—stenches, bitterness, heat, cold, and whatever is rough, hard, or heavy—it is provision for comfort, not lust for pleasure. As for the opposites which we take with comfort, if they contribute nothing to health or to avoiding the discomfort of pain or toil, though they are taken with some delight when present, they must not be desired with any lust when absent. If they are desired, it is not good. For such an appetite must be tamed and healed in any matter. Who, however great a chastiser of carnal concupiscence, entering a place scented with incense, could prevent it from smelling sweet to him unless he closed his nostrils or by intense attention withdrew from bodily senses? But when he leaves, will he desire it at home or wherever he goes? Or, if he desires it, ought he to satisfy this lust rather than bridle it and desire in spirit against the desiring flesh until he returns to the health in which he desires nothing of the kind? This is indeed a small thing, but *one who despises small things*, as it is written, *falls little by little* [^116].

**14. 67.** We need the support of food. If what is taken by mouth is not pleasant, it cannot be taken and is often rejected with nausea; dangerous aversions must be avoided. Therefore the weakness of our body needs not only food but also its flavor, not to satisfy lust but to preserve health. When nature in a manner demands the supplies it lacks, this is called not lust but hunger or thirst; when, however, need has been supplied and the love of eating troubles the mind, then it is lust, then it is evil, to which one must not yield but resist. Even the poet distinguished these two, hunger and love of eating. After the tossing of the sea, judging it enough for Aeneas's shipwrecked and wandering companions to take only as much food as their need for refreshment demanded, he said: *after hunger was removed by the feast, and the tables taken away* [^117]. But when Aeneas himself was welcomed as a guest by King Evander, deeming it more fitting to present a royal feast ampler than necessity required, he was not content to say, *after hunger was removed*, but added: *and the love of eating restrained* [^118]. How much more should we distinguish and know what the need for refreshment demands and what the lust for eating demands, whose task is to desire in spirit against the desiring flesh, to delight in God's law according to the inner man [^119], and to cloud the serenity of that delight with no lustful delights! For this love of eating must be restrained not by eating but by continence.

**14. 68.** Who of sober mind would not prefer, if it were possible, to take dry or liquid food without any biting carnal pleasure, just as we take this airy nourishment which by breathing in and out we draw in and pour out from the surrounding air? Though we take this nourishment through mouth and nostrils without ceasing, it has neither taste nor smell; yet we cannot live without it even for a very little time, as we can so long without food and drink. Its lack is felt only in distress, if mouth and nostrils are closed or, by the will's bidding, we briefly inhibit the function of the lungs for as long as the distress permits. By these, like bellows in alternating motion, we draw and return life-giving breaths. How much happier it would be if, at intervals as long as now or however much longer, we took food and drink without any alluring sweetness of flavor, thereby driving away such great distress or destruction! For if in the present life those who take nourishment temperately are called continent and sober and deservedly praised, and there are such people who take as much refreshment as nature requires or less, preferring, if they mistake the measure suited to need, to have taken less rather than more, how much more should we believe that in that dignity there was an honorable measure for taking nourishment by which the animal body was supplied with necessities and the natural measure was in no way exceeded, as we must believe the first human beings lived in paradise?

**14. 69.** Some expositors of the divine word, and not contemptible ones, rather think that they needed no food of this kind, so that the only pleasure or nourishment in paradise was that which delights and feeds the hearts of the wise. But I agree with those who, just as they understand the saying, *he made them male and female, and God blessed them, saying: increase and multiply and fill the earth*[^120], to refer to bodily and visible sex, so also understand what follows shortly afterward: *and God said: behold, I have given you every sown herb sowing seed that is upon all the earth, and every tree that has in itself the fruit of sown seed; it shall be food for you and for all the beasts of the earth, all the birds of heaven, and every serpent creeping upon the earth which has in itself a living soul; and every green herb for food*[^121]. They understand this only as meaning that both sexes bodily used these foods like the other animals, and had from this nourishment support suited and necessary to a body that, although immortal in a certain manner, was nevertheless animal, lest it be harmed by want; and from the tree of life, lest old age bring it to death. But on no account would I believe that in that place of such great happiness either the flesh desired against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, and they lived in that conflict without internal peace, or that the spirit did not resist carnal desires but carried out in shameful slavery whatever lust suggested from any quarter. It remains, therefore, that either there was no carnal concupiscence there, but such a manner of living that all necessities were fulfilled through appropriate functions of the members without any movement of lust—for because the earth is sown by farmers' hands moved by will, not lust, it does not fail to conceive its produce to be brought forth—or, lest we seem to offend too much those who defend bodily pleasure in any fashion, we should believe that lust of the carnal senses existed there so subject in every way to rational will that it was present only when through it provision was needed for bodily health or propagation of offspring. And it was present in such measure that it never brought the mind down from delight in heavenly thoughts, produced no superfluous or untimely movement, and was the means of nothing but what was useful, while nothing at all was done for its sake.

**14. 70.** But those who fight against it best feel how differently it now behaves. It intrudes upon people seeing or hearing something, though they see and hear for another purpose, so that, even if it gains no pleasure of touch from this, it may at least steal a pleasurable thought by a creeping movement amid necessary things that have nothing to do with it. Moreover, in thoughts themselves, even when nothing alluring lies before the eyes or sounds in the ears, what does it not try to revive, even things faded and dormant, through restless memories with shameful delight, and to interrupt chaste and holy intentions with a tumult of filthy intrusion? And when it comes to the use of necessary pleasure by which we restore our bodies, who can explain in words how it prevents us from perceiving the measure of necessity and, by dragging us toward whatever delightful things are present, conceals and passes beyond the very limit of caring for health? We think that what is enough is not enough, while gladly following its incitements, imagining that we are still attending to health when rather we attend to indigestion. For regrettable surfeit testifies what evil we have done; fearing it, we often eat less than suffices to remove hunger. Thus appetite does not know where necessity ends.

**14. 71.** This pleasure of eating and drinking is indeed tolerable, as we watch with all the attention we can, preferring that it should fall short rather than ever exceed the measure of sufficient nourishment. We fight against its concupiscence by fasting and taking food more sparingly, and use this evil well when through it we do only what conduces to health. I called this pleasure tolerable because its force is not so great as to tear us away and cast us down from thoughts pertaining to wisdom if we are lifted toward them with delight of mind. For often during meals we not only think about great matters but also discuss them, and between the very bites of food and swallows of drink we converse by hearing and speaking no less attentively, and receive with the utmost attention what we wish to learn or recall if it is read to us. But that pleasure for which you dispute with me so contentiously, even when approached with a good intention, that is, for propagating offspring, nevertheless in its actual operation allows whom to think of anything else—I do not say wisdom, but anything whatever? Are not the whole mind and body expended upon it, and its climax completed through a kind of submerging of the mind itself? But when it overcomes even spouses so that they unite from desire not for propagation but for carnal delight, which the Apostle testifies he spoke of by way of pardon, not command [^122], they emerge from it and, as though after that whirlpool, breathe again in the air of thought, so that what someone truly said follows: the close neighborhood of repentance and pleasure. What lover of spiritual good, then, even if married solely for offspring, would not prefer, if possible, to procreate children either without this pleasure or without so great a force of it? What holy spouses would prefer in this life, therefore, I think we should believe of that life of paradise, which was much better, if we can conceive nothing better about it.

**14. 72.** I beg you, let not the philosophy of the gentiles be more honorable than our Christian philosophy, which alone is true philosophy, since this name signifies the pursuit or love of wisdom. See what Cicero says in *the dialogue Hortensius*. These words should have pleased you more than those of Balbus speaking for the Stoics, which, although true, concerned the lower part of the human being, the body, and could not help you at all. See what he says for the mind's keenness against bodily pleasure. *Or*, he says, *should bodily pleasures be sought, which Plato truly and gravely called the enticements and baits of evils? For what destruction*, he says, *of health, what disfigurement of complexion and body, what shameful loss, what disgrace is not called forth and drawn out by pleasure? The greater its movement, the more hostile it is to philosophy. For great bodily pleasure cannot agree with thought. Who, while using that pleasure than which none can be greater, can attend with the mind, undertake reasoning, or think anything at all? And whose appetite is so bottomless that he would wish his senses to be moved day and night, without the slightest interval, as they are moved at the height of pleasure? Who endowed with a good mind would not prefer that nature had given us no pleasures at all?*[^123] These things were said by one who had believed nothing about the life of the first human beings, paradise's happiness, or the resurrection of bodies. For now, let us be ashamed before the true arguments of the impious, we who have learned in the true and holy philosophy of true piety that the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh [^124]. Cicero did not know how this came about, yet he did not favor the concupiscence of the flesh as you do; rather, he rebuked it vehemently, which you not only do not do but are vehemently angry with those who do. You try to praise both the concupiscences of spirit and flesh warring against each other, like someone timid in battle, as though fearing to have as an enemy whichever could conquer the other. Come, rather, do not be afraid, and praise the desire of the spirit against the concupiscence of the flesh, fighting the more fiercely the more chastely. But condemn without any fear, by that same law of the mind which it resists, the one resisting the law of the mind.

**14. 73.** The contemplation of beauty, even bodily beauty, whether visible, as in colors and shapes, or audible, as in songs and melodies—a contemplation only a rational animal can possess—is one thing; the stirring of lust, which must be bridled by reason, is another. For the Apostle John said that the concupiscence which desires against the spirit is not from the Father [^125]. No one can call it good except someone whose spirit does not love to desire against it. If it is not such in the movement and heat of the genitals, let the spirit not desire against it, lest by desiring against God's gift it be found ungrateful. Rather, let whatever it seeks be given it as to something from the Father; and if there is nothing to give, let this instead be asked of the Father, not that he remove or restrain, but that he fulfill the concupiscence he bestowed. If thinking this is foolishness, why do we compare it to wine and food and imagine we say something when we say, “Let neither drunkenness condemn wine, nor surfeit food, nor obscenity defame concupiscence,” when neither drunkenness nor surfeit nor any obscenity is committed if the concupiscence of the flesh is conquered by the spirit desiring against it? “Its excess is at fault,” you say. You do not perceive—what you could most easily have perceived had you wished to conquer it rather than me—that to prevent the evil of excess, one must resist the evil of concupiscence. There are therefore two evils: one we possess, the other we commit if we do not resist what we possess.

**14. 74.** We have already said that this is not an evil in beasts precisely because in them it does not desire against the spirit. For they do not have reason by which to subdue lusts through victory or wear them down through struggle. And who tells you that "imitating beasts is always sinful"? You labored with such verbosity to refute this proposition, which had not been put to you, that you collected many irrelevant observations about beasts that even the practice of medicine follows. But lest the concupiscence of the flesh be thought not to be an evil because it is a good in a beast, giving enjoyment to a nature in which there can be no desire for wisdom, it was said to be a good of the beast, whose spirit it delights without any resistance, but an evil of the human being, in whom it desires against the spirit.

**15. 75.** You have also summoned a crowd of philosophers to your aid, as though, if the natural cleverness of beasts cannot help your client, at least the errors of learned men might. But who does not see that, by recalling the names of learned men and their various schools, you have sought to parade your learning, when anyone who reads these things of yours can see that they have no bearing whatever on the question between us? For who can hear you mention "Thales of Miletus, one of the seven sages, then Anaximander, Anaximenes, Anaxagoras, Xenophanes, Parmenides, Leucippus, Democritus, Empedocles, Heraclitus, Melissus, Plato, the Pythagoreans," each with his own doctrine about natural things—who, I say, can hear this without being intimidated by the very clamor of the accumulated names and schools, if he is uneducated, as most people are, and thinking you some great man for having managed to know these things? This is what you seek: for in all these things you have heaped together, you have said absolutely nothing relevant to the matter between us. Before saying them, you introduced them as follows: "All the philosophers who, though teaching something different in their schools, nevertheless worshiped idols with the common people, and who tried to think out something about natural causes, amid the many vanities of their opinions nevertheless seemed to touch some parts of the truth. Though vain amid their darkness, they can rightly be preferred to the doctrine against which we are arguing." To prove this, you added the names of the natural philosophers that I too have just mentioned, together with their opinions about natural causes; yet you either would not or could not mention them all. In this matter you deceived the unlearned, as no learned person doubts. For you had undertaken to demonstrate that "all philosophers who tried to think out something about natural causes can rightly be preferred to the doctrine against which you were arguing." Why, then, to say nothing of many others, when you mentioned Anaximenes and his disciple Anaxagoras, did you keep silent about his other disciple, Diogenes, who differed from both his teacher and his fellow disciple in his opinion about the nature of things and established a doctrine of his own? What if he held some view that made him unworthy of being preferred to us, to whom you said all those who philosophized about nature should be preferred? And as though to prove this, you foolishly puffed yourself up by recounting philosophers' names and doctrines to no purpose. See, you passed over one whom you should have noticed nearby, whether through his teacher or through his fellow disciple, both of whom you named. Or did you perhaps fear that he would be taken for Diogenes the Cynic, or that the same name would remind readers of that man too, and they would recall that he was a better advocate of lust than you, since he was not ashamed to indulge it in public? Hence that school received its canine name. You, however, profess yourself a defender of lust, yet blush for your client, which ill becomes an advocate's loyalty and freedom.

**15. 76.** But tell me, please: if you were eager to prefer philosophers to us, why did you not rather mention those who discussed conduct most acutely—that part of philosophy they call ethics, which we call moral philosophy? This would have suited you especially, since you consider bodily pleasure a human good, although inferior to integrity of mind. But who does not see what you foresaw? You feared that, on the very question of pleasure over which we are debating, the more honorable philosophers would overwhelm you—those whom Cicero called, because of that very honor, the 'consular philosophers'—and especially the Stoics themselves, pleasure's bitterest enemies. You thought it fitting to introduce their testimony, spoken by Balbus in Cicero's dialogue: true testimony, certainly, but of no use to you at all. Wishing to conceal that they consider bodily pleasure no human good, you chose to mention no philosophers' names or doctrines on the moral question, although this was most relevant if anything was to be proved from philosophers. Against these men you would find no defense, not merely in Epicurus, who placed the whole human good in bodily pleasure, since you do not think as he did, but even in Dinomachus, whose doctrine pleased you. For he thought that pleasure should be joined to moral worth, so that pleasure, like moral worth, should be regarded as desirable for its own sake[^126]. Seeing, then, that this branch, in which conduct is discussed, was hostile to you, you were afraid to touch it. For you see how many philosophers, of what stature and what renown among the nations, are preferred to you precisely where this controversy chiefly lies. Especially Plato himself, whom Cicero does not hesitate to call almost the god of philosophers[^127]: you could not omit his name when you brought before us, or preferred to us, the philosophers' doctrines about nature rather than morals. Yet he is reported to have said, truly and gravely, that bodily pleasures are the enticements and baits of evils.

**15. 77.** Did you, however, think to mention what the philosophers you named believed about the human condition, since this too belongs to the question of nature, as the case required in some way? You certainly did not, and rightly so. For what had they learned, or what had they said, about the first man Adam and his wife, about their first transgression, about the serpent's cunning, about bodily nakedness without shame before sin and with shame immediately after sin? What, finally, had they heard like that saying of the apostle: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death, and so it passes into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^128]? Without these writings and this truth, what could they understand about this matter? Yet if you judged that nothing should be set down from the doctrines on the human condition of men estranged from our sacred writings—and you judged most rightly—how much less could you be helped by what you did set down: their views about the first principles of this visible world, over which there is no dispute between us? Rather, it is your mind that is overthrown by vain boasting, as though you had learned something great from the philosophers' books.

**15. 78.** Those who thought that this life, so full of deception and misery, could only have befallen us by divine judgment seem, however, to have approached the Christian faith with good reason, since they attributed justice to the Creator, by whom this world was made and is governed. How much better than you, then, and how much nearer the truth, were the views about the generation of human beings held by those whom Cicero mentions toward the end of his *dialogue Hortensius*, led and compelled, as it were, by the evidence of the facts themselves! For after saying much about human vanity and unhappiness that we ourselves see and lament, he says: *These errors and hardships of human* *life sometimes make it seem that those ancients perceived something, whether they were seers or interpreters of the divine mind in transmitting sacred rites and initiations, who said that we were born to pay the penalty for certain crimes committed in a former life. And that statement found in Aristotle seems true: that we are subjected to a punishment like that of those who once fell into the hands of Etruscan pirates and were killed with calculated cruelty. Their living bodies were bound as closely as possible to dead bodies, front to front; in the same way our minds are joined to our bodies, like the living bound to the dead*[^129]. Did not those who held these views see the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam and the power and justice of God far better than you, even though they did not see the grace granted through the Mediator for the deliverance of human beings? See, I have found something in those writings of pagan philosophers that may rightly be preferred to you. You prompted me to do so, though you could find nothing of the kind in them yourself; and you would not keep silent, thereby reminding me what I might find against you.

**16. 79.** What of the fact that you think the Apostle's testimony supports you when it is against you, and assert, without knowing what you are saying, that our members were shameful, although before sin they were naked and caused no shame to those whose members they were? I ought to have brought forward the testimony you yourself brought forward, where the Apostle says: *Much more, those parts of our body that seem weaker are necessary*[^130], and the rest that you yourself quoted on our behalf. But it is worth considering how you came to say this. You say: "Now, then, it is time to show, besides the testimony of nature, also by some authority of the law, that our members were so formed that some enjoyed modesty, others freedom. To establish this, let the Teacher of the Gentiles be cited, who writes to the Corinthians: *Our body is not one member, but many*[^131]." Then, after adding the Apostle's words in which he wonderfully explained the unity and harmony of the members, you say: "And because he had named only a few members of the whole body and, out of his own decency, did not wish to name the genital regions directly." Do you not refute yourself by these words of yours? So it was decent not to name directly what God had deigned to make rightly; and the herald was ashamed to mention what the Judge himself had not been ashamed to create. How could this be so unless we had made indecent by sinning what he had made decent by creating?

**16. 80.** You then add the Apostle's testimony on this matter, which you report as written thus: *For much more, those parts of our body that seem weaker are necessary; and those members of the body that we think less honorable we surround with greater honor; and our modest parts have greater decency, whereas our decent parts have no need of it. But God has tempered the body, giving greater honor to the part that lacked it, so that there may be no division in the body, but the members may have the same concern for one another*[^132]. After these words you exclaim as if victorious: "Here is a true interpreter of God's work! Here is a faithful herald of his wisdom! He says: *Our more modest parts* *are covered with greater decency*." You surely thought your whole case should be tied to this single expression, because you read that the Apostle said: *our more modest parts.* But if you had read: *our indecent parts*, you certainly would not have dared to quote this testimony. For God would never have made anything indecent in the members of the human body, especially, what would be worse, before sin. Learn, then, what you do not know because you were unwilling to inquire carefully: the Apostle said *indecent*, but some translators, among whom, I think, is the one you read, said *more modest*, presumably out of modesty themselves, where he said *indecent*. This is proved by the words written in the very manuscript from which you transcribed these apostolic words. For where you put *more modest*, the Greek reads ἀσχήμονα. And in what follows, *have greater decency*, the Greek has εὐσχημοσύνην, which has been fully translated as *decency. Hence it is clear that those parts which* were called ἀσχήμονα are called *indecent* in Latin. Finally, in the phrase added afterward, *whereas our decent parts*, the Greek has εὐσχήμονα. Hence it is plain that those called ἀσχήμονα are *indecent*. Yet even without any consideration of the Greek words, this very fact should have alerted you: those parts that receive the greater decency of a covering are indecent, since those that do not need it are called decent. For what does *Whereas our decent parts have no need* mean except that those which do have need are indecent? Decency, then, is bestowed upon indecent parts when they are covered because the very feeling of rational nature is ashamed. Their decency and honor consist in a covering, all the more abundant the more indecent they are. The Apostle certainly would not have said this if he had been describing the kind of body human beings had when they were naked and were not ashamed.

**16. 81.** See, then, how shamelessly you say: "Human beings were naked at first because covering themselves belonged to the work of human ingenuity, which they did not yet possess." It follows that you believe they were unskilled before sin and became ingenious through sin. Then, after so many empty words, how elegantly and acutely, as you suppose, you conclude: "It was not, therefore, because they had sinned that they made their genitals either diabolical or indecent; rather, because they were frightened, they covered members that remained in their original decency!" To this I reply that the members certainly are not diabolical as regards their substance, form, and quality, which God created. But if those same members remained in their former decency, why did the Apostle call them *indecent*? It is good that you yourself admitted that those members were decent before; indeed, you could think otherwise only through a blasphemous opinion. The Apostle therefore called indecent what God had made decent. I ask the reason: if this did not happen through sin, how did it happen? Who dishonored God's decent works, so that the holy Apostle called them indecent? Was it their structure, in which the Creator's skill is found, or lust, in which the sinner's punishment is found? For even now what God makes there is decent; what is contracted through birth is indecent. Yet, so that there may be no divisions in the body, God has endowed our natural feeling with this: the members care for one another, and modesty covers what concupiscence has dishonored.

**16. 82.** But you say: "Why did Adam and his wife hide themselves when they heard the voice of God walking in paradise, when those loincloths could have sufficed [^133], if they had blushed at the nakedness of their genitals?" What are you saying, because you cannot find anything to say? Do you not notice that, with trembling minds before the Lord, they sought more secluded hiding places? But the coverings they had put around their loins concealed, out of shame, the movement they felt there. If they were not ashamed when they were naked, it follows that they covered themselves because of shame. There is no doubt that shame concerns indecency. That is why it was said there: *They were naked and were not ashamed*[^134], to make it clear that shame caused them afterward to cover their private parts. Here, however, where they hid themselves among the trees of paradise, Adam answered: *I heard your voice as you walked in paradise, and I was afraid, because I am naked*[^135]. The former was manifest shame; the latter, a hidden conscience, whose inward transgression had brought about the manifest shame. Thus modesty caused the former, fear the latter; shameful concupiscence caused the former, a conscience deserving punishment the latter. He was plainly like a madman when he tried to escape, by hiding his body, from the one who saw the depths of his heart. And what does the Lord's saying mean: *Who told you that you are naked, unless you have eaten from the tree from which I commanded you not to eat?*[^136] What does nakedness revealed after tasting the forbidden food mean, except that sin had laid bare what grace had covered? Great indeed was God's grace there, where an earthly and animal body had no bestial lust. He who, clothed with grace, had nothing in his naked body to be ashamed of, when stripped of grace felt something he ought to cover.

**16. 83.** You say: "We must avoid the opinion that supposes the devil created anything either in the members of human beings or in the sensations of those members." Why do you cast your own empty aberrations at us? The devil created nothing that belongs to human nature; but by persuading it to sin he wounded what God had created good, so that through that wound, inflicted by the free choice of two human beings, the whole human race limped. Look: the misery of the human race surrounds your senses. *You are a human being; consider nothing human foreign to you*[^137]; and in sufferings you do not undergo, have compassion on those who do. Yet you yourself, however great the earthly happiness in which you excel, pass no day without an inward war, if indeed you try to fulfill what you profess. As for things you can no longer remember, look at little children: how many and how great are the evils they suffer, amid what vanities, torments, errors, and terrors they grow! Then, even when they are grown and serve God, error tempts them to deceive them; toil or pain tempts them to break them; lust tempts them to inflame them; grief tempts them to lay them low; pride tempts them to exalt them. And who could quickly explain all the things that make the yoke upon the children of Adam heavy? The evidence of this misery compelled pagan philosophers, who neither knew nor believed anything about the first man's sin, to say that we were born to pay the penalty for certain crimes committed in a former life, and that our minds were joined to corruptible bodies, like the living bound to the dead, in the punishment with which Etruscan pirates used to torment their captives [^138]. But the Apostle cuts away the opinion that individual souls are believed to be placed in different bodies according to the merits of a former life. What, then, remains, except that the cause of these evils is either God's injustice or powerlessness, or the punishment of the first and ancient sin? But since God is neither unjust nor powerless, there remains what you are unwilling, but compelled, to confess: there would not have been a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all [^139], unless the desert of original sin had preceded it.
