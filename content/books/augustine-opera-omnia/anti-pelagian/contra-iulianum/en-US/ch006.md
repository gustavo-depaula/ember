# Book 5

**1. 1.** The order itself requires that, since we have answered your first and second books, we next see what your third contains, and, insofar as the Lord helps us, answer your pestilential labors with our healing labors. We shall keep to our established method, passing over matters that involve no controversy relevant to the issue between us, lest those wishing to know these writings of ours expend more labor and time on the task of reading than on the benefit of learning. What need is there, then, for me to say anything against the usual empty statements you placed at the beginning of this book, "about the envy you boast of enduring for the truth, and about the small number of supposedly prudent people whom you rejoice to please"? This is the voice of all heretics, old and new, already soiled and worn out by its very familiarity. Yet necessity compels even your enormous pride to dress in such rags, stretching and swelling until, in its eagerness to display them, it tears them apart all the more disgracefully. Nor is it necessary repeatedly to refute your most slanderous insults, by which you seem to attack me alone by name, while, leaving their names unspoken, you either leap madly against so many great Catholic lights or stumble blindly against them. I think I have answered you on this point in the first two books in such a way that no one should require anything further.

**1. 2.** You exaggerate how "difficult is the knowledge of the holy Scriptures, suited to few learned people," namely, "that God may be proved the Creator of human beings, as of the universe, just, truthful, and merciful, and the one who heaps his gifts upon human beings, because," as you confess, "the one best reason for good studies is that God be honored." Yet you honor him in such a way that you deny him to be the liberator of little children through Christ Jesus, that is, the Savior. You say that they are washed in his baptism without obtaining salvation, as though they did not need Christ the physician, Julian having shrewdly examined the pulse of human origin and pronounced them healthy. How much better it would have been to have learned nothing at all than, through this supposed knowledge of the law, in which you proudly boast—not guided by God's law, but rather by your own vanity—to have arrived at this impious presumption, hostile to the Christian faith and to your own soul.

**1. 3.** "Your opinion," you say, "is so ugly and empty that it tries to attribute injustice to God, the creation of human beings to the devil, substance to sin, and conscience without knowledge to little children." I answer briefly: Our opinion is not ugly, because it proclaims him *beautiful in form beyond the sons of men*[^1] as the Savior of all human beings, and therefore of little children too. Nor is it empty, since it says not without reason, but because of a preceding sin, that *the human being was made like vanity, whose days pass like a shadow*[^2]. It attributes not injustice but rather equity to God, because even a little child is not unjustly afflicted by the many great evils we so often see. It attributes to the devil not the creation of human beings but the corruption of human origin; to sin not substance but an act in the first human beings and contagion in their descendants; nor conscience without knowledge to little children, who have neither knowledge nor conscience. But the man in whom all sinned knew what he had done, and each individual contracted evil from him.

**1. 4.** You prepare in advance the multitude of unlearned people, whom you call "simple people who, occupied with other business, have acquired no learning, but have taken care to come to Christ's Church by faith alone, lest they easily be frightened by obscure questions. Believing God to be the true Creator of human beings, they should also hold without hesitation that he is merciful, truthful, and just; and preserving this judgment about that Trinity, they should embrace and praise whatever they hear that accords with this opinion. No force of argument should uproot it from them, but they should detest every authority and every fellowship striving to persuade them otherwise." If you consider these very words of yours, you make them most steadfast against you. For the Christian multitude too detests your novelty—the multitude from whose supposedly ignorant judgment you appeal to your few, whom you wish to appear most prudent and learned—for no other reason than that they think of God both as the Creator of human beings and as most just, and see such great torments in their little children. Under God, the best and most just Creator, his image at that age would certainly not suffer such great evils if there were no original sin. Suppose one of them, carrying his little son, approached you without any hostile clamor, in a place where no one could hear, and said: With the mind, understanding, and reason in which I was made in God's image, I so love God's kingdom that I judge it a great punishment for a human being never to be able to enter it. Would you then, no member of the unlearned crowd but a lover of that kingdom among the few most prudent people, surely all the more ardent because the most fervent fellowship of the few inflames you toward it and the colder multitude does not make you indifferent to it, answer that man: For God's image never to be able to enter God's kingdom is not merely no great punishment but no punishment at all? I think you would not dare to say this even to a single man whose violence or testimony you did not fear. Thus, whatever your answer, or if you remained silent—as modesty, if not Christian then certainly human, would rather require of you—he would bring his little child before your eyes, saying: God is just; what evil is it that bars this innocent image of his from entering his kingdom, if not the sin that entered the world through one man [^3]? I think no wisdom will be at hand by which you can seem to yourself more learned than that unlearned man; but if impudence leaves you, you will remain more speechless than that speechless infant.

**2. 5.** But now that the preface is finished, in which you dismissed the unlearned with a warning and prepared for yourself the most learned ears of a few, let us see what argument you undertake. Some sufficiently clever thought has come into your mind that escaped you in your second book, when you said many things about the private members, which after sin were covered with fig leaves as rational nature felt shame. You tried in vain to refute what I had said: "Why, then, shame concerning those members after sin, unless an indecent movement arose there?" [^4]. What, then, did you devise that so pleased you that, even after finishing the volume in which you handled this question with such prolonged verbosity, you thought it should not be omitted? You say that it is written: *and they made themselves garments*[^5], and mention that another translation has *perizomata*. You assert that *garments* "can be understood as clothing for the whole body, pertaining to the duty of modesty." Here I wonder how the translator you read, unless he was a Pelagian, could have translated the Greek *perizomata* as *garments*. Yet even here, if modesty is present, to whose duty you say garments belong, you will never try to persuade us that the first human beings learned these duties of modesty from sin as their teacher, and that before then innocence and impudence lived fittingly in them like companions and friends. For when they were naked and unashamed, according to your argument they were shameless and alien to the natural sense of modesty. But evidently they were corrected of this perversity by sinning, and the perverse disposition of transgression became their teacher of modesty. Wickedness made them modest, whom righteousness made shameless! Rather, this opinion of yours is so indecently shameless and so hideously naked that, however many leaves of words you sew together, you cannot cover it.

**2. 6.** You mock me for having learned from painters that Adam and his wife covered their private parts, and you bid me hear that oft-quoted line of Horace: *painters and poets* *have always had an equal freedom to dare anything*[^6]. But I learned not from a painter of empty figures, but from the writer of the divine Scriptures, that those first human beings were naked before they sinned and were not ashamed. Far be it that such great innocence in them should have been so shameless; rather, what could cause shame was not yet present. They sinned, noticed, blushed, covered themselves [^7]; and you still cry: "They felt nothing indecent and new there." This utterly incredible shamelessness of yours was taught you not, of course, by any apostle or prophet—far be it from me to say so—but not even by a painter or poet. For those very people who, as it seems elegantly said, *have always had equal freedom* *to dare anything* would be ashamed to invent for ridicule something such as you are not ashamed to argue should be believed. No painter would dare paint, no poet sing of two qualities dwelling together, one the best, the other the worst—innocence and impudence—as though agreeing and harmonious with one another. None would so despair of human judgment as to believe he had equal freedom to dare even this, but rather insane vanity.

**2. 7.** As for your statement, "If preference is given to the translation that used *perizomata*, that is, loincloths, I will say that their sides rather than their thighs were covered," first I grieve that you so abuse the ignorance of those who do not know Greek that you show no respect for the judgment of those who do. But it happens conveniently that Latin usage has adopted as a word of its own language the very *perizomata* read in Greek manuscripts. Therefore, when you asserted that a *perizoma* could cover not the thighs but the sides, I think you laughed at yourself too. For who, learned or unlearned, does not know what parts of the body *perizomata* cover? This name for a covering commonly appears, with valuations, even in women's dowries, and it is tied on only by a belt encircling the loins. Ask and learn what I do not believe you do not know. Yet even if you do not know, I do not suppose that you wish to pervert not human speech but human clothing so far as to try to raise *perizomata* over the shoulders, or to claim that those people's sides were covered by them in such a way that their genitals and all their loins and thighs remained naked. Why does it help you, rather than me, from whatever upper part of the body the lower parts were veiled, where the law in the members fighting the law of the mind [^8] was felt in the experience of both and aroused by each one's sight of the other, shaming the perversity of those disobedient people by the novelty of its own disobedience? The more turbulently it moved, the more modestly would they act if the flesh whose sight stimulated it were covered somewhat more extensively. Whether, then, the coverings hung down from the loins or the sides, the private parts were covered. They would not have been shameful had not the law of sin spitefully fought against the law of the mind. But where the matter is clear, we should not add our own meaning to the meaning of divine Scripture: this is done not through human ignorance but through perverse presumption. The term *perizomata* clearly enough expresses what parts of their bodies Adam and his wife, who before sin were naked and unashamed, clothed as soon as they sinned. We see what they covered: it is excessive foolishness still to ask, excessive impudence still to deny, what they felt. For you too, though contradicting most obstinately, so fully judged that nothing else would occur to human minds except that those people, blushing at the movement of concupiscence, wished to cover their genitals, that you tried to raise the *perizomata* to their sides, either covering the side where you contend the sinners felt nothing evil or shamefully baring what you agree should especially be covered.

**3. 8.** You insert other words from my book, where I said that "the flesh's disobedience was most fittingly repaid to the disobedient human being, because it was unjust that his servant, that is, his body, should obey one who had not obeyed his Lord" [^9]. You try to show that "this disobedience of the flesh is rather praiseworthy if it is the punishment of sin." As though it were some person knowingly afflicting the sinner, you adorn it "as the avenger of crime, and in this God's minister," with the theatrical grandeur of your language, as though it were some great good. You fail to consider that in this way you could praise evil angels, who are certainly nothing but transgressors and impious, yet through whom, holy Scripture testifies, God inflicts deserved punishments upon sinners, saying: *he sent upon them the wrath of his indignation, indignation and wrath and tribulation, a sending through evil angels*[^10]. Praise these too; praise their prince Satan, for he too was the avenger of sin when the Apostle handed a man over to him for the destruction of the flesh [^11]. You are indeed very eloquent against Christ's grace and fit to deliver a panegyric to Satan and his angels, through whom God is the judge and avenger of many people's sins, repaying them according to their works, making those very wicked and damnable spirits into punishments for those to be punished, since he makes good and just use of both good and evil. Proclaim, then, the praises of the most unjust powers, because through them evils are repaid to the evil, you who praise the flesh's concupiscence because its disobedience was repaid to the sinner's disobedience. Praise the unjust King Saul, since he too was a punishment for sinners, as the Lord says: *I gave you a king in my anger*[^12]. Praise the demon that king suffered [^13], for this too was the sinner's punishment. Praise the blindness of heart that *befell Israel in part.* Nor is the reason left unspoken: *until the fullness*, he says, *of the gentiles should enter*[^14]. Unless perhaps you will deny that this too is a punishment, which, if you loved inward light, you would proclaim not merely a punishment but a very great one. Yet that blindness was in the Jews a great evil of unbelief and a great cause of sin, leading them to kill Christ. If you deny that this blindness was a punishment, you will show, even without confessing it, that you suffer a similar one. But if you contend that it was indeed a punishment, yet not a punishment of sin, you at least admit that one and the same thing can be both sin and punishment. If, however, it is not sin's punishment, it is certainly an unjust punishment, and you make God unjust, because it is inflicted on an innocent person at his command or permission, or weak, because he does not avert it. But if you concede that it is also a punishment of sin, lest by refusing this concession you yourself appear blind in heart, see now what you refused to see: the question you raised has been resolved. Just as the devil, his angels, and evil kings are not only sinners themselves but through God's justice also become punishments for sins, yet are not thereby worthy of praise because through them a just punishment is inflicted on those who deserve it, so the law in the members fighting against the law of the mind does not itself act justly because it becomes the just punishment of one who acted unjustly. And just as blindness of heart, which God the illuminator alone removes, is both a sin, by which one disbelieves in God, and a punishment of sin, by which a proud heart is deservedly chastised, and a cause of sin, when some evil is committed through a blind heart's error, so the flesh's concupiscence, against which the good spirit desires, is both sin, because in it there is disobedience against the mind's rule, and the punishment of sin, because it was repaid to the disobedient person's deserts, and the cause of sin, through the failure of one who consents or the contagion of one who is born.

**3. 9.** Accordingly, everything you said in support of this blind and thoughtless opinion of yours, by which you thought the flesh's concupiscence should not merely escape blame but even receive praise precisely because we called it the punishment of sin, has most certainly come to nothing, however lengthy the discussion in which you lingered over it. For you said: "If lust is the punishment of sin, chastity must be cast aside, lest chastity, rebelling against God, be said to weaken the sentence he inflicted," and other such things that you weave together as consequences of, and connected with, this folly. With the very same words, and a most similar error, one can say of blindness of heart: If blindness of heart is the punishment of sin, teaching must be cast aside, lest the mind's enlightenment, rebelling against God, be said to weaken the sentence he inflicted. If it is utterly absurd to say this, although blindness of heart is a punishment of sin, what you yourself said is likewise utterly absurd, although lust, that is, the flesh's disobedience, is a punishment of sin. For knowledge should resist blindness of heart, and continence should resist lust; but patience should endure the punishment that is neither error nor lust. Therefore, when by God's gift one lives from true faith, God himself is present to enlighten the mind, overcome concupiscence, and endure hardship. All this is done rightly when it is done for his sake, that is, when he himself is loved freely, a love we cannot have except from him. Otherwise, when a person is greatly pleased with himself and trusts his own virtue, if he is handed over to the desires of his pride, this evil increases all the more as his other desires yield to him and he, supposedly praiseworthy, suppresses them by gratifying this one.

**3. 10.** Therefore, put aside your eagerness to win and carefully attend to what you say you read in other little works of mine, and vainly tried to refute: "There are some sins that are also punishments of sins" [^15]. You will find this most true according to what has been discussed about blindness of heart. What, I ask you, did you accomplish by quoting the apostolic testimony with which I proved what you read in another discussion of mine, since he wrote of certain people: "God handed them over to a reprobate mind, to do what is unfitting"? You wished this to appear hyperbole, which occurs when a speaker exceeds the truth of things to move the hearers' minds. Do not, then, disdain to show where the Apostle did this. "When he inveighed against the crimes of the impious," you say, "he aggravated them with the names of punishments, and, showing how much their shamefulness horrified his heart, the dwelling of every virtue, he said such people appeared to him not so much guilty as condemned." Rather, as he himself speaks, not as you pretend he speaks, he showed them both condemned and guilty, guilty not only of the past deeds for which they were condemned, but also through that in which they were condemned. For he shows them guilty where he says: *they worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever. Amen*[^16]. Then he shows them condemned for this guilt, for he continues: *because of this God handed them over to passions of dishonor*[^17]. You hear *because of this*, and vainly ask "how *God is understood to hand over*," laboring greatly to show "that he hands over by abandoning." But however he hands over, because of this he handed them over, because of this he abandoned them. You see what followed his handing them over, of whatever kind and however you understand it. For the Apostle took care to say how great a punishment it is to be handed over by God to passions of dishonor, whether by abandonment or in any other explicable or inexplicable way in which the supremely good and ineffably just one does these things. *For their women*, he says, *changed natural intercourse into that which is contrary to nature. Likewise the males too, leaving natural intercourse with the female, burned in their desire for one another, males with males committing shamefulness and receiving in themselves the mutual recompense of their error that was fitting*[^18]. What could be more evident, clearer, or more explicit? He says they received a mutual recompense, certainly condemned to do such great evils; yet this condemnation is also guilt, by which they are more gravely entangled. Thus these are both sins and punishments of preceding sins. More remarkably, he even says it was fitting for them to receive this mutual recompense. The Apostle's earlier words, which you yourself quoted, stand in the same way: *they changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man, of birds, four-footed beasts, and creeping things. Therefore God handed them over, in the desire of their heart, to uncleanness*[^19], and what follows. Here too you certainly see the cause for which they were handed over shown without any ambiguity. For he stated what evil they had previously done and added: *therefore God handed them over to the desires of their heart*[^20]. Accordingly, this is the punishment of a preceding sin, and yet it is itself also sin, which he explains in the following words.

**3. 11.** But arguing against this, you think you have resolved the question because the Apostle said they were handed over to their own desires. "For they were already burning," you say, "with desires for shameful deeds." And you add: "How, then, should they be thought to have fallen into such deeds through the power of God who handed them over?" What more, I ask you, was done, or why would he say, *God handed them over to the desires of their heart*, if they were already in some manner possessed by their heart's evil desires? Does it follow that, if someone has evil desires in his heart, he already consents to them so as to commit those evils? Accordingly, having evil desires of the heart is one thing, being handed over to them another, so that by consenting to them he is possessed by them, which happens when he is handed over to them by divine judgment. Otherwise it was vainly said: *do not go after your desires*[^21], if one is already guilty because one feels them in tumult, striving to draw one toward evil, yet does not follow them if not handed over to them, waging glorious battles against them if one lives in grace. What do you think of the person who observes what is written: *if you grant your soul its desires*—what else are these but its evil desires?—*you will become a joy to your enemies and those who envy you*[^22]*?* Is that person already guilty of having such desires of the soul, which he must not grant it lest he become a joy to the devil and his angels, who are our enemies and envy us?

**3. 12.** Therefore, when a person is said to be handed over to his desires, he becomes guilty because, abandoned by God, he yields and consents to them: he is overcome, captured, dragged away, possessed. *For whatever has overcome someone, to it he is also assigned as a slave*[^23], and his subsequent sin becomes the punishment of a preceding sin. Is it not both sin and punishment of sin where we read: *for the Lord mingled in them a spirit of error, and they led Egypt astray in all its works, as a drunkard is led astray*[^24]? Is it not sin and punishment of sin where the prophet says to God: *why have you made us stray, Lord, from your way; why have you blunted our hearts so that we should not fear you?*[^25] Is it not sin and punishment of sin where again God is told: *behold, you were angry, and we sinned; therefore we went astray, and we all became like the unclean*[^26]? Is it not sin and punishment of sin where we read of the nations defeated by Joshua son of Nun that it came about through the Lord that their hearts were strengthened to meet Israel in battle, so that they might be exterminated[^27]? Is it not sin and punishment of sin that King Rehoboam did not listen to the people who advised him well, because, as Scripture says, *the turn of events was from the Lord, to establish his word, which he spoke about him by the prophet's hand*[^28]? Is it not sin and punishment of sin in what is written, that Amaziah king of Judah refused to listen to Joash king of Israel, who advised him well not to proceed to battle? For we read: *and Amaziah did not listen, because it was from God that he should be handed over into their hands, since they sought the god of Edom*[^29]. We can recall many other passages in which it is plainly apparent that perversity of heart arises by God's hidden judgment, so that the truth spoken is not heard, sin follows, and that sin itself is also the punishment of a preceding sin. For to believe a lie and not believe the truth is certainly sin. Yet it comes from that blindness of heart which is shown to be also a punishment of sin by God's hidden but just judgment. Such too is what the Apostle writes to the Thessalonians: *because they did not receive the love of truth so as to be saved, therefore God will send them an activity of error, so that they believe a lie*[^30]. See, the punishment of sin is sin. Both are clear; it is said briefly, said plainly, said by the man whose other words you vainly tried to twist toward your opinion.

**3. 13.** What do you mean by saying: "When they are said to have been handed over to their desires, they should be understood as abandoned through divine patience, not driven into sins through divine power"? As though the same Apostle had not joined these two, patience and power, when he said: *but if God, wishing to show his wrath and make known his power, endured with great patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction*[^31]. Which of these do you say is meant by what is written: *and if a prophet errs and speaks, I the Lord have deceived that prophet, and I shall stretch out my hand against him and exterminate him from the midst of my people Israel*[^32]? Is it patience or power? Choose either, or acknowledge both; still you see that the false prophet's action is sin and punishment of sin. Or will you say here too that *I the Lord have deceived that prophet* must be understood as: I abandoned him, so that, deceived according to his deserts, he would go astray? Have it as you will; nevertheless he was punished for sin in such a way that he sinned by prophesying falsely. But consider what the prophet Micaiah saw: *the Lord seated on his throne, and the whole army of heaven standing around him on his right and his left. And the Lord said: 'Who will deceive Ahab king of Israel, so that he will go up and fall at Ramoth-gilead?' And one said this, and another that. And a spirit came forward and stood before the Lord and said: 'I will deceive him.' And the Lord said to him: 'By what means?' And he said: 'I will go out and be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets.' And he said: 'You will deceive him and prevail; go out and do so'*[^33]. What will you say to this? Certainly the king himself sinned by believing false prophets. But this was itself also sin's punishment, God judging and God sending an evil angel, so that we might understand more plainly how it is said in the psalm that *he sent the wrath of his indignation through evil angels*[^34]. But was this through error, or through judging or doing anything unjustly or rashly? Far from it. Yet it was not for nothing that he was told: *your judgments are a great abyss*[^35]. Not for nothing does the Apostle exclaim: *O depth of the riches of God's wisdom and knowledge! How unsearchable are his judgments and untraceable his ways! For who has known the Lord's mind? Or who has been his counselor? Or who has first given to him, that he should be repaid?*[^36] He chooses no one worthy, but by choosing makes him worthy; yet he punishes no one who does not deserve it.

**4. 14.** "The Apostle says," you tell us, "*God's goodness leads you to repentance*." True, certainly; but he leads the one he predestined, although that person, according to his hardness and unrepentant heart, as far as he himself is concerned, stores up wrath for himself on the day of wrath and revelation of God's just judgment, who will repay each according to his works [^37]. For however much patience he shows, who repents unless he himself grants it? Or have you forgotten what that same teacher says: *lest perhaps God grant them repentance to acknowledge the truth, and they recover their senses from the devil's snares*[^38]? But his judgments are a great abyss. Certainly, if we allow those over whom we have authority to commit crimes before our eyes, we shall be guilty with them. Yet how innumerable are the things he permits to be done before his eyes, which he certainly would in no way permit if he had not willed it! Nevertheless he is just and good. And in showing patience he gives room for repentance, not wishing anyone to perish [^39]; *the Lord knows those who are his*[^40], and *he works all things together* *for* *good, but for those called according to his purpose.* For not all who are called *are called according to his purpose. For many are called, but few* are *chosen*[^41]. Thus those chosen are those called according to his purpose. Hence he says elsewhere: *according to the power of God, who saves us and calls us with his holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before eternal ages*[^42]. Finally, here too, when he had said, *he works all things together for good for those called according to his purpose*, he immediately added: *for those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, so that he might be the firstborn among many brothers; and those whom he predestined, he also called; those whom he called, he also justified; and those whom he justified, he also glorified*[^43]. These are those called according to his purpose. They therefore are the chosen, and this *before the foundation of the world*[^44], by him who calls things that are not as though they were [^45]. But they are chosen through the election of grace. Hence the same teacher says of Israel too: *a remnant has come to be through the election of grace*[^46]. And lest they be thought chosen before the world's foundation because of foreknown works, he continued and added: *but if by grace, no longer from works; otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^47]. From this number of the chosen and predestined, even those who have led the worst lives are brought to repentance by God's kindness; through his patience they were not removed from this life in the very perpetration of their crimes, so that it may be shown both to them and to their other fellow heirs from how deep an evil God's grace can deliver. None of these perishes, at whatever age he dies. For far be it that one predestined to life should be permitted to end this life without the Mediator's sacrament. On their account the Lord says: *this is the will of the Father who sent me, that of all he has given me I should lose nothing*[^48]. But the other mortals, who are not in this number and who, though from the same mass as these, have been made vessels of wrath, are born for these people's benefit. For God creates none of them rashly or by chance, nor is he ignorant of what good to work from them: he works good in the very fact that he creates human nature in them and adorns the order of this present world with them. He brings none of these to saving and spiritual repentance, by which a human being is reconciled to God in Christ, whether he shows them greater or equal patience. Therefore, although all, from the same mass of perdition and condemnation, according to the hardness of their hearts and their unrepentant heart, as far as they themselves are concerned, store up wrath for themselves on the day of wrath, when each is repaid according to his works, God nevertheless brings some from it to repentance through merciful goodness and does not bring others according to his just judgment. For he has the power to lead and draw, as the Lord himself says: *no one comes to me unless the Father who sent me draws him*[^49]. But did he bring the sacrilegious and impious King Ahab to repentance, or at least show patience and forbearance to him after he had been seduced and deceived by a lying spirit? Was not the purpose for which he had been deceived immediately fulfilled in him when death carried him off [^50]? Who would say he did not sin by believing a lying spirit? Who would say this sin was not sin's punishment, coming from the judgment of God, by whom he reads that the lying spirit was either sent or permitted? Who says any of these things except one who says what he pleases and refuses to hear what is true?

**4. 15.** Who could be so foolish that, on hearing what is sung in the psalm, *do not hand me over, Lord, through my desire to the sinner*[^51], he would say that this man prayed that God should not be patient toward him, if "God does not hand people over so that evils are done except by showing patient goodness while they are done"? What is it that we say daily, *do not lead us into temptation*[^52], except a prayer that we not be handed over to our desires? For each is tempted, drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence [^53]. Or do we perhaps ask God that his goodness should not be patient toward us? Then we are not invoking his mercy but rather provoking his anger. What sane person would think this? Indeed, what madman would even say it? God therefore hands people over to passions of dishonor so that unfitting things are done, but he himself fittingly hands them over. Those very sins become both punishments for past sins and grounds for future punishments, as he handed Ahab over to the false prophets' lie, and Rehoboam to false counsel [^54]. He does these things in wonderful and ineffable ways, knowing how to work his just judgments not only in human bodies but also in their very hearts. He does not make evil wills, but uses them as he wills, since he cannot will anything unjustly. In mercy he hears; in anger he does not hear. Again, in mercy he does not hear; in anger he hears. In mercy he spares; in anger he does not spare. Again, in mercy he does not spare; in anger he spares. In all these things he remains good and just. But who is sufficient for these things [^55]? Certainly, what human being is sufficient to search out and investigate these judgments of his, weighed down by a corruptible body, even if he already has the pledge of the Holy Spirit?

**4. 16.** But you, an intelligent and sharp man, say that "lust is just and should be proclaimed with praise if, by not obeying him, it punished the man who did not obey God." If you understood wisely here, you would certainly see the injustice by which a person's lower part fights against the higher and better part; yet the unjust person was justly punished by his flesh's injustice, just as the unjust king was punished by an evil spirit's injustice. Or are you preparing to praise the evil spirit too? Come, do it; why delay? It suits you, an enemy of God's freely given goodness, to praise a lying spirit. You will have no trouble finding what to say: its praises are ready if you transfer to it the very words you set down in praise of lust, as though following from my statement: "For it was unjust that his servant, that is, his body, should obey one who had not obeyed his Lord" [^56]. Denying this and ridiculing it as false, you wished supposedly to show what absurdity followed, praising lust as the avenger of sin if this is so. Certainly you do not deny that the lying spirit was an avenger of injustice, since by deceiving the impious king it drew him to the death he deserved. See, here too I say: For it was unjust that one who had not believed the true God should not be deceived by a false spirit. Praise, then, the justice of this falsehood too, and say what you said in praise of lust: "Nothing can be deemed more praiseworthy than it, if it avenged the injustice of the offense, if it vindicated the injury to God, and if, so far from sharing in the sin, it assumed the avenger's duty." According to your most acute understanding, all these things are rightly said in praise of that unclean spirit too. Therefore, in a similar case, either grant your voice's praise to the lying spirit or take it away from rebellious lust.

**4. 17.** Why flee to the most obscure question about the soul? In paradise pride indeed began in the mind, and from it came consent to transgress the commandment, because of the serpent's words: *you will be like gods*[^57]. But the whole human being committed that sin. Then the flesh became sinful flesh, whose faults are healed only by the likeness of sinful flesh. Therefore, since soul and flesh are punished together unless what is born is cleansed by rebirth, certainly either both are derived corrupted from the human parent, or one is corrupted in the other as in a corrupted vessel, within which it is enclosed by the hidden justice of divine law. Which of these is true I prefer to learn rather than state, lest I dare to teach what I do not know. Yet I know this: the true account among them is whichever the true, ancient, Catholic faith, by which original sin is believed and affirmed, has not proved false. Let that faith not be denied; then what is hidden concerning the soul may be learned at leisure, or, like many other things in this life, remain unknown without harm to salvation. For in little children and adults alike we should care more by what help the soul is healed than by what desert it was corrupted. Yet if its corruption is denied, neither will it be healed.

**4. 18.** I could not discover why you said what you did when you quoted the Apostle's statement, *and their foolish heart was filled with darkness*[^58]. You added: "It should be noted that he calls foolishness the cause of all evils." It is not sufficiently established that the Apostle said this. But I do not dispute that; I ask rather why you said it. Is it perhaps because little children are not rightly called foolish, since they cannot yet share in wisdom, that you wish it believed that no evil is in them, which you regard as the consequence if foolishness is the cause of all evils? But even if a most subtle and refined discussion is needed to determine whether foolishness made the first human beings proud or pride made them foolish, as regards our present question who does not know that all people who become wise become so from being foolish? Unless perhaps some heralds of the Mediator, by his great and most unusual grace, could pass to wisdom not from foolishness but from infancy. If you contend that this can happen by nature without faith in the Mediator, you reveal your heresy's hidden poison. For it becomes clear that all this defense and praise of nature aims only to make Christ's death pointless [^59], whereas we say that faith in him, *working through love*[^60], helps even those foolish by nature. Some are born with such dullness of heart that they seem more like beasts than human beings; for this great foolishness, apparently natural to them, you who deny any original sin can assign no desert. Who does not daily find, on the evidence of human affairs, that a little child first understands nothing, then as he grows understands empty things, and afterward, if he belongs among the wise, understands right things, thus passing from infancy to wisdom through an intervening foolishness? Therefore, you see how the human nature lying in little children, from which by your praises you withhold the Savior as though it were healthy, brings forth foolishness's fruit before wisdom's, and you refuse to see the fault in its root; or, worse, you see it and deny it.

**5. 19.** Then, inserting other words of mine, you slanderously claim that I contradicted myself: after saying that "the disobedient human being was repaid with his body's disobedience as punishment," I immediately "named other bodily members, saying that they serve our will at its command" [^61]. I said this with the genitals, which I had called the body, certainly excepted. Thus the body serves the will in the movement of its other members, and the body does not serve the will in the movements of the genitals. My words therefore do not contradict each other, although they endure your contradiction, either through your failure to understand or your refusal to let others understand. For if a part of the body could not be called the body, the Apostle would not say: *the wife has no authority over her own body, but the husband does; likewise the husband has no authority over his own body, but the wife does*[^62]. Certainly he calls those very members the body by which sex is distinguished and intercourse performed. For who would say that a husband has no authority over his own body, if by these apostolic words you understood the whole body, consisting of all its members? Accordingly, following the Apostle I too called the genital member alone the body. Human understanding recognizes that it is moved not by the will, as the hand or foot is, but by lust, and laughs at you shamelessly raising clouds over obvious matters, so that necessity compels us to speak longer about shameful things and decency compels us to speak indirectly. But whoever reads my words, which you strive to refute, and sees your ambush against them understands what I called the body in that passage; that is enough for me.

**5. 20.** But you said I contradicted my own words, which anyone who rereads them after hearing you and remembers that the Apostle called the genitals the body will see to be utterly false. You, then, who marked out and attacked what you called conflicting statements of mine, explain how you remained consistent with yourself and did not contradict yourself when you first said: "When it comes to sowing children, the members created for this task serve wholly at the will's command, and unless prevented by weakness or excess, obey the mind's command"; but afterward thought "this sort of movement should be counted among the many things in our bodies that, according to their own order and hidden arrangement, require not the will's command but its consent." Here you did in some measure yield to plain truth, but you should have erased what you had said before. How, according to your earlier statement, do the members we are discussing "serve wholly at the will's command and obey the mind's command," if according to your later statement "like hunger, thirst, and digestion they require not the will's command but its consent"? You indeed labored greatly to find these things to say against yourself rather than me. But in such a case labor would not have been necessary if modesty had been present. What use is your saying that "you are ashamed and horror forbids you to speak of such things, but necessity compels you," when you were not ashamed to leave your opinion in writing, against which, troubled by plain truth, you yourself soon uttered another? Yet even this mention of your modesty is exceedingly immodest. Still, I like it because it speaks against you. For you are a man unashamed to praise lust who says he is ashamed to discuss lust's movements.

**5. 21.** How difficult would it have been to notice that after I said, "It is within our power to move the other members," I immediately added, "when we have our body healthy and free from impediments"? Sleep, when it overcomes us unwillingly, and weariness are certainly impediments that hinder the members' agility. Then, when you said that "the members do not move in the direction we wish if we wish them to move in a way their very structure does not permit," you failed to notice that I had first said, "to move for the works appropriate to them." Accordingly, if we wish to bend them where their nature does not permit, they do not obey for works inappropriate to them. Yet when we move them obediently by a voluntary movement, we do not need lust's help; and when we wish to stop moving them, we stop immediately, nor are they aroused against our will by lust's goads.

**5. 22.** Certainly when you say that "the genitals too obey the mind's command," you describe some new lust, or perhaps a very ancient one, such as could have existed even in paradise had no one sinned. But why argue with you about this, when you remove it too by your later words, saying that "these do not move at the mind's command, but rather await consent"? Yet this is no reason to compare that lust with hunger or our other discomforts. The fact that no one becomes hungry or thirsty or digests when he wills involves needs for refreshing or emptying the body, which must be met lest it suffer injury or die. But does the body suffer injury or die if consent is withheld from lust? Distinguish, then, the evils we bear with patience from those we restrain with continence. For the former too are evils we have been able to experience in the body of this death. But how great and tranquil a power we would have had, even over those movements by which food is taken in and digested, in the happiness of paradise—who could investigate it with certainty or explain it worthily? Far be it from us to believe that there was anything there to prick our senses, inwardly or outwardly, with pain, weary them with toil, confuse them with shame, burn them with heat, constrict them with cold, or offend them with horror.

**5. 23.** What of your thinking that this most beautiful servant of yours, whom I am ashamed to name continually even in blame, while you are not ashamed to praise her, "becomes more commendable because the other parts of the body also serve her, to arouse her more ardently: the eyes by looking with desire, and other members through kisses and embraces"? You found a way to subject human ears to her too and erected a most ancient, but certainly most glorious, title of hers, recalling what Cicero included in the account *Of His Plans*: *when drunken young men, roused also by the sound of flutes, as happens, were breaking down a chaste woman's door, Pythagoras is said to have advised the flute player to play a spondaic measure. When she did so, the slowness of the rhythms and gravity of the melody quieted their furious insolence*[^63]. You see, then, how much more appropriately I said that she is in a manner her own mistress, served by the other senses so that she may deign to rise to her work or rest from her agitation. I said this precisely because, as you yourself confessed, "she is consented to rather than commanded." For your own further statement that "she is aroused by other stimuli, or broken and calmed by melody" certainly would not hold if she obeyed the human will. As for women, whom you exempt from this movement, although they can submit to male concupiscence even when not experiencing their own, let Joseph be asked how great an assault from it they themselves endure, an assault resisted by the dignity and honor of chaste women [^64]. As a churchman you should certainly have been reminded by ecclesiastical music rather than Pythagorean of what David's harp accomplished in Saul, when he was troubled by an evil spirit and, as the holy man played the harp, gained relief from that distress [^65], lest you think the flesh's concupiscence is something good because it is sometimes restrained by musical sounds.

**6. 24.** You exclaim: "How fittingly Jeremiah, with the chorus of prophets and all the saints, would cry: *who will give my head water and my eyes a fountain of tears?*[^66]" to weep for the foolish people's sins, because Christ's Church expels teachers of the Pelagian error! If you wished to weep beneficially, you would weep because you are entangled in that error and wash yourself clean of that new plague with those tears. Do you not know, or have you forgotten, or do you refuse to consider, that the holy, one, Catholic Church was also signified by the name of paradise? Why, then, are you surprised to be sent outside this paradise, you who wish to introduce the law in our members fighting the law of the mind into that paradise from which the Lord sent us out, and to which we cannot return unless we conquer that law in this paradise? For if this concupiscence you defend does not fight the law of the mind, no saint wages any struggle against it. Yet you have confessed that the saints wage "glorious battles" against what you protect. It is therefore the very thing that fights the law of the mind in the body of this death, from which the Apostle said he was freed by God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord [^67]. Do you at last perceive with how great a fountain of tears the enemies of this grace should be mourned, and with what vigilance of pastoral care they should be avoided, lest they draw others to destruction with them? For by this novelty you "increase the depravity of the declining age," which is present in all heretics. You "are the ruin of morals," striving to overthrow the foundations of the very faith on which morals must be built. You are "modesty's destruction," unashamed to praise what modesty itself fights. This is rather what the Church, called a virgin, should hear so as to beware of you; this is what married women, consecrated virgins, and all Christian chastity should hear. For they do not, as you accuse them, "say with the Manichaeans that an inescapable evil is in their flesh," an evil those men falsely claim is substantial and coeternal with God. They do indeed say with the Apostle: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*[^68]; yet it is under the mind's power, by God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord, to be disciplined in the body of death, dissolved in the body's death, and healed in the body's resurrection and death's death. For they hold their holy profession not only in their clothing but in mind and body, by resisting the flesh's concupiscence, which can be done here, not by lacking it altogether, which cannot be done here. Let them therefore hear this, that until they lack it they may beware of you. For if you ask all the saints, as though two people desired to recite before a holy audience, whether they would rather hear a critic or a praiser of lust, what do you think the toil of the continent, the modesty of the married, and the chastity of all would answer concerning them? Would they reject criticism of lust from their ears and gladly hear its praise? We should not think shame has so utterly vanished from the world that this disgrace could occur except perhaps in an audience where, as you recited, master Caelestius or Pelagius presided with his disciples seated around him.

**7. 25.** From here you pass to other words of mine, where I said: "When those first human beings felt this movement in their flesh, indecent precisely because disobedient, and blushed at their nakedness, they covered those members with fig leaves, so that at least the choice of those who were ashamed might veil what was not moved at the choice of those who willed; and because they were ashamed of what they indecently desired, by covering it they would do what was decent" [^69]. After presenting these words of mine, you boast emptily that you have already demolished this in the discussion of your second book and in the earlier part of this third, to which I now reply. But because I called this movement of lust "indecent precisely because disobedient," you now wish me to be thought to have said that "it is subject neither to the body nor to the mind, but always untamed in its savage power." Yet I never called it a power but a fault. If it does not move by desiring, what does chastity fight against in it by restraining? Where are those "glorious battles" you confessed the saints wage against it? Thus, insofar as chastity is preserved by overcoming, suppressing, and bridling this lust and permitting it nothing unlawful, you say what I do. But you say it is good, not I, that which must be overcome, suppressed, and bridled lest it snatch us toward unlawful things for which it never ceases to gape. Let the chaste judge which of us speaks truly, listening not to your tongue but to their experience. Let the Apostle judge, saying: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*[^70].

**7. 26.** "But the Paternianists," you say, "also called Venustians, heretics [^71] like the Manichaeans, say that the devil made the human body from the loins to the feet, while God placed its upper parts upon it as upon a base. They add that nothing is required of human effort except to keep clean the soul, which they say dwells in the stomach and head; but if the pubic region is smeared with the filth of every shameful act, they say it is no concern of theirs. Thus always," you say, "to serve lust shamefully themselves, they attach to it a title of independent power." You assert that, through kinship with this opinion, I wrote in my book: "The choice of those ashamed veiled what was not moved at the choice of those willing," and that "lust, not serving the will, inflames the body in its own right." Can you escape truth's force by slanderously imposing partners in falsehood upon us? What I put in my book, to which I wish you had preferred to yield rather than resist, is far removed from these Paternianists or Venustians. For according to the Catholic faith I attribute the whole human being, namely his entire soul and entire body, to God the supreme and true Creator. But I say that the devil did not create human nature or any part of it, but corrupted it, and that we must fight against that very wound inflicted by the devil, which must be healed by God's aid, until we are completely freed from it. Nor can the whole soul by which the body lives be kept clean, as far as a person can be clean in this life, if it consents to the flesh's concupiscence in committing shameful acts and all sorts of uncleanness. So, as regards this slander of yours, have you anything to say against these things? If that is not enough, behold: I condemn and anathematize what you said the Paternianists or Venustians believe. I add the Manichaeans too: both, with the other heretics, I abhor, condemn, anathematize, and detest. What more do you ask? Strip yourself of slanders; wrestle with strength, not fraud. Answer where that comes from which, unless resisted, allows no chastity to be kept. Certainly it is not a nature and substance, as the Venustians and Manichaeans hold; if it is not a fault of nature either, what is it? It rises, I suppress it; it resists, I bridle it; it fights, I overcome it. In my whole soul and whole body I have the God of peace as Creator: who sowed this war within me? Resolve the question, Apostle, and answer: *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and thus it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^72]. But Julian refuses. Answer this too, blessed Apostle: *if anyone announces to you anything beyond what you received, let him be anathema*[^73].

**7. 27.** But "if I concede," as you say, "that the evil of lust is unconquered, I shall confess myself an advocate of shamefulness; if, however, I say that I called this a natural evil, but one that can be conquered, that is, avoided," you immediately dance in triumph over the other part of your opinion. "For human beings," you say, "can avoid every sin when they can conquer the evil of concupiscence. For if lust is a natural evil and is conquered by love of virtue, much more can all those faults be overcome that arise from the will alone." Your assertions have already been answered often and in many ways. For while we live here, where *the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh*[^74], however victorious we may be in this conflict, not offering our members to sin as weapons of iniquity by obeying its desires [^75], nevertheless—to say nothing of the bodily senses and the excesses of pleasure creeping into things we lawfully use—certainly in the very movements and affections of our thought, *if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^76]. Therefore you dance in vain over the other part of your opinion, unless perhaps with sacrilegious presumption you reject the judgment of the Apostle John. As for the matter now at issue, I too say lust is natural, because every human being is born with it; you say this far more strongly, asserting that the first human being was created with it. Likewise, that lust must be conquered and that, to conquer it, it must be resisted and fought, both I and you affirm, lest you hear from me what you yourself told me: "You confess yourself an advocate of shamefulness if you deny that lust must be conquered." It certainly is not conquered if no war is waged against it. Since, then, we both say that lust is natural and can be conquered, the question between us is whether what we conquer is good or evil. But see how absurd you are, wishing thus to overcome lust as an enemy, yet refusing to settle the question of its evil, so that if the devil does not conquer you through concupiscence's hostility, he may conquer you through the perversity of your opinion.

**7. 28.** Do you still not awaken to understand that what we fight with virtue is not our nature but its fault? For we conquer not good by good, but certainly evil by good. Consider with whom it conquers and with whom it is conquered. When lust conquers, the devil too has conquered; when lust is conquered, the devil too is conquered. The one whom lust conquers and by whom it is conquered is therefore its enemy; the one with whom it conquers and is conquered is its author. Open your eyes, I beg you, and see what is plain. There is no battle without evil. When a battle is waged, either good and evil fight, or evil and evil, or, if two goods fight each other, the battle itself is a great evil. When this happens in the body, so that its constituents—wet and dry, hot and cold—though contrary to each other, do not maintain peace and concord, diseases and illnesses arise. Who would dare say any of these is not good, since every creature of God is good, and in the hymn of the three young men cold and heat bless the Lord [^77]? These opposites nevertheless maintain concord for the preservation of things; but when they disagree in our body and fight one another, health is disturbed. All this, like death itself, descends through the propagation of that sin. No one would say that, if no one had sinned, we would have suffered these things in paradise's blessedness. But the qualities of bodily things, tempered by their opposites to keep us well, are one thing: though good in their various kinds, when they disagree they cause ill health. The soul's desires are another, called desires of the flesh because the soul desires according to the flesh when it desires in such a way that the spirit, that is, its better and higher part, ought to resist it. These faults require no physicians of bodies, but are healed by Christ's grace: first, so that they do not bind us in guilt; next, so that they do not conquer in conflict; finally, so that, wholly healed, none at all remain. Therefore, since desiring evil things is evil and desiring good things good, and this war does not cease as long as we live here, *when the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, who will free me from the body of this death except God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord?* We shudder at your teaching, so hostile to this grace.

**7. 29.** But you, most courageous man, if not the practitioner then certainly the exhorter and herald of nocturnal battles, say that "the opinion that in paradise the genitals could have served at the will's command is feeble and soft." To you, as a chaste man, the mind seems more effeminate the more power it has over the body. But look: let us not dispute with you about lust's absence or presence, nor offend the love we see you owe it. At least subject it to the will's command in that place of happiness. Remove from there the most evident battle that occurs when the mind resists its movement; remove the most shameful peace that occurs when the mind serves its domination. And certainly, since you do not see it as you are compelled to place it there, if not called back by reason then compelled by shame, confess the original fault in it as it now is. If we serve it, we perish; we resist it so as not to serve. See what you praise, unafraid of prompting us to say of you instead that you incite people to shameful deeds, lest they resist the concupiscence you commend as a natural good. What help is it that you appear to blame the excess of something whose movement you approve? For it exceeds the lawful limit when one yields to its movements. Yet it is evil even when one does not yield, because evil is resisted lest the good of chastity perish if this evil is not resisted. By calling it naturally good, you cunningly decree that it should always be consented to, lest a natural good be opposed by wicked resistance. Thus that opinion of yours, that a human being is without sin if he wishes, can very easily be true. For there is no way for him to do what is unlawful when whatever he desires is lawful, because what he naturally desires is good. If pleasures are present, then, let him enjoy them; if absent, let him delight in the very thoughts of them, as Epicurus thought, and he will be without sin, depriving himself of no good. Nor should he resist natural movements through the opinions of any teaching, but, as Hortensius says, *let him obey nature when, without a teacher, he perceives what nature desires*[^78]. For a nature that is good cannot desire what is evil, nor should any good be denied to what is good. Let everything good lust desires therefore be done, lest the one who resists good be evil himself.

**7. 30.** I do not say this, you will reply, and it is unjust for you to suspect I mean something other than what I say. Then do not do what you do not wish to suffer, and say that "we invite to sweet, furtive pleasures those to whom we recite the Apostle's words: *I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, good does not dwell*[^79]." For although here they do not perfect the good they will, namely not to desire, they nevertheless do good by not following their desires [^80]. If you think you teach chastity by saying, Do not be conquered by good, but conquer good with good, how much more do we teach it when we say: *do not be conquered by evil, but conquer evil with good*[^81]? See how unjust it is not to believe that we fight what we condemn, when you do not wish it believed that you want to enjoy what you praise. How can lust's enemies not be chaste if even its friends can be chaste? Therefore, in this work we refute in you your denial of original sin, your withholding of Jesus the Savior from little children, and your wish to introduce the law of sin fighting against the law of the mind even into paradise before sin. We do not wish to judge what we do not see in you or hear from you; we have no concern with what open praisers of lust do in secret.

**8. 31.** After presenting other words of mine, you thought you should refute the distinction I made concerning the first human beings' marriage and concupiscence, saying: "What they afterward did in propagation is the good of marriage; what they first covered in shame is the evil of concupiscence" [^82], by saying that "it is impossible for what is good not to share praise with that without which it cannot exist." Thus you wished one and the same praise to be common to marriage and lust. Attend briefly to how this supposedly definitive statement of yours collapses. First, the universe God created cannot exist without evils, yet evils cannot therefore share praise with goods. Next, if "it is impossible for what is good not to share praise with that without which it cannot exist," then certainly it is impossible for what is evil not to share blame with that without which it cannot exist. Let us therefore blame God's works as we blame evils, which cannot exist without them. For no evil exists except in some work of God, nor can it exist anywhere at all without it. Blame, then, to send you no farther, human members as you blame adulteries, which cannot exist without those members. If you do not do this, lest you appear manifestly insane even to yourself, then the good of marriage can indeed lack praise in common with lust, without which it cannot now exist, just as any evil can lack blame in common with God's work, without which it can never exist. And just as this definition of yours is false and empty, so is everything you have linked to it as though following from it.

**8. 32.** I have certainly never called "the pleasure of the flesh unconquerable," as you accuse me of habitually doing. We both say that it should and can be conquered, but you as a good opposed by another good, I as an evil opposed by good; you by one's own powers, I by the Savior's grace, so that it may be conquered not by another reprobate desire but by the love of God, which is poured into our hearts not by our own powers but *through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^83].

**8. 33.** You vainly recall "having shown something, with the Apostle's testimony, about those people's shame and their covered private parts." For you call "more modest" what he calls *dishonorable*; we have already discussed this as much as seemed sufficient [^84]. You therefore flee again in vain to Balbus and the philosophers' writings [^85], as though Balbus could make you eloquent when you cannot find what to say about the first human beings' shame. But if on some true judgments you yielded even to the philosophers' writings, you would not hear with a deaf heart their saying that pleasures are the enticements and bait of evils and that lust is a vicious part of the soul. As for Balbus's saying that the places of excretion in our bodies are removed from the senses, this is true because what we excrete does not attract our senses but offends them. Therefore the part through which it is expelled is naturally hidden by other parts projecting on either side, just as these were also when those people were naked and unashamed, who immediately after sin covered members that were not hidden but plainly in view. The more their sight was attracted by delight rather than offended by horror, and aroused your protégé, the more covering them belonged to the duty of modesty.

**8. 34.** Unless you are acting fraudulently, you did not understand what I said "about limping and arriving" [^86]. For I did not intend "arrival to mean the human being born from marital intercourse," as you suppose or pretend I meant. By arrival I meant the good marriage has at the completion of its duty, even if no one is born from it. Sowing belongs to the man, receiving to the woman; this is as far as spouses can go by their own activity. I certainly said that one cannot arrive at this without limping, that is, without lust. But the conception and birth of offspring belong to divine work, not human. Yet it is with this intention and will that marriage performs even the good belonging to its own work. But because the child itself is born to condemnation unless reborn, Christian marriages extend their purpose so far—not the end of their activity as though a journey on foot, but the end of their will—that they beget those who are to be reborn. For this reason their chastity is true, that is, pleasing to God. *For without faith it is impossible to please God*[^87].

**9. 35.** Next you come to where we argued from the Apostle's testimony: *that each may know how to possess his own vessel*, that is, his wife, *not in the sickness of desire, like the gentiles who do not know God*[^88]. Explaining this I said: "Marital intercourse, that is, lawful and honorable intercourse, was not forbidden, but the purpose of this act should be the will to have offspring, not the flesh's pleasure; and what cannot be done without lust should nevertheless be done so that it is not done for lust's sake" [^89]. There you exclaim: "*O depth of the riches of God's wisdom and knowledge!*[^90] Beyond the future recompense of works, he willed free choice to fulfill in large measure the pattern of judgment. For most justly," you say, "both the good and the evil person are entrusted to themselves, so that the good may enjoy himself and the evil may suffer himself." This exclamation of yours has no bearing on the matter by which you feel yourself pressed. Your cry does not lift the weight crushing you, while you stubbornly hold your impious teaching, according to which you are pleased to have even each good person entrusted to himself by divine judgment, so that he should have no need of God's grace, as though fit to direct himself. But far be it that this should be so. Those entrusted to themselves and directed by themselves are certainly not good, because they are not God's children. *For as many as are led by God's Spirit, these are God's children*[^91]. I think you recognize in this statement the apostolic teaching that overthrows yours.

**9. 36.** Yet you say something against yourself that I must not pass over in silence. Do you remember how long you argued against the clearest truth expressed through the Apostle, asserting that "nothing can possibly be both sin and punishment of sin"? What, then, is this now? Forgetting so much verbosity of your own, you praise the depth of the riches of God's wisdom and knowledge because "beyond the future recompense of works he willed free choice to fulfill in large measure the pattern of judgment. For most justly," as you define it, "the good and evil person are entrusted to themselves, so that the good may enjoy himself," certainly in good work, "and the evil may suffer himself," certainly in evil work. This is surely both sin for him, because he does evil, and punishment of sin, because he suffers his evil self. Thus free choice already fulfills in large measure the pattern of judgment, by which good things are repaid to the good and evils to the evil: through it, by acting rightly the good person enjoys himself, and by sinning the evil person suffers himself. You certainly see how, as you boastfully brandish your empty and blunt weapons, you have exposed the place where you could be struck, or rather struck yourself. Moreover, you boast that my statements contradict each other, because I said—not, as you slanderously claim, "that bodily intercourse was invented by the devil," since even if no one had sinned children would have been born only through intercourse between the sexes—but that "the flesh's disobedience, appearing in the flesh's desire against the spirit, occurred through the devil's wound." And again, because I said that "this law of sin fighting the law of the mind was inflicted by God as retribution and is therefore sin's punishment." You assert that these contradict each other, as though one and the same evil could not be imposed on sinners both through the devil's injustice and through God's justice, when even the devil himself is hostile to human beings through his own malice and is permitted by God's judgment to harm sinners. Nor do the divine utterances therefore contradict themselves because it is written, *God did not make death*[^92], and also, *life and death are from the Lord God*[^93]. For the devil, the human being's deceiver, is the cause of death, which God inflicted not as its first author but as sin's avenger. Yet you yourself resolved this question clearly enough by saying that the evil person was entrusted to himself, so that his being his own punishment is both divine judgment and free choice itself. These two do not contradict each other: that in his punishment he himself is the author and God the avenger.

**9. 37.** But you abuse slower minds. I do not wish to say that you too fail to understand, so that you cannot distinguish these two things and, through malicious cunning or dark blindness, confuse will and pleasure. Just as their very names sound the same to deafened ears, you trust you can persuade deafened hearts that the things themselves are one and the same. Hence you think, or wish others to think, my statements contradictory, as though I condemn what I earlier approved or embrace what I earlier rejected. Hear, then, my plain opinion, and understand, or let others understand, without casting the darkness of a cloudy discussion over the serenity of the purest truth. Just as it is good to make good use of evils, it is honorable to make good use of dishonorable things. Hence the Apostle called those very members *dishonorable*, not because of the beauty of God's work but because of lust's ugliness [^94]. Nor are the chaste compelled by necessity to shameful acts, because they resist dishonorable lust lest it drive them to commit dishonorable things, although without it they cannot honorably beget children. Thus in chaste spouses there is both will in the procreation of offspring and necessity in lust. For honorable procreation is achieved from something dishonorable when the chastity of intercourse does not love lust but tolerates it.

**9. 38.** You like to quote the opinions of secular authors by which you think yourself helped. Consider with an honorable heart, if you can, what the poet sang of Cato: *he is father to the city, he says, and husband to the city;* a worshiper of justice, guardian of rigid honor, good to all; into none of Cato's acts *did inborn pleasure creep and take a share for itself*[^95]. What sort of man Cato was, and whether true virtue and honor were praised in him, is another question. Yet to whatever end he directed his duties, he certainly did not beget children without pleasure; nevertheless, into none of Cato's acts did inborn pleasure creep and take a share for itself, because even what he did not do without pleasure, he did not do for pleasure's sake. Nor did he possess his vessel in the sickness of this desire, although he did not know God, if he was such as he is proclaimed. And you refuse to understand the Apostle's words: *that each of you may know how to possess his own vessel, not in the sickness of desire, like the gentiles who do not know God*[^96].

**9. 39.** You distinguish well between the lesser good of marriage and the greater good of continence, but you do not abandon your teaching, so hostile to grace. For you say "that the Lord honored the glory of continence by freedom of choice, saying: *whoever can receive it, let him receive it*[^97]," as though it were received not by God's gift but by freedom of choice. You suppress what he had said above: *not all receive this word, but those to whom it has been given*[^98]. See what you suppress and what you say. I think conscience pricks you, but the opinion rashly advanced, now needing defense by any means, conquers rightful fear by instilling perverse shame. Again and again you blame only lust's excess; you never cease praising lust itself. You do not notice, feel, or understand that what temperance is compelled to fight, lest it exceed necessity's limit, is evil.

**9. 40.** Yet you thought the Apostle's warning that each should possess his vessel not in the sickness of desire should be understood of fornication, not marriage. Thus you have removed all the honor of temperance from marital intercourse, so that no one should think he possesses his vessel in the sickness of desire, however frenziedly lustful he may become with his wife's genitals. For if you thought any measure should be maintained there, you could have criticized excess of concupiscence there too, saying that this is what the Apostle signified by the sickness of desire, instead of wickedly denying that each man's wife was called his own vessel. Peter the Apostle also uses that word on the same subject when he admonishes husbands to honor their wives as a weaker vessel and as fellow heirs of grace, adding: *and see that your prayers are not hindered*[^99]. Likewise his fellow Apostle prescribes times of prayer for marital restraint and grants by pardon, though with one's spouse, intercourse intended not for offspring but for pleasure [^100]. Let Christian marriages hear him, not you, who wish the concupiscence you defend never to be bridled in them, but whenever it stirs, to be satisfied in its greed and reign secure. Let Christ's faithful joined in marriage hear him, I say, and restrain themselves by agreement to be free for prayer. When they return to the same act because of their lack of self-restraint, let them know that for this too they must say to God: *forgive us our debts*[^101]. For what is said by so great a teacher by pardon, not command, is certainly forgiven, not commanded.

**10. 41.** But after inserting other words of mine, in which I commended the intention of truly devout, because Christian, spouses, who beget children in this age so that for the sake of the other age they may be reborn in Christ [^102], you recall that you already demolished this opinion of mine in your second book. Those who wish should read there what I answered you. For adultery must not be committed even with the intention of begetting those to be reborn, just as theft must not be committed even with the intention of feeding the holy poor. Yet the latter should be done, not by perpetrating theft but by making good use of the mammon of iniquity, so that they too may receive you into eternal dwellings [^103]. Likewise children should be begotten with the intention of reigning with them forever, not by committing adultery but by making good marital use of the evil of lust.

**10. 42.** Yet you praise your protégé elegantly, saying—and saying truly—"that nothing of that sort can be thought during intercourse." It is entirely so. What can be thought then, when the very mind by which one thinks is submerged in that carnal delight? Hence that man spoke excellently in discussing pleasure, whose words I have already quoted in the previous book: *its movement*, he says, *is most hostile to philosophy precisely when it is greatest. For great bodily pleasure cannot accord with thought. For who, when enjoying pleasure*, he asks, *than which none can be greater, can attend with his mind, reason, or think anything at all?*[^104]. You yourself therefore could not accuse what you praise more gravely than by confessing that in its onrush no one can think holy thoughts. Yet a religious mind making good use of this evil thinks, in order to endure lust in intercourse, what it cannot think while enduring it. So a person thinks of health in order to surrender himself to sleep, which he certainly cannot think about while sleeping. But sleep, when it occupies the members, does not make them disobedient to the will, because it removes the will itself from this kind of command by turning the soul to dream visions, in which even future things have often been shown. Thus, if there was an alternation of waking and sleeping in paradise, where there was no evil of concupiscence, the sleepers' dreams were as happy as the waking life.

**10. 43.** As you boast and vainly pour out foaming eloquence, "comparing parents to murderers of their children and asserting that they are the cause of their children's being born under condemnation," you are wholly borne aloft by the wings of your tongue, as though exulting and applauding. Amid this din you make for yourself you do not look to God. Why are these or similar things not said to the very Creator of human beings rather than to their parents, when he certainly is the author and Creator of all goods? Yet he does not cease creating those whom he foreknew would burn in eternal fires, and nothing but goodness is attributed to him for creating them. Moreover, some even baptized infants whom he foreknew would become apostates he does not remove from this life, adopted into the eternal kingdom; nor does he confer on them the great benefit he conferred on the one of whom we read: *he was snatched away lest wickedness change his understanding*[^105]. Yet nothing is attributed to God but goodness and justice, by which from good and evil he does all things well and rightly. How much easier is it to understand that nothing should be attributed to parents except their desire for children, since they certainly do not know what those children's future will be?

**11. 44.** As for what you quoted from the Gospel, *it would have been better for that man not to be born*[^106], was not his birth accomplished more by God's work than by his parents'? Why did God himself not grant his image what was better, when he foreknew the evil that would befall him, which parents cannot know? Yet those who understand rightly attribute to God only what should be attributed to the Creator's kindness. Likewise, without the knot of a difficult question, what is attributed to parents is that they desired children whose future they did not know. But I do not say that little children dying without Christ's baptism are to suffer so great a punishment that it would have been better for them not to be born, since the Lord said this not of sinners indiscriminately but of the most criminal and impious. For if, as he said of Sodom—and certainly he did not intend this to be understood of Sodom alone—one will be punished more tolerably than another on judgment day [^107], who would doubt that unbaptized little children, having original sin alone, unburdened by any sins of their own, will undergo the lightest condemnation of all? Though I cannot define its nature and extent, I nevertheless dare not say that it would be better for them not to exist than to exist there. But you too, who contend that they are supposedly free from all condemnation, refuse to consider the condemnation with which you punish them: alienating so many images of God from God's life and kingdom, and finally separating them from their devout parents, whom you so eloquently exhort to beget them. They suffer these things unjustly if they have no sin at all; or if justly, then they have original sin.

**11. 45.** Next, after presenting other words of mine in which I recalled how honorably the holy fathers of old lived with their wives, you say "that they did not devote themselves to offspring with the intention of begetting guilty children to be cleansed by baptism, because the baptism by which we are now adopted had not yet been instituted" [^108]. You speak truly about baptism, but we should not therefore believe that even before circumcision was given God's servants, since they possessed faith in the Mediator to come in the flesh, offered their little children no help through any sacrament of his, although for some necessary reason Scripture chose to leave its nature hidden. For we also read of their sacrifices [^109], by which certainly that blood was prefigured *which* alone *takes away the sin of the world*[^110]. More plainly, already in the law's time, sacrifices for sins were offered when little children were born. Answer: for which sins? Observe also that, with those fathers begetting children, the little child's soul would have perished from his people if he were not circumcised on the eighth day [^111]. Answer by what desert one would perish whom you deny to have been liable for original sin.

**12. 46.** Now "concerning Joseph, whose wife," on the Gospel's testimony, "I called Mary" [^112], you argue at length against my opinion and try to show that "because intercourse was absent, there was no marriage at all." Accordingly, in your view, when spouses stop having intercourse, they will no longer be spouses, and that cessation will be divorce. To prevent this, let the decrepit do as best they can what they did as young people, and not spare even bodies worn out by age from this work in which, even though continent, you delight excessively. Let them not consider themselves grown old regarding the incentives of lust, so that they may remain spouses. If this pleases you, that is your affair. But I—since human decency agrees that wives should be married for the procreation of children, however weakness yields to lust—have observed, besides the fidelity spouses owe each other lest adulteries occur and the offspring for whose generation the sexes should unite, also a third good that ought to exist in spouses, especially those belonging to God's people. This seemed to me a certain sacrament, so that divorce should not occur even from a wife unable to bear children, nor should a husband unwilling to have more children hand her over to another for impregnation, as Cato is said to have done [^113] [^114]. For this reason I said that in what I called marriage according to the Gospel all three goods of marriage were fulfilled: "fidelity, because there was no adultery; offspring, the Lord Christ himself; sacrament, because there was no divorce." Therefore, because I said that every good of marriage, that is, this threefold good, was fulfilled in those parents of Christ, I should not be thought to have said this, as you accuse me, "so that whatever is otherwise appears evil." For I say that marriage is good also in the other case, where offspring can be procreated only through intercourse. If it could be otherwise, yet spouses had intercourse, they would most openly yield to lust and use that evil badly. But since the human being, for whose sake both sexes were established, is born only through their union, spouses united for this purpose use that evil well; if, however, they also seek pleasure from lust, they use it badly, but pardonably.

**12. 47.** "Joseph," you say, "was regarded by everyone as a husband." You wish Scripture to be understood as speaking according to this opinion, not according to truth, when it called the Virgin Mary his wife. We might think the Evangelist could do this when narrating his own or some other human being's words, speaking according to human opinion. But would an angel too, speaking one to one, have spoken against both his own knowledge and that of the man he addressed, according to opinion rather than truth, when he said to him: *do not fear to take Mary your wife*[^115]? Again, why was it necessary for the generations to be traced down to Joseph [^116], unless this was done according to the truth by which the male sex takes precedence in marriage? When I set this down in the book you answer [^117], you were simply afraid to touch it. But Luke the Evangelist says of the Lord that *he was thought to be Joseph's son*[^118], because he was thought so in the sense that people believed him begotten through Joseph's intercourse. He wished to remove this false opinion, not to deny that Mary was that man's wife against the angel's testimony.

**12. 48.** Yet you yourself admit that "she received the name of wife from the fidelity of betrothal." That fidelity certainly remained inviolate. For when he saw her now a consecrated virgin endowed with divine fruitfulness, he did not seek another wife, although he would not have sought even this one had he not needed a wife. But he did not therefore judge the bond of marital fidelity to be dissolved because the hope of bodily union had been removed. Yet think what you will about that marriage; we do not, as you slanderously claim, say "that the first spouses were established as spouses without union of the two sexes." Rather, what we are discussing is whether in paradise before sin the flesh desired against the spirit; whether this does not now occur in spouses when the excess of this same concupiscence is restrained even through marital chastity; whether it is not evil, to which in its resistance consent must not be given lest it proceed to excess; whether the one in whom you deny any evil is not born from and with this very concupiscence; and whether any human being can be freed from an inborn evil except through regeneration. In these questions your impious novelty is suffocated by the Catholic truth handed down from ancient times.

**13. 49.** As for your thinking you should heap up testimonies from the holy Scriptures to prove something on which there is no question between us, "that the human being is created by God," which it is unlawful to deny even of any little worm, what purpose did it serve except to show that you sought open fields of words in which to run windily and emptily? But when you used holy Job's testimony so verbosely, why did you not remember what that same man of God says while speaking of human sins: *no one is clean from defilement, not even the infant whose life on earth is one day*[^119]? Who would deny that mercy is shown to all, great and small, by him from whom comes the salvation of human beings and beasts, and *who makes his sun rise upon the good and evil*[^120], except one who does not believe God exists or cares for earthly things? You wished to teach this through holy Job's testimony, as though we disputed it, because he said: *you joined me together with bones and sinews; you granted me life and mercy*[^121]. Here he could have meant not every human being, but given thanks for himself because the one who created him did not abandon him after his carnal birth, but himself showed him mercy so that he might live truly, that is, righteously. Or certainly, because the life he received at birth was not enough, he added *and mercy*, lest he remain by nature a child of wrath like the rest and be placed among vessels of wrath rather than vessels of mercy.

**13. 50.** Why the faithful person is not guilty because of the evil present beside him and in his members, yet one born from that evil contracts guilt, we have said I know not how many times. For regeneration, not generation, conferred this benefit on the faithful person. The offspring must therefore be freed from this guilt by the same means by which the parent was freed.

**14. 51.** But dialectic has taught you something great: "A thing that is in a subject cannot exist without that thing in which it inheres. Therefore," you think, "the evil that is in the parent, as in a subject, cannot transmit guilt to another thing, that is, the offspring, to which it does not reach." You would be right if the evil of concupiscence did not reach the offspring from the parent. But since, just as no one is sown without it, so no one is born without it, how can you say it does not reach where it passes? For it is not Aristotle, whose categories you foolishly understand, but the Apostle who says: *through one man sin entered the world, and it passed through all human beings*[^122]. Certainly that dialectic does not lie to you, but you do not understand it. What you learned there is true: things in a subject, such as qualities, cannot exist without the subject in which they are, as color or shape exists in the body as a subject. But they pass by affecting, not by migrating. Thus Ethiopians, being black, beget black children, yet parents do not transfer their color to their children like a tunic; rather, by their body's quality they affect the body propagated from them. It is more wonderful when the qualities of bodily things pass into incorporeal things, yet this happens when we in a manner absorb the forms of bodies we see, store them in memory, and carry them with us wherever we go. They have not left their bodies, yet, with our senses affected, they have passed to us in a wonderful way. As they pass from body to spirit, so too they pass from spirit to body. For the colors of the rods Jacob variegated passed by affecting into the souls of the mother animals and thence, passing again through the same impression, appeared in the bodies of their offspring [^123]. That something similar can happen in human offspring too is written by Soranus, a most distinguished medical author, who confirms it with a historical example. He relates that the tyrant Dionysius, because he himself was ugly and did not wish to have children like himself, used to place a beautiful picture before his wife during intercourse, so that by desiring its beauty she might somehow seize it and by its impression transmit it to the offspring she conceived [^124]. For God does not create natures so as to remove the laws he gave to the movements of each nature. Likewise faults, though existing in a subject, pass from parents to children not as by migration from their own subject into another, which those *Categories* you read most truly show to be impossible, but by impression and contagion, which you do not understand.

**15. 52.** Why do you strive through great arguments to reach the precipice of impiety, so that "Christ's flesh, because he was born of Mary, whose virgin flesh had been propagated from Adam like everyone else's, differs in nothing from sinful flesh, and the Apostle should be believed to have said without any distinction that *he* was *sent in the likeness of sinful flesh*[^125]"? Or rather, you insist "that there be no sinful flesh, lest Christ's flesh be such too." What, then, is *the likeness of sinful flesh* if there is no sinful flesh? Yet you said "that I had not understood this apostolic statement," but did not explain it so that, with you as teacher, we might learn that something can be like a thing that does not exist. If saying this is madness, and Christ's flesh is undoubtedly not sinful flesh but like sinful flesh, what remains for us to understand except that, with its exception, all other human flesh is sinful? Hence it is apparent that the concupiscence through which Christ refused to be conceived produced the propagation of evil in the human race. Although Mary's body came from it, she did not transmit it into the body she conceived without it. Anyone, however, who denies that Christ's body was called the likeness of sinful flesh because all other human flesh is sinful, and so compares Christ's flesh with that of other newborn human beings as to assert that both possess equal purity, is found to be a detestable heretic.

**15. 53.** You seem to yourself to have discovered something great, and argue most copiously that "even if newborns could contract some evil from their parents, it would be expiated by God's hands, since he himself forms them in their mothers' wombs." You most insistently prove that human beings are formed by him, as though we denied it, adducing many scriptural testimonies. Among them, after quoting words from Ecclesiasticus calling God's works hidden [^126], you immediately added your own: "This statement exposes the vanity of those who think the depth of nature can be comprehended by any investigation." Say this to yourself, and do not rashly define anything about the soul's origin that cannot be comprehended either by most certain reason or by a wholly unambiguous divine utterance. Rather, think what that most wise woman, the Maccabees' mother, thought. For you quoted her words too, spoken to her sons: *I do not know how you appeared in my womb*[^127]. Certainly she should not be thought to have said this of their bodies, which she did not doubt she had conceived from male seed. Rather, whether her sons' souls were derived from their father's soul or began to exist in her womb from elsewhere, this she assuredly did not know; nor was she ashamed to confess ignorance so as to avoid rashness. Why, then, do you object: "Why are the children not cleansed by the very act of their creation, so that," you say, "by the Maker's majesty they are purified from the alleged defilements of their parents?" You fail to notice that this can also be said about the manifest bodily defects with which not a few infants are born. Although far be it to doubt that the true and good God forms all bodies, yet from so great a Maker's hands proceed so many not only defective but even monstrous bodies that some call them errors of nature. Unable to trace the divine power at work and why it does what it does, they are ashamed to confess that they do not know what they do not know.

**15. 54.** As for original sin's passage into all human beings, since it passes through the flesh's concupiscence, it could not pass into the flesh the Virgin conceived without it. For you also wished to quote, as though setting a binding precedent against me, from another book of mine, written to Marcellinus of holy memory, the statement about Adam "that he infected with corruption in himself all who would come from his stock." But Christ did not enter his mother's womb through that by which Adam infected others. I shall state the words most relevant to the matter in that same statement of mine, because you refused to state them; immediately the reason for your refusal will appear. "By the hidden corruption of his carnal concupiscence," I said, "he infected with corruption in himself all who would come from his stock" [^128]. Thus he did not infect the flesh in whose conception this corruption was absent. Christ's flesh therefore derived mortality from the mortality of his mother's body, because it found her body mortal; but it did not derive the contagion of original sin, because it did not find the concupiscence of intercourse. Had he taken from his mother not mortality but only the substance of flesh, his flesh could have been neither sinful flesh nor even the likeness of sinful flesh.

**15. 55.** But you compare and equate me with "the error of Apollinaris, who denied that bodily sensation existed in Christ," to stir up clouds from every quarter for the ignorant, lest they attend to truth's light. Bodily sensation, without which no human being living in a body ever was, is, or will be, is one thing; the concupiscence by which the flesh desires against the spirit is another. The first human being was without it before sin, and Christ as man exhibited that same human nature to us, because just as the former was created from earth, so the latter was created from a woman without such concupiscence. Yet he also assumed from her the weakness of mortality, such as did not exist before sin in the first man's flesh, so that his flesh should be what that flesh then was not: the likeness of sinful flesh. Thus, to offer us an example of endurance, he had no evils of his own but bore those of others; he was in sufferings for us, not in disordered desires.

**15. 56.** Therefore, those born from Adam must be transferred by rebirth to Christ, lest God's images perish from God's kingdom. Whoever says this can happen without evil has neither love nor fear of God. But with this evil a human being must necessarily be born from a condemned origin. Far be it, however, that "we reduce the reborn," as you slanderously claim, "to the necessity of crimes while God bestows the gifts of virtues." Therefore, although we see another law in our members fighting the law of our mind, the person whose spirit, aided by a spiritual gift, desires against the flesh's concupiscence not only has no necessity of crime but rather has the honor of praise. Yet whichever way you turn, however you boast, whatever you gather from anywhere, inflate, brandish, and scatter, that against which the good spirit desires is not good.

**15. 57.** You say: "A dissimilar nature could not provide an example." Indeed it could. For why does he urge us to imitate the Father, *who makes his sun rise upon the good and the evil*[^129], so that by his example we may love our enemies? Nevertheless, the nature of Christ as man was not dissimilar to our nature, but was dissimilar to our fault. For he was born a man without the fault that no other human being lacks. As for the life in which we ought to imitate Christ, this too makes a very great difference: each of us is a man, but he is also God. For a man who is merely man cannot be as just as a man who is also God. You did say something great and true when, after quoting the testimony of the apostle Peter, *Who committed no sin*[^130], you said we should note that the Apostle judged it sufficient to show there was no sin in Christ to say that he *committed* *no sin*, so as to teach, as you say, "that one who did not commit it could not have it." This is entirely true. For he would certainly have committed sin as an adult if he had had it as an infant. Indeed, the reason there is no human being except him who has not committed sin on reaching a more advanced age is that there is no human being except him who did not have sin at the beginning of infancy.

**15. 58.** You say: "Remove his role as an example, and you will also remove his role as the ransom he became for us." It is no wonder that you place only an example in Christ, when you attack the help of grace, of which he was full. You say: "In the hope of being free from evil we fly to the protection of faith; but we do not become free from what is inborn, since manhood itself remains after baptism." You call the concupiscence of the flesh by the name of manhood. Certainly there remains, as you cannot deny, something against which the spirit must desire, lest the human being, already reborn, be drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence. And surely the concupiscence that fights back to draw him away, even if it does not draw him because the spirit desires against it and resists, and therefore neither conceives nor brings forth sin [^131], is not a good. It is the very thing of which the Apostle says: *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^132]. But if Christ had this thing that is not good in his own nature, he would not heal it in ours.

**16. 59.** You insert another passage from my book. Because I said that "marital intercourse undertaken with the intention of procreating is not itself a sin, because the good will of the mind leads bodily pleasure as its follower, rather than following it as its leader" [^133], you reply that "sins do not arise from something free from sin." In this way you think you can destroy original sin, which only the Savior destroys, the Savior whom you begrudge to little children. He destroys it, however, by releasing them from their guilt, not by denying it. For marital intercourse undertaken with the intention of procreating is not a sin precisely because it makes good use of the law of sin, that is, concupiscence, which is in the members fighting against the law of the mind. If it does not hold the parent guilty because he has been regenerated, why is it surprising if it holds the newborn guilty because he has been generated from it? Therefore, lest he remain guilty, he too must be regenerated. As for your statement that "sins do not arise from something free from sin," if you consider how much it helps the Manichaeans, you will want to erase it from your book and from the hearts of everyone who has read your book. For if sins do not arise from something free from sin, they have another nature of their own, according to the Manichaeans, from which they arise. I already showed in the first book of this work how much you help them by other similar statements [^134]. See, what you have said here amounts to the same thing. Do you see that, besides your particular error by which you are Pelagians, we must labor to overthrow certain statements of yours, such as this one, if we wish to defeat the Manichaeans? You say: "Sins do not arise from something free from sin." But truth contradicts you and overthrows both you and the Manichaeans, with whom you share this assertion. The angel God created was something free from sin; the human being God first created was something free from sin. Whoever, then, denies that sins arose from things free from sin is either an open Manichaean or an unwary supporter of the Manichaeans.

**16. 60.** Then, quoting other words of mine, you argue as if I had said: "Lust is honored when it serves married people in propagating offspring." You tell yourself whatever you please; I neither said nor thought this at all. For how is lust honored when it serves, when it is restrained by the mind's rule lest it proceed to unbridled excesses? We therefore did not say: "Using lust always incurs guilt." Yet you reason as if we had said it, concluding that "adulterers sin less than husbands, because," you say, "lust serves husbands so that they may sin, but rules adulterers." Since I did not say that, however, this conclusion of yours, whatever its worth, which you wished to make follow from it, has nothing to do with me. For I say that using lust is not always a sin, because using an evil well is not a sin. Nor is something good merely because a good person uses it well. For it is written even of two human beings: *A well-taught son will be wise, but he will* *use a foolish man as his servant*[^135]. Is being foolish therefore good because a wise person uses the fool well? Hence the apostle John does not say: Do not use the world, but: *Do not love the world*, where he also includes the concupiscence of the flesh [^136]. For one who uses something without loving it uses it as though not using it, because he does not use it for its own sake, but for the sake of something else that he regards with love, so that he uses even this without loving it. That is why Paul, his fellow apostle, says: *And those who use* *this world, as though not using it*[^137]. What does *As though not using it* mean except: Let them not love what they use, because it is such that they cannot otherwise use it well? This must indeed be observed even with things that are good in this world, but ought nevertheless not to be loved. For who would rightly say that money is evil? Yet no one who loves it uses it well. How much more is this true of lust! For an evil spirit desires money, but money itself does not desire against a good spirit, as lust does. Therefore one who denies that this is an evil sins, and one who uses this evil well does not sin. You would, then, rightly argue that "lust, if it is evil, binds spouses whom it obeys with greater guilt than adulterers whom it rules," if we said that spouses who use the service of the evil of concupiscence solely for the duty of procreation were using it for evil deeds, just as a murderer uses a servant to perpetrate a crime. But since we say that the duty of procreation is good in married people, although the child born contracts from the contagion of the first sin a wound that can be healed in one reborn, it follows that good spouses use the evil of concupiscence just as a wise person uses a foolish servant for good works.

**16. 61.** But you most acute men condemn neither the measure nor the kind of pleasure, but its excess, and judge it something for which the obscene should be reproached, "because you know," as you say, "that it can be kept within permitted limits by the power of the mind." Let the mind's power, if it can, prevent lust from stirring to transgress the limits from whose transgression it restrains it. If it cannot do this, then surely resistance is being offered to a wicked enemy that strives to transgress those limits, so that it may not transgress them. You say: "But we testify that virgins and the continent hold it in complete contempt." Does this mean that virgins and the continent do not fight against the concupiscence of the flesh? And what is it against which they wage those glorious struggles, praised even by you, to preserve virginity and continence [^138]? If, then, they fight, what they fight against is an evil. And where is this evil, except within themselves? Therefore they too truthfully say: *Good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^139].

**16. 62.** You say that "marriage is nothing other than the mingling of bodies," and afterward say, quite truly, that "propagation cannot occur without mutual desire and the natural act." Yet do you deny that adulterers too are joined to one another by mutual desire, the natural act, and the mingling of bodies? This, then, is not a definition of marriage. For what marriage is differs from that without which even marriage cannot propagate children. Human beings can be born without marriage, and spouses can exist without the mingling of bodies. Otherwise, to say nothing else, they would certainly cease to be spouses when they grew old and either could no longer unite or felt ashamed and were unwilling to do so without hope of having offspring. You see, then, how thoughtlessly you defined marriage by saying that "it is nothing other than the mingling of bodies." Perhaps it would have been more tolerable to say that marriage does not begin without the mingling of bodies, because wives are certainly taken for the sake of procreating children, and children cannot be procreated otherwise. But the mingling of bodies for procreation would have been different from what it is now if no one had sinned. Far be it from that most honorable happiness in paradise always to obey the stirrings of lust! Far be it from that peace of mind and body to contain anything on account of which humanity's original nature would fight against itself! If, then, lust was neither to be served nor fought against there, either it was not there or it was not of the kind it is now. For now whoever is unwilling to serve lust must fight against it, and whoever neglects to fight must serve it. Of these two, one is troublesome though praiseworthy; the other is shameful and miserable. Thus in this world one of them is necessary for the chaste, but in paradise both would have been foreign to the blessed.

**16. 63.** But again you say that I contradict myself, quoting other words of mine where, distinguishing the duty of propagation from the desire for carnal delight, I said: "It is one thing to have intercourse only with the will to procreate, which is without fault; another to seek the pleasure of the flesh in intercourse, though only with one's spouse, which involves a venial fault" [^140]. These two statements contain nothing by which you can show that I contradict myself, as all who see the truth see along with me. Nevertheless, hear it again and again, so that it may be impressed more firmly upon the understanding of those whom you seek to deceive. For you slander us by saying that "we provide an excuse for shameful and criminal people, so that when they have committed unspeakable and unclean acts, they may say that they acted against their will and therefore have no sin," as though we did not urge much more vigorously that lust must be fought against. For if you call it a good and nevertheless do not wish us to believe that your wars against this good grow cold or even lukewarm, how much more watchfully and ardently do we judge that we must fight against an evil! We say that what is against the will is the flesh's desiring against the spirit [^141], not the spirit's desiring against the flesh. Through this good desire, spouses do not use the lust of the flesh except for the sake of procreation, and thus use an evil well. This good use of an evil makes intercourse honorable and truly marital; but intercourse for pleasure rather than offspring is culpable, though venial within marriage. Yet even one born of honorable intercourse contracts something that must be washed away by rebirth, because even in honorable intercourse there is an evil that the goodness of marriage uses well. But what harmed them when born does not harm them when reborn. It follows, therefore, that it also harms the child born of them unless that child is reborn.

**16. 64.** Amid your arguments, in which you twist yourself vainly against my words, you certainly do not see that you repeatedly help the Manichaeans. For it seems to you that a child born of marital intercourse does not contract original sin "because," as you say, "fault cannot arise from this act, which has no fault." Why, then, did the angel's fault and the human being's fault arise from God's work, which had no fault? You see how much you support those whose detestation you try to use to cover up your opposition to the most firmly established Catholic faith. For if, according to your definitive statement, "fault cannot arise from this work, which has no fault," behold, none of God's works has any fault: whence, then, did fault arise? Here the Manichaean, with your help, strives in his folly to introduce another, evil nature, so that there may be something from which fault is believed to have arisen, because, according to your words, "fault does not arise from God's work." Can the Manichaean be defeated unless you too are defeated along with him? For both the angel and the human being are God's works without fault; yet fault arose from them when, through the free choice given to them without fault, they departed from him who is free from fault. They became evil not through an admixture of evil but through a falling away from good.

**16. 65.** You say that I praised the continence of the Christian era not to kindle in people a desire for virginity, but to condemn the good of marriage instituted by God. Yet, lest you seem tormented by a malicious suspicion about my intention, you tell me, as if wishing to test me: "If you sincerely invite people to pursue continence, you therefore admit that the virtue of chastity can be preserved by those who will it, so that whoever wills may be holy in body and spirit." I reply that I admit it, but not as you do. For you attribute this to the powers of the mind itself; I attribute it to the will assisted by God's grace. Nevertheless, what is restrained by the mind's command to prevent sin, except the evil whose victory produces sin? Lest we say with the Manichaeans that this evil has been mingled with us from some alien nature of evil, it remains for us to confess that it is in our own nature like a wound to be healed, whose guilt, we already confess, has been healed by regeneration.

**16. 66.** See, in vain you have enumerated the deceits of so many heretics to whom you compare me. Would that you did not increase their number! You assert that "the apostolic judgment condemning heretics who forbid marriage [^142] also touches me," as though I said that "marriages are shameful after the coming of Christ." Hear, then, what we say, so that, hearing the same thing often and in many ways, you may not ignore the truth by a kind of feigned deafness. We do not say that marriages are shameful; indeed, incontinence must be supported by the honorableness of marriage lest it fall into a damnable crime. But Christian teaching does not say what you say, namely, to quote your words, that "a human being is sufficient to lay down laws for the impulses born within him." We do not say this; we say what the Apostle said when speaking of this matter: *Each has his own gift from God*[^143]. And what the Lord said: *Without me you can do nothing*[^144]. And: *Not all receive this saying, but those to whom it is given*[^145]. He could have said: Not all receive this saying, but whoever wills, if what you say were true. I ask, then, for what kind of inborn impulses you say a human being is capable of laying down laws: good ones or evil ones? If good ones, the spirit therefore desires against a good, and two goods oppose each other within the human being. If that were so, the very opposition between the two goods could not be good. But if evil ones, then confess that evil impulses are born within the human being, against which chastity fights. And lest you be compelled to say with the Manichaeans that an alien nature of evil is mingled within us, confess instead our original infirmity. Marital chastity uses the evil of this infirmity well; against this evil of infirmity, the incontinent apply the remedies of marriage and the continent wage glorious struggles. I think that the promise I made when I began answering everything in which you had brought forward knots of questions to be untied will be fulfilled more conveniently if I do not exceed the number of your books. Let this book of mine therefore end here, so that we may refute your last book with a fresh beginning.
