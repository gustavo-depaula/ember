# Book 3

**1. 1.** Now, since you will undoubtedly say that so many great saints erred—men learned in the Sacred Scriptures, whose government of the Church enjoys such illustrious remembrance and praise—if you do not yield and submit to their authority, whether you treat them most insultingly, as you treat me, or more respectfully and gently, showing some regard for their persons, I must, with the Lord's gift, answer you, dear son Julian, and refute your books and arguments in such a way that, if you can, you may understand that you were wrongly persuaded of what you strive to persuade others, and may profitably repent of the rash, reckless advance and fall of your youth. Your correction will benefit not only yourself but many others, when you acknowledge and confess how far from empty, and how true, was the teaching that so many great rulers and teachers of Christian peoples learned and taught in God's Church, which you, deceived by a plausible novelty, desired to overthrow. But if—which may the Lord avert from you—your heart is so darkened that you cannot understand these things, or if you belong among those whom truth marks out in the holy Psalms, saying, *He refused to understand so that he might act well*[^1], or among those of whom it is likewise written, *A stubborn slave will not be corrected by words; for even if he understands, he will not obey*[^2], even so, neither my labor nor that of the other brothers who defend the Catholic faith against this error through Christ's grace will be fruitless. For if there have been people whom this unfamiliar, creeping error overturned or unsettled, far more will be instructed or corrected by the ancient truth when it is defended. To avoid making this too long, I shall not quote all your words; but, if the Lord helps me, I shall leave none of your supposedly acute arguments unresolved and undemolished.

**1. 2.** Concerning the judges, before whom you say you could not plead your case because no one deliberates well about doubtful matters unless he brings a heart free from hatred, anger, and friendship—and you say those who judged your case were not such men, because they began to hate it before they knew it—I have already answered in the preceding book. If you sought judges such as Sallust described, from whom you took this saying[^3], you would surely yield to holy Ambrose and his fellow bishops, whose hearts, when they delivered their true and tranquil judgments on this matter, were free from hatred or friendship toward you, or anger, or pity—which Sallust included though you omitted it—either for you or against you. Now it is not enough for you to refuse them as judges: you even dare to make them defendants. But tell me, please, how did those who condemned your case begin to hate it before knowing it? Undoubtedly they hated it because they knew it. They knew that you say little children have no evil by birth that needs to be purified by rebirth. They knew that you say God's grace is given according to our merits, so that grace would no longer be grace[^4], since it would not be given freely but repaid as a debt. They knew that you say a human being can be without any sin in this life, so that the words the whole Church says in the Lord's Prayer, *Forgive us our debts*[^5], would not be necessary for him. These things they knew in you, and most rightly hated. Let them learn that you have been corrected of them, and they will love you. For it is not as you say: “Anyone who affirms either free choice in human beings or that God is the Creator of those who are born is called a Pelagian and a Celestian.” Rather, anyone who does not attribute the freedom to which we are called to God's grace, who denies that Christ is the deliverer of little children, and who says that any petition in the Lord's Prayer is unnecessary on his own behalf for any righteous person in this life receives the name of this error, because by sharing the error he shares the guilt.

**1. 3.** There is no need to say here which Catholic lights you dare to defame with the charge of Manichaeism, whether you do not know it or pretend not to know. Certainly, if, as you say, “the emperor's response was rather in your favor,” why do you not come forward and voluntarily submit it to the public authorities, showing yourselves to be the people whose faith the Christian ruler approved? But if you understand God's law as you please, rather than as it is, what wonder if you do the same with the emperor's law? You promise to discuss these matters more fully elsewhere. If you do, what you produce will either be exposed as deceitful or disregarded as empty.

**1. 4.** How splendid and witty is your congratulation that “one man has come forth who wishes it understood that the entire outcome of the battle rests with him”! Evidently you are to appear to the Pelagians as David, and I as Goliath. You must see whether you have entered this agreement and compact with the Pelagians: that if you are defeated, they will dare nothing further. But far be it from me to challenge you to single combat, when Christ's army, spread everywhere, defeats you wherever you appear! It defeated Celestius at Carthage when I was not there, and again at Constantinople, so far from the regions of Africa. It defeated Pelagius in Palestine, where, for fear of his own condemnation, he condemned your cause: there your heresy fell altogether. And since he whose figure David was fights against his adversaries in all his soldiers, he cut down your error even with Pelagius's tongue, as with the sword of a man struck down and lying on the ground. For the point you resent—that you are called new heretics because you assert that every evil defined as sin exists not in nature but in the will alone—Pelagius has already cut away from you, or rather the Lord has done so through Pelagius's tongue. Fearing condemnation, he condemned those who say that infants have eternal life even if they are not baptized. You, then, who deny that there is an evil in the little child to be washed away by baptism, tell us for what desert an unbaptized little child is punished with eternal death. But what will you say, unless perhaps you curse Pelagius? Yet if he says to you as you curse him, “What would you have had me do? When Christ says, *Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have life in you*[^6], was I to say that a little child would have life who ended this life without that sacrament?” I think you will regret having cursed the man. Then repent now of this entire error.

**1. 5.** Do not use the utterly wretched argument used by all heretics whom the laws of Catholic emperors restrain from destructive license. All such people say what you yourself said: “The party that substitutes terror in debate suffers from a lack of argument: it wins no assent from the prudent but extorts blind assent from the fearful.” You are new heretics, certainly, but you have recognized and adopted, along with the others, this old cry of almost all heretics. Do not deceive yourselves or others, as though you had a complaint against us like the one we had against the Donatists, whom we compelled by imperial orders to attend a conference with us. Their frenzy had occupied all Africa, and they would not allow Catholics to preach the truth against their error; with violent assaults, banditry, ambushes on the roads, plunder, fires, and murders, they devastated much and terrified all. We could conduct no proceedings with them before bishops, since we had none in common, and the proceedings our predecessors had conducted with them nearly a hundred years earlier were no longer remembered by the peoples. This necessity therefore compelled us to crush their shamelessness and restrain their audacity at least by producing the recorded proceedings of our conference. Your case, however, has just been concluded before the competent tribunal of bishops common to us. As regards your right to an examination, nothing further remains to be done with you except that you should peacefully follow the judgment pronounced on this matter; if you refuse, you must be restrained from turbulent or insidious agitation. You are more like the Maximianists. Wishing to console their scant numbers at least with the name of a contest, and thus to seem important to those who despised them because they were allowed to enter an examination with us, they appealed to us, submitted a petition, and challenged us; we disregarded them. For they desired the reputation gained from a contest more than they feared defeat in it. They did not hope for the glory of victory but sought the fame of a conference, because they lacked the fame of numbers. If, then, you think yourselves victors because you were not granted the examination you desire, the Maximianists preceded you in this empty talk. Yet the Catholic Church did give you the judgment it ought to give, in which your case was concluded; it did not consider them worthy of any judgment, because they had separated from the Donatists, not from us as you had. If, however, you see in the Maximianists that it does not follow that people not permitted to debate before some tribunal must be considered confident in the truth, stop boasting of these empty things. Let it suffice you that the Catholic Church both endured you with a mother's gentleness and condemned you with judicial severity, or rather medicinal necessity.

**1. 6.** But to avoid lingering over superfluities, I pass over the tumult of your curses and insults, whether those you heaped up at the beginning of your work or those you hardly ever ceased scattering throughout those same four books. Otherwise serious people might see neither of us as a serious disputant but both as frivolous litigants. Let us rather now see what you bring forward to demonstrate, as you promise, that I attribute human beings and marriage to the devil as their author.

**2. 7.** You certainly set out my words for refutation. Having set them out, you reply, as it were, and try to persuade your readers that I contradicted myself: although I said in my defense that the new heretics call us condemners of marriage and of God's work, I afterward withdrew this by saying that God and the devil each possess half of a newborn human being, or rather that the devil possesses him entirely, God being wholly excluded from the whole of his possession, which is the human being. Where is your acuteness, by which you think you have mastered Aristotle's categories and other subtleties of dialectic? Do you really fail to notice that the objection you have brought against me concerning little children could be brought against both of us by some adversary of the truth concerning any wicked adults? For I ask you what you would answer about any utterly wicked person not yet regenerated. Do you at least admit that he is under the devil unless he is reborn in Christ, or do you deny even this? If you deny it, who are those whom God rescues from the power of darkness and transfers into the kingdom of the Son of his love[^7]? If you admit it, I ask whether God has any power over such a person, still placed under the power of darkness. If you say, “He has none,” you will be answered: “Then God has been excluded from his possession by the devil.” If you say, “He has,” you will be answered: “Then God and the devil each possess half the man.” In this way the ignorant will stir up against you the same odium that you, who wish to be thought learned, wanted to stir up against me concerning children newly born. See how easily your first argument is destroyed, because you neglect to observe that human beings are under the devil's power before they are redeemed by Christ in such a way that neither they nor even the devil himself are withdrawn from God's power.

**3. 8.** As for the question of baptism, about which you complained that our lies had made you odious to the uninformed, one can scarcely describe how elegantly you escaped, clearing yourselves of this odium because you acknowledge that little children must be baptized. For you say, “The grace of baptism must not be altered according to the circumstances, since it distributes its own gifts according to the capacity of those who approach.” “Therefore Christ,” you say, “who is the redeemer of his own work, increases his benefits toward his image with continual generosity, and those whom he made good by creating he makes better by renewing and adopting.” Is this the whole reason why you think no odium should attach to you regarding the baptism of little children? As though any of us had said you deny that little children ought to be baptized! You do not say they should not be baptized; rather, in your immense wisdom, you say wonderful things: they are baptized in the Savior's sacrament but are not saved; they are redeemed but are not delivered; they are washed but are not cleansed; they are exorcized and breathed upon but are not rescued from the devil's power. These are the monstrosities of your opinions, the unexpected mysteries of your new doctrines, the paradoxes of the Pelagian heretics, more astonishing than those of the Stoic philosophers. For you say these things because you fear hearing: if they are saved, what in them was sick? If they are delivered, what in them was held in the bonds of slavery? If they are cleansed, what uncleanness lay hidden in them? If they are rescued, by what desert were they under the devil's power, though not guilty of personal iniquity, unless because they contract the original sin you deny? And you deny it, not to establish that they are saved, free, clean, and subject to no enemy—for your false testimony gives them no help before the true judge—but so that, while they remain in their evil old condition, you may pursue a new vanity. For the true judgment is not yours but his who said, *Unless someone is born again of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^8].

**3. 9.** But you, most excellent lovers of that future life eternal with Christ, think it no punishment for God's image to be exiled forever from God's kingdom. Yet even if you called it a small punishment, that would be the voice not of a blessed lover of that kingdom but of a wretched despiser. Furthermore, if you acknowledge as at least a small punishment what is a great one—that God's image is not permitted to enter God's kingdom—and this suffices for the present case, please open whatever eyes you have and see by what justice this punishment is to be inflicted upon a little child whom, with eyes closed, you deny to be subject to original sin. I refrain from mentioning the evils almost all infants suffer in this very transitory life and how this saying is explained: *A heavy yoke is upon the children of Adam, from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all*[^9]. Surely, under a just and almighty God, these evils would not be inflicted on his image—evils by which infancy cannot be said to be trained in virtue—unless evil deserts were contracted from the parents. But these evils of little children, not those you deny they have but those we all see them suffer, you entirely pass over without looking at them. Instead, most eloquent man, you roam at leisure, exercising your talent and tongue in nature's praise. That nature, fallen into such great and manifest miseries, needs Christ as savior, deliverer, cleanser, and redeemer, not Julian, Celestius, or Pelagius as its praiser. Indeed, you would not acknowledge its redemption in little children had not Celestius confessed it at Carthage in the ecclesiastical proceedings, unable to withstand Christian voices. But tell me, how can this redemption be understood except as redemption from evil, by him *who redeems Israel from all its iniquities*[^10]? Wherever redemption is mentioned, a price is understood; and what is it except the precious blood of the spotless Lamb, Jesus Christ[^11]? Why ask anyone else why this price was poured out? Let the redeemer himself answer; let the buyer himself speak. *This is*, he says, *my blood, which will be poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins*[^12]. Go on, go on! Just as you say, “They are baptized in the Savior's sacrament but are not saved; they are redeemed but are not delivered; they are washed but are not cleansed; they are exorcized and breathed upon but are not rescued from the devil's power,” say also, “Blood is poured out for them for the forgiveness of sins, but they are cleansed by the forgiveness of no sin.” What you say is astonishing, new, and false: we marvel at the astonishing, guard against the new, and refute the false.

**4. 10.** Did you not yourself say that “the administration of the body has been entrusted to the mind in such a way that the fruit of the work becomes common to both, and it experiences either the joys of practiced virtue or the penalties of insolence along with the affliction of the flesh it has not governed well here”? Answer, then, why in this very life an infant's soul is tormented by the affliction of its flesh, when it cannot yet be charged with deserving this for having governed its flesh badly. You say that “human nature at the beginning of birth is rich in the endowment of innocence.” We certainly admit this as regards personal sins; but when you deny that infants are subject even to original sin, answer by what desert such innocence is sometimes born blind, sometimes deaf. This defect even impedes faith itself, as the Apostle testifies when he says, *Faith, then, comes from hearing*[^13]. Who, moreover, could tolerate that, as regards the mind itself, God's image, “rich,” as you assert, “in the endowment of innocence,” should be born foolish, if no evil deserts pass from parents to little children? Is any of you so foolish as to think foolishness no evil, when Scripture says, *The dead are to be mourned for seven days, but a fool* *all his days*[^14]? Who does not know that those commonly called simpletons are so foolish by nature that the perception of some of them may almost be compared to that of cattle? Yet you refuse to confess that the human race, from the beginning when it abandoned God, contracts the guilt of a condemned origin, most deserving of all these punishments, except where the Creator's unsearchable wisdom spares it according to his hidden ordering. He does not withhold the goodness of his work even from the whole mass of perdition: from the evils of vices he creates rational and mortal nature, good insofar as it is nature, though accompanied by evils; none but he can be its creator. And amid condemned generation, he provides the help of regeneration to the vessels of mercy.

**5. 11.** You therefore think in vain that “there is no offense in little children because it cannot exist without a will, which they lack.” This is rightly said of each person's own sin, not of the original contagion of the first sin. If that did not exist, little children, bound by no evil, would assuredly suffer no evil in either body or soul under the mighty power of the just God. Yet even this contagion began with the evil will of the first human beings. Thus there is no origin of anyone's sin except an evil will. If you understand this, you will confess Christ's grace toward little children simply and truthfully, and will not be forced to say utterly impious and absurd things: either that little children should not be baptized—which perhaps you will say later—or that so great a sacrament is such a mockery in them that they are baptized in the Savior but are not saved; redeemed by the deliverer but not delivered; washed in the washing of regeneration but not cleansed; exorcized and breathed upon but not rescued from the power of darkness; that their price is the blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins, yet they are purified by the forgiveness of no sin. All this because you are afraid to say, “Let them not be baptized,” lest not only men smear your faces with spit but even little women soften your heads with their sandals.

**5. 12.** We certainly say that the reason why one who is born is under the devil until reborn in Christ is the contagion of sin from his origin. But you who deny this, look at least at what is manifest: why do some infants even suffer possession by a demon? Unless perhaps you will deny either that such infants exist or that they are under the devil, and the Gospel will not remind you, where the Lord, perhaps on your account, asked what he knew, so that the boy's father would answer that his son had been grievously afflicted from infancy by so powerful a demon that Christ's disciples could not cast it out[^15]. See, I do not say that marriage is the reason little children are in the devil's power, as you falsely accuse me of saying. Marriage has its own order and blessing and could not lose its goodness even when sin entered. But answer, if you can, why at least that infant is under the devil who is most manifestly afflicted by the devil, sometimes to the point of dying from that very affliction. For you do not want anyone to undergo any punishment for another's sins, lest this make it credible that the contagion of sins can also pass from parents to those born of them.

**6. 13.** But evidently, as an outstanding dialectician, “you will not allow me to escape, but will ask me closely and briefly whether I consider action or nature guilty in little children.” Answering both possibilities, you say: “If action, show what they have done; if nature, show who made it.” As though an evil action made anything guilty except a nature! For the one guilty through a human action is a human being, and a human being is a nature. Thus human beings are guilty: adults through the act of sin, little ones through the contagion from adults; the former through what they do, the latter through those from whom they derive their origin. Accordingly, it is good in little children that they are human beings, which they would not be at all unless he who is supremely good had created them. But if they contracted no evil from their origin, they would never be born even with bodily defects. For the Creator of souls is also the Creator of bodies; he certainly does not inflict defects upon human nature in its very creation without its deserving them. Of the countless little children born with such a variety of defects in both mind and body, one cannot say what the Lord said of the man born blind: *This happened not because of his sin or his parents', but that God's works might be manifested in him*[^16]. Many are not healed at all but die with the same defects, either at some later age or in infancy itself. Such evils also persist in some little children already reborn, having been born with them, or befall them afterward. Far be it from us to say this is undeserved; rather, let us understand from it that their rebirth benefits them for the age to come. The ordering of this age, however, because of the vice of human pride by which humanity apostatized from God[^17], proceeds through the diverse evils of human beings under the heavy yoke *upon the children of Adam, from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all*[^18].

**7. 14.** As for your attempt in this work to teach how dialecticians construct syllogisms, a matter on which no one has challenged you, you displease serious readers as much as you please yourself. Worse still, you invent statements I do not make, conclusions I do not draw, concessions I do not grant, and yourself infer what I reject. When did I ever deny that “human nature is praiseworthy,” insofar as human beings are human? When did I say that “they are guilty for the very reason that they exist,” when assuredly they would exist without being guilty if no one had sinned? When did I say that “fruitfulness must be condemned,” when it belongs to the blessing of marriage? How could I ask you to concede precisely what I myself did not say?

**7. 15.** As for your claim that I assume “every mingling of bodies is evil,” you need only add that I also accused the mingling of wine and water when our drink is mixed. For there too bodies are undoubtedly mingled, and if I called every mingling of bodies evil, I certainly did not omit this one. But I have not censured even the mingling of the two sexes if it is lawful and marital. Without it human generation would not occur, even if no one's sin had preceded it. Accordingly, that other statement you add as mine, “Children are born from the mingling of bodies,” I certainly make; but the conclusion you wished to introduce as mine is not mine. I do not say, “Children, then, are wicked, since they proceed from an evil activity.” For I do not call the spouses' activity undertaken to beget children evil, but rather good, because it makes good use of the evil of lust. Through that evil human beings are generated, God's good work, though not without an evil on account of which they must be regenerated to be delivered from evil.

**7. 16.** Next you weave another syllogism of your own, since that first one too is yours, not mine. You say, then, “The purpose of the sexes is the mingling of bodies,” and want me to concede this. There: I concede it. You proceed and add, “If mingling is always evil, the constitution of bodies distinguished by sex is also shameful.” Even if this followed, it would not press me, since I not only do not call marital union—that is, union for the sake of procreating children—evil, but call it good. Moreover, it does not follow that, if the mingling of the two sexes is always evil, the constitution of bodies distinguished by sex is therefore shameful. For even if human beings were so enslaved to the evil of lust that, with the honor of marriage removed, all coupled indiscriminately and promiscuously like dogs, the constitution of bodies, whose author is God, would not thereby be shameful because every union of male and female was evil. Even now, from an adulterous union, certainly evil, God's work in the constitution of bodies is good. Surely you see how, for all your dialectic, you have said nothing, and how far you have wandered from its path, without any fault in the discipline of dialectic itself. You see that you use the language of that art to puff yourself up and astonish the unlearned, wishing to seem what you are not. Even if you were that, you would be nothing in relation to the manner in which these matters should be discussed. As it is, you are plainly both inept and unskilled; then you would be an inept expert. Yet, laden as though with dialecticians' sharp javelins, you advance to battle and throw leaden daggers, saying, “If mingling is always evil, the constitution of bodies distinguished by sex is also shameful.” Without seeing how your supposed necessary argument does not follow, you add, “Since you will be unable to deny this.” What am I unable to deny, thoughtless man? What am I unable to deny? Precisely what you yourself, if you come to your senses even now, cannot but deny! For certainly, if the mingling of adulterers is evil, the constitution of the human beings born from them is not therefore shameful. The former belongs to human beings using good members badly; the latter belongs to God working well from evil human beings. But if you say, “Even when adultery occurs, the mingling in itself is good, since it is natural, but adulterers use it badly,” why do you refuse to agree that lust can likewise be evil, yet married people use it well for the sake of generation? Can there be an evil use of good things but no good use of evil things? We find how well the Apostle used Satan himself, handing a man over to him for the destruction of the flesh so that his spirit might be saved on the day of the Lord[^19], and others so that they might learn not to blaspheme[^20].

**8. 17.** As for your further addition, “God cannot be the author of an evil thing,” in what sense do you say this? He knows better how to speak than you do, and through the Prophet he says that he creates evils[^21]. Besides, whatever the standing of this addition of yours, what has it to do with me, since I did not concede what it is attached to? I showed that the constitution of bodies does not follow as shameful even if I conceded that every mingling of bodies, or of the two sexes, is evil. For let us concede that God is the author of no evil: does this mean he is not the author of the constitution of bodies, which I in no way conceded to be evil, since nothing conceded earlier forced me to do so? Your inference therefore remains empty and laughable when you conclude, “All bodies, then, are attributed to an evil author.” A much truer conclusion is this: if even the evil mingling of adulterers does not make the constitution of bodies evil, or if the mingling of the two sexes is good even in adulterers but evil people use it badly—and therefore still less can the constitution of bodies be evil on that account—then bodies are rightly attributed to God as their author. Thus there is no pit whose fear would make me return, as it were, to the road to which you seem to want to recall me. Still, tell me what that road is, and unfold your reasoning.

**9. 18.** “The good God,” you say, “through whom all things were made, himself formed the members of our body.” I concede that this is entirely true. You continue and add, “He who made bodies also divided the sexes; he distinguished in appearance what he would join in activity, and the difference of members provided the reason for their union.” I assent to this too. You then infer, “Therefore the mingling of bodies comes from him from whom their origin comes.” Who would deny this? “Since you will have conceded this unwillingly,” you say, “it follows that so many good things—bodies, sexes, unions—cannot have evil fruits.” Here too you speak truly: the fruit of these good things is a human being, who, insofar as he is human, is good. But the evil in him—from which he must be healed by the Savior, delivered by the Redeemer, cleansed by the washing, rescued by exorcism, and absolved by the blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins—is the fruit not of bodies, sexes, and unions but of original and ancient sin. For if I said of the offspring of adulterers, “So many evils—wantonness, shamefulness, crime—could not have a good fruit,” you would rightly answer me that the human being born of adulterers is not the fruit of wantonness, shamefulness, and crime, evils whose author is the devil, but of bodies, sexes, and unions, good things whose author is God. So I most rightly tell you that the evil with which a human being is born is not the fruit of bodies, sexes, and unions, good things whose author is God, but of the first transgression, whose author is the devil.

**9. 19.** Far be it from us to say, as you falsely charge, that “God makes human beings so that the devil may hold them by a lawful right.” Though it belongs to divine rather than diabolical power that unclean generation is subjected to the unclean prince unless purified through regeneration, God does not make human beings so that the devil may have, as it were, a household. He makes them by that goodness by which he grants existence to all natures and by which he makes even the devil himself subsist. Were he to withdraw that goodness from things, they would immediately cease to exist. Just as he does not create cattle in the herds and flocks of the impious in order that they be sacrificed to demons, though he knows they will do this, so also, where he sees generation subject to sin, he does not withhold his goodness from its creation, according to the most beautiful order of the ages that he has established.

**10. 20.** But after this argument, by which you deceive yourself and imagine you have accomplished something, you heap on the usual insult and then add: “Perhaps I shall say that the testimony of the Scriptures, not syllogisms, should prove that children born from the mingling of bodies are attributed to God's work”—as though anyone denying this could be a Christian. Thus you try to show through scriptural testimonies, as though it were disputed between us, something we most readily confess and most gladly proclaim. You labor in vain, not to answer us but to fill books. Yet your statement, “To express the reality of the acts, the Prophet came close to endangering modesty, because he said, *The two shall be in one flesh*[^22],” should have sufficiently reminded you that nothing in God's works would have been shameful unless there had first been a reason why human nature should be ashamed at the disgrace it had deserved.

**11. 21.** In praise of lust, you also say that “it was restored by God's gift to the bodies of Abraham and Sarah, worn out and deadened by old age”[^23]. And with an accusatory cry you challenge me “to assert, if I can, that something I see God sometimes bestow as a gift belongs to the devil's work.” As though, if God raises a lame man and restores him to the living—a man who, when dead, certainly could no longer limp—he must also be thought to have bestowed his lameness as a gift! Thus, if the vigor their bodies possessed in younger years was restored, it was certainly restored according to the condition of this body of death. They did not need to be recalled to the state Adam had before sin, so that they could procreate children without the law in their members that fights against the law of the mind[^24].

**11. 22.** Indeed, Abraham's body should be understood as deadened in this sense: he was no longer able to beget children from every woman capable of bearing them. For advancing age is said to bring it about that an old man can beget a child from a young woman when he can no longer do so from an older one, although she could bear a child by a young man. Those who then lived so long undoubtedly reached much later the decrepitude that made them altogether incapable of being aroused for intercourse—if, indeed, this can happen through age to any healthy man. For while I was working on this book, we were told of an old man in his eighty-fourth year who had lived chastely for twenty-five years with his devout wife and had now bought himself a female lyre player for his lust. By the measure of human life today, he is certainly in more advanced old age than Abraham was at a hundred, when he still had another seventy years to live. Hence it is more reasonable to believe that God granted his servants the fertility they lacked. Two causes are given why Sarah could not bear children and her womb was deadened: one was the barrenness present from her youth; the other was age, not because she was ninety but because her monthly courses had already ceased. For it is established that, if women's menstrual discharges cease through age, they can no longer conceive, even if they were fertile before they ceased. Scripture therefore chose not to leave this unmentioned, so that the glory of the miracle God was to work in their offspring might be increased. But when she gave her husband the slave woman through whom she wished to obtain children because she herself could not, she was moved not by age but by her barrenness. For Scripture says, *Sarah, Abraham's wife, bore him no children*[^25]. These are her words to her husband: *Behold, God has shut me up so that I cannot bear children*[^26]. Considering the ages of both, they would then have been decrepit if they had been people of our time: Abraham was about eighty-five and Sarah seventy-five. For it is written, *Abraham was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to Abraham*[^27]. Accordingly, we find that he had intercourse with the slave woman about a year earlier, when Ishmael was conceived. What married couple nowadays begets children at that age unless such a miracle occurs by divine power? Yet they could have done so if Sarah had not been barren, since he could do so with Hagar and she had not yet advanced so far in age that her monthly courses had ceased. Abraham's body was therefore deadened in this sense: he could no longer beget from Sarah, even if she were fertile, since she was approaching the age when that flow of blood at monthly intervals would cease. Medical authorities have determined that women in whom it has completely ceased cannot conceive. If this were false, Scripture would certainly not have taken care to say, *Sarah's monthly courses had ceased*, after already saying, *Abraham and Sarah were old, advanced in their days*[^28]. By the measure of time in which people then lived far longer, they could no longer generate at that age, when Abraham was a hundred and Sarah ninety, even if she had not been barren and they had intercourse a year earlier, when perhaps her monthly courses still flowed. She might therefore still have conceived if her husband had been young; but she could not then, because Abraham's body was so deadened by old age that he could not beget from a woman of that age, though he could from a young woman, as he later could from Keturah[^29]. Yet there too it could be said that the gift of fertility he received for Isaac's birth persisted in him. By today's measure, however, when human beings live far shorter lives, a couple is said to be capable of generation when their combined ages are under a hundred. But if their ages added together exceed a hundred, they are asserted to be incapable of procreating children, even if the woman is fertile and, her monthly courses still flowing, could conceive by a young man. Thus it was even legally established that no one should have *the privilege of children* unless the combined computation of both spouses' ages was shown to have passed a hundred.

**11. 23.** God's miracle, then, made Isaac's conception possible not by restoring lust to his parents but by granting fertility. The former could exist even at their age; the latter could not, with so many causes preventing it. Yet even if lust, as we discussed above, had itself revived when their deadened old members in a sense came back to life through God's gift, it would certainly follow the condition of corruptible flesh: it would exist in this body of death, though it could not exist in paradise before sin in that body of life. God now bestows the gift of fertility on each person according to the condition of this penal body, not according to that happiness where there was nothing in the flesh to desire against the spirit and be restrained by the spirit's desire against it. For peace, not war, befitted human nature before sin. But in that part of your reasoning you labored in vain, as though we could say that “Isaac was procreated without the concupiscence of the flesh or a man's seed.” We do not say this, and therefore we disregard and pass over everything you argued about it.

**12. 24.** You thought you had discovered something with remarkable acuteness when you said, “Even if the devil created human beings, they would be evil through no fault of their own, and therefore would not even be evil, since no one can be other than what he was born, and it is unjust to demand of him more than he can do.” We too habitually say this against the Manichaeans, who say that the nature they imagine evil in their fables is not a good nature corrupted but one evil without beginning and unchangeably. Human nature, however, according to the Catholic faith, was established good but was corrupted by sin and justly condemned. It is neither surprising nor unjust that a condemned root produces condemned offspring, unless he whose hand was not lacking in creating them also does not withhold his mercy in delivering them. You begrudge this mercy to the wretched when you say that little children have no evil from which to be delivered.

**12. 25.** But you who crush wretched infancy with a false defense and assail it with destructive praise, why do you not admit to God's kingdom so many images of God in little children who deserve no evil, if they are not baptized? Have they failed themselves, that they should be deprived of the kingdom and punished with such mournful exile because they did not do what they were utterly unable to do? Where do you place the further fact that they will also lack life because they have not eaten the flesh or drunk the blood of the Son of man[^30]? Hence, as I mentioned above, unless Pelagius had condemned in an ecclesiastical court those who say, “Infants have eternal life even if they are not baptized,” he would have left condemned himself. By what justice, I ask, is God's image alienated from God's kingdom and God's life, having in no way transgressed God's law? Do you not hear how the Apostle denounces certain people whom he says are alienated *from the life of God through the ignorance in them, because of the blindness of their heart*[^31]? Will an unbaptized little child be bound by this sentence or not? If you say, “He will not,” the Gospel's truth, with even Pelagius's tongue as witness, will defeat and punish you. For where is God's life except in God's kingdom, which they cannot enter unless reborn of water and the Spirit[^32]? But if you say, “He will,” you confess the punishment: state the fault. You confess the penalty: state the desert. You find nothing in your doctrine that you can produce. Now at last, if there is any Christian understanding in you, acknowledge even in little children the transmission of death and condemnation, to be punished by justice as their due and delivered freely by God's grace. In their redemption God's mercy can be praised; in their perdition God's truth cannot be accused, because *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth*[^33].

**13. 26.** You divide, define, and discuss, like a physician, the genus, species, measure, and excess of concupiscence, asserting that “its genus lies in vital fire, its species in genital movement, its measure in the conjugal act, and its excess in the intemperance of fornication.” Yet after this whole supposedly subtle and lengthy discussion, when I ask you briefly and clearly why that vital fire has rooted warfare in the human being, so that through it the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit must desire against the flesh[^34], why someone who chooses to consent to this vital fire is struck with a mortal wound, I think the very ink of your book will blush and turn to red lead. Behold the vital fire, which not only fails to obey the choice of our soul, the true life of the flesh, but is often aroused against its choice with disordered and shameful movements! Unless the spirit desires against it, this vital fire kills our good life.

**13. 27.** After a long discussion you conclude, as it were: “Rightly, then, the origin of concupiscence is defined as vital fire; once this is established, carnal concupiscence must be attributed to him through whom carnal life exists.” You say this as though you could prove—or, however shameless you are, dare truly to suppose—that in the original constitution of human beings, before deserved condemnation followed their fault, this carnal concupiscence either existed in paradise or waged its most disgraceful battles against the spirit with the disordered movements we now observe. You then add: “Therefore this appetite's fault lies neither in its genus nor its species nor its measure, but in its excess, because its genus and species belong to the Creator's work, its measure to honorable choice, and its excess to the vice of the will.” How neatly those empty words sounded to you, a man who does not think about what he says! If the measure of this appetite belongs to honorable choice, what honorable married person's choice would wish this appetite to be aroused except when needed? Yet he cannot do what he wishes. What honorable continent person's choice would wish this appetite to be aroused at any time? Yet he cannot do what he wishes. Hence the human being cries, *To will is present with me, but I find no way to accomplish the good*[^35]. Since, then, in its very movement it has no measure according to the choice of our will, and in its effect it does not moderate itself, but an honorable spirit imposes a measure on it through vigilant struggle, what is it you praise, evil men, instead of crying to God, *Deliver us from evil*[^36]?

**14. 28.** What help is your statement that “lust is interrupted by weakness,” as though it were not entirely extinguished by death, when the person it has overcome and enslaved is no longer called to battle but receives the defeated person's due? Here is the more bitter point, where in your contentiousness you fail to understand the transmission of death: this unhealthy movement is present when we are healthy. “Either it is honorably exercised in married people,” you say, “or restrained by virtue in the chaste.” Is that really so? Is that your experience? Is this evil, then—or this good thing of yours—not restrained by married people? Surely, whenever desire comes, spouses throw themselves down and rush upon one another whenever it tickles them; this appetite is not even deferred until bedtime, but bodily mingling is deemed lawful whenever this natural good of yours is spontaneously aroused! If that is the married life you led, stop entrusting your argument to your own experience, and inquire instead of others how it should be lived or taught. Yet I would be surprised if you had not restrained at least adulterous desires or recognized that they ought to be restrained. But if conjugal chastity too restrains this plague, both because of lust's immoderate wallowing even within marriage and because of damnable concupiscences, lest anything be done beyond the natural use of one's spouse, why do you say, “It is honorably exercised in married people,” as though this appetite were always honorable in a spouse, and nothing were granted it at least by way of pardon, as the Apostle says[^37]? How much better you would have said, “It is honorably exercised in spouses' moderation”! Were you afraid that here too it would be understood as an evil, which even spouses bridle through the care of moderation? Finally, now at least, living continently, recognize the evil horse in Ambrose's four-horse chariot, and do not praise with heart or mouth what you are compelled to bridle by virtue. “The fourth,” you say, “is perpetrated by the wanton, namely the excess of that pleasure, and because it arises from insolence, not from nature, it is rightly condemned.” From whose insolence, I ask? Wantonness's or concupiscence's? To avoid offending your client, you will answer, “Wantonness's.” But everyone judges that there is no sin of wantonness except through consent to concupiscence. Is that, then, no evil, consent to which is sin? Yet this evil exists in the flesh that desires against the spirit, even if it is absent from the spirit that refuses consent and even desires against it. Cry out, therefore, *Deliver us from evil*[^38], and do not add the evil of false praise to this evil.

**15. 29.** You eloquently place conjugal chastity between the wanton and the continent, as though in the middle: “It is indignant that the former have committed unlawful acts and marvels that the latter have even disdained lawful ones. Assigned the region of the outermost boundary, it abhors the barbarity of those rushing beyond it and reveres the splendor of those shining above it; with modest hands it both comforts those burning and praises those who do not need such a remedy.” I greatly delight in truth so eloquently expressed. But tell me, please: as you say—and say most eloquently and truly—conjugal chastity praises the continent precisely because they do not need the remedy it sees that it itself needed, namely, according to the Apostle, *let the one who does not contain himself marry*[^39]. Why, then, when I call this concupiscence a disease, do you deny it, though you confess that it needs a remedy? If you acknowledge the remedy, acknowledge the disease; if you deny the disease, deny the remedy. I ask you, yield at last to the truth speaking to you even through your own mouth: no one provides a remedy for health.

**16. 30.** You also rightly say, “Marriage cannot commend itself upon examination if it is praised by comparison with an evil.” This is true. Marriage is certainly a good in its own kind, but good because spouses keep the faith of the marriage bed, because they unite the two sexes for the sake of receiving offspring, and because they abhor the impiety of separation. These are the marital goods that make marriage good, and we have very often said they could have existed even if no one had sinned. After sin, however, a struggle was added to them, arising not from happiness but from necessity, so that marriage too should fight with its own good against the evil of concupiscence, allowing it to reach nothing unlawful, though it never ceases to incite to such things with movements sometimes weaker, sometimes more vehement; and so that spouses should use its evil well in propagating children. But who could deny the evil—unless he ignores the Apostle's warning, *I say this by way of pardon, not by way of command*[^40]—when spouses have intercourse, overcome by the desire not of receiving offspring but of satisfying carnal pleasure? This is not praised but pardoned by comparison with something worse, with marriage intervening and pleading on its behalf.

**17. 31.** After this you return, for some reason I cannot see, to the example of Abraham and Sarah, to which I think I have already replied sufficiently. But something had escaped you that you wished to add when it came to mind later. It is human; it happens. Let us hear, then, what it is. You say that “what has now been manifested in the region of Africa was a prophetic event: neither the husband nor the modesty of the beautiful and holy woman who bore the figure of the Church was safe, and there she was preserved intact by divine power.” To avoid lingering uselessly over all your words, you turn to the person you are writing to and say: “We must pray to this God, most blessed brother Turbantius, most beloved fellow priest, that with equal powers in this age he may not delay to rescue the Catholic Church, his Son's bride, mature, fruitful, chaste, and beautiful, from violation by the Manichaeans who plunder in Africa or from Africa.” This prayer is rather ours against the Manichaeans, the Donatists, and other heretics, or any enemies of the Christian and Catholic name established in Africa. But against you, since you are an overseas pestilence to be overcome by Christ the Savior, are we therefore plundering from Africa because we set against you one martyr from here, Cyprian, through whom we prove that we defend the ancient Catholic faith against the empty, profane novelty of your error? What wickedness! Did the Church of God established in Africa lack your prayers when most blessed Cyprian preached these things you assail? When he said, “Much less should an infant be barred from baptism, who, newly born, has sinned in nothing except that, born carnally according to Adam, he contracted the contagion of ancient death in his first birth, so that not his own but another's sins are forgiven him”[^41]—when Cyprian had learned and was teaching these things, was the help of your prayers lacking, by which Sarah might be preserved intact in the region of Africa and the Church's beauty rescued from violation by the Manichaeans, who according to your understanding had deceived even Cyprian himself before Mani's name was heard in Roman lands? See what monstrous and mad things you say against the most ancient Catholic faith, because you have nothing to say.

**17. 32.** But however much you evade, O Pelagian heresy, building new engines and devising new ambushes against the walls of the most ancient truth, the “Punic disputant,” as your defender insultingly calls me—the Punic disputant, I say, not I but Cyprian the Punic—“with this wound sacrifices you and exacts the penalty for your wicked doctrine”[^42]. What if I had named as many bishops from Africa as I named from other parts of the world? Or what if more of those very men were Africans? One comes from here, the others from elsewhere, and their agreement from East and West pierces you through. Yet you are blinded by such obstinacy that you do not see it is rather you who wish to corrupt the Church's ancient beauty, that is, its ancient faith, like the chastity of the most beautiful elderly Sarah. For if the Manichaeans violated the Church through God's holy bishops and memorable teachers—Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Basil, John, Innocent, and Jerome—tell me, Julian, who gave birth to you? Was she chaste or a prostitute who bore you through the womb of spiritual grace into the light you have abandoned? Or, to defend Pelagian doctrines, do you defame the womb of Christ's bride and your mother at the instigation not of wicked error but of madness? So utterly unable is this new ugliness to find a lie against Sarah's ancient beauty that it accuses of Manichaean blasphemy the agreement of so many glorious Catholic bishops, whose opinions are so clearly manifest, including men who never even heard Mani's name.

**17. 33.** “But from this digression, to which an impulse” not “of grief,” as you say, but of lost shame “had carried you away, you return to what you had undertaken”—your ravings. Bringing in the Apostle's testimony[^43], you try to confirm what you said about the deadened members of Abraham and Sarah. Let it suffice that I have already discussed this above as much as seemed sufficient. What Christian does not know that “he who made the first human being from dust fashions all others from seed”? But from seed already corrupted and condemned, whose offspring in part remain in punishment through truth and in part are delivered from evil through mercy. Therefore, contrary to your supposition and conclusion, “the assertion of natural sin is not strangled by any of your snares.” For it is not your defense with empty words through your novel doctrine that purifies nature, depraved by the first transgressor's will, but God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord.

**18. 34.** Accordingly, I do not think, as you falsely charge, that “spouses generated without bodily heat”; nor do I think that “God did not make the human being, or made him for the devil, or that the devil made him.” For even the parents themselves cannot make a human being: God makes him from the parents. Not even the devil can withdraw himself from God's power, much less human nature, which he subjected to himself through the desert of sin by God's condemnation. Since this is so, it is rather you who, though not the devil's worshiper, as you call me, are proved his helper, however much you appear to accuse him. For you contend that all little children ought not to be healed by Christ from the evil through which the devil holds them, when with unsound teaching you maintain that they are sound. I, however, according to sound faith, say that “Isaac too was formed from the same pleasure of concupiscence” as other human beings, with the sole exception from this evil of him through whom we are delivered from evil. Nor do I deny that “the hand of divine providence intervenes even in sinners' generative organs.” For it reaches mightily from end to end and orders all things sweetly[^44], and nothing defiled enters it[^45]. Thus it works whatever it wills even from things unclean and contaminated, while itself remaining clean and uncontaminated. There is no need to try to prove to me by lengthy circuitous arguments what I concede. Instead, answer this if you can: why would Isaac's soul have perished from his people unless he had been circumcised on the eighth day with the sign of Christ's baptism[^46]? Explain, if you can, for what desert he would have been struck with so great a punishment unless delivered from it by that sacrament. It cannot be denied that God brought to life Sarah's deadened womb for the emission of seed and Abraham's deadened body to generate as young men do, so that offspring might be born to parents of that age. But Isaac, born innocent as regards personal sins, as he would have been even if born of adulterers, had deserved what, that his soul should perish from his people unless circumcision came to his aid? Do not wander through a mass of obscure, entangled, superfluous matters: answer this one clear, simple, necessary question.

**18. 35.** You insert the Apostle's testimony, not for the purpose for which he used it but for one of your own imagining. It would now be lengthy and superfluous to discuss this, since it is not our subject. Still, you insert his words: *And how will God judge the world? For if God's truth has abounded through my lie to his glory, why am I still judged as a sinner?*[^47] You then add that “through these words the Apostle shows that God has lost the authority to judge if he did not observe due measure in commanding.” If, therefore, as you think, “the Apostle said this so that those who maintained that mortals' sins advance God's praise and that he therefore commanded impossibilities to provide himself material for mercy should restrain themselves, and this apostolic statement demonstrates”—as you suppose—“that human beings are rightly judged because they failed to perform possible commands, but would be unjustly judged if they failed to do things commanded that were impossible,” what will you say of Isaac? Nothing either possible or impossible was commanded him, yet he would have been struck with the penalty of his soul's destruction if he were not circumcised on the eighth day. Even now, do you not at last look at that original command given in paradise, possible and easy, through whose contempt and violation all who come from one human being have that sin in common, as though in the mass of their origin? Hence there is *a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until* *the day of burial in the mother of all*[^48]. And because no one is delivered from this condemned generation in Adam unless regenerated in Christ, Isaac would therefore have perished unless he had received the sign of that regeneration. He would not have perished undeservedly, because he would have left this life without the sign of regeneration, having entered it condemned through condemned generation. Or, if this is not the cause, give another. God is good; God is just. He can deliver some without good merits because he is good; he cannot condemn anyone without evil merits because he is just. An infant eight days old had no evil merit from personal sins. Why would he have been condemned if not circumcised, unless he contracted it from his origin?

**19. 36.** Proceed to the rest, and weave things still emptier than the empty things before—not, I mean, the words of Holy Scripture that you recall, but what you strive to infer from them. You say that “perfect ignorance must be understood to be called righteousness because God said to Abimelech, who was about to have intercourse with Sarah, unaware that she was another man's wife: *I too knew that you did this with a clean heart*[^49]. Therefore the condition of those born is not injured by the will of their parents, because even if that will were evil, it would in no way come to their knowledge.” Why, then, do you not call them righteous, if perfect ignorance should be called righteousness? For nothing is more perfect than little children's ignorance; therefore nothing should be called more righteous. Where is what you thought must be maintained above: “Little children are born neither righteous nor unrighteous, which they will become by their own acts, but infancy is merely rich in the endowment of innocence”? Are these not your words: “A human being, then, is born full of innocence but capable of virtue, to deserve praise or blame through the addition of purpose”? Will you say that righteousness is not a virtue? How, then, is an infant not yet full of virtue but capable of it, when he is full of ignorance, which you call righteousness, unless you deny that righteousness is a virtue? Will you not wake up even now, struck by this absurdity, and regret having said it? For the Lord's words are awake, but you are asleep. He did not say to the king, “I knew that you have a righteous heart,” or “that you have a clean heart,” since it is written, *Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God*[^50]. You, however, present this Abimelech as an example of a sinner. Instead, God said, *I knew* *that you did this with a clean heart.* Not everything, or any other deeds, but *this*, in which he had no consciousness of adultery.

**19. 37.** Yet I am surprised that with this example you cite you try to establish what you cannot, while failing to see there what you do not want to hear. You try to make us believe that Abraham's prayer restored lust to the women because it is written, *God had shut every womb from outside in Abimelech's household because of Sarah, Abraham's wife*[^51]. You want this shutting understood as though God's anger had deprived those women of lust, whereas these words rather indicate that a womb was closed by some illness, so that a woman could not have intercourse to become pregnant or give birth when pregnant. And you did not notice—you who refuse to allow that anyone can be punished by divine judgment for another's sins rather than his own—how Abimelech could sin, although he did not sin with an adulterous heart, and God could punish his sin, however slight, in the women belonging to him. You see the contagion of sin pass from a man to the women with whom he had intercourse or over whom he ruled, and yet you refuse to allow it to pass from parents to offspring propagated from their seed. Consider, then, how *unsearchable is the depth of the judgments of God's wisdom and knowledge*[^52], and stop chattering against the secrets of original sin.

**20. 38.** Next you begin to discuss the excess of concupiscence, which you call blameworthy, as though when it is moderated and a married person uses it well, the horse itself, which is evil, should be praised rather than its charioteer. What help, then, are testimonies from the Scriptures showing how God either forbids or condemns lust's excess? Consider rather that whatever we shudder at in the shamefulness of any disgraceful acts perpetrated with the genital members, carnal concupiscence, unless restrained, brings about through its own movements, which in sleep it carries through even in the bodies of the chaste to an effect that makes them groan.

**20. 39.** You say, “Why would God seek righteous people in Sodom[^53] if nature made them such?”—as though we said that carnal concupiscence could not be restrained by the superior nature of the mind. We say, rather, that it is so much an evil that it must be overcome by fighting against its resistance, until, like a wound in the body, it is healed by a complete cure.

**20. 40.** But if you think “the Apostle praised lust by calling the use of a woman natural, where he says that some abandoned the natural use of a woman and burned in desire for one another”[^54], you will certainly be compelled to praise every use of a woman. Thus you will also praise illicit relations committed with women, since there too the use is certainly natural, though condemnable because it is not lawful. Hence the children born from it are called not legitimate but natural. The Apostle therefore did not praise carnal concupiscence by that word: he called natural the use from which human nature can continue to exist through birth.

**20. 41.** You say that “the Sodomites also sinned with the created things bread and wine.” Thus you want us to understand that lust is good but the people who use it badly are blameworthy, just as the creation of bread and wine is good, though sin occurs through their misuse. You so little understand what you are saying that you fail to see that the created things bread and wine do not desire against the spirit; rather, those who use them badly desire them shamefully, and they enter our body from outside. They must therefore be used more sparingly and restrictively, lest concupiscence itself, which is an evil within us and our own, be aroused against us more violently and irresistibly as the corruptible body weighs down the mind much more heavily through an abundance of material. This evil, then—which anyone who fights against it, or anyone whom it subjugates, shows to be evil—is well used both by a parent when chastely begetting a child and by God when providentially creating a human being.

**21. 42.** Now, then, consider, I ask you; now lay aside the desire to win, so that truth may conquer you to your healing, and consider whether you should accept my opinion or yours. It is good that you say you are “giving a brief reminder, so that what you have accomplished throughout the book may cling to the reader's heart.” You explain what this brief reminder is: “Whoever keeps the measure of natural concupiscence uses a good well; whoever fails to keep the measure uses a good badly; but whoever, through love of holy virginity, has disdained even that measure does better by not using a good. For, confident of his own health and strength, he has disdained remedies to engage in glorious struggles.” I answer your words thus: whoever keeps the measure of carnal concupiscence uses an evil well; whoever fails to keep the measure uses an evil badly; but whoever, through love of holy virginity, has disdained even that measure does better by not using an evil. For, confident of divine assistance and gift, he has disdained the lowest remedies to engage in more glorious struggles. The whole case between us turns on this dispute about using this thing well: is it good or evil? I would indeed wish you not to reject the distinguished judges whom I showed you in my preceding books to be instructed in sound doctrine and to have pronounced judgments on this matter without any partisan interest. But since, unless you correct yourself, you will undoubtedly prepare to accuse them too—or, to put it more gently, criticize them—I shall use you instead as judge for my opinion against yours, not somewhere else but in your very book, and not in another passage but this one. For you said that “holy virginity, confident of its own health and strength, disdained remedies to engage in glorious struggles.” I ask: what remedies did it disdain? You will answer, “Marriage.” I ask: against what disease are these remedies necessary? A remedy takes its name from remedying, that is, healing. We both see the remedy of marriage together; why do you praise the disease of lust, which you see leads to death unless opposed by the restraint of continence or the remedy of marriage? I already argued this with you above, where you so eloquently placed conjugal chastity between the wanton and the continent, “which with modest hands both comforts those burning and praises those who do not need such a remedy.” I therefore repeat the same words; hear again what I answered you there briefly and clearly: “When I call this concupiscence a disease, why do you deny it, though you confess that it needs a remedy? If you acknowledge the remedy, acknowledge the disease; if you deny the disease, deny the remedy. I ask you, yield at last to the truth speaking to you even through your own mouth: no one provides a remedy for health.”

**21. 43.** What are the “glorious struggles,” as you call them, of holy virgins, except that they are not overcome by evil but overcome evil with good? I preferred to call them not glorious but more glorious. For conjugal chastity too wins a palm, though a lesser one, from the subjection of this evil. It too wages war against carnal concupiscence lest it exceed the limits of the marriage bed; it wages war lest concupiscence invade a time for prayer fixed by the spouses' agreement. And if this conjugal chastity has such strength and is so great a gift of God as to carry out what the marriage contract prescribes, it wages a fiercer conflict even in the marriage bed, lest it touch even the spouse's body beyond what suffices for generating children. Such chastity does not use women during menstruation, pregnancy, or an age when it is certain they can no longer conceive. The inclination to intercourse does not conquer it at all; it relaxes only when the result of generation is hoped for. If something is done with one's spouse that is not against nature but exceeds the limits of the marriage contract, it is found pardonable in the Apostle[^55], because the boundary of the spouse's flesh is not crossed. Yet even to avoid crossing that boundary, battle is waged against the evil of concupiscence. It is so much an evil that the struggle of chastity resists it to prevent its doing harm.

**21. 44.** And you, unless I am mistaken, are engaged in this struggle; because you think you are fighting faithfully, you fear defeat. By what, I ask you, do you fear being defeated: good or evil? Or are you so afraid of being defeated by me that you still deny it is evil and praise as good what you fear will defeat you? Placed between two adversaries, you are driven into great straits: you want to defeat me through eloquence and lust through continence. But by fighting against it you confess it is evil; by praising it you abandon the good of truth. Yet I defeat you, both assailant and praiser of evil, by bringing you before a judge who is none other than yourself. You want to defeat concupiscence by attacking it and me by praising it. But I reply: to defeat the praiser, let the fighter judge. If it is evil, why praise it? If it is good, why attack it? If it is neither good nor evil, why praise it, why attack it? Therefore, as long as you assail lust, you will be a judge for me against yourself. Or will you perhaps stop fighting lust so as not to be defeated in your contest with me, saying to yourself, “Better not to fight than to show by fighting that what I praise is evil”? Please do not! For what am I, whom you so greatly desire to defeat? Let truth defeat you instead, so that you may defeat lust. For if you stop fighting it, you will be defeated, bound, and dragged into every uncleanness. Though this is a dreadful evil, it will not even help you achieve what you desire: even so you will be defeated by me, or rather by the very truth I proclaim. But as both praiser and vanquisher of concupiscence you are defeated even by your own judgment, since you praise the evil whose defeat you glory in. If, however, you now stop fighting lest the fighter's struggle defeat the praiser's tongue, I shall defeat a captive of concupiscence, a deserter from continence, no longer by his own judgment but by wisdom's.

**21. 45.** Our case, then, is settled. However much you praise carnal concupiscence, as long as you fight against it you see how truly the Apostle John said of it, and of nothing else, that it *is not from the Father*[^56]. For if, as you say, “whoever does not keep its measure uses a good badly,” it is itself good even in those who use it badly. What, then, is that which *is not from the Father*? Do you intend to praise even that, however you understand it? But if it is evil, where will it be, when will it be? For this concupiscence, even if someone uses it badly, will be good; and, as you say, it will not itself be evil, but the one who uses a good badly. Thus John said in vain that carnal concupiscence is not from the Father, for you maintain that it is good and therefore from the Father even when someone uses it badly, because he uses a good badly. Nor can you say, “When moderated, it is from the Father; when immoderate, *it is not from the Father*,” because even then, according to you, it is a good that an evil person uses badly. But you will be freed from these straits if you believe not your tongue but your struggle. For continence is from the Father, and it would not fight against concupiscence unless concupiscence were not from the Father. Therefore this very thing against which, if you live continently, you fight fiercely is not from the Father. You would not fight against it unless it fought back; and it would not fight back against you as you do what is given and loved by the Father, if it were from the Father.

**21. 46.** From this and with this a human being is born, God's good work, not without the evil that the origin of generation transmits and the grace of regeneration heals. Thus I rightly said, “The good of marriage cannot be accused because of the original evil contracted from it, just as the evil of adultery and fornication cannot be excused because of the natural good born from them”[^57]. By natural good I meant what you yourself praise along with me; by original evil I meant what you resist in its movements along with me, though fighting against me with its praises. What you were born is not evil, but that with which you were born and against which you fight spiritually because you have been reborn. What you were born belongs to God's creation and your parents' fertility; what you fight because you have been reborn belongs to the transgression sown by the devil's cunning, from which Christ's grace has delivered you. Thus you first used this evil well in marriage and now oppose it in yourself, no longer guilty from it as you were at birth, but rescued from that guilt only because you were reborn, so that, redeemed, you may reign with Christ, provided this heresy does not make you perish with the devil, but rather, as we desire, you confess the evil against which you wage war, so that you may rejoice in perpetual peace when it has not been separated off as though it were another nature but completely healed within you.

**21. 47.** I am therefore not “like a medicine seller,” as you say, “who promised a beast that would eat itself.” But take care lest that beastly movement against which you seem to struggle in your flesh, just as it has perverted you when praised, afterward consume you when released. For I did not say, as you falsely charge, that “marriage is both a great good and a great evil,” as though that statement thus ate itself. I said that in one human being nature is good and vice evil, something you yourself certainly confess in adulterers, neither condemning their nature because of their vice nor approving their vice because of their nature. I called marriage good, from which you were born; I called evil that which was contracted not from marriage but from a corrupted origin, and against which, reborn, you wage war.

**21. 48.** It is ridiculous that you say I “walk in Epicurus's ways and cut all the reins by which desires are restrained.” What if I were praising bodily pleasure? For you do quite eloquently what Epicurus did unlearnedly and crudely, as though you were therefore his opponent because he did not know how to say eloquently what you say! Again, I suspect, you will labor to show how you can be both a praiser of pleasure and no Epicurean. But do not labor; I will free you from this concern. You are no Epicurean, because he placed the whole human good in bodily pleasure, while you try to place a great part of human good in virtue, though you do not know true virtue, that is, the virtue of true piety. For God said to the human being, *Behold, piety is wisdom*[^58]. Where does a human being get this unless given it by him of whom it is written, *The Lord makes the blind wise*[^59], and of whom we also read, *If anyone lacks wisdom, let him ask God*[^60]? But if you are no Epicurean, though you have borrowed something from him in your praise of pleasure, how much less am I, who hold regarding fleshly pleasure what Ambrose holds: namely, that it is the enemy of righteousness, and that a human being formed through the pleasure of concupiscence undergoes the contagion of sins before being born[^61]. As for our conduct and how we live, that is evident to those among whom we live. The present question concerns Catholic doctrine and faith: let there be no deserter's faithlessness in you. For your tongue, as though a censor's, does not frighten me. I confess that I teach people what I learned in the Apostolic Letters: *if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^62]. I confess that among God's people and with God's people I beat my breast and truthfully say, *Forgive us our debts*[^63]. Do not insult us for this; precisely because these things displease you, you are heretics. We trust in God's true mercy, you in your false virtue. You say God's grace is given according to our merits, though Pelagius would have been condemned by Catholic bishops unless he condemned that proposition. We confess that grace is given freely, and therefore is called grace, and that all the merits of the saints come from it, as the Apostle says, *By God's grace I am what I am*[^64]. For this you mock us and, with neck held high, despise us in comparison with yourselves. *For we have become a reproach to those who abound and an object of contempt to the proud*[^65]*. For you have confounded the poor man's counsel, because the Lord is his hope*[^66].

**21. 49.** Yet in the matter we are now discussing, I do not see why you say I “cut all the reins by which desires are restrained,” when I maintain that all desires must be restrained with all the strength given to human beings according to God's grace. But I ask you whether these desires that you declare must be restrained, and accuse me of releasing, are good or evil. I think they belong not to horses or any animals other than ourselves but to us. Therefore there are evil desires within us, which we restrain by living well. You therefore accuse me of cutting the reins by which evil desires, not good ones, are restrained. One of these is carnal concupiscence, from which and with which infants are born, and on account of which they are reborn. I say chaste spouses use this evil well and adulterers badly; you, on the contrary, say adulterers use this good badly and chaste spouses well. We both say continence does better by not using it, but I call it an evil, you a good. Accordingly, although our conscience is known to God and our conduct also to the people among whom we live, we both profess continence. If we do what we profess, we both certainly restrain this concupiscence, both wage war against its resisting movements, both defeat it if we make progress. Yet the difference is this: I say I restrain an evil, you a good; I say an evil resists me, you a good resists you; I wage war against an evil, you against a good; I desire to defeat an evil, you a good. Hence one might think you are more eager to incite this concupiscence by praising it than to restrain it by continence.

**21. 50.** You profess to engage in glorious struggles through continence. Against what, I ask? What will you answer except against carnal concupiscence? But as friend or adversary? Surely you will answer, as adversary. *For the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh; these oppose one another*[^67], as the Apostle testifies. Perhaps, then, you pretend to praise it, if you are not pretending to fight it. For I do not see how you sincerely do both at once: praise it as a friend and attack it as an enemy. We believe one of these; choose which we should believe. If you fight from the heart, you do not praise from the heart. If your praise is sincere, your battle is collusion. Certainly, because I am not your enemy as the evil dwelling in your flesh is your enemy, although my greatest desire is that you should both condemn this evil with sound teaching and defeat it with holy living, yet of these two things, one of which you may be thought to do sincerely and the other to feign, I would prefer you to praise concupiscence falsely rather than fight it falsely. A lie in your tongue is more tolerable than in your life; a pretended opinion more tolerable than pretended continence. You feign the praise by which you oppose my opinion if you do not feign the chastity by which you oppose your concupiscence. Thus you will not much longer utter false words against me if you truly wage war against lust. But whether you feign one of these things or do both—which I do not know how you can do sincerely, without any pretense, both fighting what you praise and praising what you fight—I nevertheless believe what is more favorable about you and deal with you as a vanquisher of lust. I do not call marriage evil but say it uses an evil well; you say it uses a good well, calling carnal concupiscence good, though by defeating it in yourself you prove it evil. How married people, even those who use it well, wage war with it I have already discussed above.

**22. 51.** Since this is so, marriage is good insofar as it is marriage, and a human being, whether born of marriage or adultery, is good insofar as he is human, because insofar as he is human he is God's work. Yet because he is generated with and from that evil which conjugal chastity uses well, he must be released through regeneration from the bond of that evil. Why, then, do you ask me “where original evil is,” when the lust within yourself, against which you fight, speaks more truly to you than you yourself do when you praise it? Why do you ask “how the human being God made is under the devil”? For how is he in death, which God did not make? “What does the devil recognize there as his own,” you ask, “if he created neither what was made nor that from which it was made?” Clearly what was made is a human being; that from which it was made is human seed; both are good. The devil created neither, but he himself sowed the defect of the seed. He does not recognize there his own good, since what we both praise is not his; he recognizes his own evil, against which we both fight. Nor is it rightly praised by one of us when both fight against it. Observe how you ask me “where evil in little children comes from among so many good things” and keep silent about what I placed in the very book you are answering, among the very words you are now answering, namely the Apostle's words: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^68]. You do not want people to hear or read this at this point, where it was most needed, lest they recognize their faith and despise your argument.

**23. 52.** You say I defined it thus: “A human being born of fornication is not guilty; one born of marriage is not innocent.” This is an outrageous and obvious slander. Rather, according to the Catholic faith that the fathers defended most clearly against you before you were born, I defined a human being, wherever born, as both innocent through having no personal sin and guilty through original sin. The substance of nature, whose author is God, is good even in adult sinners, who are certainly evil also through their own personal sins, which they have added to the evil with which they were born. Why should I fear the objection you mention, “that I impute all parents' sins to all their children”? Even if this were true—not regarding those already exercising their own choice of will, but regarding those being born—the substance of nature, whose author is God, would in no way be evil, but rather the vices within it, against which you too, to use your words, “engage in glorious struggles.”

**23. 53.** Just as, in your words, “when adulterers beget, a human being is born from the power of the seed, not from the shamefulness of their crimes,” so when spouses beget, a human being is born from the power of the seed, not from the honor of marriage. Or, if from both here, from both there. The fruit of marriage, insofar as it concerns marriage itself, is not the generation of human beings, who can also be born of adulterers, but the orderly reception of children. Why, then, is “that part of my opinion utterly false in which I said that the good of marriage cannot be accused because of the original evil contracted from it,” when the married couple used well the evil against which you fight so that you might be born, and that evil contracted by you from there would hold you guilty unless you were reborn? Therefore the spouses who use an evil well must not be accused, and the children must be regenerated to be delivered from evil. For if the good of marriage were only the good use of a good, one might then wonder how evil could be contracted from it. But since the good of marriage is the good use of an evil, what wonder if from the very evil that the goodness of marriage uses well there is contracted the evil that is original sin? What is astonishing is that, although the Apostles were *Christ's good fragrance*, good was drawn from it and evil too: *for to some they were the fragrance of life unto life, to others the fragrance of death unto death*[^69], though that fragrance was the use not of an evil but of a good. For they were *the good fragrance* of *Christ* because they used Christ's grace well. Therefore your statement is false: “If evil is contracted from it, it can be accused and cannot be excused.” Original evil is contracted from it, though good is also drawn from it, namely the orderly propagation of children. That evil is contracted from it not because marriage is good but because among its goods is the use of an evil. The marital union was established not because of carnal concupiscence, as you think, but because of the good produced from this evil. That good would indeed have come about without this evil if no one had sinned. Now, then, it cannot exist without this evil, but is not therefore evil. Conversely, no evil can exist without some good, but is not therefore good. God's work in nature is good, yet without it an evil will cannot exist. Accordingly, just as adultery cannot exist without nature's good but is not therefore good, so marriage cannot exist without the evil of concupiscence but is not therefore evil. Thus, even if you are granted that “every cause of evil is devoid of good,” this has no bearing on marriage, which is not the cause of evil. Marriage did not produce the evil of concupiscence; it found it already present, to use it well.

**24. 54.** “That thing,” you say, “does not escape punishment through participation in which other things too contract guilt.” If you think this of carnal concupiscence, your thought is not absurd. Faithful spouses use this evil well, and those generated from it contract guilt and therefore must themselves be regenerated. This evil does not escape punishment: its punishment is to be punished with those generated if they are not regenerated, but in the regenerated to be consumed when they have been healed in every respect. “If original evil is contracted even from marriage,” you say, “the marital union is a cause of evil.” What if someone else says, “If an evil will is derived from nature, the establishment of natures is a cause of evil”? Is that not utterly false? So too is your statement, though original evil is contracted not from marriage but from carnal concupiscence, the evil against which you yourself struggle. Spouses use this evil well if they unite solely for the sake of propagation. If the sin that passed into all human beings had not preceded, there certainly would have been no evil for them to use well, yet they would still have united to procreate children.

**24. 55.** As for the good and evil trees, I think I sufficiently demonstrated your error in the first book of this work. Since you repeat the same knots already untied, there is no need to linger over superfluities. You ask “through what sin is found in little children,” and enumerate many goods while keeping silent about the evil against which you fight. But while silent about that, you cry out: “The parents who through their union provided the cause of sin are rightly condemnable, since their labor brought it about that the devil attained dominion over human beings.” You can say this to God himself—not, I say, because he creates human beings who contract original sin, since you deny that, but because he nourishes and clothes countless impious people, including those whom he knows will persist in their impiety. Had he not done so, the devil certainly would not have human beings as slaves. But perhaps you will say: “In feeding them God attends only to the good whose author he is, that they are human beings.” So too, in begetting children, parents attend only to the good that they are human beings, especially since they do not know what they will become. Certainly, as you too confess, there would be no sin if no evil will had preceded, since even original sin—which we affirm and you deny—would certainly not exist unless nature had been corrupted by the first man's evil will. But there would also be no evil will if no nature, angelic or human, had preceded. Will you therefore call God the cause of sins because his will is the cause of mutable natures? Just as what is attributed to God the Creator is not that rational natures fall away from good but that they are good, so what should be attributed to parents who beget and use the evil of concupiscence well is not that their children are born with that evil but that they are good. It does not follow, as you think, that “the origin of those born, insofar as they are human beings, is derived from the devil because the origin of sin, without which no human being is born, is said to come from the devil.” For neither does the origin of mortal beings come from the devil because the origin of death does.

**24. 56.** You seek “a crack among so many defenses of innocence through which sin could have entered,” while the Apostle Paul shows you not a crack but a wide-open door, saying, *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all human beings*[^70]. But suppressing these words, you speak your own: “The devil's work is not allowed to pass through God's work.” Yet human beings are God's work, and sin is the devil's work, which the Apostle says passed into all. You cry, “If nature comes through God, there can be no original evil in it,” as though someone else might not think himself more religious in crying, “If nature comes through God, no evil can arise from it,” or “No evil can exist in it.” Yet that is false, because evil cannot arise except from nature, nor has it anywhere to exist if it is not in nature. I therefore confess that one who is born is God's work, even though contracting original evil, since what is God's work in him is good. For God's work is good even together with evil, not only in little children but at any age: that is, substance, form, life, sensation, reason, and all other goods, even in any evil human being. Who works to make a human being live except him *in whom we live, move, and are*[^71]? He does this by a certain hidden work of his beneficence, apart from the visible foods by which we are sustained from outside. He who works to make a human being live, though his conduct is vicious, himself works to make a human being be born, though his origin is corrupted.

**25. 57.** Why, after setting out other words of mine from my book, do you invent that I said, “Marriage had a different institution before Adam's sin, and could have existed without concupiscence, without bodily movement, and without the need for the sexes”? Remove from marriage the concupiscence by which the flesh desires against the spirit; remove the evil against which you fight, engaging in glorious struggles through the virtue of continence. You need not remove the rest if you seek marriage as it could have existed before the first human beings' sin. For who ever imagined any marriage without bodily movement and without the need for the sexes? But the war that the chaste feel within themselves, whether continent or even married, is what we say could in no way have existed in paradise before sin. Marriage, then, is the same even now, but in generating children it would then have used no evil, whereas now it uses the evil of concupiscence well. Through this evil it has not lost its own goods: the fidelity of chastity, the bond of union, and the production of offspring. Thus a man would then too have cleaved to his wife to beget children, but they would have had in the flesh no movement of turbulent lust, only of tranquil will, by which we command the other members.

**25. 58.** You accuse me of defining “those little children who have filled the world, for whom Christ died, as the devil's work, born from disease and guilty from the beginning.” Little children are not the devil's work in their substance; but through the devil's work they are guilty in their origin. Therefore Christ, as you too confess, died even for little children, because the blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins belongs to them too[^72]. You alienate them from that blood when you deny that they contract original sin. Nor should you resent my calling concupiscence a disease, for which you too confessed that a remedy was provided. The beginning of little children, from which they would be born, was in Adam; since he was corrupted and condemned through sin, Christ established another beginning for them, from which they would be reborn.

**26. 59.** “If before sin,” you say, “God created that from which human beings would be born, but the devil that by which parents would be aroused, holiness is undoubtedly ascribed to those born, and guilt to those generating.” What do you mean by “parents would be aroused”? If by the piety of the will by which each desires to receive children, this too was instituted through God. But if by the disturbance of lust, which the choice of the will is insufficient either to arouse or to remove, it is a wound of nature inflicted through the transgression persuaded by the devil. I therefore rightly said, “The sowing of children would have occurred without this disease in that body of life, though it cannot now occur without it in this body of death”[^73].

**26. 60.** But when you argue that “little children belong to the good of fertility, instituted by God's blessing before the disease of lust, not to the disease of lust itself, if it afterward befell those who sinned, and therefore holiness must be ascribed to those born and guilt to those generating,” you fail to consider that through that great sin the whole nature from which offspring would be derived was changed for the worse. For you could likewise say that Eve alone, not other women, should have felt the pains of childbirth, since the blessing *Increase and multiply*[^74], from which children are procreated, was given before the female sex was punished by that curse. But if we said this, the answer would certainly be that, just as through that sin, so through the curse the whole nature was changed for the worse, from which original sin and the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam are contracted.

**26. 61.** For it is not, as you argue, that “the Apostle was describing a Jew placed under the law, saying, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*; and, *It is not I who do it, but the sin dwelling in me*; and, *Evil is present with me*; and, *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind*[^75].” He was describing human nature in this corruptible flesh, not originally established with a defect by God's work, but wounded by the defect of the first human beings arising from their choice of will. Whose voice is it: *Unhappy man that I am, who will deliver me from this body of death? God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^76]? A Jew's? Certainly not, but undoubtedly a Christian's. Therefore those preceding words are his too, from which this conclusion followed. The same man said, *God's grace will deliver me from this body of death through Jesus Christ our Lord*, who said, *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind*[^77].

**26. 62.** But lest perhaps you think this the voice of a catechumen still hoping for the washing of regeneration, after which he would have no law of sin in his members resisting the law of his mind—although you yourself, as you want us to believe about you, engage in glorious struggles after the washing of regeneration, opposing the evil of this concupiscence through the good of continence—recall what he writes to the Galatians, certainly baptized people. *I say*, he says, *walk in the spirit, and do not fulfill the concupiscences of the flesh*[^78]. He does not say, “Do not have them,” since they could not be without them, but, *Do not fulfill them*, that is, do not carry out their works by consenting with your will. *For the flesh*, he says, *desires* *against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these oppose one another, so that you do not do what you will*[^79]. See whether this is not the same as in Romans: *For I do not do the good I will, but I do the evil I do not will.* Then he adds in Galatians: *But if you are led by the spirit, you are no longer under the law*[^80]. See whether this is not the same as in Romans: *It is no longer I who do it*; and, *I delight in God's law according to the inner human being*; and, *Let sin not reign in your mortal body, so that you obey its desires*[^81]. For if obedience is not given to the concupiscences necessarily present in sinful flesh and in this body of death, what the Apostle forbids to be fulfilled will not be fulfilled when he says, *Do not fulfill the concupiscences of the flesh*[^82]. For these are precisely the works he wonderfully follows through in full, saying, *The works of the flesh are manifest: fornications, uncleannesses, debaucheries, service of idols*[^83], and the other such works. If, then, consent is not given to the concupiscences of the flesh, though enacted in movements, they will not be fulfilled in works. Therefore, when the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, so that we do not do what we will, neither are the concupiscences of the flesh fulfilled, though they occur, nor are our good works perfected, though they occur. For just as carnal concupiscence is fulfilled when the spirit consents to its evil works, desiring not against it but with it, so our good works will be perfected when the flesh so consents to the spirit that it too does not desire against it. This is what we will when we desire the perfection of righteousness; this we ought to will with uninterrupted intent. But because we cannot accomplish it in this corruptible flesh, he therefore said to the Romans, *To will is present with me, but I find no way to accomplish the good*[^84]. Or, as the Greek manuscripts have it, *To will is present with me, but to accomplish the good is not*, that is, accomplishing the good is not present with me. He did not say “to do,” but *to accomplish the good*. For doing good means not following concupiscences[^85]; accomplishing the good means not having concupiscence. Therefore the words in Galatians, *Do not fulfill the concupiscences of the flesh*[^86], correspond inversely to those in Romans, *But I find no way to accomplish the good*[^87]. For neither are those concupiscences fulfilled in evil when the assent of our will is not added to them, nor is our will perfected in good as long as their movement, to which we do not consent, remains. This conflict itself, in which even the baptized fight as though in an athletic contest, when the flesh desires against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh—where the spirit does a good work by not consenting to evil concupiscence but does not perfect it because it does not destroy the evil desires themselves, and the flesh produces an evil desire but does not fulfill it either because, without the spirit's consent, it does not reach condemnable works—this conflict, then, belongs not to Jews or any others but plainly to faithful Christians who struggle in this contest by living well. It is briefly shown in Romans where he says, *Therefore I myself serve God's law with the mind, but sin's law with the flesh*[^88].

**26. 63.** If, then, this is the condition in this body of death—a condition certainly absent in paradise, in that body of life—it is undoubtedly evident enough whence little children contract the bond of sin when born carnally, a bond dissolved only when they are reborn spiritually. They contract it not from the work by which God made human nature but from the wound the enemy inflicted on human nature. Not an enemy who, according to the Manichaeans, sprang from a nature of evil that God did not create, but an enemy who was once an angel, good through God's work and made evil through his own work. He first wounded and overthrew himself, so that, cast down, he might cast another down and through evil persuasion inflict the wound of transgression, from which the human race limps even in those who walk God's way.

**26. 64.** Yet you are angry with me because I said, “For this shameful concupiscence, which shameless people shamelessly praise”[^89]. In your anger you say many things still more shamelessly, exalting yourselves “as though, agreeing with the Holy Scriptures and most certain reason, you defend the absence of evil in nature for the purpose of inciting people to pursue virtue. You impress upon them that no summit of virtue is so lofty that, with God's help, a faithful mind cannot reach it; and you therefore say that there is no necessity of evil in the flesh so that everyone, praiseworthily created, may be ashamed to live disgracefully, and thus shame, arising from the commendation of inborn nobility, may confront shameful sloth.” You boasted of many similar things quite eloquently in praise of yourselves, while conversely disparaging us and saying that “there is no doubt that the overthrow of holiness, the contamination of chastity, and the corruption of morals agree with, indeed inhere in, our doctrines; nor will I deny it, since”—as you say—“I pour the filth of evil conduct back upon nature to discredit it, so as to remove fear from sinners, whose obscenities I console by insults to the Apostles and all the saints, because I recall that the golden vessel, the apostle Paul, often said, *For I do not do the good I will, but I do the evil I hate*[^90],” and other things of this sort.

**26. 65.** Yet while praising yourselves and accusing us, you fight against the evil of concupiscence, confessing by fighting what you deny by speaking. You wish to appear to have climbed virtue's summit in such a way that on the very summit you think you have reached you struggle, as in a citadel, with pursuing concupiscence. However much you occupy the higher ground, you do not cease fighting an internal enemy. Nor are you ashamed to praise concupiscence—which will undoubtedly destroy you all the more if it conquers you—against one who seeks you when lost, even as he defeats you. Especially since at the end of this same book you say that “the sole aim of my thought is to swear allegiance to the vices in order to wage war on the virtues, to strive with every cunning and every fury for the destruction of God's city, to terrify those resisting shamefulness by making them despair of attaining chastity, and to lie about the power of obscene lust, as though it were so great that reason cannot govern and restrain it, and even the legion of the Apostles did not resist it.” These are outright lies you yourself tell about me. I wage no war against the virtues; I wage it against the vices insofar as I am helped, and proclaim that it must be waged. If you do this too, why do you praise those against whom you fight? How am I to believe that by virtue you defeat enemies whom you dare not assail with words? If we both defeat concupiscence, why do we not both condemn it? Why do you refuse to condemn in your judgment what you boast of defeating through continence? You say I lie that lust's power is so great that reason cannot govern and restrain it. See, I do not say lust's power is so great that human reason, divinely enlightened and aided, cannot govern and restrain it. But why do you deny it is an evil when it kills unless restrained? See, I cry out with all the voice I can what you claim I deny: the legion of the Apostles resisted lust, certainly when it resisted them. You falsely charge us with insulting the Apostles and pretend indignation: why do you adorn with praises their enemy and yours? Who would defend that which the legion of the Apostles resisted, except an enemy of the Apostles?

**26. 66.** Does lust deserve both friendship and war with you, so that you attack it within yourself and defend it against me? Your war is hidden; your friendship is plain. What is plain makes what is hidden suspicious. What do you want us to believe about the war you wage in secret, when we see your friendship openly? How do you want us to think you fight against lust's sting when you fill books with lust's praise? But see, I overcome my suspicion: I believe you fight what you praise, but grieve that you praise what you fight. From this evil and with this evil, which we believe you too fight, a human being is generated whom you deny is saved through regeneration. Married people use this evil well, while the continent do better by not using it. Furthermore, if that by which *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^91] is an evil, an evil that, as you confess, the legion of the Apostles resisted, married people using it undoubtedly use not a good well but an evil. Children generated from this and with this must therefore be regenerated to be delivered from evil. Their parents too were born guilty through this original evil, even if they were freed from that guilt by being reborn. What, then, would we wish them to generate—from that by which they were born, not that by which they were reborn—except what they themselves were at birth? Guilty people, therefore, since they themselves were born guilty, and generating from that by which they were born, they could generate nothing other than what they were at birth. But through that by which they were reborn they were delivered from the guilt with which they were born. Accordingly, those whom, though delivered, they generated guilty must be delivered by the same regeneration. For they were born guilty from this evil, which those reborn use well so that children may be born who must be regenerated. If you do not fight against this evil, believe those who do; if you fight it, confess your adversary, and do not, by praising the disease, treat as a friend what by fighting you experience as an enemy.
