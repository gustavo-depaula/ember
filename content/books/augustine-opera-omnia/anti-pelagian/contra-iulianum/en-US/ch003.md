# Book 2

**1. 1.** I must now undertake what I placed third in my plan: with the Lord's help, to overthrow your devices, Julian, by the judgments of bishops who expounded the holy Scriptures with great distinction. This is not to show that they held the Catholic faith concerning original sin. I have already done that in the first parts of this work, to show how many and how great are the men, holy and illustrious teachers of the Church, against whom you fling the charge of Manichaeism, and how, in your desire to blacken me in the judgment of the unlearned, you accuse those who defended the Catholic faith against heretics of a wicked heresy. But now your very arguments, by which you seek to prevent belief that human beings' first birth is bound by original sin, must be refuted by the words of the saints. Christian peoples ought to prefer these men to your profane novelties and choose to adhere to them rather than to you.

**1. 2.** These, certainly, are the supposedly formidable principal points of your arguments, with which you terrify the weak and those less instructed in sacred letters than they need to be against you. For you say: “By asserting original sin, we call the devil the creator of human beings at their birth, condemn marriage, deny that all sins are forgiven in baptism, accuse God of the crime of injustice, and instill despair of perfection.” You contend that all these consequences follow if we believe that infants are born bound by the first man's sin and are therefore under the devil unless they are reborn in Christ. “For the devil creates,” you say, “if they are created from the wound that the devil inflicted on human nature as it was first created. And marriage is condemned if it is believed to possess something through which those worthy of condemnation are begotten. Nor are all sins forgiven in baptism if some evil remains in baptized spouses from which evil people are begotten. Moreover, how is God not unjust if, while forgiving the baptized their own sins, he condemns an infant because, when created by him, it unwittingly and unwillingly contracted others' sins, even from parents whose sins had been forgiven? Nor is virtue, whose contrary is understood to be vice, believed capable of perfection, because it is incredible that inborn vices can be destroyed. Indeed, these should no longer even be reckoned vices. For someone who cannot be other than he was created does not sin.”

**1. 3.** If you investigated these matters with faithful diligence and did not attack with faithless audacity what is established by the truth and antiquity of the Catholic faith, you would come, nourished by Christ's grace, to those things *that are hidden from the wise and prudent and revealed to little ones*[^1]. For great is the abundance of the Lord's sweetness, which he certainly did not begrudge, yet hid for those who fear him, and brought to fulfillment for those who hope, not in themselves, but in him [^2]. We therefore say what is held by that faith concerning which it is written: *unless you believe, you will not understand*[^3]: the creator of human beings is not the devil but the true and truly good God, who ineffably makes clean things even from unclean things, although no human being is born clean and is therefore necessarily under an unclean spirit until cleansed by the Holy Spirit. Nor is any impurity in natures, however great, a charge against marriage; for the good proper to marriage is distinguished from any vice of natures. Nor does the guilt of that sin remain without being loosed by the regeneration that takes place in Christ, although weakness remains, against which the regenerate person must fight within himself if he is to advance. Nor is God unjust when he gives sins, whether original or personal, their due recompense. Rather, he is shown to be unjust or powerless if *the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam, from the day*, as it is written, *of their birth until the day of their burial in the mother of all* [^4]—the yoke beneath which his image is crushed—is imposed either by himself without any preceding original or personal sin, or by someone else against his will. Nor must we despair of the perfection of virtue through the grace of him who can change and heal a nature corrupted from its origin.

**2. 4.** I shall therefore undertake to fulfill my promise. I shall not take up your five arguments, from which you construct everything you argue in many ways against the Catholic faith on this question, in order to refute them individually with the testimonies of the saints. Rather, as much of them as can be struck and overthrown when I produce individual passages from the writings of Catholic bishops will be struck and overthrown, whether one of them, two, several, or all, according to the force of the passage produced. Such is the passage in the book blessed Ambrose wrote *On Noah's Ark*: “The salvation that will come to the nations through the one Lord Jesus is proclaimed,” he says, “who alone could not have been righteous, when every generation went astray, unless, born of a virgin, he had by a privilege been free from the bondage of a guilty generation. *Behold*, he says, *I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^5], says the man considered righteous above the rest. Whom, then, shall I now call righteous except one free from these bonds, whom the bonds of our common nature do not hold? All are under sin; from Adam death reigned over all. Let the one righteous man come into God's sight, of whom it may now be said without qualification, not: *he did not sin with his lips*[^6], but: *he committed no sin*[^7].” Tell this man, if you dare, that he made the devil the creator of human beings born from the mingling of both sexes, since he exempted Christ alone, because he was born of a virgin, from the bonds of guilty generation, while all others are born from Adam under the obligation of sin—a sin that the devil certainly sowed. Refute him as a condemner of marriage, since he says that the Virgin's son alone was born without sin. Accuse him of denying the attainment of virtue, since he asserts that vices are born into the human race at the very beginning of conception. And tell him what you seem to yourself to have said against me so aptly and acutely in your first volume: “Those who are said to sin do not sin at all, since, by whomever they were created, they must live according to what they were created to be and not struggle against their own nature.” Say all these things to Ambrose, or about Ambrose, which you fling at me so arrogantly, insultingly, brazenly, and insolently. (For perhaps it cannot be said that by these words he also discredits the sacrament of baptism, as though forgiveness of sins were not complete in it, and makes God unjust for condemning in children the sins of others that he forgave in their parents, since when he said these things he was not speaking of the offspring of the baptized.) Now, if Saint Ambrose was not among those who make the devil the creator of human beings, condemn marriage, or think human nature incapable of virtue, but rather among those who acknowledge and confess the supreme and supremely good God as the author of the whole human being—that is, the whole soul and whole body—and honor marriage in the goodness of its own rank, and do not despair that a human being can be perfectly justified, then these three arguments of yours, for now, have been overthrown by the authority of so great a man. They should no longer be raised against us, who say about original sin what was said by one who neither attributed the creation of humanity to the devil, nor condemned marriage, nor despaired of the perfection of righteousness in human nature.

**3. 5.** As for your two remaining arguments, which concern baptism, we shall soon see what that man thought and how he crushed you beneath the immense weight of his authority. For he says in the book *Against the Novatianists*: “All of us human beings are born under sin, for our very origin is in vice, as you have read in David's words: *for behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^8]. That is why Paul's flesh was a body of death, as he himself says: *who will deliver me from the body of this death?*[^9] But Christ's flesh condemned sin, which he did not experience in being born and which he crucified in dying, so that in our flesh there might be justification through grace where previously there was defilement through fault” [^10]. Here, indeed, all your arguments have been overthrown together. For if all of us human beings are born under sin, since our very origin is in vice, why do you accuse me on this account of calling the devil the creator of human beings, when you see me saying what was said by one who did not say that the devil was their creator? If it is because a human being's origin is in vice that David says, *for behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^11], and this utterance accuses not the marital union but original sin, why do you call me a condemner of marriage, as you will not dare to call Ambrose? If, because all of us human beings are born under sin and our very origin is in vice, Paul's flesh was therefore a body of death, as he himself says, *who will deliver me from the body of this death?*[^12], do you finally perceive that the Apostle intended himself also to be understood in these words of his? It was therefore he who, while his inner man delighted in God's law, saw another law in his members fighting against the law of his mind, and for that reason called his flesh a body of death. In his own flesh, therefore, good did not dwell; hence he did not do the good he wished, but did the evil he hated [^13]. See how your whole case has been struck down, laid low, crushed, and, like *dust that the wind casts away from the face of the earth* [^14], cast out of the hearts of those whom you had begun to deceive, if they are willing to put aside their passion for controversy and consider these things. For was the Apostle Paul not baptized? Or had any of his sins, original or personal, of ignorance or knowledge, not been forgiven him? Why, then, did he speak in this way, unless what I said in my book, which you boast of having answered, is entirely true? For this law of sin, which is in the members of the body of this death, has both been forgiven through spiritual regeneration and remains in mortal flesh. It has been forgiven because its guilt was loosed by the sacrament through which believers are reborn; it remains because it produces desires against which believers also fight. This is precisely what utterly overthrows your heresy. You see and fear this so clearly that you try to escape these apostolic words only by asserting as forcefully as you can that the person to be understood there is not the Apostle himself, but some Jew still under the law and not yet under grace, against whom the habit of his evil way of life was fighting—as though the very force of habit were put aside in baptism, and the baptized did not also struggle against it, all the more strongly and vigorously as they strive more to please the eyes of him whose grace helps them not to be overcome in such a contest. If you had been willing to consider this attentively and without obstinacy, you would certainly have discovered even in the force of habit itself how concupiscence is forgiven in its guilt and remains in its activity. For something certainly takes place in a human being when he is troubled by the goads of his desires, even those to which he does not consent. Yet it was not because of the force of habit that the Apostle called his flesh a body of death, but for the reason Ambrose understood most correctly: because we are all born under sin, and our very origin is in vice. He could not doubt that the guilt of this vice had already been forgiven him in baptism. Yet, fighting against its troubling activity, he first feared being overcome and enslaved by it; then, even though unconquered, he preferred not to fight for long but to have no enemy, when he said: *unhappy man that I am, who will deliver me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^15]. He knew that by his grace we are healed of this movement of concupiscence, who healed us of its original guilt through spiritual regeneration. This war that we have undertaken to wage within ourselves against ourselves is experienced inwardly, and cannot be denied, by the fiercest assailants of lust, not its most shameless praisers.

**3. 6.** Indeed, the most victorious Cyprian also says in his letter *On the Lord's Prayer*: “We ask that God's will be done in heaven and on earth, both of which pertain to the completion of our soundness and salvation. For since we possess a body from earth and a spirit from heaven, we ourselves are earth and heaven, and we pray that God's will be done in both, that is, in body and spirit. For there is a struggle between flesh and spirit, a daily clash as they disagree with one another, so that we do not do the very things we wish, while the spirit seeks heavenly and divine things and the flesh desires earthly and worldly things. Therefore we ask that, by God's aid and help, harmony may be established between these two, so that, while God's will is accomplished in both spirit and flesh, the soul reborn through him may be preserved. The Apostle Paul declares this openly and plainly in his own words: *the flesh*, he says, *desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh, for these are opposed to one another, so that you do not do the things you wish*[^16]” [^17]. See how this outstanding teacher instructs a baptized people—for what Christian does not know that *the Lord's Prayer* pertains to the baptized?—so that they may understand that the soundness and salvation of human nature consist not in the separation of flesh and spirit, as if they were naturally hostile to one another, as Mani foolishly imagines, but rather in their agreement, healed of the vice of discord. For this is to be delivered from the body of this death: that what is now a body of death may become a body of life, with death itself dying within it, as discord ends, not nature. Hence it will also be said: *where, death, is your strife?*[^18] That this is not brought to perfection in this life the same martyr also attests in his letter *On Mortality*, where he says that the Apostle Paul desires to depart and be with Christ [^19] precisely so that he may no longer be subject to any sins and vices of the flesh [^20]. And how watchfully does he explain this in *the Lord's Prayer* against that doctrine of yours by which you trust in your own strength! For he teaches that “this should be asked of the Lord rather than presumed upon from our own powers, so that divine grace, not human strength, may bring harmony between flesh and spirit” [^21], in complete agreement with the Apostle's words: *who will deliver me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^22].

**3. 7.** Saint Gregory also bears witness to these things, saying: “For when the soul is in labors and distress, when it is pressed like an enemy by the flesh, it then takes refuge in God and recognizes from whom it ought to seek help” [^23]. And lest anyone, in these words of Bishop Gregory, suspect that the flesh pressing like an enemy comes from a contrary evil nature, in accordance with the madness of the Manichaeans, see for yourself how he too harmonizes with his brothers and fellow teachers, teaching that the spirit desires against the flesh for no other purpose than that both may be recalled to their author after the most grievous conflict between the two in this life, in which the life of all the saints labors. For in his *Apology* he says: “I make no mention yet of those words about how within ourselves we are attacked by our own vices and passions and pressed day and night by the fiery goads of this body of humiliation and body of death, while the allurements of visible things everywhere provoke and incite us, sometimes secretly, sometimes openly. This muddy residue to which we cling breathes out the stench of its mire through wider channels; and the law of sin in our members also fights against the law of the spirit, seeking to lead captive the royal image within us, so that whatever flowed into us through the gift of that divine and original creation may fall as its spoils. From this, perhaps, scarcely anyone, directing himself by prolonged and strict philosophy and gradually recalling the nobility of his soul, may recall and turn back to God the nature of light within him, joined to this lowly and dark mud. Or certainly, if he acts with God's favor, he will recall both together, provided that by long and constant meditation he becomes accustomed always to look upward and to lift up, with tighter reins, the matter bound to him that evilly drags him downward and weighs him down” [^24]. Recognize, my son Julian, these harmonious Catholic voices, and cease to be at variance with them. When blessed Gregory says, “Within ourselves we are attacked by our own vices and passions and pressed day and night by the fiery goads of this body of humiliation and body of death” [^25], a baptized man is speaking, and speaking of the baptized. When he says, “The law of sin in our members fights against the law of the spirit” [^26], a baptized man is speaking, and speaking of the baptized. This struggle belongs to faithful Christians, not unbelieving Jews. Believe it if you do not fight; recognize it if you do, and in this struggle also defeat the rebellious pride of the Pelagian error. Do you now distinguish, now perceive, now return to your senses, understanding that in baptism all sins are forgiven and that a kind of civil war of inward vices remains in the baptized? For these are not vices that should now be called sins if concupiscence does not draw the spirit into unlawful deeds and conceive and give birth to sin. Yet those things we must strive to conquer, as we labor and advance in this contest, are not outside us. They are ours; they are passions; they are vices; they must be bridled, restrained, and healed, but while being treated they are hostile. And although they diminish more and more as we advance toward better things, they do not cease to exist while we live here. They will perish when the devout soul departs from here; they will not return in the risen body.

**4. 8.** Let us therefore return to blessed Ambrose. “Paul's flesh too,” he says, “was a body of death, as he himself says: *who will deliver me from the body of this death?*” [^27]. This is how Ambrose understood it, how Cyprian understood it, how Gregory understood it, to say nothing for now of other teachers endowed with similar authority. To this death it will be said at the end: *where, death, is your strife?*[^28] But this grace belongs to the regenerate, not to those merely born. “For Christ's flesh,” as Ambrose adds, “condemned sin, which he did not experience in being born and which he crucified in dying” [^29]: in being born he did not experience it in himself; in dying he crucified it in us. Thus the law of sin fighting against the law of the mind, which was in the members even of so great an apostle, is forgiven in baptism, not ended. From this law of the flesh fighting against the law of the mind, Christ's body contracted nothing, because the Virgin did not conceive through it. From this law of the flesh fighting against the law of the mind, everyone without exception contracted that same law at their first birth, because no woman conceived except through it. Therefore the venerable Hilary did not hesitate to say that all flesh is from sin [^30]. But did he therefore deny that it is from God? When we say both that flesh is from flesh and that flesh is from a human being, do we thereby deny that it is from God? Thus it is from God, because he creates; from a human being, because that person begets; and from sin, because it corrupts. But God, who begot the Son coeternal with himself, who was the Word in the beginning, through whom he created everything that did not exist, also created him as a man without vice, born through a virgin, not from a man's seed. In him he regenerates the begotten human being and heals the corrupted one: of guilt immediately, of weakness gradually. If the regenerate person already has the use of reason, he struggles against this weakness as in a contest, with God watching and helping him, because *strength is perfected in weakness*[^31], while that part of us which advances toward righteousness fights against that part of us which falls short of righteousness, so that, with progress victorious, the whole may rise toward the better, rather than, with decline victorious, the whole tending toward the worse. An infant, however, who as yet has no use of reason, is by his own will neither in good nor in evil, because he entertains no thought in either direction. Both lie dormant and inactive in him: the natural good of reason and the original evil of sin. But as the years advance and reason awakens, the commandment comes and sin revives. When it begins to fight against the growing child, what lay hidden in infancy will then appear; either sin conquers, and the person will be condemned, or sin is conquered, and the person will be healed. Yet this evil would not therefore have done no harm if the infant had departed this life before it began to appear in him, because the guilt of this evil—not guilt by which the evil itself is guilty, but by which it makes the one in whom it exists guilty—is contracted by generation and can be loosed only by regeneration. This is why infants are baptized: that they may not only enjoy the good of Christ's kingdom but also be rescued from the evil of death's kingdom. This can take place only through him who “condemned sin in his flesh, which he did not experience in being born and which he crucified in dying, so that in our flesh there might be justification through grace where previously there was defilement through fault” [^32].

**4. 9.** Thus, according to these words of blessed Ambrose, the devil did not create humanity by goodness but corrupted humanity by malice; the evil of concupiscence did not take away the good of marriage; no guilt of any sin is left unloosed in the sacrament of baptism; and God is not unjust in condemning by the law of justice someone made guilty by the law of sin, even if he was born from that law which can no longer make his parent guilty because the parent has been reborn. Moreover, why should we despair of strength, which is perfected in weakness, if these things are true, since through Christ's flesh, which condemns the sin he did not experience in being born and crucified in dying, justification through grace takes place also in our flesh, where previously there was defilement through fault? Hence these five arguments of yours, with which you chiefly terrify people, will be able to disturb neither others nor yourselves, if you believe Ambrose, Cyprian, Gregory, and other holy and illustrious Catholic teachers—or indeed even yourselves—that the law of sin in a human being's members fighting against the law of the mind [^33], through which the flesh desires against the spirit [^34], imposes even on baptized saints the necessity of fighting. Against what, if not against an evil? Not a substance, but a vice of substance, which is not imputed through God's regenerating grace, must be bridled through God's helping grace, and must be healed through God's rewarding grace.

**5. 10.** But you may perhaps say that the baptized fight rather against what they have made for themselves through the evil habit of their previous life, not against what they were born with. If you say this, you already undoubtedly see and concede that there is something evil in a human being of which not the thing itself but the guilt contracted from it is taken away in baptism. Nevertheless, because this is insufficient to resolve the question unless it is shown to have been born in us from the first man's sin, hear what Saint Ambrose says more explicitly about it elsewhere in his *Exposition of the Gospel according to Luke*. He was discussing, in various ways yet without departing from the one rule of faith, the passage where the Lord says that in one house three are divided against two and two against three [^35]. “The flesh and soul,” he says, “may also be seen as separated from the smell, touch, and taste of luxury, dividing themselves in one house against the vices that assail them, submitting to God's law, and withdrawing from the law of sin. Although their disagreement became natural through the first man's transgression, so that they by no means agreed in equal zeal for virtue, nevertheless, through the Cross of the Lord and Savior, with both enmities and the law of commandments abolished, they came together in the harmony of fellowship, after Christ, our peace, descended from heaven and made both one [^36]” [^37]. Again in the same work, speaking about spiritual and incorruptible food, he says: “For reason is food for the mind and a splendid nourishment of sweetness, which does not burden the members or turn into nature's shameful things, but into its ornaments, when the wallow of lusts is changed into God's temple and the lodging of vices begins to be a sanctuary of virtues. It does this when the flesh, returning to its nature, recognizes the nurse of its vigor and, laying aside its audacious stubbornness, is joined to the judgment of the governing soul, such as it was when it received the hidden places of paradise as its dwelling, before, infected with the poison of the deadly serpent, it knew sacrilegious hunger and, in its eagerness to devour, disregarded the memory of the divine commandments fixed in the soul's awareness. From here sin is shown to have flowed, as though body and soul were its parents, while the body's nature is tempted and the soul shares the suffering of its sick companion. Had the soul bridled the body's appetite, the origin of sin would have been extinguished at its very birth. But, evilly impregnated with it, as though it had been infused by the movement of a male body, the soul, with its own vigor also corrupted, was heavy with another's burdens and brought it forth” [^38].

**5. 11.** Here, surely, that teacher Ambrose, so exceptionally praised by your teacher's own mouth, declared most openly and abundantly both what original sin was and where it came from, and how that first confusion arose: the disobedience of the flesh at odds with the soul, a discord healed by God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord. You see why the flesh desires against the spirit; you see the source of the law in the members fighting against the law of the mind. You see that the discord of soul and flesh became natural and that through these enmities miseries abound for us, to be ended only by God's mercy. Oppose me no longer; for if you still do, you recognize whom, or how many, you oppose at the same time. You said, did you not, that “I labor at nothing more than not being understood.” And in certain places you interpret my meaning according to your own fancy, abusing the somewhat slow minds of people who do not understand that you were more unwilling to remain silent than able to answer my one volume with your four. See how Ambrose pours forth a clear and transparent river of eloquence: there is nowhere for the reader to get stuck or the hearer to be left in darkness. He says most plainly that the Apostle said, *who will deliver me from the body of this death?*[^39], because we are all born under sin and our very origin is in vice. He says most plainly that the Lord Christ was without sin because, born of a virgin, he was not held by the bonds of guilty generation and our common nature, and that he condemned the sin he did not experience in being born. He says most openly that the disagreement of flesh and soul became natural through the first man's transgression. He says most openly that the flesh, the wallow of lusts and lodging of vices, is then changed into God's temple and a sanctuary of virtues when, returning to its nature, it recognizes the nurse of its vigor and, laying aside its audacious stubbornness, is joined to the judgment of the governing soul, such as it was when it received the hidden places of paradise as its dwelling, before it was infected by the deadly serpent's poison. Why do you still ask what books to contrive against me? Look at him; dare something against him who confronted the poisons of your heresy before they were born and prepared these antidotes by which they could be expelled. If these are not enough, hear more.

**5. 12.** In his book *On Isaac and the Soul* he also says: “A good driver, then, restrains and recalls the bad horses and urges on the good. The good horses are four: prudence, temperance, fortitude, and justice; the bad horses: anger, concupiscence, fear, and iniquity” [^40]. Does he say that a good driver has good horses and has no bad ones? Rather, he says: “He urges on the good and restrains and recalls the bad.” Where do these horses come from? Surely, if we say or think that they are substances, we favor or adhere to the madness of the Manichaeans. To keep that far from us, we understand these horses in a Catholic sense as our vices, which resist the law of the mind through the law of sin. These vices will not be separated from us and exist somewhere else; rather, healed within us, they will exist nowhere. But why did they not perish in baptism? Will you not yet confess that their guilt perished, while their weakness remained—not guilt by which they themselves were guilty, but by which they made us guilty in the evil deeds to which they drew us? Nor did their weakness remain as though they were animals that become weak: they themselves are our weakness. Nor should he be thought to have named among these bad horses the iniquity that is blotted out in baptism. For that belonged to the sins we committed, all of which have been forgiven and no longer exist at all, though their guilt remained as the deeds were done and passed away. But he called this law of sin, which remains although its guilt was forgiven in the sacred font, iniquity, because it is unjust for the flesh to desire against the spirit. Yet righteousness is present in our renewal, because it is just for the spirit to desire against the flesh, so that we may walk in the spirit and not fulfill the desires of the flesh. For we find this righteousness of ours named among the good horses.

**5. 13.** Hear further what he says in the book he wrote *On Paradise.* “Perhaps,” he says, “Paul said, *which it is not lawful for a human being to speak*[^41], because he was still established in the body, that is, he saw the passions of this body, saw the law of his flesh fighting against the law of his mind” [^42]. Again in the same book: “When he calls the serpent wiser [^43], you understand whom he means: that adversary of ours, who nevertheless has the wisdom of this world. But pleasure and delight are also rightly called wise, since there is also what is called the wisdom of the flesh, as you read: *because the wisdom of this flesh is hostile to God* [^44]. And those who desire pleasures are cunning at seeking out kinds of delights. Thus even if you understand delight,” he says, “there is a kind opposed to the divine command and hostile to our understanding. Hence Saint Paul says: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin*[^45]” [^46]. What kind of pleasure this teacher means here is readily apparent, because, so that we might understand, he brought in the Apostle's testimony: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin.* This is that pleasure you have taken under your protection, although you too reproach its excess. In this you plainly admit its character; but you defend and praise it when moderated with such ornaments of language as though it had set that measure for itself, rather than the spirit which desires against its assault. Against that assault the man stood firmly who cried: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind.* If this opposing assault is unleashed, into what uncleanness will it turn? Over what precipices will it not drag and cast us? But now, to the more pressing point: behold, not some Jew, according to you, but the Apostle Paul speaking of himself, according to blessed Ambrose, says: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin.* Again, elsewhere in the same work, this teacher says: “Paul is attacked and sees the law of his flesh fighting against the law of his mind and taking him captive in the law of sin. He does not presume on his own conscience but trusts that through Christ's grace he will be delivered from the body of death. And you think that someone with knowledge cannot sin? Paul says: *for I do not do the good I wish, but the evil I do not wish, that I do*[^47]; and you think that knowledge benefits a human being when it increases the reproach of his offense?” [^48]. Again, in that work, the same bishop, addressing all of us and earnestly pleading our common cause, says: “For the law of the flesh fights against the law of the mind, and we must labor and sweat to chastise our body and bring it into servitude, and to sow spiritual things” [^49].

**5. 14.** In another book of his, written *On the Sacrament of Regeneration*, or *On Philosophy*, he speaks as follows: “Blessed, then, is the death that snatches us from sin to refashion us for God. *For whoever has died has been justified from sin*[^50]. Is someone justified from sin,” he says, “by the end of nature? Certainly not; for one who dies a sinner remains in sin. Rather, one is justified from sin when all one's sins are forgiven through baptism.” Have you anything to say to this? Do you see how the venerable man has expressed that a human being's blessed death occurs in baptism, where all sins are forgiven? But attend to something else; attend to what you do not wish to hear. “We have observed,” he says, “what mystical death is; now let us consider what the burial ought to be. For it is not enough that vices die unless the body's luxury withers, the structure of all carnal bonds is dissolved, and every knot of bodily practice is untied. Let no one flatter himself because he has put on a different form, received mystical precepts, and applied his mind to the discipline of continence. We do not do what we wish, but we do what we hate. Sin accomplishes many things in us. Though we resist, pleasures often revive and rise again. We must struggle against the flesh. Paul struggled against it; indeed, he says: *but I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin*[^51]. Are you,” he says, “stronger than Paul? Do not trust your flesh as though it were dutiful or entrust yourself to it, when the Apostle cries out: *for I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh; for willing is at hand for me, but I do not find how to accomplish the good. For I do not do the good I wish, but the evil I do not wish, that I do. But if I do what I do not wish, it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^52].” However great the obstinacy of mind that carries you away, Julian, however great the stubbornness with which you stand against us for the Pelagian error, through blessed Ambrose you are surrounded by such evident facts and battered by such clear words that, if no reason, reflection, or consideration of religion, piety, humanity, and the truth to be recognized within yourself recalls you from your obstinate purpose, you will assuredly show how powerful among human evils it is for someone to have advanced to a place where he no longer wishes to remain and from which he is already ashamed to return. For that, I believe, is how you will feel when you read these things. But oh, may Christ's peace conquer in your heart, and good repentance win the palm over bad shame!

**6. 15.** But now attend for a little while to how every human being is begotten from that law of sin whose movement even the mortal condition of the continent is compelled to endure, on which the chastity of marriage labors to impose a measure, where the concupiscence of the flesh and the pleasure you praise make their assaults against the will's resolve when aroused, even though they do not complete their acts when bridled. Attend, then, for a little while to how Saint Ambrose declares, in that same book *On the Sacrament of Regeneration*, or *On Philosophy*, that every human being is begotten from this law of sin and therefore contracts original sin. “There is,” he says, “a house that wisdom builds [^53], and a table filled with heavenly sacraments, at which the righteous man feasts on the food of divine delight, drinking the sweet draught of grace, if he rejoices in the abundant offspring of lasting merits. Begetting these children, David shuddered at those births from bodily mingling, and therefore desired to be cleansed by the flowing of the sacred font, so that spiritual grace might wash away the carnal and earthly stain. *Behold*, he says, *I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^54]. Eve brought forth evilly, so as to leave childbirth as an inheritance to women, and so that everyone, formed through the pleasure of concupiscence, poured into the generative womb, coagulated in blood, wrapped in swaddling clothes, might undergo the contagion of offenses before drawing the gift of life's breath.” If at least some measure of human sense has not deserted you, you see what that memorable teacher Ambrose—praised, as must often be said, by the testimony of your teacher—pronounced concerning the pleasure of concupiscence, the very thing for which you provide such shameless advocacy: everyone formed by it, poured by it into the generative womb, coagulated by it in blood, and wrapped, as it were, in inherited swaddling clothes of a corrupted origin—not, of course, woolen, linen, or similar cloths in which infants are wrapped after birth—undergoes the contagion of offenses before breathing and receiving a breath of this life-giving air. Into this air, as into an immense fountain of shared and unceasing nourishment, after the hidden breathing passages of the mother's womb, the newborn is poured forth, to lament at birth the guilt contracted before birth. Would those first human beings, then, not have blushed at the movement of this concupiscence, by which they themselves were shown guilty and their children were foretold to be subject to their parents' sin? Would that, just as they were ashamed to leave naked those parts of the body in which they felt the disobedience of lust, you, obedient to the Catholic faith, were ashamed to praise what deserves shame!

**6. 16.** Notice also what this same teacher wrote about the covering of fig leaves itself in his book *On Paradise*. “What is more serious, then,” he says, “is that, according to this interpretation, Adam girded himself at the place where he should instead have girded himself with the fruit of chastity. For certain seeds of generation are said to be in the loins, which we gird. Therefore Adam was badly girded there with useless leaves, where he signified not the future fruit of future generation but certain sins” [^55]. Here surely this holy man has frustrated your so elaborately worked out argument, so excessively anxious to prevent belief that after their sin Adam and Eve, their eyes opened, girded their loins [^56]. For in laboring there with such verbosity, you were opposing everyone's understanding and wished to overwhelm it with a din of many words. What could be more obvious than that human loins are covered or girded with girdles or aprons, called perizōmata in Greek, which common people also call coverings? Nor did that man of God, whose words we press upon you, explain this as though it were obscure. Rather, he showed what was signified by what everyone understood. “For certain seeds of generation,” he says, “are said to be in the loins, which we gird; therefore Adam was badly girded there with useless leaves.” Why badly? He continues and says: “Where he signified not the future fruit of future generation but certain sins.” Have you anything to say to this? Behold the source of that shame, behold the source of the weaving and girding of leaves, behold the source of original sin in posterity.

**6. 17.** Saint John, bishop of Constantinople, however, as far as modesty allowed, plainly expressed that whole action of the first human beings in their shame in two words, saying: “They were covered with fig leaves, concealing the appearance of sin.” To whom is it not apparent what appearance of sin, and of what kind, had to be covered in the region of the loins by those ashamed, who were not ashamed of their body's nakedness before sin? I beg you, understand; or rather, allow people to understand what they understand along with you, and do not force us to discuss shameful parts at length almost shamelessly.

**6. 18.** Rightly does the same blessed John, like the martyr Cyprian, commend circumcision of the flesh as commanded to be a sign of baptism. “And see how the Jew,” he says, “does not postpone circumcision because of the threat that every soul not circumcised on the eighth day will be cut off from its people [^57]. But you,” he says, “postpone the circumcision not made by hands, which is accomplished in the body by the stripping off of the flesh, though you hear the Lord himself saying: *amen, amen, I say to you, unless someone is reborn of water and spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^58]?” [^59]. Do you see how a man instructed in the Church's teaching compared circumcision to circumcision and threat to threat? Thus not being circumcised on the eighth day corresponds to not being baptized in Christ, and perishing from one's people corresponds to not entering the kingdom of heaven. Yet you deny that in the baptism of infants the stripping off of the flesh, that is, the circumcision not made by hands, is celebrated, because you contend that they have nothing of which they ought to be stripped. For you do not confess that they are dead in the uncircumcision of their flesh, which signifies sin, especially that contracted by origin. For through this our body is the body of sin, which the Apostle says is destroyed through Christ's Cross [^60].

**7. 19.** But I have now undertaken to press you with the judgments of bishops who came before us and expounded these divine words faithfully and memorably. Let us therefore return to Bishop Ambrose. He does not doubt that human beings—that is, both human souls and bodies—are God's work; he honors marriage; he proclaims that all sins are forgiven in Christ's baptism; he knows that God is just; and he does not deny that human nature is capable of virtue and perfection through God's grace. You make these your five arguments, contending that none of these five things can be true unless it is false that people contract original sin at birth. Yet this very thing you try to remove by these five arguments he sets out wherever necessary in his discourses, so that anyone can see clearly enough what Catholic truth customarily proclaims and what profane novelty tries to overthrow. Or will you perhaps doubt that Ambrose himself knew and taught that God is the creator of human beings, both of soul and body? Hear, then, what he says in the book *On Philosophy* against the philosopher Plato, who asserts that human souls revolve into beasts and thinks God the author of souls alone, assigning the making of bodies to lesser gods. Saint Ambrose therefore says: “I wonder how so great a philosopher confines the soul, to which he attributes the power of conferring immortality, within little owls or frogs, and even clothes it with the savagery of beasts, although *in the Timaeus* he mentioned that it is God's work, made by God among immortal things. Yet he asserts that the body does not appear to be the work of the supreme God, because the nature of human flesh differs in no way from the nature of a beast's body. If, then, the soul is worthy to be believed God's work, how is it unworthy to be clothed with God's work?” [^61]. See: against the Platonists, Ambrose defends as God's work not only the soul, which they too affirm, but also the body, which they deny.

**7. 20.** Or will you say that he brings a charge against marriage because he says that even what is born from it, formed through the pleasure of concupiscence, undergoes the contagion of offenses? Hear, then, Ambrose's judgment on marriage *in his Apology for Saint David.* “Marriage is good,” he says, “the union holy. Yet those who have wives should be as though they had none. The marriage bed itself is undefiled, and no one ought to deprive the other of it except perhaps for a time, to devote themselves to prayer [^62]. Yet, according to the Apostle, a person does not devote himself to prayer at the time when he practices that bodily union” [^63]. Hear another judgment in the book *On Philosophy:* “Continence is good,” he says, “a kind of foundation for piety. For on the precipices of this life it steadies the steps of those who slip, a diligent lookout lest anything unlawful creep in. But incontinence is the mother of all vices, turning even lawful things into vice. Therefore the Apostle not only draws us back from fornication but teaches a certain measure even within marriage itself and prescribes times for prayer [^64]. For an intemperate man in marriage is what else but a kind of adulterer with his own wife?” Do you see how he wishes marriages to be truly honorable even within their own union? Do you see how he says that incontinence turns even lawful things into vice, thereby showing that marriage is lawful and refusing to allow incontinence within it to defile what is lawful? Do you perceive how you ought to understand with us what disease of desire the Apostle did not wish anyone to possess his vessel in, like the gentiles who do not know God [^65]? But to you lust seems blameworthy only outside one's wife. What, then, will you judge concerning Ambrose, who calls a man intemperate in marriage a kind of adulterer with his wife? Do you honor marriage better, granting lust the most unrestrained scope within it, lest, perhaps offended, it find another advocate? For you were unwilling even to touch verbally on what I cited: that the Apostle grants spouses something by way of pardon—where fault is undoubtedly noted, even if forgiven. Nor did you dare in your answer to reopen the point that spouses are admonished to cease that activity to devote themselves to prayer [^66], all of which I cited. I believe you feared that your defense would appear to have betrayed its cause if it appeared, even on your admission, that the prayer of married people is hindered by the lust you are not ashamed to defend. Thus, though you desired to answer me on its behalf, because you dared not resist the Apostle and could not twist the apostolic testimony itself to another meaning, as you usually do, you preferred to say nothing about it at all. Do you, then, honor marriage better, discoloring its dignity with the wallow of carnal concupiscence as though it were entirely irreproachable? Or does he, who calls marriage not only lawful but also good and a holy union, yet recalls that the Apostle prescribed times for prayer when the pleasure of lust ceases? He is so far from wishing spouses devoted to that disease from which original sin is contracted that, according to the same Apostle, he wishes those who have wives to be as though they had none. Nor does he hesitate to call an intemperate husband an adulterer even with his own wife, weighing every good of marriage not by the desire of the flesh but rather by fidelity in chastity, not by the disease of passion but by the covenant of union, not by the pleasure of lust but by the will for offspring. For he declares that woman was instituted for man solely for the sake of generation, a point you thought required such prolonged and fruitless argument, as though any of us denied it. These are his words on this matter in the book *On Paradise*: “If, then,” he says, “woman is the author of fault for man, how does she seem to have been added for a good purpose? But if you consider that God cares for the whole, you will find that what contained the cause of the whole deserved more to please the Lord than what contained the cause of sin deserved condemnation. Therefore, because the human race could not be propagated from man alone, the Lord pronounced that it was not good for the human being to be alone [^67]. For God preferred that there be many whom he could save, to whom he could forgive sin, rather than Adam alone, free from fault. Indeed, because the same one,” he says, “is the author of both works, he came into this world to save sinners. Finally, he did not allow even Cain, guilty of killing his brother, to perish before he begot children. Therefore,” he says, “woman had to be added to man for the generation of human succession” [^68].

**7. 21.** There you have Ambrose, my teacher, memorably praised also by yours, not merely acknowledging but defending both that every human being and human flesh are God's work and that marriage is good insofar as it is marriage. Moreover, I have already shown above that he detracts nothing from holy baptism because of original sin, when I set down his words saying: “One is justified from sin when all one's sins are forgiven through baptism.” And where does he not proclaim that God is just? Or what Catholic Christian can doubt this, which almost all the impious also confess?

**8. 22.** The fifth point remains: whether Ambrose considers human nature capable of justification and perfection, and whether he is turned from this judgment by frequently saying, in many ways, that every human being is born under sin and that his very origin is in vice. But I have already taught this too above, where I recalled that a little later in that same passage he said: “Sin was condemned by Christ's flesh, which he did not experience in being born and which he crucified in dying, so that in our flesh there might be justification through grace where previously there was defilement through fault.” There he showed that human nature—even this nature which is born under sin and whose origin is in vice—is capable of justification, but certainly through grace, which is hostile to you, cruel enemies of that same grace. Yet if this is insufficient, attend again to what he says *in his Exposition of the Prophet Isaiah.* “Let us see,” he says, “whether there is some regeneration of ours after the course of this life, of which it was said: *in the regeneration, when the Son of man sits on the throne of his glory*[^69]. For just as the regeneration of the washing is spoken of, through which we are renewed when the defilement of sins is wiped away, so that also seems to be called regeneration through which, purified from every stain of bodily formation, we are reborn into eternal life with the soul's perception clean” [^70]. Surely this holy and truthful man distinguished the justification of this life, which occurs through the washing of regeneration, from its perfection, when our bodies too will have been renewed in immortality. Thus Ambrose, although he confessed the vice of our origin at birth, did not despair of the perfection of justification. For just as human nature can be formed by God its creator, so it can be healed by its redeemer.

**8. 23.** But you hurry, and in hurrying you send your presumption headlong. For you wish a human being to be perfected here—and would that you wished it by God's gift, not by the free, or rather enslaved, choice of his own will! You indeed perceive yourselves to be far from that perfection, but deceit is in your mouth, whether you say you are sinners and wish to be thought righteous, or profess a perfection of righteousness which you certainly perceive is not in you. Now justification is conferred on us in this life in these three ways: first, by the washing of regeneration, in which all sins are forgiven; next, by the struggle with vices from whose guilt we have been absolved; third, when our prayer is heard in which we say: *forgive us our debts*[^71]. For however bravely we fight against vices, we are human beings. God's grace so helps us as we fight in this corruptible body that there is still reason for him to hear us asking pardon. You think this mercy of God upon us unnecessary for yourselves, because you are among those of whom it is said in the psalm: *those who trust in their own strength*[^72]. But how much better it is for us to hear Ambrose saying in the book *On Flight from the World*: “We speak frequently about fleeing this world; would that our disposition were as watchful and attentive as our speech is easy! But what is worse, the lure of earthly desires frequently creeps in and a flood of vanities occupies the mind, so that you think about and turn over in your mind the very thing you strive to avoid. It is difficult for a human being to guard against this, and impossible to strip it away. Indeed, the prophet testifies that it is more a matter of desire than accomplishment, saying: *incline my heart to your testimonies and not to greed*[^73]. For our heart and our thoughts are not in our power. Pouring in unexpectedly, they confuse mind and spirit and draw them elsewhere than you intended; they recall worldly things, insert mundane things, thrust pleasurable things upon us, weave alluring things in, and at the very moment when we prepare to raise the mind, we are often cast down to earthly things by the intrusion of empty thoughts” [^74]. If you do not suffer these things, forgive us: we do not believe you, but rather recognize in these words of Saint Ambrose a kind of mirror of our shared human weakness, even if we are making progress. And even if we believe you and say, “Pray for us, so that we too may not suffer these things,” we find you so exalted and high-minded that you answer us not only that you do not suffer them, but also that it is within a human being's power not to suffer them, and that there is no reason to ask God's help for this.

**8. 24.** How much better, then, to hear Ambrose again confessing God's grace and not trusting in his own strength! After saying these things, he added: “But who is so blessed as always to ascend in his heart? And how can this happen without divine help? Certainly in no way. Indeed,” he says, “the same Scripture says above: *blessed is the man whose help is from you, Lord; ascents are in his heart*[^75].” Again, the same man, in the book *On the Sacrament of Regeneration*[^76], says: “Who, then, uses the flesh to act, if not the soul? Therefore the soul is naturally the ruler and mistress of the flesh, and ought to tame and govern it. Hence, supported by the protection of the Holy Spirit, it says in the psalm: *I will not fear what flesh may do to me*[^77]. The same soul says in Paul: *but I chastise my body and bring it into servitude*[^78]. Paul therefore chastises what is his, not what he is. For what is his is one thing, what he is another. He chastises what is his, so that as a righteous man he may bring about in himself the death of bodily wantonness” [^79]. When Saint Ambrose said these things, was he not fighting against vices? Was he not conquering vices? Was he not, like an outstanding soldier of Christ, defeating an army of various desires within himself? Was he not chastising his own body? Was not God's work seeking, with God's work—that is, the soul with the flesh—the peace of righteousness, after attacking and overcoming the devil's works, so that, not trusting in its own strength, but, as he says, “supported by the protection of the Holy Spirit, it might say: *I will not fear what flesh may do to me*[^80]”? See how human nature is shown capable of justification; see how strength is perfected in weakness [^81].

**8. 25.** But let us also hear on this matter the most victorious martyr Cyprian in his letter *On Mortality*: “We have a contest,” he says, “with greed, with shamelessness, with anger, with ambition; we have a constant and troublesome struggle with carnal vices and worldly allurements. The human mind, besieged and surrounded on every side by the devil's attacks, scarcely meets each assault, scarcely resists. If greed is laid low, lust rises; if lust is restrained, ambition follows; if ambition is scorned, anger enrages, pride inflates, drunkenness invites, envy breaks concord, jealousy severs friendship. You are forced to curse, which divine law forbids; you are compelled to swear, which is not lawful. The mind suffers so many persecutions daily, the heart is pressed by so many dangers, and yet is it a delight to stand long here amid the devil's swords, when we ought rather to desire and wish to hasten more swiftly to Christ with death's assistance?” [^82]. Far be it, however, from us to think that Saint Cyprian was greedy because he fought against greed; shameless because he fought against shamelessness; subject to anger because he fought against anger; ambitious because he fought against ambition; carnal because he fought against carnal vices; a lover of this world because he fought against worldly allurements; lustful because he fought against lust; proud because he fought against pride; drunken because he fought against drunkenness; or envious because he fought against envy. On the contrary, he was none of these precisely because he bravely resisted these evil movements, coming partly from his origin and partly from habit, refusing to be what they pressed him to be. Yet it does not follow that in so dangerous and laborious a contest he was struck by no hostile weapon, since he says in his letter *On Almsgiving*: “Let no one so flatter himself on having a pure and spotless heart that, relying on his innocence, he thinks no medicine need be applied to his wounds, since it is written: *who will boast that he has a chaste heart? Or who will boast that he is clean from sins?*[^83] And again,” he says, “John sets down and says in his letter: *if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^84]. But if no one can be without sin, and whoever says he is blameless is either proud or foolish, how necessary, how kindly is divine mercy, which, knowing that even those healed will not lack some subsequent wounds, has given saving remedies for treating and healing their wounds anew!” [^85]. O most illustrious teacher and most glorious witness, so you taught, so you admonished, so you offered yourself to be heard and imitated. Rightly, after finishing the other contests against every desire and healing the wounds, you fought for Christ's truth against the last and greatest of all, the desire for this life, and conquered by the abundance of his grace within you. Your crown is secure; your teaching is victorious, and in it you conquer also these people who trust in their own strength. For they cry: “The perfection of virtue comes to us from ourselves”; but you cry back: “No one is strong by his own powers, but he is safe through God's pardon and mercy.”

**8. 26.** Hear also the most blessed Hilary, where he places his hope for human perfection. For, speaking of the Gospel's peace, where the Lord says, *my peace I give you*[^86], he says: “Because the law was a shadow of good things to come, by this prefigured signification it taught us that in this dwelling of an earthly and death-bound body we cannot be clean unless through the washing of heavenly mercy we attain cleansing, after the transformation of the resurrection has made the nature of our earthly body more glorious” [^87]. Again in the same discourse: “He taught that the Apostles themselves, although already cleansed and sanctified by the word of faith, nevertheless were not without evil, because of the condition of the origin we share, saying: *if you, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children*[^88].” You see how this venerable Catholic expositor does not deny our cleansing in this life, and yet hopes for a more perfect human nature, that is, one more perfectly cleansed, in the final resurrection.

**8. 27.** In a certain homily on the book of Saint Job, notice what he says: how he asserts that the devil's unceasing war against us occurs when he stirs up against us the evils within us. He wishes to teach that this happens for our benefit, since divine mercy turns the devil's malice toward our purification. “For the goodness of God's mercy toward us,” he says, “is so great and so wonderful that through the one by whom we lost, in Adam's offense, the nobility of that first blessed creation, we may deserve to regain what we lost. For then the devil harmed us through envy; now, however, while striving to harm, he is conquered. For through the weakness of our flesh he sets in motion every weapon of his power: when he kindles wantonness, entices to drunkenness, goads to hatred, provokes to greed, instructs in killings, and incites to curses. But when the creeping incitements of all these things are restrained through firmness of mind, we are cleansed from sin through the glory of this victory. For thus it has been said: *or how will one born of a woman cleanse himself?*[^89] For when no enemy exists, there will be no war; then, when war ceases, victory will be lacking. But without obtaining victory over the vices clashing against us, there will be no cleansing of vices, because, once the pirate of our body is conquered amid these snares, we are purified by the contest against the passions struggling against us. Therefore,” he says, “mindful and aware that these very bodies of ours are the material of all vices, through which, polluted and filthy, we possess nothing clean or innocent within us, let us rejoice that we have an enemy, in contesting whom we may fight in a kind of war of our own struggle” [^90].

**8. 28.** In his *Exposition* of *the First Psalm*, the same teacher does not hesitate to say that our nature—certainly this nature which derives sickness from sickness—is borne toward sinning, and that, in order not to sin, we thus fight against it in a certain way through the religion of faith. “For there are many,” he says, “who, though separated from impiety by confessing God, are not thereby free from sin, since they do not keep the Church's discipline: the greedy, drunken, disorderly, insolent, proud, hypocritical, lying, and rapacious. And toward these vices,” he says, “the impulse of our nature drives us; but it is useful for us to turn away from the way toward which we are borne and not to stand there, returning without delay to depart from it. Hence: *blessed is the man who has not stood in the way of sinners*[^91], nature indeed bearing us into this way, but the religion of faith bearing us back from it” [^92]. Shall we think that this man was an accuser of the nature God created? Certainly not. For this Catholic man did not doubt that human nature is God's work; rather, he surely accused the vices with which we are born, holding to the Apostle's words: *for we too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^93]. Yet if these words I have quoted from Saint Hilary's discourse were mine, how much you would say against me, and with what puffing of cheeks you would blare out the name and charge of Manichaeism! Now, therefore, lest the bag of your stomach burst from the undigested surfeit of abuse, vomit your slanderous vanities and lying madness upon this man, if you dare. “Toward these vices,” he says, “the impulse of our nature drives us.” What nature is this? Is it the race of darkness introduced by the Manichaeans' fable? Far from it. A Catholic is speaking; an eminent teacher of the churches is speaking; Hilary is speaking. It is therefore our nature corrupted by the first man's transgression, not to be separated by some division from another nature, but itself to be healed. You falsely accuse us of making the devil its author, while you begrudge it Christ as savior and contend that its life can be led to perfection here, altogether without any sin.

**8. 29.** But hear how blessed Hilary admonishes you on this point too. For, expounding Psalm 52 (51), he says: “Hope is in God's mercy forever and for ages of ages [^94]. For even those works of righteousness will not suffice to merit perfect blessedness unless God's mercy, even amid this will for righteousness, does not reckon the faults of human changes and movements. Hence the prophet's saying: *your mercy is better than lives*”[^95]. Do you see that this man of God belongs among the blessed of whom it was foretold: *blessed is the man to whom the Lord has not imputed sin, and in whose mouth there is no deceit*[^96]? For he confesses the sins even of the righteous, asserting that they place their hope in God's mercy rather than trusting in their own righteousness. Therefore there is no deceit in his mouth, or rather in the mouth of all those to whose truthful humility, or humble truthfulness, he bears witness. This deceit abounds in your mouth. For where virtue is absent and boasting is so great, there is hypocrisy; and where hypocrisy is, surely there is deceit. In truth, as much as the saints presume on God's mercy, which is great, you presume on your own virtue, which is nonexistent; and as much as they, helped by God's grace, wage war against inborn vices, you wage war against God's grace itself. But would that, just as it conquers you in those who belong to it, so, making you its own, it may conquer you yourselves within you!

**8. 30.** Do you dare, then, to say in your heart that when people hear you they are fired with zeal for virtue, but when they hear such great men as Cyprian, Hilary, Gregory, Ambrose, and the other priests of the Lord, they are broken by despair and abandon the pursuit of perfection? Do these monstrous thoughts rise into your heart without crushing your impudence? Is it you, then, who honor God's saints, the patriarchs, prophets, and apostles, by praising nature, while these lights of the Church disfigure them by blaming nature, because they say that in the body of this death they fought against the inborn evil of concupiscence to preserve the good of chastity, an evil first to be conquered in conflict through God's grace and afterward healed in the final regeneration? You understand a Jew to be the one who says: *for I do not do the good I wish*[^97], and with this splendid understanding “neither turn the filth of one's way of life into a reproach against nature nor console its obscenities with insults to the Apostles and all the saints.” And these evils which you do not do were done by Ambrose, together with his colleagues who held the same view, when he understands the blessed Apostle to have said of himself: *I do not do the good I wish, but the evil I do not wish, that I do*, and: *I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind*[^98], and other things of this kind? These saints, then, by teaching such things, “undermine,” as you accuse me of doing, “the wall of modesty,” while you suffer hostility for preaching perfection? Yet, as you write, you console yourself greatly because it is a kind of praise to have displeased someone who did not spare the Apostles. If I did not spare the Apostles by saying such things, then neither did Ambrose spare the Apostles, nor his fellow bishops who held the same view. But if they both learned these things from the Apostles and taught them in accordance with the Apostles, why do you attack me alone? Look at them; lay aside for a little while your stiff-necked pride and gaze at them again and again. Surely, then, most self-confident young man, should you console yourself because you displease such men, or mourn?

**9. 31.** But now let us gather as briefly as we can into a summary what we have done throughout this book. We proposed here, by the weight of the authority of saints who were bishops before us and vigorously defended the Catholic faith not only by speech while they lived here but also by writings left to posterity, to break your arguments, by which you say: “If God creates human beings, they cannot be born with any evil. If marriage is good, no evil arises from it. If all sins are forgiven in baptism, those born of the reborn cannot contract original sin. If God is just, he cannot condemn parents' sins in their children when he forgives even the parents' own sins. If human nature is capable of perfect righteousness, it cannot have natural vices.” To these we reply that God is indeed the creator of human beings, that is, of both soul and body; that marriage is good; that all sins are forgiven through Christ's baptism; that God is just; and that human nature is capable of perfect righteousness. Yet, although all these things are true, human beings are born subject to a corrupted origin derived from the first man and therefore go to condemnation unless they are reborn in Christ. We proved this by the authority of Catholic saints who both assert what we say about original sin and confess that all those five things are true. Hence it does not follow that this is false because those things are true. For such great men confirm, according to the Catholic faith spread throughout the world from antiquity, that both this and those things are true, so that your frail and supposedly clever novelty is crushed by their authority alone, apart from the fact that what they say is such that truth itself attests that it speaks through them. But now your obstinacy must first be checked by their authority, so that, driven back from your impetuous presumption and in a manner wounded, you may restrain your headlong audacity. For you do not believe that such men of God could have erred in the Catholic faith so as to say something from which it would follow that God is not the creator of human beings, that marriage must be condemned, that not all sins are forgiven in baptism, that God is unjust, and that no hope remains to us of perfecting virtue—to think all or any of which is impious. Thus, as though recovering from madness, begin to recall, recognize, and take up again the truth in which you were nourished.

**9. 32.** Blessed Ambrose says that the one man, the mediator of God and human beings, alone is not held by the bonds of guilty generation because he was born of a virgin and did not experience sin in being born. But all human beings are born under sin, for their very origin is in vice, because, formed through the pleasure of concupiscence, they undergo the contagion of offenses before drawing life's breath from this air. And concupiscence itself, as the law of sin in the body of this death, so fights against the law of the mind that not only any good believers but even the greatness of apostolic virtue fought against it, so that through Christ's grace the flesh, subjected to the soul, might be recalled to concord. Discord arose between these two, originally created without sin, through the first man's transgression. And who says these things? A man of God, a Catholic, and a most vigorous defender of Catholic truth against heretics even at the risk of bloodshed, so praised by your teacher's testimony that it was said that “not even an enemy dared to reproach his faith and his most pure understanding of the Scriptures.” He asserted, against the error of the Platonic philosophers, that God is creator not only of souls but also of bodies. He proclaimed that the good of marriage was divinely instituted to propagate the human race and that its union is holy through marital chastity. He said that no one is justified from sin unless all his sins have been forgiven through baptism. He justly worshiped the just God. Far be it from him to hinder human perfection in virtue and righteousness by despairing of it; yet he hopes for that perfection to which nothing need be added in another life, to be fulfilled by the resurrection of the dead. In this life, however, he places human righteousness in a kind of military service and warfare, not only against hostile powers of the air but against our own desires, through which those external enemies also attempt to cast us down or penetrate us. In this war he calls the flesh itself a formidable adversary. Its nature would surely have remained in complete concord with us as it was first created, had it not, corrupted by the first man's transgression, fought against us through its weakness. In this war that holy man admonishes us to flee the world and shows how great is the difficulty of that flight, indeed its impossibility, unless God's grace assists. He says that vices are indeed dead through the forgiveness of all sins in baptism, but that we must in some way attend to their burial. And in this very work he describes our conflict with dead vices as such that we do not do what we wish, but do what we hate; that sin accomplishes many things in us despite our resistance; that pleasures often revive and rise again; that we must struggle against the flesh, against which Paul struggled when he said: *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind*[^99]. He commands us not to trust our flesh or entrust ourselves to it, since the Apostle cries: *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh; for willing is at hand for me, but I do not find how to accomplish the good*[^100]. See how great a struggle with dead sins this vigorous soldier of Christ and faithful teacher of the Church shows that we have. For how is sin dead when it accomplishes many things in us despite our resistance? What many things, if not foolish and harmful desires that plunge those who consent into ruin and destruction [^101]? Surely, to endure these and not consent to them is a contest, a conflict, a fight. A fight between what, if not good and evil—not nature against nature, but nature against a vice already dead, yet still to be buried, that is, completely healed? How, then, do we say that this sin is dead in baptism, as this man also says, and how do we confess that it dwells in the members and produces many desires despite our resistance, which we resist by not consenting, as this man also confesses? Only because it is dead in respect of the guilt by which it held us, and until healed by the completion of its burial it rebels even though dead. It is no longer called sin in the sense in which it makes one guilty, but because it was brought about through the first man's guilt and because, by rebelling, it strives to draw us into guilt, unless God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord helps us, lest sin, even though dead, rebel in such a way as to revive and reign by conquering.

**10. 33.** As we labor in this war, as long as human life on earth is a trial [^102], it is not because what is called sin in this sense works in our members fighting against the law of the mind, even when we do not consent to it in unlawful things, that we are not without sin. For, as far as we are concerned, we would always be without sin until this evil was healed if we never consented to it for evil. Rather, in those things in which we are overcome by its rebellion, even if not mortally but venially, we contract that for which we say daily: *forgive us our debts*[^103]. This occurs, for example, when spouses exceed the measure necessary for generation for the sake of pleasure alone; likewise, when the continent linger with some delight over such thoughts, not indeed deciding upon a shameful deed, but failing to turn their mind's attention away as they ought, lest it fall there, or to snatch it away if it has fallen. Concerning this law of sin, which in another sense is also called sin and which fights against the law of the mind, about which blessed Ambrose said much, the saints Cyprian, Hilary, Gregory, and very many others also bear witness. Thus one who is begotten in Adam, to be regenerated in Christ, dead in Adam, to be made alive in Christ, is bound by original sin because he is born from the evil by which the flesh desires against the spirit, not from the good by which the spirit desires against the flesh [^104]. What wonder, then, if a human being must be reborn when born from that evil against which the reborn human being fights, by which he too would be held guilty unless freed by rebirth? This evil is not the material of God's creation but the wound inflicted by the devil corrupting that same material. This is not the evil of marriage but the sin of the first human beings, transmitted to their descendants through propagation. The guilt of this evil too is forgiven by baptism's sanctification. But if the just God imposed on infants who contracted no sin such great evils as I cannot now adequately recount, he would rather appear unjust. Nor is a human being's capacity for perfect righteousness denied, because neither is the fullest healing of all vices despaired of under the almighty physician. On behalf of this Catholic truth, the holy and blessed priests most distinguished in expounding the divine words—Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Innocent, John, and Basil, to whom I add, whether you like it or not, the priest Jerome, to omit those not yet asleep—pronounce against you their judgment concerning the succession of all human beings bound by original sin. No one delivers from this except him whom the Virgin conceived without the law of sin fighting against the law of the mind.

**10. 34.** Why do you exult and insult me as though victorious, “as if I could not find what to do or where to take refuge if I were pressed by the authority of judges, if I stood with you among the learned, if the trumpet of true reason,” as you call it—blown, of course, by you, the great trumpeter—“and the weapons of the surrounding hearers,” favoring you, “resounded”? For in this way you place our contest in debate before your eyes and imagine as you please that you argue and I have no reply. Thus the vain and insane imagination of your heart spins tales to you, as though you placed me with you before Pelagian judges, whose applause would allow you to lift your voice like a trumpet and preach, against the Catholic faith and against Christ's grace, by which the small together with the great are delivered from evil, the error of this novel impiety that you share with them. Your instructor Pelagius could not find such judges in God's Church even without an opponent standing on the other side. He emerged from that judgment apparently acquitted as far as human eyes could see, yet with your doctrine openly condemned. But I, wherever you are, wherever you can read these things, place you before these judges within your own heart. I have not appointed as arbiters in our dispute friends of mine and enemies of yours, inclined toward my side by some favor, alienated from you by some offense you deservedly gave, and therefore opposed to you. Nor have I invented in empty thought men who never existed or do not exist, or whose judgments about what we dispute are uncertain. Rather, I have named, as was fitting, holy bishops of God, illustrious in the holy Church, trained not in Platonic, Aristotelian, Stoic, and similar writings, Greek or Latin—though some of them in these too—but all in sacred letters. I have set out their judgments, issued without any ambiguity, as far as seemed sufficient, so that in them you may fear not the men themselves, but him who fashioned them as useful vessels for himself and built them as holy temples. They judged this cause at a time when no one can say they could have wrongly opposed or favored anyone. For you had not yet arisen, against whom we would undertake a conflict on this question. You did not yet exist to say what you put in your books: “That we have lied about you to the multitude, that we frighten people with the name of Celestians or Pelagians, and that by terror we extort people's assent.” Certainly you yourself said that “all judges ought to be free from hatred, friendship, enmity, and anger.” Few such men could be found, but Ambrose and his other colleagues whom I mentioned with him must be believed to have been such. Even if they were not such in the cases brought before them and investigated between parties, which they settled by their judgment while they lived here, they were nevertheless such regarding this case when they pronounced their judgments on it. They considered no friendships with us or you and exercised no enmities; they were angry with neither us nor you, and pitied neither us nor you. What they found in the Church, they held; what they learned, they taught; what they received from the fathers, they handed on to the children. We were not yet pleading anything against you before these judges, and our case was pleaded before them. Neither we nor you had been known to them, and we recite their judgments pronounced for us against you. We were not yet contending with you, and through their verdict we won.

**10. 35.** You say that “if I were pressed under the authority of judges”—such as you devise for yourself—“I would have had nothing to do and nowhere to take refuge, since I could find no way to meet your arguments.” Certainly I would have something to do and somewhere to take refuge: I would appeal from Pelagian darkness to these splendid Catholic lights, as indeed I am doing now. Answer, then, what you will do; say where you will take refuge. I appeal from the Pelagians to these men; from these men, to whom will you appeal? Or because you judge that “opinions should be weighed, not counted,” and add—what I too agree is true—that “a multitude of blind people is of no use for finding anything,” will you dare to call these men blind as well? Has the long passage of time so mingled the highest with the lowest [^105], is darkness so called light and light darkness, that Pelagius, Celestius, and Julian see, while Hilary, Gregory, and Ambrose are blind? But whatever kind of man you are, because you are human, I seem to see your shame—if indeed not all hope of soundness has died in you—and in a way I hear your voice. You reply: “Far be it from me to dare even to think, much less say, that these men are blind.” Weigh their judgments, then. I do not wish them to be so many that you tire of counting them; but they are not so light that you should disdain to weigh them. Indeed, they are so weighty that I see you labor beneath their burden. Will you say of these men too that “I oppose you with the feeble opinion of an accomplice, and, as though panic-stricken with fear, name my confederates”?

**10. 36.** You say: “In judging a case, with the din of crowds removed, from every rank of human life—priests, administrators, or prefects—for the discussion of such matters one should select not mere names but prudence, and honor the few whom reason, learning, and freedom elevate.” It is indeed as you say. But I do not unsettle you with the numbers of any multitude, although, by God's favor, the Catholic multitude too thinks soundly concerning this faith you oppose. Everywhere among it very many, where they can, as they can, helped by God, refute your empty arguments. Hence far be from me the arrogance you accuse me of, “promising to conduct this case against you alone on behalf of everyone.” But when you do this among the Pelagians, unashamed to say and write that “it brings you greater glory before God to defend abandoned truth,” they are greatly debased and truly abandoned, and greatly dependent on you, if they do not judge this intolerable arrogance. By it you put yourself even before Pelagius and Celestius themselves, the teachers of you all, as though they too had already yielded and you alone remained to defend what you think the truth, now abandoned. But because you delight in weighing the few rather than counting the multitude, apart from the Palestinian judges who condemned your heresy while acquitting Pelagius, whom they compelled, restrained by fear, to condemn the Pelagian doctrines themselves, I have set against you as judges of this case ten bishops already dead and one priest, who judged it while they lived here. If your small number is considered, they are many; if the multitude of Catholic bishops, they are very few. Of these you may perhaps try to withdraw Pope Innocent and the priest Jerome: the former because he condemned Pelagius and Celestius, the latter because in the East he defended the Catholic faith against Pelagius with devout intent. But read what Pelagius says in praise of most blessed Pope Innocent [^106], and see whether you can easily find such judges. As for that holy priest, who according to the grace given him so labored in the Church as greatly to aid Catholic learning in the Latin language by many necessary writings, Pelagius usually alleges only “that he envied him as a rival.” But I do not wish you to think that on this account he must be removed from the number of these judges. For I have not quoted the judgment he held and defended against your error at the time of their hostility, but what he set down in his writings, free of every partisan interest, before your damnable doctrines sprouted.

**10. 37.** About the rest, certainly, you have absolutely nothing to say. Were Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Gregory, Basil, Ambrose, and John “stirred up against you from the plebeian dregs of sedentary craftsmen,” as you jest in Ciceronian style? Were they “soldiers”? “Students of the lecture halls”? “Sailors, tavernkeepers, fishmongers, cooks, and butchers”? “Dissolute young men from among the monks”? Finally, were these men from “the throng of clerics of whatever sort,” whom you harry with urbane wit, or rather vanity, and despise “because they cannot judge doctrines according to Aristotle's categories”? As though you, who chiefly complain that “an examination and episcopal judgment are denied you,” could find a council of Peripatetics where a dialectical judgment against original sin would be pronounced concerning a subject and the things in a subject! These are bishops, learned, weighty, holy, most vigorous defenders of truth against garrulous vanities, in whose reason, learning, and freedom—the three goods you assigned to a judge—you can find nothing to despise. If an episcopal synod were gathered from the whole world, it would be remarkable if so many such men could easily sit there. For these men did not live at one time. God himself distributes his few faithful stewards, far more excellent than many, through different ages and distant places, as he pleases and judges expedient. Thus you see them gathered from different times and regions, from East and West, not into a place to which people must sail, but into a book that can sail to people. As much as these judges would be desirable to you if you held the Catholic faith, so much more let them be terrible to you because you attack the Catholic faith which they drank in as milk, which they took as food, whose milk and food they ministered to small and great, which they defended most openly and bravely against enemies, even against you, then not yet born, as now becomes evident. Through such planters, waterers, builders, shepherds, and nourishers after the Apostles, the holy Church grew. Therefore she recoiled at the profane voices of your novelty; and, cautious and sober at the Apostle's admonition, lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, her mind too should be corrupted from the chastity that is in Christ [^107], in the virginity of the Catholic faith she shuddered at your doctrine creeping in, and trampled, crushed, and cast it away like a serpent's head. By these words, then, and such great authority of the saints, either you will surely be healed by the gift of God's mercy—how greatly I wish this for you is seen by him who can accomplish it—or, if, as I abhor, you persist in what seems wisdom to you but is great foolishness, you will seek judges not before whom to clear your own case but before whom to accuse so many holy, outstanding, and memorable teachers of Catholic truth: Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Gregory, Basil, Ambrose, John, Innocent, Jerome, and their other associates and companions, and, beyond them, the whole Church of Christ. Faithfully ministering the Lord's food to this divine household, they shone with immense glory in the Lord. Against this miserable madness, which may God avert from you, I see that your books must be answered in such a way that their faith too is defended against you, just as even the Gospel itself is defended against the impious and professed enemies of Christ.
