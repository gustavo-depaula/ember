# Book 1

**1. 1.** If I said that I despise your insults and abusive words, Julian, which you have breathed out in four books with burning anger, I would be lying. For how can I despise them, when, considering the testimony of my conscience, I see that I ought either to rejoice for myself or to grieve for you and for those you deceive? Who would despise the material of either his own rejoicing or his sorrow? For we can in no way despise that which partly gladdens and partly saddens us. The cause of my joy is the promise of the Lord, who says: *when they falsely say every evil against you because of me, rejoice and exult, for your reward is great in heaven*[^1]. Again, the cause of my sorrow is the Apostle's affection, where I read: *who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to stumble, and I do not burn?*[^2] But you too can say these things on behalf of your teaching, which you think is the truth. Let us therefore, if you please, set aside these commonplaces, which can be said on both sides, although they cannot truthfully be said on both sides.

**1. 2.** First I ask you why you boast of having answered my book, even to outward appearances, when in your four books your criticism has not touched even a quarter of my one book, and you have made such leaps in passing over my arguments as though you utterly despaired that anyone could read both works, mine and yours, and detect this. Indeed, even the few passages that, as I said, scarcely amount to a quarter of my volume, and that you thought weaker and tried to overthrow and trample under the noise of your four larger volumes, as though under the charge of four-horse chariots, are shown to remain unshaken when one considers the far more numerous other passages you were afraid to touch. It is almost superfluous to demonstrate this. Those who wish to know it should instead be urged not to shrink from reading both what I wrote and what you chose to answer. For the matter so plainly declares and reveals itself that those who demand a demonstration from us must be exceedingly slow.

**1. 3.** Now, therefore, since I see that, deserted by truth, you have turned to abuse, I will arrange my argument as follows. First I will show how great and distinguished are the teachers of the Catholic Church whom you do not hesitate to outrage intolerably by calling them Manichaeans, and at whom you hurl your sacrilegious weapons when you attack me. Next I will show that you yourself so assist the Manichaeans' damnable error and unspeakable impiety that they could not find such a champion even among their own admirers. Third, as briefly as I can, I will refute your empty subtleties and elaborate arguments with statements that are not mine but those of men who came before us and defended the Catholic faith against the impious. Finally, since unless you correct yourself you will necessarily have to attack those teachers of the Catholic Church as well, and contend that they too failed to hold Catholic truth on this question, with God's help I will defend both their faith and ours against you. There it will also become apparent how much assistance the Manichaeans receive not only from your own words, as I promised to demonstrate in the second place, but from the Pelagian teaching itself, which you all share.

**2. 4.** Pay attention for a little while, then, to how I carry out the first part of my arrangement. As regards the purpose of my book, which you boast of having answered with your four, the question between us is this: I say that marriage must be praised in such a way that the fact that all human beings are born liable for the sin of those first human beings in no way brings blame or condemnation upon marriage. Whoever denies this attempts to overthrow the very foundations of the Christian faith. This was why I wrote the book *On Marriage and Concupiscence*: to distinguish the good of marriage from the evil from which original sin is contracted. You, however, say that marriage is undoubtedly condemned unless what is born from it is free from every bond of sin; and for this reason you boast of having refuted my one book with your four. In these books, wishing to turn people away from the most firmly established Catholic faith and bring them to the novelty of your error, you repeatedly strike your readers' minds with horror at the Manichaean plague, as though anyone who says that infants, born carnally according to Adam, contract the contagion of the ancient death in their first birth, and therefore need a second, shared the Manichaeans' belief in a natural evil. Yet by the washing of regeneration they must first be purified through the remission of original sin and, adopted as God's children, be transferred to the kingdom of the Only-begotten. Jovinian likewise used the name and accusation of the Manichaeans when he denied that holy Mary's virginity, which existed when she conceived, remained when she gave birth: as though we shared the Manichaeans' belief that Christ was a phantom if we said that he was born without impairing his mother's virginity. But with the Savior's own help, Catholics rejected the supposedly very acute argument Jovinian had produced. They believed neither that holy Mary had been violated by giving birth nor that the Lord was a phantom, but that she remained a virgin after giving birth and that nevertheless Christ's true body was born from her. In the same way they will reject your slanderous empty talk, neither asserting with the Manichaeans a natural principle of evil nor doubting in any way, according to the ancient and true Catholic faith, that they should believe Christ to be the liberator of little children, canceling the written bond of their ancestral debt.

**3. 5.** But you, who so frequently brand us with the name of Manichaeans, wake up, if you can, and consider what men, what distinguished men, what great defenders of the Catholic faith you dare to attack with so execrable an accusation. I do not promise to gather everyone's statements on this matter, or all the statements of those I shall mention, because that would take far too long and I do not think it necessary. Instead I shall set down a few statements from a few men, which should nevertheless compel our opponents to blush and yield, if any fear of God or shame before men in them can overcome so great an evil as their obstinacy. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, lived not long after the time of the apostles. He says: "Human beings are saved from the serpent's ancient wound in no other way than by believing in him who, in the likeness of sinful flesh, was lifted up from the earth on the tree of martyrdom, drew all things to himself, and gave life to the dead" [^3]. Again, the same man says: "Just as the human race was bound to death through a virgin, so it is released through a virgin, virginal disobedience being balanced in equal scales by virginal obedience. For, as the sin of the first-formed man received correction through the Firstborn's reproof, and the serpent's cunning was defeated through the simplicity of the dove, we were released from those bonds by which we had been bound to death" [^4]. Do you see what this ancient man of God thinks of the serpent's ancient wound? What he thinks of the likeness of sinful flesh, through which the serpent's wound is healed in sinful flesh? What he thinks of the first-formed man's sin, through which we had been bound?

**3. 6.** The most blessed martyr and bishop Cyprian speaks much more plainly about the same faith: "If anything could prevent human beings from obtaining grace, more serious sins could rather prevent adults, the mature, and those advanced in years. But if remission of sins is granted even to the gravest offenders, who previously sinned greatly against God, when they afterward believe, and no one is barred from baptism and grace, how much less should an infant be barred, who, newly born, has committed no sin except that, born carnally according to Adam, he has contracted the contagion of the ancient death in his first birth? He approaches the reception of remission of sins all the more easily precisely because what is forgiven him is not his own sins but those of another" [^5].

**3. 7.** That Reticius, bishop of Autun, enjoyed great authority in the Church during his episcopate is shown by those ecclesiastical records in which, at Rome, with Miltiades, bishop of the Apostolic See, presiding, he served as judge with others, condemned Donatus, who first originated the Donatist schism, and acquitted Caecilian, bishop of the Church of Carthage. In discussing baptism, he spoke as follows: "No one is unaware, therefore, that this is the foremost pardon in the Church, in which we lay down the entire burden of the ancient crime and wipe away the former misdeeds of our ignorance, where we also put off the old man with his inborn offenses" [^6]. You hear of the burden of an ancient crime; you hear of former misdeeds; you hear of the old man with inborn offenses. And you dare to build your ruinous novelty against these things!

**3. 8.** Olympius, a Spanish bishop, a man of great renown in the Church and in Christ, says in a certain ecclesiastical sermon: "If faith had ever remained uncorrupted on earth and had kept its feet planted in the tracks it abandoned after they were marked out, it would never, through the first-formed man's deadly transgression, have spread corruption into the offspring, so that sin was born along with the human being" [^7]. Do you have anything that you think you can say against us that you are not compelled to say against him as well, or rather against all these men? For the Catholic faith is one among all those who believe with one heart and confess with one mouth that *sin entered the world through one man, in whom all sinned*[^8], and who overthrow your novel presumptions by Catholic antiquity.

**3. 9.** Hear still more that may stir and trouble you, and would that it might change you for the better. Who does not know the venerable Hilary, a bishop of Gaul, the most vigorous defender of the Catholic Church against heretics? When he discusses Christ's flesh, attend to what he says: "Therefore, *when he was sent*, he says, *in the likeness of sinful flesh*[^9], he did not have sin as he had flesh. But because all flesh comes from sin, being derived, that is, from the sin of Adam our parent, he was sent in the likeness of sinful flesh, there being in him not sin but the likeness of sinful flesh" [^10]. Again, in his Exposition of Psalm 118, when he comes to what is written, "*My soul shall live and praise you*[^11]," he says: "He does not reckon himself alive in this life, since he had said: *behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in sins my mother bore me*[^12]. He knows that he was born under the origin of sin and under the law of sin" [^13]. Do you realize what you are hearing? Are you searching for something to say? Dare, if you have the impudence, to accuse this man, distinguished by such praise among Catholic bishops, so conspicuous in renown and reputation, on the subject of original sin.

**3. 10.** But hear yet another excellent steward of God, whom I revere as a father, for he begot me in Christ Jesus through the Gospel [^14], and through him as Christ's minister I received the washing of regeneration. I mean blessed Ambrose, whose grace, steadfastness, labors, and dangers on behalf of the Catholic faith, in deeds and in words, I have myself experienced, and whose praises the Roman world does not hesitate to proclaim along with me. In expounding the *Gospel* *according to Luke*, this man says: "*The Jordan*, he says, *turned back*[^15]; it signified the future mysteries of the saving washing, through which little children who have been baptized are restored from evil to the original state of their nature" [^16]. Again in the same work: "For no intercourse with a man opened the secret places of the virgin's womb, but the Holy Spirit poured an unstained seed into her inviolate womb. For among those born of woman, the Lord Jesus alone is holy in every respect: through the novelty of an unstained birth he did not experience the contagion of earthly corruption, and by his heavenly majesty he repelled it" [^17]. Again in that same work: "We all die in Adam, because *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and thus it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^18]. His fault, therefore, is the death of all" [^19]. Elsewhere on the same Gospel: "Beware, therefore, lest you first be stripped, as Adam was stripped, abandoned by the protection of the heavenly command and divested of the garment of faith. Thus he received the mortal wound in which the whole human race would have perished, had not that Samaritan come down and healed his grievous wounds" [^20]. Again, elsewhere in that work: "Adam existed, and in him we all existed; Adam perished, and in him all perished" [^21]. Again, in his *Defense of the Prophet David*: "Before we are born we are stained by contagion, and before enjoying the light we receive the injury of our very origin: we are conceived in iniquity. He did not specify whether it was our parents' or our own. And in sins each person's mother gives birth to him. Here too he did not declare whether the mother gives birth in her own sins or whether the newborn already has sins of his own. But consider whether both are to be understood. Conception is not free from iniquity, since the parents themselves are not without a fall; and if not even a one-day-old infant is without sin [^22], much less are those days of conception in the mother without sin. We are therefore conceived in our parents' sin and born in their transgressions [^23]. But birth itself also has its contagions, and nature itself has more than a single contagion" [^24]. Again in his *Exposition of the Book* *of Tobit*: "Who is this usurer of sin but the devil, from whom Eve borrowed sin and then lent it at interest to the whole human race through the interest payments of an indebted succession?" [^25]. Again in the same work: "The devil deceived Eve so as to overthrow the man and bind his inheritance with debt" [^26]. Again in his *Exposition of Psalm* 48: "Our iniquity is one thing, and the iniquity of our heel is another. In that heel Adam was wounded by the serpent's tooth, and through his wound he left a debt-laden inheritance to human posterity, so that we all limp from that wound" [^27].

**4. 11.** Go on, then, and reproach me with original sin. Ignore these men; pretend not to know what they say. As though you did not see so many great teachers of the holy Church, who lived exemplary lives, vanquished the errors of their times, and departed this world most gloriously before you people bubbled up, launch your attack against me. As though unaware that they are being torn apart under my name, insult me in supposed security. I confess that I would believe you ignorant of the evil you do, and would attribute to your imprudence rather than your impudence your daring to persecute as enemies these lights of the city of God, whom you should have faithfully followed. I would believe, I say, that you were committing this enormous outrage unknowingly, had I not included the clearest testimony from a discussion by holy Ambrose in the book that you either think you have answered or wish people to think you have answered [^28]. Did you not read there how the bishop just mentioned, explaining Christ's birth from a virgin, said: "Therefore, as man, he was tempted in all things and in the likeness of human beings endured everything; but as born of the Spirit he refrained from sin. For *every human being* is *a liar*[^29], and no one is without sin except God alone. Thus it has been maintained that no one born of man and woman, that is, through that union of bodies, is seen to be free from transgression. But he who is free from transgression is also free from this manner of conception" [^30]. If you did not read these words of the venerable Ambrose there, how did you undertake to refute the book in which they are set down? But if you did read them, why do you rage against me and first assail him in me? Why do you strive to tear apart my name, and, without mentioning his name, make Ambrose a Manichaean?

**4. 12.** Surely you see with whom I endure your abuse; you see with whom I share the cause that, without any sober consideration, you try to batter with slanders and overthrow? You see how destructive it is for you to charge such men with so horrible a crime, and how glorious it is for me to hear any accusation in company with such men? If you see, then look, and at last be silent. Restrain your Pelagian tongue before so many Catholic tongues; bow your insolent mouth before so many venerable mouths. Had you entered Xenocrates' school drunk from a banquet before dawn, as Polemo did, you would not have had to be restrained by such shame as you should feel in this gathering of so many saints. Here shame should certainly be greater, since the wisdom learned is truer. The faces of so many memorable bishops deserve greater reverence than that of Xenocrates alone, just as Christ, their teacher, is greater than Plato, Xenocrates' teacher. For my part, mindful of your father Memor of blessed memory, who had formed a worthy friendship with me through correspondence and had made you yourself very dear to me, when I saw you in your books, not drunk from a predawn banquet but turbulent with insane abuse, I brought you, to calm and heal you, not into some philosopher's lecture hall but into the peaceful and honorable gathering of the holy fathers. Let this labor bear fruit, I beg you. Look at them as though they were looking at you and saying mildly and gently: Is it true, son Julian, that we are Manichaeans? I ask you, what will you answer? With what eyes will you look at them? What arguments will come to your aid? What categories of Aristotle, with which you wish to appear polished so as to pounce on us like an expert disputant? What glass edge or leaden daggers of your arguments will dare to be drawn in their presence? What weapons will not flee from you and leave you naked? Or will you perhaps say that you named none of them in this accusation? And what will you do when they say: You would have been more tolerable if your abusive tooth had torn our names apart rather than our faith, through whose merit those very names of ours are written in heaven? Or will you perhaps say to them: I did not violate your faith with any such accusation either? But with what assurance will you dare to say this, you who say that it is a Manichaean belief to confess that those who are born derive original sin from Adam? Yet these men confessed and professed this: they learned it in Christ's Church at the time of their first instruction, and taught it to Christ's Church when they held their offices. Through the Holy Spirit they forgave so many sins in all whom they were able to baptize, and in so many little children original sins alone. Again I admonish you, again I beg you: look at so many and such great defenders and teachers of the Catholic Church; see upon whom you have inflicted so grievous and impious an insult.

**4. 13.** Or do you think they should be despised because they all belong to the Western Church and we have mentioned no Eastern bishop among them? What, then, shall we do, since they are Greeks and we are Latins? I think that part of the world in which the Lord chose to crown the first of his apostles with a most glorious martyrdom should be enough for you. Had you been willing to listen to blessed Innocent, who presided over that Church, you would already then have freed your imperiled youth from the Pelagian snares. For what could that holy man have answered the African councils except what the Apostolic See and the Roman Church together with the others have steadfastly held from ancient times? Yet you accuse his successor, Zosimus, of betrayal because he was unwilling to oppose apostolic teaching and his predecessor's judgment. But I say nothing of this for now, lest praising the man who condemned you wound your spirit, which I desire to heal rather than provoke. Consider what answer you can make to holy Innocent, whose belief in this matter differs in no way from that of these men into whose company I have brought you, if indeed it does any good. He too takes his seat with them, though later in time, first in rank. He shares with them one true Christian judgment concerning the deliverance through Christ's grace of miserable little children from the original evil derived from Adam. He said that Christ, by the washing of his baptism, cleansed every past fault, namely that of the first man, who through free choice sank into the depths [^31]. Indeed, he determined [^32] that little children can have no life at all unless they eat the flesh of the Son of man [^33]. Answer him, or rather the Lord himself, whose testimony that bishop employed, and say why God's image is punished by so capital a penalty as deprivation of life, if original sin is not contracted by those who are born. But what can you say, or what answer can you give, when, even if you dare to call blessed Innocent a Manichaean, you will not dare to call Christ one?

**4. 14.** There is therefore no reason for you to appeal to the bishops of the East, since they too are certainly Christians, and this faith is one in both parts of the world, because this faith is Christian. Moreover, a Western land gave you birth, and the Western Church gave you rebirth. Why do you seek to introduce into her what you did not find in her when you entered her membership? Rather, why do you seek to take from her what you yourself received in her? For original sin, which you deny to the destruction of other little children, was forgiven you, either alone or together with other sins, at whatever age you were baptized. But if what we have heard is true, that you were baptized as an infant, then you too, though innocent of any sins of your own, had nevertheless, because you were born carnally according to Adam, contracted the contagion of the ancient death in your first birth and were conceived in iniquity. Certainly you were exorcised and breathed upon so that, rescued from the power of darkness, you might be transferred into Christ's kingdom [^34]. O son, badly born from Adam but well reborn in Christ, you try to take from your mother the sacraments through which she gave you birth. Was she a Manichaean when she bore you in the way you now refuse to let her give birth? And do you heap reproaches upon her for that birth, so as to close to others her compassionate womb from which you yourself were born? You divide for her the name of her husband, so that for the rebirth of little children he should be merely Christ, but for older people Christ Jesus, because Jesus means Savior; and you do not wish him to be this for little children, since you proclaim that they have nothing from which he should save them.

**5. 15.** But you shall not lack, even from the regions of the East, a bishop of great name and most illustrious renown, whose words, deservedly enjoying enormous favor, became famous everywhere even in Latin translation. Let holy Gregory also sit with these fathers and endure with them the empty odium of your accusation, provided that with them he too pronounces a healing judgment against your novel plague. Hear, then, what he says: "Let the image of God purge the stain of the bodily flood and raise the flesh joined to it on the wings of God's word. And although it would have been better not even to need such purification, but to have remained in that first dignity toward which we hasten again after our present correction, and better not to have fallen away from the tree of life through the bitterest taste of sin, nevertheless the next best thing is to be amended and corrected after the fall, rather than remain in wickedness." Again he says: "Just as in Adam we have all died, so let us all be made alive in Christ. Let us therefore be born with Christ and crucified with Christ, and buried with him into death, so that with him we may also rise again to life. For we must undergo this beneficial and necessary change: just as we slid down from good things to sorrowful things, so may we be restored from sorrowful things to better ones. For where sin abounded, grace abounded still more, so that those whom the taste of the forbidden tree condemned may be justified by Christ's Cross with more abundant grace" [^35]. Again: "Revere the birth through which you have been freed from the bonds of earthly birth. Honor little and least Bethlehem, through which a way back to paradise has been opened for you" [^36]. Elsewhere, speaking of baptism: "On this point also let Christ's words persuade you, when he says that *no one can enter the kingdom of heaven unless he has been born again of water and the Spirit*[^37]. Through this the stains of the first birth are cleansed, through which we are conceived in iniquities and our mothers gave birth to us in sins" [^38]. Will you say that this man too savors or spreads Manichaeus's poison? You hear them all saying the same thing with one heart, one mouth, and one faith, and that this is the Catholic faith established by their harmonious testimony.

**5. 16.** Does the authority of the Eastern bishops seem slight to you in Gregory alone? He is certainly so great a figure that he would not have said this except from the Christian faith most familiar to everyone, nor would they hold him so illustrious and venerable unless they recognized that his words accorded with the rule of the truth most familiar to them. But if you wish, let us add holy Basil to him; indeed, whether you wish it or not, he must be added, especially since in the fourth volume of your work you thought fit to quote something from his book against the Manichaeans that has nothing whatever to do with the question of original sin entering the world through one man and passing through all human beings. There he is arguing against the belief that evil is substantial, possessing some material of its own, as the Manichaeans think. He therefore says that it "is not a substance but a manner of life arising from the will alone," not in those who contracted the contagion of the ancient death in their first birth, but "in those who have acquired the sickness of their manner of life from their own will," that is, those old enough to use reason and the choice of their will. He says that "this manner of life, which arises accidentally, can very easily be separated from the will of the sick." He also says: "If evil had arisen in such a way that it could no longer be removed from the will—that is, although it had arisen accidentally, if it had so arisen that it could no longer be separated from the will—one could rightly say that evil was indeed not substantial, but that the substance itself could no longer exist without the evil that had befallen it. But if it came upon it, and its coming had its beginning not in substance but in the will, evil can easily be separated from the substance, so that the substance subject to the will can be possessed entirely pure, with not even the traces of any evil remaining" [^39]. Holy Basil said these things so rightly that they can also properly be understood of the very evil that entered the world through Adam and passed through all human beings, since it too came upon human nature. For human nature was not originally created in that condition, and its coming had its beginning not in substance but in the will, whether of the woman deceived by the serpent or of her husband, who consented to sin with his deceived wife. But when he says that "evil can easily be separated from the will or from the substance," this is easy not for the human will but for God's mercy. That certainly suffices against the Manichaeans, who think it impossible for the nature of evil to be changed into good. Thus holy Basil did not say that the human will itself, or its substance or nature, can easily separate evil from itself, but that evil "can easily be separated from it." He weighed his words so as both to refute the Manichaeans, against whom he was arguing, and not to exalt human pride against divine grace. For the Almighty, to whom, according to the Gospel, what is impossible for human beings is easy [^40], will so consume by the generosity of his grace the evil that has befallen us through the first man's will or through our own will in addition, "that the substance subject to the will," as you recalled Basil's words, "can be possessed entirely pure," so utterly pure "that not even the traces of any evil remain." This will happen, and this is the undoubted hope of the faithful. But when it will be accomplished is readily apparent to the Catholic faith. For then it will be said to death, the last enemy: *where is your contention? Where is your sting*[^41]*?*

**5. 17.** Again, you recall Basil saying: "If chastity is a virtue, but the body were substantially evil, it would be impossible to find a chaste body, because a body of shame would not become a body of virtue. But when it is sanctified, it becomes a body of virtue, and thus virtue shares in the body and the body in virtue, through which it also becomes God's temple. Therefore, if every body belonged to fornication, it would certainly be impossible to find chastity in bodies, and only then could we assign substantial evil to the nature of bodies. But if the body's merits have advanced so far, if it has been adorned with such honor and clothed with such a garment of purity that it deserved to be the house of its Maker and became the bridal chamber of God's Son, so that the Father and the Son deigned to come and choose bodies as their dwelling, how is Manichaeus's discourse not proved execrable and ridiculous?" What could be said more truthfully or more agreeably to the Catholic rule? This was said against the Manichaeans, who think and assert that bodies originate from the race of darkness, which they call an evil nature coeternal with the good God, and that bodies themselves too are unchangeable evils. It was not said against those who, holding the true and truly Christian faith, confess that the body is now indeed corruptible and weighs down the soul [^42], but that it was neither created and placed in paradise in this condition nor will always remain so. Rather, it will be changed into incorruption and immortality, and already now begins to be God's temple, adorned with married, widowed, or even virginal chastity, when, although *the flesh desires against the spirit*[^43], the spirit nevertheless desires against the flesh in such a way that it does not offer even the body's members to sin as weapons of iniquity [^44].

**5. 18.** But hear what bears on the present matter: what this holy Basil says, without any ambiguity, about the first man's sin as it concerns us also. Although I found it translated, for more exact faithfulness to the truth I preferred to translate it word for word from the Greek. In his sermon *On Fasting* he says: "Fasting was established by law in paradise. For Adam received this first commandment: *you shall not eat from the tree of knowing good and evil*[^45]. *You shall not eat* is fasting, and the beginning of the establishment of law. Had Eve fasted from the tree, we would not need this fast. For *the healthy* have *no need of a physician, but the sick do*[^46]. We grew sick through sin; let us be healed through repentance. But repentance without fasting is empty. *The cursed earth shall bring forth thorns and thistles*[^47]. You have been appointed to sorrow; surely not to luxury?" And a little later in the same sermon he says: "Because we did not fast, we fell from paradise. Let us therefore fast, so that we may return to it" [^48]. Had you read these and other similar statements of holy Basil, or had you been willing to consider them faithfully after reading them, surely you would never have included in these books of yours, with what intention I do not know, passages from his writings that have no bearing on the question between us, to cast a fog before the ignorant. You hear that we would not need this fast had the human being not transgressed the law of fasting in the happiness of paradise; and you deny that other human beings are born liable for those people's sin. You hear the added words, "For the healthy have no need of a physician," and you deny that through those people's sin we lost the health in which we were created? You hear that the sentence pronounced upon the first human sinner, *the earth shall bring forth thorns and thistles for you*, concerns us also; and you deny liability for sin in those whom you see liable to the same sentence? You hear that we must return to paradise, from which we fell, and you deny that the sin of those who alone were then human beings in paradise, in whom we too were present, concerns us?

**5. 19.** Why say more about this? Consider now whether these two such distinguished men from the East, endowed with such illustrious holiness and, as is reported, brothers even in the flesh, suffice for you. But say that they do not suffice. We have fourteen other Eastern bishops—Eulogius, John, Ammonianus, Porphyry, Eutonius, Porphyry, Fidus, Zoninus, Zoboennus, Nymphidius, Chromatius, Jovinus, Eleutherius, and Clematius—whom, found together in one place, we can bring into this assembly. These are the very men who presided as judges over Pelagius and, being human, with no opponent pressing the other side, thought him Catholic and pronounced him Catholic. For unless, in their presence and hearing, he had condemned those who say "that Adam's sin harmed him alone and not the human race; that newborn infants are in the state Adam was in before sin; and that infants have eternal life even if they are not baptized," he would certainly have left there condemned. What profit is it to you that you insert some sort of handles and hooks, by the art of some inscrutable entanglement, to keep simple things from being plain and clear things from shining? For who does not see how those judges could have understood these statements: according to the Catholic faith, which everywhere rescues little children from the power of darkness by exorcising them and breathing upon them, and not as these statements are explained, or rather fabricated, by you? Yet you have been able to say: "Adam's sin harmed the human race not through propagation but through imitation; and newborn infants are not in the state Adam was in before sin because he was capable of receiving a commandment and they are not yet capable." With these clouds Pelagius thinks he fooled that tribunal; you nod your whole heads in assent to this outrage and laugh that he made fools of so many bishops. But have you been able by any cleverness to twist "infants have eternal life even if they are not baptized" into another meaning, or cover it with any fig leaves? For before Catholic judges Pelagius could do nothing but condemn those who say this, and he condemned before men what he himself believed, fearing to be condemned by men. For if you do not believe this, you certainly agree with us. But since you do not agree with us, you undoubtedly believe it. Thus the Eastern judges condemn you, and Pelagius, fearing their condemnation, condemned those who hold these opinions, though he himself assuredly deserved condemnation with those he condemned, because he kept in his heart what he denied with his mouth. What his words anathematize is found in his writings. But I am not now dealing with him. What do you say, with whom I am now dealing? Here stand so many Eastern judges; the ecclesiastical proceedings held before them are read. We read that Pelagius was charged with "saying that even unbaptized infants have eternal life." We read that Pelagius condemned those who say this, for otherwise he could not have escaped those judges. Now what do you yourself say? Will little children have eternal life even if they depart this life unbaptized, or will they not? If you say, They will, the words of your own Pelagius will condemn you, and so will all those by whom he feared to be condemned. But if you say, They will not, I ask why God's innocent image is punished by deprivation of life if no sin is contracted through human propagation. But if it is contracted, why are those who believe this called Manichaeans, when Pelagius would have been condemned by them unless he had pretended to believe it himself?

**5. 20.** You now have before your eyes bishops not only of the West but also of the East. Indeed, we have found more from the East, where they seemed to be lacking to us. They all believe in one and the same way that *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death, and thus it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^49]. That way is this: all are believed to be born liable for the sin of that one first man. If you call anyone who says this a Manichaean, look at these men, be ashamed before them, spare them—or rather yourself, lest perhaps their ruler and indweller spare you not. But if you do not call these men Manichaeans, you will be unable to find a reason for calling me one. For you call me this for no other reason than that, concerning the first man's sin, to which carnal birth has bound everyone and from which spiritual birth alone releases anyone, I believe what they believe, hold what they hold, teach what they teach, and preach what they preach. Yield to them, and you will not strike me; acquiesce in them, and you will cease attacking me. Finally, if you refuse to become my friend through them, I ask at least that you not become their enemy through me. But how will you avoid becoming their enemy if you remain in this error? How much better, then, to approach these men and withdraw from that error. Do Pelagius and Caelestius carry such weight with you that from sunrise to sunset you dare not merely to desert so many great teachers and defenders of the Catholic faith, ancient and close to our own age, asleep and still living, but even to call them Manichaeans? I wonder whether this could ever pass your lips, though the perversity of your error compels you to shout it. But it is strange if, in a human face, the distance between forehead and tongue is so great that in this matter shame in the forehead does not restrain the tongue.

**6. 21.** But I know what you are muttering. Say it now, say it; let us hear. At the end of the work of yours we are now discussing, that is, in the very last part of the fourth book, you say: "Holy John of Constantinople denies that there is original sin in little children. For in the homily he delivered on the baptized, he says: 'Blessed is God, who alone has done wonders, who has made all things and transformed all things. Behold, those who a little while ago were held captive enjoy the serenity of freedom, and those who were wandering as strangers are citizens of the Church, and those who were in the confusion of sin share the lot of righteousness. For they are not merely free, but also holy; not merely holy, but also righteous; not only righteous, but also children; not only children, but also heirs; not only heirs, but also brothers of Christ; not merely brothers of Christ, but also fellow heirs; not only fellow heirs, but also members; not merely members, but also a temple; not merely a temple, but also instruments of the Spirit. You see how many the gifts of baptism are. Some think that heavenly grace consists only in the remission of sins, but we have counted ten honors. For this reason we baptize infants too, although they are not defiled by sin, so that holiness, righteousness, adoption, inheritance, and brotherhood with Christ may be added to them, so that they may be his members'" [^50].

**6. 22.** Do you dare to set these words of holy Bishop John in opposition to the judgments of so many distinguished colleagues of his, to separate him from their most harmonious fellowship and set him up as their adversary? Far be it, far be it from us to believe or say such an evil thing of so great a man. Far be it, I say, that John of Constantinople should oppose so many great fellow bishops concerning the baptism of little children and their liberation through Christ from the written bond of their ancestral debt—especially Innocent of Rome, Cyprian of Carthage, Basil of Cappadocia, Gregory of Nazianzus, Hilary of Gaul, and Ambrose of Milan. There are other matters in which even the most learned and excellent defenders of the Catholic rule sometimes disagree while preserving the unity of faith, and one speaks better and more truly about a matter than another. But the subject we are now discussing belongs to the very foundations of faith. Whoever wishes to undermine in the Christian faith what is written, *through man came death, and through man the resurrection of the dead; for just as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^51], strives to take away everything we believe in Christ. Certainly Christ is the Savior of little children too; certainly, unless they are redeemed by him, they will perish, since without his flesh and blood they cannot have life. This John too held, believed, learned, and taught. But you turn his words toward your own teaching. He said that little children do not have sins, but he meant their own sins. Hence we rightly call them innocent according to the Apostle's statement that *those not yet born had done nothing good or evil*[^52], not according to his statement: *through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners*[^53]. Our own Cyprian too could have said what John said about little children, since he says "that a newborn infant has committed no sin, and that what is forgiven him is not his own sins but another's" [^54]. John, therefore, comparing them with older people, whose own sins are forgiven in baptism, said that they "have no sins," not, as you yourself quoted his words, that they "are not defiled by sin," because you wish this to mean that they are not defiled by the first man's sin. But I would attribute this to the translator, not to you, although in other copies containing the same translation one reads not "sin" but "sins." Thus I wonder whether someone in your number preferred to write the singular, so that it would be understood as that one sin of which the Apostle says: *for the judgment came from one unto condemnation, but grace from many transgressions unto justification*[^55]. For there by "one" he means nothing but a transgression. Since you refuse to believe that little children are defiled by it, you preferred to say not that they "have no sins," as John said, lest their own sins be understood, nor that they are not defiled "by sins," as the same translation reads in other copies, but "are not defiled by sin," so that the first man's single sin should come to mind. But let us not proceed on suspicions, and let this be considered either a copyist's error or a translator's variation. I shall set down the very Greek words spoken by John: Διὰ τοῦτο καὶ τὰ παιδία βαπτίζομεν καίτοι ἁμαρτήματα οὐκ ἔχοντα, which in Latin means: "Therefore we baptize infants too, although they have no sins." You certainly see that he did not say that "little children are not defiled by sin," or "by sins," but that they "have no sins." Understand "their own," and there is no dispute. But you will say: Why did he not add "their own" himself? Why, we think, except that, speaking in the Catholic Church, he did not suppose he would be understood otherwise, was being challenged by no such question, and spoke with greater security when you were not yet stirring up disputes?

**6. 23.** Do you wish to hear what he himself also said most plainly on this subject? See, I add him too to that number of saints. See, I place among my witnesses, or among our judges, the man you thought your advocate. See, you will call him a Manichaean too. Enter, holy John, enter and sit with your brothers, from whom no reasoning and no temptation separated you. Your judgment too is needed, yours especially, since this young man thinks he has found in your writings something with which, he imagines, he can strike down and nullify the judgments of so many great fellow bishops of yours. Had he really found something of that sort, and had it become clear that you held his own opinion, we could never, with your leave, have preferred you alone to so many such men in a matter on which the Christian faith and the Catholic Church have never varied. But far be it that you should have held another opinion while standing so preeminent in her. You too, then, say something that will confound this young man and make him feel shame, because he seeks a stain upon me; and forgive me, because when I have shown him what you think on this subject, he will seek a stain upon you too. For he says it is a Manichaean belief to believe that little children need the aid of Christ the liberator to be freed from the condemnation by which they are bound through the first man's sin. Accordingly, when he learns that you believe this, he will either correct himself of Pelagius's error or charge you too with Manichaeus's crime. But, to confer a true benefit upon him, let us disregard his false reproach.

**6. 24.** Hear now, Julian, what John too says with the other Catholic teachers. Writing to Olympias, he says: "For when Adam committed that great sin and condemned the whole human race together, he paid the penalty in sorrow" [^56]. Again, on the raising of Lazarus: "Christ wept because mortality had sinned to such a degree that, cast down from everlasting life, it had come to love the underworld. Christ wept because the devil made those who could have been immortal become mortal" [^57]. What could be said more explicitly? What do you answer to these things? If Adam by his great sin condemned the whole human race together, can a little child be born except under condemnation? And by whom but Christ is he freed from this condemnation? If in Lazarus too mortality is said to have been cast down from everlasting life and to have come to love the underworld, what mortal does not share in that fault and fall by which the first man fell from the everlasting life he had received on condition that he not sin? If the devil made all who could have been immortal become mortal, why do little children die too, if they are not liable for that first man's sin? Is it not, then, only through him in whom all will be made alive that little children too are rescued from the kingdom of death?

**6. 25.** In a certain sermon John also discusses the question of why beasts injure or kill human beings, when the Lord's decree is clear that he subjected them to the human being, who was to have dominion over them [^58]. He resolves the question by saying that before sin all beasts were subject to the human being, but that their now harming human beings is the punishment of the first sin. The discussion is lengthy, so I do not insert it into this work; nevertheless I ought to quote something from it. "We fear beasts and tremble," he says; "I do not deny it. And we have fallen from our dominion; I too say this. But this does not show God's law to be false, for things were not arranged that way in the beginning: they feared and trembled and were subject to their master. And because we fell from our confidence, we also fell from our honor. How is this clear? He brought the beasts to Adam to see what he would call them, and Adam did not recoil as though afraid." A little later: "This is one sign that beasts were not terrifying to the human being in the beginning. Another, a second, clearer than this, is the conversation with the woman through the serpent. For if beasts had been terrifying to human beings, the woman certainly would not have stayed at the sight of the serpent; she would not have accepted its advice or conversed with it so confidently, but at the mere sight she would immediately have trembled and recoiled. As it is, she converses and does not fear. For that fear did not yet exist; but because sin entered, the things that belonged to honor were taken away." Again a little later: "As long as he had confidence before God, he was terrifying to beasts; but because he offended, he now fears even the lowest of his fellow servants. If this is not so, show me that before sin beasts were terrifying to human beings. But you will have nothing to show. If, however, fear entered after such events, this too belongs to the Lord's protective care. For if, after the human being disturbed and broke the commandment given him, the honor God had bestowed upon him had remained undisturbed, he would not readily have risen again" [^59]. It is certainly clear that by this discussion holy John showed that the sin that entered through one man became common to all, since fear of beasts is common to all. They certainly do not spare even infants, whom, according to this discussion of John's, they would assuredly neither injure nor terrify unless they were bound by liability for that ancient sin.

**6. 26.** Say it, Julian, call this man too—why hesitate to say it now?—a Manichaean, since he has done such injury to nature, whose innocence you defend, and has asserted the propagation of condemnation. Rather, restrain yourself, if your mind retains anything capable of healing, and correct yourself. At last understand how John could say that little children have no sins: not because they are not bound by the first human beings' sin, but because they have committed none of their own. Indeed, you could have found this in that very homily had you read it all. Or if you did read it, I do not know how it could have escaped you; or if it could not escape you, I wonder why it did not correct you, if John's authority carries any weight with you. Or if, having read that sermon and examined and understood the passage I shall quote, you thought you should remain in your opinion, why did you insert any words from it into your work? Was it perhaps to prompt us to read the whole thing and discover the means to detect and refute your ambush? For what could be clearer than what he said there: "Christ came once and found us bound by the paternal bonds Adam had drawn up. He laid down the beginning of the obligation; through our sins the interest increased" [^60]? Do you hear a man both learned and teaching in the Catholic faith distinguish the debt of the paternal bond, which clung to us by inheritance, from those debts whose interest increased through our own sins? Do you hear what is remitted in baptism to little children, who have not yet contracted their own debts but nevertheless could not be exempt from the paternal bond? For his untranslated words read as follows in Greek: Ἔρχεται ἅπαξ ὁ Χριστός, εὗρεν ἡμῶν χειρόγραφον πατρῷον, ὅ τι ἔγραψεν ὁ Ἀδάμ. Ἐκεῖνος τὴν ἀρχὴν εἰσήγαγεν τὸ χρέος, ἡμεῖς τὸν δανεισμὸν ηὐξήσαμεν ταῖς μεταγενεστέραις ἁμαρτίαις. Translated word for word, they are: "Christ came once; he found our paternal bond, which Adam wrote. He introduced the beginning of the debt; we increased the interest by subsequent sins." Was he content to say "paternal bond" without adding "our"? This was so that we should know that, before we increased the interest by our own subsequent sins, the debt of that paternal bond already belonged to us.

**6. 27.** Read also how this same holy man explains that very passage of the Apostle where it is written: *through one man sin entered the world*[^61]. There he abides in the truth of this Catholic faith more clearly than daylight. Since it would be too long to weave the whole passage into this work, I shall touch on a few points. "It is clear," he says, "that it was not the sin arising from transgression of the law, but the sin arising from Adam's disobedience, that contaminated everything" [^62]. A little later: "*Death reigned*, he says, *from Adam to Moses, even over those who did not sin*[^63]. How did it reign? *In the likeness of Adam's transgression, who is a figure of the one to come*[^64]. For this reason Adam is also a figure of Christ. How is he a figure, they ask? Because, just as he became for those born from him, although they had not eaten from the tree, the cause of the death introduced through that food, so Christ became for those who belong to him, even if they had done nothing righteous, the provider of the righteousness he bestowed upon us all through the Cross" [^65]. Elsewhere in the same sermon: "So that when the Jew says to you, 'How was the world saved by the power of Christ alone?' you may say to him: 'In the same way that by the disobedience of Adam alone the world was condemned.' Although grace and sin are not equal, nor are death and life equal, nor God and the devil equal" [^66]. Again a little later: "*But the gift is not like the transgression*, he says. *For if through the transgression of one many died, much more did God's grace and the gift in the grace of the one man Jesus Christ abound for many*[^67]. What he says means this: If sin prevailed, and the sin of one man, how will grace, and God's grace, and not only the Father's but also the Son's, not prevail still more? For this is far more reasonable. That one person should be condemned on account of another does not appear very reasonable, but that one should be saved on account of another appears both more fitting and more reasonable. If, therefore, the former happened, much more the latter" [^68]. Again, further on: "*The judgment*, he says, *came from one unto condemnation, but grace from many transgressions unto justification*[^69]. This means that sin was able to bring in death and condemnation, but grace destroyed not only that one sin but also the sins that entered afterward" [^70]. A little later on the same point: "That more good things were therefore introduced, and not only that sin but also all the rest were destroyed, he shows by saying: *but grace from many transgressions unto justification*" [^71]. And a little later he says: "First he said that if the sin of one man killed all, much more can the grace of one man save them. After this he showed that not only that sin but all the rest were destroyed through grace; and not only were sins destroyed, but righteousness was also bestowed. Christ did not merely benefit us as much as Adam harmed us, but far more and more abundantly" [^72]. Afterward in the same sermon, discussing baptism, he inserted the Apostle's words: "*Or do you not know*, he says, *brothers, that all of us who were baptized in Christ were baptized into his death? We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death*[^73]. What does *we were baptized into his death* mean? That we too should die as he did. For baptism is a cross. What the Cross and the tomb were for Christ, baptism has become for us, although not in exactly the same respects. For he died and was buried in the flesh; we in both respects to sin. Therefore he did not say, *planted together* with death, but *with the likeness of death*[^74]. For both this and that are *death*, but they do not have the same subject: his is the death of Christ's flesh, ours of sin. Just as that death is real, so is this" [^75].

**6. 28.** Can you now doubt that holy John is as far from your opinion as he is faithful to the Catholic opinion? In his discussion explaining the Apostle's passage essential to the question between us, where it is written, *through one man sin entered the world*[^76], and the connected statements that follow, is there anywhere a trace of what you say, that this was said on account of imitation, not carnal birth? Does he not say that everything was contaminated by that one sin, and distinguish it from the other sins afterward committed and introduced, which you say concern imitation, not propagation, so that he says that not only those, but both this one and those, are destroyed through Christ's grace? Does he not explain the Apostle's words on baptism, *all of us who were baptized in Christ were baptized into his death*[^77], by saying that one baptized in Christ dies to sin just as Christ died in the flesh, because being baptized into Christ's death is nothing other than dying to sin? To what sin, then, does a little child die if he has not contracted original sin? Or are little children perhaps not baptized into Christ's death? Yet the Apostle did not say, "Some of us," but, *all of us who were baptized in Christ were baptized into his death.* Or will you say that when they receive Christian baptism they are not baptized in Christ, lest Bishop John strangle you with his definition? He says that for those baptized in Christ baptism is what the Cross and the tomb were for Christ, so that we understand that just as he died in the flesh, so they died to sin. See to what man, to what great defender of the Christian faith and of this Catholic teaching, you wished falsely to attribute your own teaching, as though he had said that "little children are not defiled by the first man's sin," when he said that "they have no sins." His many and very clear testimonies show that he meant this to be understood only of their own sins.

**7. 29.** What help, then, did you gain by quoting John of Constantinople as though he supported you? Was it to seize with supposedly sharp cunning upon one word as though he had omitted it, and to stir up against yourself such an immense mound of his words to bury you? So improvident and incautious were you that even in the very homily in which you could scarcely find a single testimony to misunderstand, deceiving others as you were deceived, you failed to notice that holy John of Constantinople expressed clearly enough that all human beings, apart from their own debts, are debtors under the paternal bond. Yet after inserting the words through which you thought your purpose gained some help, you go on to say: "Since, therefore, it is plainly clear that this is the sound and true opinion, fortified first by reason and then by the authority of Scripture, and always honored by the learning of holy men—who, however, did not confer authority upon truth by their agreement, but received testimony and glory from fellowship with it—let no prudent person be disturbed by the conspiracy of the lost." What do these words of yours accomplish except to show either how you neglected to learn the opinions and discourses of Catholic teachers on this subject or, if you did take care to know them, with what fraud you try to entrap the ignorant? For, leaving aside for now reason and the authority of the holy Scriptures, has the learning of holy men always honored this opinion by which you deny that little children are born liable for the first man's sin? This is not shown by the testimonies I have quoted from so many great and learned saints. On the contrary, I think you now see how much this opinion has deceived you, unless you are acting deceitfully and knew this already. But to believe better of you: if you are learning these things for the first time now, if you now first see that, concerning the first man's sin, by which carnally begotten infants are bound and from which they are released only by spiritual regeneration, so many saints learned and taught what we too have learned and teach, change this mind of yours at last. Forget this error and almost madness by which you hurl the reproach of Manichaeism at so many such fathers. If you did this unknowingly, why do you not reject your wretched ignorance? If knowingly, why do you not lay aside your sacrilegious audacity?

**7. 30.** You are refuted on every side: so many testimonies of the saints are clearer than daylight. Look into whose gathering I have brought you. Here is Ambrose of Milan, whom your teacher Pelagius praised so highly that he said: "In his books the Roman faith shines especially brightly; he shone forth as a beautiful flower among Latin writers, so that not even an enemy would dare to criticize his faith and his most pure understanding of Scripture." Here too is John of Constantinople, whom you ranked as most excellent among learned and holy men in this very argument of yours to which I am replying. Here is Basil, whose words, irrelevant to the matter now at issue, you thought afforded you some help. Here too are the others, whose great agreement ought to move you. This is not, as you write with an abusive pen, a "conspiracy of the lost." In the Catholic Church they shone through their devotion to sound teaching; equipped and girded with spiritual arms, they waged vigorous wars against heretics; having faithfully fulfilled the labors of their stewardship, they fell asleep in the bosom of peace. You say that "one man has come forward," meaning me, "who wishes it to be understood that the whole weight of the battle rests upon himself." See, it is not I alone, but so many great holy and learned teachers who answer you for me or with me, and for the salvation of all of us and of you yourself, if you are wise.

**7. 31.** We do not, as you slanderously claim, "oppose you merely with the people's murmuring," although the people themselves murmur against you precisely because this is not a question beyond even popular understanding. Rich and poor, high and low, learned and unlearned, men and women know what is forgiven at each age in baptism. Hence every day throughout the world mothers run with their little children not merely to Christ, that is, to the Anointed, but to Christ Jesus, that is, also to the Savior. But look: this assembly of saints into which I have brought you is no popular multitude. They are not only children but also fathers of the Church. They belong to the number of those of whom it was foretold: *in place of your fathers, sons are born to you; you will establish them as princes over all the earth*[^78]. They were born as her children to learn these things; they became her fathers to teach them.

**7. 32.** Why do you boast that you rejoice because this truth, which you think an error or wish to have thought one, "cannot find a defender out of so great a multitude"? As though it were a slight defense that the multitude itself, spread throughout the world, does not disagree with itself on this most firm and ancient foundation of faith. But if you demand defenders whose writings can be found and whose learning is renowned, behold the memorable and venerable assembly and agreement of its defenders. Holy Irenaeus says that the serpent's ancient wound is healed through faith in Christ and the Cross, and that we were held as though in chains by the first-formed man's sin. Holy Cyprian says that a little child perishes unless baptized, although it is not his own sins but another's that are forgiven him. Holy Reticius says that the sins of the old man, whom we put off through the washing of regeneration, are not only old but inborn. Holy Olympius says that corruption was spread from the first-formed man into the offspring, so that sin was born together with the human being. Holy Hilary says that all flesh comes from sin except that of him who came without sin in the likeness of sinful flesh. He says that the man whose voice says, *I was conceived in iniquities*[^79], was born under the origin of sin and under the law of sin. Holy Ambrose says that little children who have been baptized are restored from evil to the original state of their nature. He says that among those born of woman the holy Lord Jesus alone, through the novelty of an unstained birth, experienced no contagion of earthly corruption. He says that we all die in Adam, because through one man sin entered the world, and his fault is the death of all. He says that in his wound the entire human race would have perished, had not that Samaritan come down and healed his grievous wounds. He says that Adam existed and in him all existed, that Adam perished and in him all perished. He says that we are stained by contagion before we are born and that human conception is not free from iniquity, for, he says, we are conceived in our parents' sin and born in their transgressions; but birth itself too has its contagions, and nature itself has more than a single contagion. He says that the devil is a usurer, from whom Eve was stained and then lent at interest to the whole human race through the interest payments of an indebted succession. He says that Eve was deceived by the devil so that he might overthrow the man and bind his inheritance with debt. He says that Adam was so corrupted by the serpent's bite that we all limp from that wound. He says that no one born through the bodily union of man and woman is free from transgression, but that he who is free from transgression, namely the Lord Christ, is also free from this manner of conception. Holy Innocent tells you that through the washing of regeneration every past fault is cleansed that befell us through the man who fell by free choice and sank into the depths. He says that little children cannot have life unless they eat the Lord's flesh and drink his blood. Holy Gregory says that it would have been better not to fall away from the tree of life through the bitterest taste of sin, but that we must be corrected after the fall. He says that we slid down from good things to sorrowful things and wishes us to be restored from sorrowful things to better ones, so that those whom the taste of the forbidden tree condemned may be justified by Christ's Cross with more abundant grace. He says that we must revere the birth through which we are freed from the bonds of earthly birth. He says that rebirth from water and the Spirit cleanses the stains of the first birth, through which we are conceived in iniquities. Holy Basil tells you that we contracted the sickness of sin because Eve refused to fast from the forbidden tree. Finally, he says that we fell from paradise because we did not fast, and commands us to fast so that we may return there. So many holy bishops speak to you with one mouth—Eulogius, John, Ammonianus, Porphyry, Eutonius, another Porphyry, Fidus, Zoninus, Zoboennus, Nymphidius, Chromatius, Jovinus, Eleutherius, and Clematius: We acquitted Pelagius only because he condemned those who say that little children have eternal life even if they are not baptized. Now answer whether the just God can deprive his own image of eternal life when it is liable for no sin.

**7. 33.** Finally, holy Bishop John speaks, whom you mentioned with honor, whom you praised as holy and learned, whom you said had received testimony and glory from fellowship with the truth. He too says that Adam committed that great sin in such a way that he condemned the entire human race together. He says that Christ wept at Lazarus's death because mortality, cast down from everlasting life, came to love the underworld, and because the devil made those who could have been immortal become mortal. He says that before the human being's sin beasts were entirely subject to him, but after that sin entered, we began to fear beasts. So clearly did he wish that first man's sin to be understood as concerning all human beings! Who does not see the consequence, that no beast would injure little children unless carnal birth bound them too by that sin's bond? In the same sermon from which you wished to creep up on the unwary, he says that Christ found us bound both by our paternal bond written by Adam and by subsequent debts of our own. He explains the Apostle's passage on which this whole case rests, where it is written: *through one man sin entered the world*[^80], and the statements connected with this sentence. Nor in his lengthy discussion does he say what you say, that this sin passed into all human beings not by the propagation of the race but by imitation. On the contrary, without disagreeing with his fellow bishops' judgments, he demonstrates how differently the matter stands. For he says that everything was contaminated by the first man's sin; and lest this be thought to have happened through moral imitation rather than carnal generation, he says that Adam was called a figure of the one to come for this reason: just as he became for those born from him, although they had not eaten from the tree, the cause of the death introduced through that food, so Christ became for those who belong to him, even if they had done nothing righteous, the provider of the righteousness he bestowed upon all through the Cross. He says that the Jew who denies that the world can be saved by the power of Christ alone must be refuted from the first man's sin, through whose single disobedience the world was condemned. He says that it does not seem very reasonable for one to be condemned on account of another, yet this happened through Adam; from this he urges us all the more to believe what appears more fitting and reasonable, that one be saved on account of another, which is fulfilled in Christ. But who does not see that, if the first man's sin passed through all human beings not by propagation but by imitation, no one is condemned on account of another, but each for his own sin, which another did not transmit to him by generation but he himself committed by imitation through his own will? He says that through grace not only that single sin of the first man but also the sins entering afterward were destroyed. There he certainly distinguishes sufficiently the later sins, which may be called sins committed by imitation, from that one sin that passed through propagation. He shows that both were destroyed through grace, so that, according to the Apostle's intention, regeneration may be seen to have benefited us more than generation had harmed us. For thus he explains what was said: *the gift is not like the transgression; for the judgment came from one unto condemnation, but grace from many transgressions unto justification*[^81]. By this sentence, written by the Apostle Paul and explained by Bishop John, that imitation of yours, a novel contrivance of Pelagian error, is overthrown. He also says of baptism itself, explaining the Apostle's words, *all of us who were baptized in Christ were baptized into his death*[^82], that being baptized into Christ's death is nothing other than dying to sin as he died in the flesh. Hence little children must either not be baptized in Christ or, if they are baptized, be baptized into his death, and thus themselves also die to sin; and because they have none of their own, they are cleansed from the contamination of that other's sin, that is, original sin, which became common to all.

**7. 34.** Surrounded by so many massive bulwarks of holy and learned men, will you still think that our cause could find no defender out of so great a multitude? Or will you call so great an agreement of Catholic priests a conspiracy of the lost? Nor should you think holy Jerome beneath consideration because he was a presbyter. Learned in Greek and Latin and also Hebrew, he passed from the Western to the Eastern Church and lived among the holy places and in the holy Scriptures to extreme old age. He read all or nearly all who before him, in either part of the world, had written anything on ecclesiastical teaching, and he held and expressed no different opinion on this subject. In explaining the prophet Jonah he said most plainly that "even little children were held liable for the sin of the offending Adam" [^83]. Will you then so love your error, into which you fell incautiously through youthful confidence and human frailty, that on so great a question, in which the heart of the Christian religion consists, you dare not only to disagree with these priests of Catholic unity and truth, who from different parts of the world agree with one another in such concord of faith, but even to call them Manichaeans? If you do not do this, neither do you justly do it to me, whom you see to have followed their footsteps in this same cause in my arguments, by which you are fiercely provoked against me. But if you sprinkle me alone with such reproach for no other reason than that, concerning the first man's sin, I think what they think, hold what they hold, and preach what they preach, who does not see that while you hurl an open insult against me, you hold a hidden but similar judgment about them? Yet, to pass over such things, when you consider either Bishop John's words about our paternal bond written by Adam, which I believe you found in the sermon from which you quoted what you wanted, or Bishop Ambrose's statement that no one born of the union of male and female can be free from sin, which you read in my book but feared to touch in yours, then, even if your forehead is hardened before men, your mind blushes before God.

**7. 35.** For my part, because of the love I bear you—which, with God's favor, may you never uproot from the depths of my heart by any abuse—I would prefer, son Julian, that with better and stronger youthfulness you conquer yourself and overcome with more powerful piety that contentiousness—what else is it but human?—by which you desire your opinion, whatever it may be, to prevail because it has now become yours. Just as Polemo gradually removed the garlands of luxury from his head and threw them aside, tucked his hand under his cloak, composed his mouth and face in modesty, and at last gave himself wholly as a disciple to the man he had come to mock, so you too, as so many venerable men speak to you—especially Bishop Ambrose, praised for the integrity of his Catholic faith even by the mouth of your evil teacher and deceiver, and Bishops Basil and John, whom you too have placed among holy and learned men with truthful testimony—should cast off, like drunkards' garlands, the praises of the Pelagians, which exalt you as their great defender. Your pen, abusive to put it mildly, you should not hide as if beneath a cloak of shame, but break as if with a corrected hand. You should give your heart, to be filled with truth, not to the Platonist Xenocrates, but to these Christian priests, or rather, in them, to the Lord Christ himself—not give it as though you had come for the first time, but give it back as one who had withdrawn. But if my advice displeases you, do as you please. For if you are corrected, which is my greatest wish, I shall rejoice more abundantly and richly; and if, what I abhor, you persist in this perversity, I shall receive fruitful profit from your reproaches: for myself an increase of heavenly reward, and for you the sting of compassionate sorrow.

**8. 36.** But since I have now shown how many men, what distinguished and great defenders and teachers of the Catholic faith, you falsely make into Manichaeans, hear for a little while how greatly, through ignorant rashness, you help the real Manichaeans. For I promised to demonstrate this in the second part of my argument. As you have shown that you know well enough, the Manichaeans, with the sacrilegious vanity of their unspeakable error, introduce two natures, one of good, the other of evil, from different, mutually opposed, and coeternal principles. Against them the Catholic faith proclaims that only God's nature is without beginning, namely the supreme and unchangeable good, that is, the ineffable Trinity. It declares that from this supreme and unchangeable good, as stated, the whole creation was made, and that all natures are good, although unequal to the Creator, because created from nothing and therefore changeable. Thus there is absolutely no nature that is not either God himself or made by him, so that every nature, however great or of whatever kind, is good insofar as it is a nature.

**8. 37.** They ask us, then, where evil comes from. We answer: From good, but not from the supreme and unchangeable good. Evils therefore arose from lesser and changeable goods. Although we understand these evils to be not natures but faults of natures, we also understand that they cannot exist except from and in certain natures, and that nothing is evil except a falling away from goodness. But whose falling away, unless undoubtedly that of some nature? For even an evil will is certainly the will of some nature: both angel and human being are natures. A will, when it is a will, cannot belong to no one. And these wills have such power that they determine the qualities of the natures to which they belong. For if someone asks what an angel or human being of evil will is like, the most correct answer is: Evil. He receives the name of his quality from his evil will rather than from his good nature. For nature is the substance itself, capable of goodness and evil: capable of goodness through participation in the good by which it was made, but receiving evil not through participation in evil but through privation of good. That is, it does not receive evil by mingling with a nature that is some evil—for no nature, insofar as it is a nature, is evil—but by falling away from the nature that is the supreme and unchangeable good, precisely because it was made not from that nature but from nothing. Otherwise it could not even have an evil will unless it were changeable. But its nature would not be changeable if it were from God and not made by him from nothing. Therefore God is the author of good things, since he is the author of natures. Their voluntary falling away from good indicates not by whom they were made but from what they were made. And this is not something, since it is absolutely nothing; and what is nothing therefore cannot have an author.

**8. 38.** Therefore, just as the Manichaean opposes the Catholic faith, that is, the faith of truth and true piety, by saying that good and evil are contrary to each other in such a way that he does not say a nature becomes evil when it falls away from good, and that this very falling away is its evil, but instead says that evil itself is a nature and, more insanely, eternal without beginning, calling it body and spirit—body from which spirit operates, and spirit that operates from body—so it cannot be expressed how much this enemy of the faith is helped by anyone who denies that evils arise from goods and interprets in this sense the Lord's words: *a good tree does not bear evil fruit*[^84]. Yet the divine Teacher certainly does not identify the tree from which the fruit he was discussing comes as nature, but as the will, whether good or evil; the fruits are deeds, which cannot be good when the will is evil or evil when the will is good. For this is what he says: *an evil tree does not bear good fruit, and a good tree does not bear evil fruit*[^85], as though saying: Neither does an evil will produce good deeds nor a good will evil deeds. For if we ask about the origins of the trees themselves, that is, of the wills themselves, what presents itself except the natures God created good? Accordingly, evils arose from goods: not evil deeds from good wills, but evil wills from good natures. What, then, does the Manichaean wish so much to hear as that evils cannot come from goods? Since the existence of evils cannot be denied, nothing would remain but that they came from evil if they could not come from good natures. Thus evils would have as their beginningless origin a nature of evil that had always existed without beginning, and there would be two natures, one of good, the other of evil. For necessarily either evils do not exist, or they come from good natures or from evil ones. If we say that evils do not exist, we say to God in vain: *deliver us from evil*[^86]. But if we say that evils arise only from evils, the Manichaean pestilence will triumph, devastate everything, and violate God's own nature, as though it were changeable, by mingling it with an evil nature. It therefore remains for us to confess that evils come from goods, because if we deny this, they will come from evils, and we shall certainly be supporting the Manichaeans.

**8. 39.** Thus, when you say, "Since according to the Gospel's judgment a tree should be recognized by its fruits, should we think we ought to listen to someone who calls marriage good, when he determines that nothing but evil comes from it?" you certainly wish marriage to be reckoned a good tree, from which you refuse to allow a human being bound by the contagion of original sin to be born as an evil fruit. You fail to see that you must make adultery an evil tree if marriage is a good tree. Accordingly, if one born from marriage is marriage's fruit and must therefore be without fault, lest evil fruit be born from a good tree, it follows that one born from adultery must not be born without fault, lest good fruit be born from an evil tree, since the Lord determines by divine authority that neither does evil fruit come from a good tree nor good fruit from an evil one. To escape this, then, since you say that a human being cannot be born with a fault even from adultery, you will deny that adultery is an evil tree, lest one who, according to you, is born without fault from adultery appear, against the Lord's determination, to be good fruit born from an evil tree. Deny, then, that marriage too is a good tree, and confess that you erred in saying so. But you will say that a human being begotten through adulterous intercourse is not born from adultery. From what, then? "From human nature," you say, "which even in adulterers is God's work, not theirs." Why, then, do you not understand in the same way that a human being begotten through marital intercourse is not born from marriage either, but likewise from human nature, which in married people too is God's work, not theirs? Thus the evil that newborns contract from nature's fault is not attributed to the goodness of marriage, just as the good that newborns derive from nature's creation is not attributed to the iniquity of adultery. But you understand the good tree not as Christ wished it understood, namely the human being's good will, but as God's own work, namely human marriage or nature. Because these works of God are good, you say evil cannot arise from them, since a good tree cannot bear evil fruit. Thus the Manichaean completes his argument against you on behalf of his teaching. By these words of yours you help him so much that he wishes to hear nothing more than that evils cannot come from goods. Having accepted this, he infers and says to you: If evil cannot come from good, from where will it come except from evil? For evils could not suddenly arise by themselves, with no author. But you say that evil cannot come from good, lest, against the Gospel's judgment, *a good tree bear evil fruit.* It remains, he says, that a nature of evil must have existed eternally, capable of generating evils, since you admit they cannot arise from goods.

**8. 40.** Are you now willing to change the opinion you put forward to assist the Manichaean pestilence, not from favoring the Manichaeans but from not knowing what you were saying? For how could Christ say, *either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree evil and its fruit evil*[^87]—since he was saying this to human beings whom he himself had created—if evil could not arise from his good work, which is the human being, as you think? You identify the good tree with God's good work, namely human marriage or nature, and therefore say that evil cannot be born from it, because if we say evil can be born from good we shall oppose, as you think, him who said: *a good tree does not bear evil fruit*[^88]. Yet you know that from the good natures of angel and human being, which no evil parents had begotten but which the supremely good God had made without parents, arose not evil fruits but the evil trees themselves from which evil fruits would be born. The Lord Jesus, however, defeats the Manichaeans, because one human being, that is, one nature, can make either tree, and he defeats you, because an evil tree can arise from a good nature. This shows the falsehood of what you say in support of the Manichaeans, that "evils cannot come from goods," so that evils are thought capable of coming only from the nature of evil introduced by their impious error.

**8. 41.** For not only in the one passage where you thought you should mention the good tree from the Gospel, but in other passages of your argument as well, you have supported the Manichaeans with this perversity. Such is the passage where you said again: "Sin cannot be contracted through nature, because the devil's work is not allowed to pass through God's work." To this I answer you: How is the devil's work allowed to remain in God's work if it is not allowed to pass through God's work? For who doubts that it is worse for it to remain there than to pass from there? Perhaps you ask how the devil's work remains in God's work. Do not look elsewhere; recall the devil himself. He is God's work, certainly an angelic nature; but envy is the devil's own work, which both proceeded from him and remains in him. It is therefore empty to say that "the devil's work does not pass through God's work," where you plainly see it also remaining. Have you still not noticed how many thanks the Manichaean should offer you? Are you still not awake? For the Manichaean labors to show that evil cannot arise from God's good work, so that people may believe what he wants, that evil can come only from evil. Here you are his marvelous helper: "Evil," you say, "is not allowed to pass through God's work," so that he may conclude with complete ease that much less can it arise from that through which it is not even permitted to pass.

**9. 42.** Hear another similar statement, indeed a more serious one, in which you again support the Manichaeans. "Original sin has vanished," you say, "because the root of evil cannot be planted in what you call God's gift." See with what manifest truth I refute this. Is not the human mind God's gift? Yet that enemy, the sower, planted the root of evil there when through the serpent's deceit he persuaded the human being to sin [^89]. For unless the human mind had then received the root of evil, it would in no way have given assent to the evil persuader. What shall I say of avarice, the root of all evils? Where is it except in the human soul? And what is the human soul except God's gift? How, then, can you say, unless by not considering what you are saying, "The root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift"? But attend to what the Manichaean, whom you greatly support through these thoughtless statements, may say to you. If being a rational creature is God's gift, and you say that the root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift, how much more plausibly is it said that the root of evil cannot arise from God's gift? Thus, with your support, the Manichaean introduces a root of evil from that nature of evil he invents as not created by God but coeternal with God. For if you say that the root of evil arose from the free choice of a good nature created by God, as Catholic truth says, he easily defeats you with your own words, where you say: "The root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift," because free choice too is undoubtedly God's gift. Accordingly, when you said that "the root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift," you yourself gave the Manichaean something to say against you. For if evil cannot be planted in good, as you say to me, much less can evil arise from good, as he says to you. Thus he will conclude that evil comes only from evil; in this he will seem victorious to himself and certainly will be, unless both he and you are opposed. The truth of the Catholic faith therefore defeats the Manichaean in these statements of yours precisely because it defeats you too. For if it did not defeat you when you say that "the root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift," much less could it defeat the Manichaean when he says that the root of evil cannot arise from God's gift. But to defeat both, it says that the root of evil can neither arise from anywhere nor exist anywhere except from and in a rational nature. For that nature, being rational is nothing but God's gift. Yet because it was made from nothing by the supreme and unchangeable good, to be a good, though changeable, its falling away from the good by which it was made is the root of evil arising from it or in it, because evil is nothing other than the privation of good.

**9. 43.** Further, when you say, "The reason of things does not permit anything evil to be brought forth from good or anything unjust from what is just," you are speaking the Manichaeans' very words. For they assert that evil can be brought forth only from evil, and their entire impious sect rests on this, because they first contend that evil cannot be brought forth from good. If, following your authority, we grant them this, absolutely nothing will remain by which we can refute their impious teachings. "The unjust from the just" is the same as "evil from good." Therefore, to oppose both you and them, the Catholic faith says that evil has been brought forth only from good, and the unjust only from the just, because the angel and the human being from whom these arose were certainly good and just beforehand. Thus we do not oppose the Manichaeans unless we also obtain your admission that evils arose only from goods, and that those evils are not substances but faults of created substances, through which they fall away from good because they are changeable, having been made from nothing. This is the soundness of Catholic teaching by which the poison of the Manichaean pestilence is expelled.

**9. 44.** Hence Ambrose, my teacher, praised even by the mouth of your evil teacher, says in the book he wrote *On Isaac and the Soul*: "What, then, is evil but a lack of good?" And again: "Evils therefore arose from goods. For nothing is evil except what is deprived of goods; yet through evils it came about that goods stood out. The lack of good, therefore, is the root of evil" [^90]. Do you see with what true reasoning of the Catholic faith blessed Ambrose refutes the Manichaeans? Do you see how, although he did not name them in that discussion, he nevertheless refuted them by such true and concise statements? See what man of God you assail with the reproach of the Manichaean name, impelled by abusive rage, on account of original sin, which he defended as a Catholic in a Catholic manner! Against the support you give them, he offers Catholic disputants such unconquerable assistance in defeating them. For he cries against the Manichaeans: "Evils arose from goods." Against him, on behalf of the Manichaeans, you cry back: "That from which and through which evil fruit appeared must necessarily be evil"; and, "The devil's work is not allowed to pass through God's work"; and, "The root of evil cannot be planted in God's gift"; and, "The reason of things does not permit anything evil to be brought forth from good, or anything unjust from what is just." You cry these things on behalf of the Manichaeans against the voice of Catholic truth spoken through God's priest, so that, if you are heard, the Manichaeans win, saying—to pass over other things—"If the reason of things does not permit anything evil to be brought forth from good," then evils arose not from goods, as Ambrose says, but from the nature of evil, as we say. See into what an abyss you have fallen by misunderstanding the Lord's words: *a good tree does not bear evil fruit*[^91]. This was said not of nature or marriage, which God established, but of the human being's good will, from which evil works are not done.

**9. 45.** But perhaps you or someone else may say: How is it that evil fruits do not come from the tree the human being makes, that is, the good will, while from the nature God makes is born the evil tree itself, which bears evil fruits? It is as though the human being made something better, from which evil fruit cannot arise, than what God makes, from which an evil tree can arise. Lest anyone err in this way, let him listen carefully to Ambrose saying: "What is evil but a lack of good? For nothing is evil except what is deprived of goods, because the lack of good is the root of evil." Let him understand from this that the evil tree is an evil will precisely because it is a falling away from the supreme good, in which the created good is deprived of the creating good, so that the root of evil in it is nothing other than a lack of good. But the good tree is a good will precisely because through it the human being turns toward the supreme and unchangeable good and is filled with good so as to bear good fruit. Accordingly, God is the author of all goods, that is, both of good nature and of good will, which the human being does not produce unless God works it in him, because *the will is prepared by the Lord*[^92].

**9. 46.** But I now see that the order of my arrangement requires me, by God's gift, to do what I promised to do in the third place: to shatter your glassy subtleties and fragile arguments, in which you seem to yourself so very sharp and brilliant, by whatever statements of Catholic bishops before us I can find appropriate to the matters at issue. We shall undertake this with another beginning, bringing the length of this book to an end here.
