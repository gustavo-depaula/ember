# Book 6

**1. 1.** Your third book has now been answered; let your fourth also receive an answer. The Lord will be present, so that we may show you not only truth but also love. Whoever holds fast to both will be neither foolish nor envious. You said much about these faults at the beginning of the book just mentioned. For error must be driven out by truth, and envy by love. In this discussion of yours, however, when you spoke about folly, calling it "the mother of all faults," you brought forward testimony from Scripture, where we read: *God loves no one except the one who dwells with wisdom*[^1]. Inquire carefully, then, whether the vanity of childhood can dwell with wisdom—that vanity through which a little one must pass on leaving infancy, if he does pass through it. Consider what first fruit springs from the root you praise, and how great a change in the opposite direction he needs in order to be loved by God, who loves no one except the one who dwells with wisdom. In predestined little ones, of course, he consumes what he hates, so that, once freed from vanity, they too may dwell with wisdom and be loved by him. If their last day takes them away, snatched from the breast[^2], I wonder whether you dare say that they will dwell with wisdom outside the kingdom of God. According to you, "the good of an uninjured and blameless nature" does not allow them to enter it, unless the grace of the true Savior redeems them and sets them free from the folly of their false praiser. I pass over those who are foolish by nature, who, as Scripture testifies, are to be mourned far more than the dead[^3]. God's grace, through the blood of the Mediator, can indeed rescue them even from this great evil. But how could they have fallen into so great an evil if, by God's judgment, no punishment were owed to a corrupted origin?

**1. 2.** See, you rightly and severely rebuked those "who have either neglected to learn what they ought to know or do not fear to condemn what they do not understand." Can you say this about those born without understanding? Yet you will not discover how this could have happened to them under a just God, unless children inherit some desert from their parents. "But in our envy of you we go mad in a kind of noonday of discovered truth, without any shadow of ignorance," as you put it. Can it really be that you, who do not envy, do not see such great evils in little ones? God is good; God is just. There is certainly no alien nature of evil which, according to the Manichees, we should believe to be mixed with our nature. Where, then, do such great human evils come from—I do not say in conduct, but in the very capacities with which people are born—if human origin is not corrupted, if there is no condemned mass? And what of this: you, a man free of folly and untouched by the stings of envy, describe envy itself in such a way that in your description this fault appears as both a sin and a punishment for sin? Is envy not a sin of the devil? Is it not a punishment for sin when it "immediately torments the very person in whom it arises"? These are your words. Yet with prolonged verbosity you imagined you had argued most acutely that "one and the same fault cannot be both a sin and a punishment for sin." But perhaps, because you are not envious, you scarcely managed at last to see envy in another book, so as to say these things about it and, since you do not envy me, contradict yourself on my behalf.

**2. 3.** After finishing your preface, in which you also took pains, as you usually do, to prove what I proclaim, "that God is the creator of human beings," you next set out my words, where I said: "A human being who is born of the concupiscence of the flesh is born to the world, not to God; but he is born to God when he is reborn of water and the Spirit."[^4] You lay a trap for these words of mine by asserting that I must therefore be understood to have said that whatever belongs to the world belongs to the devil, because elsewhere, of course, I had said that "those born of the union of bodies belong to the devil's jurisdiction," affirming that "they are rescued from the power of darkness when they are regenerated in Christ." I therefore answer your false charge. You want it to be thought that, in calling the world subject to the devil's power, I meant that the devil either made or possesses heaven and earth and everything in them. I do not say this: I detest, refute, and condemn anyone who says it. In this passage, however, I used the word "world" as the Lord did when he said: *Behold, the prince of the world is coming*[^5]. For he did not mean that the devil is the prince of heaven and earth and everything made through the Word, that is, through Christ himself, of whom it is written: *The world was made through him*[^6]. Rather, the word is used as in the saying: *The world lies in the evil one*[^7]; and again: *Everything in the world is the concupiscence of the flesh, the concupiscence of the eyes, and worldly ambition, which is not from the Father but is from the world*[^8]. For it is not the case that heaven and earth are not from the Father through the Son, or that angels, stars, trees, animals, and human beings are not from the Father through the Son, at least as regards the very substance by which human beings are human. Rather, the devil is the prince of the world, and the world lies in the evil one: that is, all human beings who are liable to eternal condemnation unless they are set free from it, so that they no longer belong to the prince of sins, having been redeemed by the blood shed for the forgiveness of sins. It is to this world, then, whose prince is the one of whom he who conquered the world[^9] says, *Behold, the prince of the world is coming, and he will find nothing in me*[^10]—it is to this world, I say, that a human being is born, until he is reborn in the one who conquered the world and in whom the prince of the world found nothing.

**2. 4.** For what is the world of which the Savior of the world and conqueror of the world says: *The world cannot hate you; but it hates me, because I bear witness about it that its works are evil*[^11]? Are the works of earth and sea, heaven and stars, evil? Surely this world consists of human beings. Nor is anyone chosen to be set free from this world except by the grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, who gave his flesh for the life of the world. He would not have done this unless he had found the world in death. What is the world of which he said to the Jews: *You are of this world; I am not of this world*[^12]? Finally, what is the world out of which Jesus chose his disciples, so that they would no longer be of the world, and the very world of which they no longer were would hate them? For this is how the Savior of the world, the light of the world, speaks; this, I say, is how he speaks: *These things I command you, that you love one another. If the world hates you, know that it hated me before you. If you were of the world, the world would love what was its own; but because you are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world, therefore the world hates you*[^13]. If he had not added, *I have chosen you out of the world*, one might think that he had said, *You are not of the world*, in the same way that he had said of himself, *I am not of this world.* For he himself was not of the world and then chosen out of it so that he would not be of the world. What Christian would say that? Even insofar as he deigned to be a human being, the Son of God was not of the world. Why, unless because there was never any sin in him? It is because of sin that every human being is first born to the world, not to God; and to be born to God, the one who is reborn is chosen out of the world, so that he may no longer be of the world. For this reason the prince of the world is cast out from him, as he himself testifies, saying: *Now is the judgment of the world; now the prince of this world will be cast out*[^14].

**2. 5.** Unless perhaps your audacity will go so far that you say little ones are not chosen out of the world when they are washed in the baptism of him of whom it was said: *God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself.* If you deny that little ones belong to this reconciliation, denying that they are of the world, I do not know how you have the face to live in the world. But if you acknowledge that, when they pass into the body of Christ, they are chosen out of the world, they must be born to that out of which they are chosen to be reborn. For they are born through the concupiscence of the flesh; they are reborn through the grace of the Spirit. The former is of the world; the latter comes into the world, so that those predestined before the world may be chosen out of the world. When the apostle said, *God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself*, he immediately added how he does this, saying: *Not counting their offenses against them*[^15]. The whole world, then, is guilty through Adam, while God does not withhold his forming hand from his own work, from the seeds he established, although corrupted by the first father's transgression. And when the world is reconciled through Christ, it is set free from the world by him who came into the world, not to be chosen out of the world but to choose; not by an election of merits but by an election of grace, because *a remnant has been saved through the election of grace*[^16].

**3. 6.** Next you set out other words of mine, where I said, "Regeneration alone forgives the guilt of this concupiscence; hence generation contracts it," and immediately added, "Therefore let what has been generated be regenerated, so that likewise what has been contracted may be forgiven, since it cannot be forgiven otherwise."[^17] In vain you repeatedly try to conceal that you consider baptism superfluous for little ones, saying that "the grace of Christ's mysteries is rich in many gifts." Willingly or unwillingly, you acknowledge that little ones believe in Christ through the hearts and mouths of those who carry them. Therefore that saying of the Lord applies to them too: *Whoever does not believe will be condemned*[^18]. For what reason, by what justice, if they contract no original sin? As for your saying, "He rather shows them to be his own by enriching what he has made in them with increased benefits before the obedience of their own will," if this is how he shows these to be his own, then he certainly does not show those to whom he does not give this gift to be his own. Yet since they too are his for the same reason, because he created them, why does he not show them likewise to be his own? Here you offer no denial concerning fate or partiality. Acknowledge grace with us, then. For what else is it, where nothing is theirs? Thus, in one and the same condition, one is left by the judgment of justice, not by fate; another is taken by the gift of grace, not by merit.

**3. 7.** You contend altogether in vain that little ones are not cleansed even of original sin through regeneration. This is not what he shows who said: *All of us who were baptized into Christ were baptized into his death*[^19]. For by saying *all of us*, he certainly did not make an exception of little ones. And what is it to be baptized into Christ's death but to die to sin? Hence the same apostle says of Christ himself elsewhere: *For in that he died to sin, he died once*[^20]. This was certainly said because of the likeness of sinful flesh; hence also the great mystery of his cross, where *our old man was crucified with him, so that the body of sin might be destroyed*[^21]. If, then, little ones are baptized into Christ, they are baptized into his death. If they are baptized into his death, being planted together in the likeness of his death, they certainly die to sin. *For in that he died to sin, he died once; but in that he lives, he lives to God*[^22]. And what is it *to be planted together in the likeness of his death* but what follows: *So you too must reckon yourselves dead to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus*[^23]? Are we to say that Jesus died to a sin that he never had? Far from it. And yet, in that he died to sin, he died once. For his death signified our sin, through which death itself came about. Having died to that death—that is, so that he would no longer be mortal—he is said to have died to sin. What he signified in the likeness of sinful flesh, then, we accomplish through his grace in sinful flesh: just as, by dying to the likeness of sin, he is proclaimed to have died to sin, so whoever is baptized into him dies to the very reality of which that was the likeness; and just as in his true flesh there was a true death, so in the case of true sins there is a true forgiveness.

**4. 8.** If, then, this whole passage of the apostolic letter does not correct you in this perversity, you have become exceedingly hardened. For although everything he says in writing to the Romans is interconnected, so that God's grace through Jesus Christ may be commended, we cannot review and discuss the entire letter here: that would take far too long. Let us therefore consider it from the point now at issue, where he says: *But God commends his love toward us, because while we were still sinners, Christ died for us*[^24]. You want it to be thought that little ones are excepted from this statement. Here, if I ask you, 'If they are not to be counted among sinners, how did he who died for sinners die for them?' you will answer that he did not die for sinners alone, although he died for sinners too. Yet nowhere in the divine authorities do you read that Christ also died for those who had no sin at all. But consider how forceful are the testimonies that press upon you. You say he died for sinners too; I say he died only for sinners. Thus you are compelled to answer that, if little ones are bound by no sin, he did not die for little ones. For he says to the Corinthians: *Since one died for all, therefore all died; and he died for all*[^25]. Here you are in no way permitted to deny that Jesus died only for those who were dead. Who, then, do you understand these dead to be? Those who have departed from the body? Who could be so foolish as to understand it this way? We therefore understand those dead, for all of whom the one Christ died, in the sense in which he says elsewhere: *And you, when you were dead in offenses and in the uncircumcision of your flesh, he made alive with him*[^26]. Accordingly, he says, *one* *died for all; therefore all died*, showing that he could not have died except for the dead. For he proved that all were dead from the fact that one died for all. I thrust this upon you, press it upon you, force it upon you as you resist: accept it; it brings health; I do not want you to die. *One died for all; therefore all died.* See that he intended it as a necessary consequence that all should be understood to be dead if he died for all. Since they were not dead in the body, then, it follows that no one who does not deny or doubt that he is a Christian should deny or doubt that all for whom Christ died were dead in sin. Therefore, if little ones contract no sin, they are not dead. If they are not dead, he who died only for the dead did not die for them. Yet already in your first book you cried out against us and said that "Christ died for little ones too."[^27] You are therefore in no way permitted to deny that they contract original sin. For from what were they dead, if not from that? Or because of what death of little ones did he die who died only for the dead? And you acknowledge that he died for little ones. Return with me, then, to what I had begun to discuss from the letter to the Romans.

**4. 9.** *God commends*, he says, *his love toward us, because while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. While we*, he says, *were sinners*—that is, while we were dead—*Christ died for us; much more, now justified in his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him. For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved in his life*[^28]. This is what is said elsewhere: *God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself*[^29]. Then here he continues: *And not only so, but also glorying in God through our Lord Jesus Christ. Not only*, he says, *saved, but also glorying. Through whom we have now also received reconciliation*[^30]. And as though the reason were being sought why this reconciliation takes place through one human mediator, he says: *For this reason*, *just as through one man sin entered this world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^31]. What, then, did the law accomplish? Could it not bring reconciliation? No, he says: *For until the law, sin was in the world*; that is, the law too could not take away sin. *But sin was not reckoned when there was no law*[^32]. Sin indeed existed, but it was not reckoned because it was not recognized. *For through the law*, as he says elsewhere, *comes knowledge of sin*[^33]. *But death reigned from Adam until Moses*, because not even through Moses, that is, not even through the law, was its reign taken away. *And it reigned even in those who did not sin.* Why, then, if they did not sin? Hear why: *In the likeness*, he says, *of Adam's transgression, who is the figure of the one to come*[^34]. For from himself he gave his descendants a form, even though they had no sins of their own, so that those begotten through his carnal concupiscence would die through the contagion of their father's sin. *But the gift*, he says, *is not like the offense. For if through the offense of one many died, much more did the grace of God and the gift in the grace of the one man Jesus Christ abound to many*[^35]. It certainly abounded much more, because those in whom it abounds die for a time but will live forever. *And the gift*, he says, *is not as through the one who sinned. For the judgment was from one offense to condemnation, but grace is from many offenses to justification*[^36]. For even that one offense could drag us into condemnation; but grace has wiped away not only this one, but also the many sins added to it. *For if through the offense of one*, he says, *death reigned through one, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and righteousness reign in life through the one Jesus Christ*[^37]. The earlier sense is repeated: they will reign in life much more, since they will reign without end, than death reigned in them, since its reign had an end. *Therefore, as through the offense of one*, he says, *it came upon all human beings to condemnation, so also through the justification of one it came upon all human beings to justification of life*[^38]. Thus there it is *all*, and here it is *all*, because no one comes to death except through the former, and no one to life except through the latter. *For as through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners,* *so also through the obedience of one many will be made righteous. But the law came in so that the offense might abound. Yet where the offense abounded, grace abounded all the more, so that, just as sin reigned in death, grace too might reign through righteousness unto eternal life, through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^39].

**4. 10.** *What, then, shall we say?* he asks. *Shall we remain in sin so that grace may abound? Far from it*[^40]. For what has grace bestowed, if we are to remain in sin? Accordingly he continues: *We who have died to sin, how shall we live in it?*[^41] Now pay careful attention here, and listen attentively so that you understand what follows. After saying, *We who have died to sin, how shall we live in it? Or do you not know*, he says, *that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?*[^42] Are the little ones who are baptized included here, or are they not? If they are not included, then his statement is false: *All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death*, because little ones are not baptized into his death. But since the apostle speaks the truth, no one is understood to be excepted. For if you think this was said only of older people who already exercise free choice, although he says *all of us*, then you are needlessly frightened by that saying of the Lord: *Unless someone is reborn of water and the Spirit*[^43]. For you have a great shortcut: assert that this too was said only of older people and that little ones are by no means included in this general statement. Why, then, must you struggle over the question of baptism, whether there is eternal life outside God's kingdom, or whether so many innocent images of God must be deprived of eternal life and therefore punished with eternal death? But if you dare not say this, because the statement was uttered universally, *Unless someone is reborn of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God*[^44], the same universality presses upon you when the apostle says: *All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death.* Therefore little ones too who are baptized into Christ, since they are baptized into his death, die to sin. For these following statements are connected with what had been said above: *We who have died to sin, how shall we live in it?* As though someone were asking what it means to die to sin, he says: *Or do you not know* *that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?* He thereby proves what he had said above: *If we have died to sin, how shall we live in it?* Thus those who knew they had been baptized into Christ's death when they were baptized into Christ would know also that they had died to sin, because to be baptized into Christ's death is nothing other than to die to sin. Explaining this more carefully, he adds: *We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ rose from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life. For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall also be in the likeness of his resurrection, knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him, so that the body of sin might be destroyed and we might no longer serve sin. For one who has died has been justified from sin. But if we have died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him, knowing that Christ, rising from the dead, dies no more; death will no longer rule over him. For in that he died to sin, he died once; but in that he lives, he lives to God. So you too must reckon yourselves dead indeed to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus*[^45]. If, then, little ones do not die to sin, they are undoubtedly not baptized into Christ's death. If they are not baptized into Christ's death, they are not baptized into Christ. *For all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death.* But they are baptized into Christ; therefore they die to sin. To what sin, I ask you, if not the original sin they contracted? Let human arguments fall silent: *The Lord knows the thoughts of human beings, that they are vain*[^46]. *He has hidden these things from the wise and prudent and revealed them to little ones*[^47]. If the Christian faith displeases you, confess it more openly, for you cannot find another Christian faith. There is one man leading to death, one leading to life. The former is merely human; the latter is God and man. Through the former the world became God's enemy; through the latter the world, chosen out of the world, is reconciled to God. *For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. As we have borne the image of the earthly man, let us also bear the image of him who is from heaven*[^48]. Whoever strives to overturn these foundations of the Christian faith is himself overturned while they remain standing.

**5. 11.** What I said in the book you oppose is entirely true: "For that what has been forgiven in the parent should be contracted by the offspring happens in wonderful ways, but it does happen; and because the manner itself is not easily investigated by reasoning or explained in speech, unbelievers do not believe it."[^49] You dishonestly lay a trap for these words of mine, as though I had said that it "cannot be grasped by reasoning or explained in speech," removing what I said, "not easily," whether by "reasoning" or by "speech." "Not at all," which you say, is one thing; "not easily," which I said, is another. What do you show yourself to be here but a slanderer? Yet even if it could be investigated by no reasoning and explained by no speech, what has from ancient times been proclaimed and believed with true catholic faith throughout the whole Church is nevertheless true. She would neither exorcize nor exsufflate the children of the faithful unless she were rescuing them from the power of darkness and from the prince of death. I set this down in my book, to which you pretend to reply[^50]; but you were afraid to mention it, as though the whole world would exsufflate you yourself if you chose to oppose this exsufflation, by which the prince of the world is cast out even from little ones. In vain you writhe amid empty arguments, not against me but against our common spiritual mother. She gave birth to you in precisely the way in which you now refuse to let her give birth. Against her very womb you imagine yourself armed with sufficiently sharp weapons, collecting arguments from God's justice against God's justice, from God's grace against God's grace. For God's justice is true if the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day they come forth from their mother's womb[^51] is not unjust. But how is the heavy yoke not unjust, if there is no evil in little ones for which they are justly pressed beneath a heavy yoke? God's grace is true if it exhibits in deeds what it declares in words. But how does it do this if it exsufflates one in whom it knows there is nothing to drive out, if it washes one in whom it knows there is nothing to wash away?

**5. 12.** Would you seem to yourself or to any of your followers to be saying anything at all if, with holiness of mind, you could reflect on how great an evil the concupiscence of the flesh is—so great that whoever is born of it must be reborn, and whoever is not reborn must be condemned? And could you reflect on what grace bestows when it absolves its guilt, by which it made a human being guilty from his origin, when full forgiveness of sins takes place in him? Concupiscence itself nevertheless remains, for the spirit of the regenerate person to desire against it, either making good use of this evil in the lesser struggle or making no use of it at all in the greater struggle. For the experience of this evil remains within us as it is resisted and restrained. But just as that guilt, forgiven only through regeneration, was not felt when it was present, so its removal is believed by faith, not felt in the flesh or the mind. Thus you boast amid the obscurity of this matter and fight against a truth that cannot be displayed to human senses, especially those of carnal people; and the more fiercely you think you fight, the more faithlessly you fight.

**5. 13.** But *turn yourself into every shape, and muster whatever* *strength you have in courage or in skill*[^52]. *All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death*[^53]. Therefore it is true that we have died to sin in Christ's death, which was without sin; and this includes both older people and little ones. For it is not the former and not the latter, or the latter and not the former, but *all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death. We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death.* Not without little ones, because *all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death. So that, just as Christ rose from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life. For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall also be in the likeness of his resurrection*[^54]. Here little ones too are planted together in the likeness of his death. For this applies to all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus. *Knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him.* Whose old man, if not that of *all of us who were baptized into Christ?* Here, then, let us recognize little ones too, because we do not deny that they too have been baptized into Christ. And why *was our old man crucified with him? So that the body of sin might be destroyed*, he says, *and we might no longer serve sin*[^55]. Because of this body of sin, *God sent his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh*[^56]. With what shamelessness, then, do we deny that little ones too have a body of sin, when what he says applies to all of us who were baptized into Christ? *For one who has died has been justified from sin. But if we have died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Christ, knowing that Christ, rising from the dead, dies no more; death will no longer rule over him. For in that he died to sin, he died once; but in that he lives, he lives to God. So you too*, he says, *must reckon yourselves dead* *to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus*[^57]. To whom does he say this? Are you awake and paying attention? Surely to those to whom he was saying, *But if we have died with Christ.* And who are these, unless those to whom he had said, *Our old man was crucified with him, so that the body of sin might be destroyed?* Unless those to whom he had said, *We have been planted together in the likeness of his death?* Unless those to whom he had said, *We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death?* And to whom, or about whom, had he said this? Read the preceding words, with which all these statements are connected, and you will find: *Or do you not know that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?* And when he said this, what did he wish to prove? Read a little further back still, and you will find: *If we have died to sin, how shall we live in it?* Either, then, acknowledge that little ones have died to sin in baptism, and confess that they had original sin to which they might die; or say openly that they were not baptized into Christ's death when they were baptized into Christ, and convict the apostle of lying when he says: *All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death*[^58].

**5. 14.** I do not lay down these heavenly weapons, which conquer Caelestius; to them I entrust my faith and my speech. The arguments you put forward are human; these defenses are divine. *Who understands offenses?*[^59] Are they therefore not offenses? Likewise, who understands the original offense that is forgiven in a regenerated parent, yet passes into the offspring and remains unless the offspring too is regenerated? Is it therefore not an offense? *One died for all; therefore all died*[^60]. With what heart, what mouth, what face do you deny that little ones are dead, when you do not deny that Christ died for them? If Christ did not die for them, why are they baptized? *For all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death*[^61]. But if he who alone died for all died also for them, then they too are dead along with all. And because they are dead in sin, they also die to sin so that they may live to God, when they are reborn of God. If I cannot explain how a living person begets a dead one—for a parent who has died to sin and lives to God nevertheless begets one who is dead in sin, unless that one too dies to sin through regeneration and lives to God—is it therefore false because it cannot be explained in words, or can be explained only with the greatest difficulty? Deny, if you dare, that one is born dead for whom you do not deny Christ died. For *one* *died for all; therefore all died.* These are the apostle's words, but they are our weapons. If, however, you refuse to fight against them, you understand that what you do not understand must nevertheless be believed without doubt. For a human being who is spiritually born and carnally begets has both kinds of seed: the immortal seed through which he rejoices that he is alive, and the mortal seed through which he begets one who is dead. Christ's death would in no way be necessary to bring that one to life unless he had been born dead. For *one* *died for all; therefore all died.* Nor do you raise from this death those whom you proclaim not to be dead. Rather, you prevent them from living when you attack, with the siege engines of impious arguments, the faith in their parents through which alone they can come back to life.

**6. 15.** But let us now come to your lengthy and laborious discussion, in which you tried to refute the example I thought should be used, for the sake of however slight a resemblance, in a matter difficult to discern[^62]: namely, that the seed of an olive tree degenerates into a wild olive. First you assert that "examples offer no support to a proposition that cannot be defended on its own." Why, then, did the apostle, immediately after raising the question of how the dead rise and with what body they come, undertake at once to prove this unknown and unexperienced reality by an example, adding: *Fool, what you sow is not brought to life unless it dies*[^63], and so on? This example is not altogether inappropriate to the matter now at issue either. For wheat is cleansed of chaff, as a human being is cleansed of sin; and yet from it another grain is born with chaff.

**6. 16.** And what did you mean to say about the crocodile: that "Albinus declares it alone among all animals moves its upper jaws, and that fire, although destructive to all, is a plaything for the salamander"? Have you not brought these examples forward against yourselves instead, when something is found that demonstrates the possibility of what was denied universally? Since, then, you have universally denied that parents can pass on to their offspring what they do not themselves possess, if something is found that can do this, you are defeated—just as someone who said that animals can move only their lower jaws is unquestionably defeated when the crocodile is found, and someone who said that no animal lives in fire is unquestionably refuted when what is reported of the salamander is demonstrated. Therefore, when you lay down the rule that "natural characteristics are not altered by an accident," if even one person is found who, after being impaired by some accident, begot a son with the same impairment, and what was accidental in the parent became natural in the offspring, then surely this rule of yours will be invalidated. Likewise, when you lay down the rule that "a parent cannot transmit to his offspring what he himself does not possess," will not this rule too be destroyed when you are shown people born with all their limbs perfectly intact from parents who had lost some of theirs? We used to hear from our elders, who said that they knew and had witnessed it, that Fundanius, a teacher of rhetoric at Carthage, had lost an eye through an accidental injury and begotten a one-eyed son. This example overturns your assertion that "natural characteristics are not altered by an accident." For what was accidental in the father became natural in the son. As for your other assertion, that "parents cannot pass on to their children what they do not themselves possess," another son of Fundanius overturns it, having been born—as is very usual—with two eyes from a one-eyed father; so do countless people born with sight from blind parents. For by transmitting to those they beget what they do not themselves possess, these parents show that it is you, rather than their own children, who resemble them, since you have been so blind in your rules.

**7. 17.** Indeed, while speaking at length about things irrelevant to the matter, you said something that reminded me of a point that does bear upon it. You said that "curiosity is usually less amazed at what it has grasped, and that divine provision was made against it, so that many things would spring from the earth, distinguished by countless peculiarities." And this is indeed the usefulness of God's hidden works: that readily available things should not become cheap, that understood things should not cease to be wonderful. Hence Scripture too says: *As with the bones in the womb of a pregnant woman, so you do not know all the works that God will do*[^64]. You yourself, then, rightly said that God's works are incomprehensible, to oppose the curiosity that usually admires less what it has managed to understand. Why, then, do you try to overthrow by human opinion what you are less able to comprehend in the divine ordering? I certainly did not say, as you falsely charge, that "it cannot be understood by any reasoning," but that "it cannot be understood by easy reasoning." Yet what if God wished to hide this, like many other things, so that human conjecture could not investigate and comprehend it, in opposition to the fault of human curiosity, to which, as you yourself recall, understood things usually become cheap? Should you therefore arm yourselves against the Church your mother with your petty arguments like little parricidal daggers? Should you seek out the hidden power of her sacrament, by which she conceives little ones to be cleansed, although born of cleansed parents, as though seeking the bones in a pregnant woman's womb—not by touching but by tearing? Were I not unwilling to weary the reader with a lengthy discussion, I would now overwhelm you with a thousand kinds of things whose incomprehensible ordering creeps away from nature's usual paths as if through dark wildernesses. Among them I would show many seeds that degenerate, not into a wholly different kind—for the wild olive is not as different from the olive as a vine is—but into a kind of similar dissimilarity, if one may say so, just as the wild vine differs from the vine, yet is produced from the vine's seed. And why should we not believe that the Creator willed this so that we would believe that human seed too can contract from its parents a defect that is not present in those who beget it? Thus even the baptized would hasten with their little ones to his grace, by which human beings are rescued from the power of darkness and transferred into his kingdom, just as your holy father hastened with you, not knowing how ungrateful you would become toward that same grace.

**7. 18.** But as nature's most penetrating investigator, you discover its boundaries and establish its rules, saying: "By the nature of things it is impossible for parents to be shown to transmit what they are believed to have lost. If they transmit it," you say, "they have not lost it." These are Pelagian definitions, which you should already have rejected after reading the little work of ours addressed to Marcellinus of devout memory that you mention[^65]. For Pelagius had previously said of believing parents that "they could not transmit to their descendants what they themselves did not possess." But how false this is is thoroughly demonstrated by the clearest examples, some of which I have given above; another is the one I shall now give. What foreskin does a circumcised man retain, although an uncircumcised child is begotten from him, and what is no longer in the man is contracted in the man's seed? Nor should we believe that God commanded the ancient fathers to circumcise little ones on the eighth day for any other reason than to signify the regeneration that takes place in Christ. After the seventh day, the Sabbath, on which he lay in the tomb, *having been delivered up for our offenses*, he *rose for our justification*[^66] on the following day, that is, the eighth in the succession of weeks. Who, even moderately instructed in the sacred writings, would not know that this sacrament of circumcision preceded baptism as its figure, when the apostle says most clearly of Christ: *He is the head of every principality and power; in him you were also circumcised with a circumcision not made by hand, in the stripping off of the body of flesh, in the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you also rose with him through faith in the working of God, who raised him from the dead. And you, when you were dead in offenses and in the uncircumcision of your flesh, he made alive with him, forgiving us all our offenses*[^67]? That circumcision made by hand, which was given to Abraham, was therefore sent ahead as a likeness of this circumcision not made by hand, which now takes place in Christ.

**7. 19.** [For one cannot say: The foreskin is a body, whereas what is contracted at one's origin is a defect; although the former is cut off, its power could not thereby be removed from the seed, whereas this defect, which is not a body but an accident, could not remain in the seed after being remitted by forgiveness. This, I say, cannot be said by anyone, however clever, because he is defeated by the divine authority that commanded this very part of the body to be cut off precisely so that this defect might be cleansed. Unless it were in the seed, it would in no way reach the little ones from whom it must be removed by that circumcision of the body; nor, if it had not reached them, would it in any way need to be removed by this bodily circumcision. But since a little one has absolutely no sin of his own, it follows that no other sin is taken away from him except original sin by that remedy without which his soul perishes from his people. This would not happen under a just God unless there were guilt for which it happened. And since there is no guilt of his own, it follows that the guilt is solely that of a corrupted origin].

**7. 20.** See: a circumcised man transmits to one born from him what he himself has lost. What, then, becomes of your saying that "by the nature of things it is impossible for human beings to be shown to transmit what they are believed to have lost"? The foreskin is good, not evil, because God made it, just as you argued at great length about the wild olive. The answer to you is this: the wild olive is good in the natural order, but in the writing of mysteries it signifies evil. So do wolves, foxes, a washed sow wallowing in the mire, and a dog returning to its vomit. In the natural order all are good, just as sheep are; for *God made* *all things very good*[^68]. But in the holy books wolves signify evil people, while sheep signify good people. When we discuss the difference between good and evil people, we draw comparisons from these animals according not to what they are but to what they signify. Likewise, because the foreskin is a small part of the human body, which is entirely a good substance, it is certainly good by nature; yet it signifies evil figuratively when a little one is commanded to be circumcised on the eighth day because of Christ, in whom, as the apostle says, we have been circumcised with a circumcision not made by hand, which circumcision made by hand undoubtedly prefigured. Thus the foreskin is not sin but signifies sin, and original sin especially, because the origin of those born is through that very member. Because of this sin we have been called children of wrath by nature; for that member itself is also properly called 'nature.' Therefore circumcision of the flesh not only unquestionably overturns that purportedly general proposition of yours that "by the nature of things a parent cannot transmit to his offspring what he himself has lost"; it also, because the foreskin signifies sin and is found in the child born although it was no longer in the parent, plainly demonstrates that original sin, already forgiven in baptized parents, remains in little ones unless they too are baptized, that is, cleansed by spiritual circumcision. It convicts you of denying what is most true, because, while denying original sin, you cannot discover why, under a just judge, that little one perishes of whom it was said: *His soul will perish from his people if he has not been circumcised on the eighth day*[^69].

**7. 21.** Let us leave the forests of wild olives and the hills of olives, whether African or Italian. Let us not question farmers who might give you one answer and me another, neither of whom we could refute by a swift test if a tree were planted for this experiment, *to cast its shade for late descendants*[^70]. We have an olive tree, neither African nor Italian but Hebrew, into which we rejoice to have been grafted, though we were a wild olive. Circumcision was given to that olive tree, and it settles this question for us beyond dispute. The offspring contracts a foreskin that its parent no longer has; he lacked it, yet handed it on; he lost it, yet transmitted it. And this foreskin signifies sin. Therefore sin itself can perish in parents and yet pass to their children. Let the infant himself also testify and speak without words: “My soul will perish from my people unless I am circumcised on the eighth day. You, then, who deny original evil and confess a just God, tell me what sin I have committed.” Since your loquacity answers this infant's silent cry with no reasonable word, join your voices instead with ours in the Apostle's words. We are free to inquire what contagions of sins come from parents, or whether there are others too, whether finding the answer be easy, difficult, or impossible. Yet that *through one* *man sin entered this world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^71], we should not think it lawful to understand otherwise than by believing that all for whom Christ died were dead in the first man's sin, and that all who are baptized in Christ die to sin.

**8. 22.** But concerning other words of mine, which you insert into your discussion as though to refute them, you say that “I tried to stir up the populace against you,” because I said, “The Christian faith, which the new heretics have begun to attack, does not doubt that those washed in the washing of regeneration are redeemed from the devil's power, and those not yet redeemed by such regeneration, even the little children of the redeemed, are captives under that same devil's power unless they too are redeemed by the same grace”[^72]. To confirm this by apostolic testimony I said, “The benefit of God of which the Apostle speaks belongs to every age: *He rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^73].” If this statement moves the populace against you, should you not rather recognize from this that this Catholic faith is so widely spread and so confirmed among all that it cannot escape even popular knowledge? For all Christians necessarily had to know what they were doing with their little children regarding Christian mysteries. Why do you say that “forgetting single combat I took refuge in the populace”? Who promised you single combat with me? Where, when, how, in whose presence, with what judges? Was “a treaty offered,” as you put it, by which *it was agreed to settle the war*[^74], so that our contest would resolve everyone's battles? Far be it from me to claim for myself among Catholics what you are not ashamed to claim for yourself among Pelagians. I am one among many who refute your profane novelties as we can, according to the measure of faith God has allotted each of us[^75]. Before I was born to this world and before I was reborn to God, many Catholic lights anticipated your future darkness by refuting it. I discussed them with as much clarity as I could in my two preceding books. You have somewhere else to turn if you still delight in raging against the Catholic faith.

**8. 23.** Nor should you ridicule Christ's members with the name “bench-bound craftsmen.” Remember that *God chose the weak things of the world to confound the strong*[^76]. What do you mean by saying that “when you begin to show them, they will become more bitter against me”? Do not lie to them, and they will not. For I do not, as you falsely charge, “assert that they are the devil's property,” since I know they have been redeemed by Christ's blood. Nor do I “transfer any marriages to the devil,” insofar as they are marriages; nor do I “imagine him the artificer of the genitals”; nor do I assert that he “arouses men,” except to unlawful acts, or “makes women fertile,” or is “the creator of little children.” Therefore, if you tell them these things about me, which are foreign to me, you will be lying; and if any of them believes you and grows more bitter against me, he may have been deceived, not taught. Those who know both of us and the Catholic faith do not want to learn anything from you; rather, they guard against your taking away what they know. Many among them learned these things that your new error attacks not only independently of me but even before me. Since, therefore, I did not make them but found them companions in this truth you deny, how am I the author for them of what you think an error?

**9. 24.** “Explain,” you say, “how sin can justly be ascribed to a person who neither willed to sin nor could do so.” Committing one's own sins is one thing, the contagion of another's sins another, insofar as the actions of each person's own life are concerned. For if you did not wish to twist a right statement into your twisted meaning, you would hear the Apostle explain this briefly when he said there was one in whom all sinned. In that one all died, so that another one might die for all. For *one* *died for all; therefore all died*[^77], for whom Christ died. Deny, therefore, that Christ died also for little children, to exempt them from the number of the dead, that is, from the contagion of sins. “How can it happen,” you ask, “that a matter of choice is mingled with the condition of seed?” If it could not happen, there would be no reason to call little children dead before they have departed from the body. For if Christ died for them too, then they too are dead, *because if one died for all, therefore all died.* Do you hear, Julian? These are the Apostle's words, not mine. Why ask me “how it happened,” when you see that it happened somehow, if you believe in any way the Apostle, who could in no way lie about Christ and those for whom Christ died?

**9. 25.** Someone else perverse like you, but with a different error, could say of God precisely what you say we think concerning little children: that he is “always industrious for his enemy's gain.” For he does not cease to create, nourish, and clothe those whom he knows will be under the devil not temporarily but burning with him forever, and to supply life and health to them as they sin with the most obstinate iniquity. But God does this as one who knows how to use both the good and the evil well. By no art of wickedness does the devil withdraw either those he oppresses or deceives, or even himself, from the uses of God's majesty. Accordingly, those rescued from the devil's power do not belong to the devil; those who do belong to him are, like him, in God's power.

**9. 26.** How empty, then, is what you think you said so cleverly, as though we asserted that “the devil and God entered this treaty with each other: God should claim whatever is washed, and the devil whatever is born, yet on this condition,” as you say, “that God should fertilize with an obligated power the very union the devil instituted”! The devil certainly did not institute the union, since it would exist even if no one had sinned, but in such a way that your client would either not exist there or not be restless. Nor does God fertilize women's wombs by an obligated power, but by a free and most mighty one, even when they will give birth to the devil's vessels. Just as to evil human beings, so to seeds corrupted in their origin, whose substance created by him is good, he bestows growth, form, life, and health, with gratuitous goodness, no necessity, invincible power, and irreproachable truth. Since both what is washed and what is born are in God's power, from which even the devil himself is not found exempt, how have you set down these two things? Did you wish being born to be better than being washed? Or being washed better, since being born is included in it? For one who has not been born cannot be washed. Or do you weigh both in an equal balance? If you think being born better, you insult spiritual regeneration by preferring carnal generation to it in a sacrilegious error. For we do not believe without reason that you refused to say “what is reborn,” but said “what is washed,” so that, since you wished to show that in our understanding the devil and God had divided shares between them, you could make God's share less valuable by a cheap word. You could have said “what is reborn,” or “what is regenerated,” or at least “what is baptized,” since Latin usage has adopted this Greek word so that it is ordinarily understood only of the sacrament of regeneration. You refused all these and chose a word to make what you said contemptible. No reader could prefer someone born to someone reborn, regenerated, or baptized; but you thought someone born could easily be preferred to someone washed. Yet if being washed to bear the heavenly man's image is as much better than being born to bear the earthly man's image as heaven is distant from earth[^78], this malicious partition of yours has already vanished. It is no wonder if God claims for himself the heavenly man's image received through sacred washing, while allowing the earthly man's image, fouled by earthly pollution, to be under the devil until it is reborn in Christ to receive the heavenly man's image.

**9. 27.** But if you weigh being washed and being born equally, so that little children not yet reborn are not believed to be under the devil lest God and the devil seem to divide equal shares between them, if God claims the washed and the devil the born, then certainly, because on this understanding being washed is worth as much as being born, you persuade people that being washed is superfluous: being born, which is worth just as much, suffices. Yet we give thanks that you do not think this. For you do not admit those born to God's kingdom unless they are washed, and thus you declare being washed much better than being born. Now, then, give yourselves the explanation, so that you do not think it unworthy that those not admitted to God's kingdom should be under him who fell from God's kingdom, and those who lack life under him who lost life. That little children do not have life unless they have Christ—whom they certainly cannot have unless they put him on in the way written, *All of you who were baptized in Christ have put on Christ*[^79]—that they do not have life unless they have Christ, the Evangelist John testifies in his letter: *Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son does not have life*[^80]. Those who do not have life, for whom Christ died so that they might have it, are therefore rightly understood to be dead. For *one* *died for all; therefore all died*[^81]. He died, as we read in Hebrews, *so that through death he might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil*[^82]. What wonder, then, if little children, as long as they are dead, before they begin to have him who died for the dead, are under him who has the power of death?

**10. 28.** You enumerate things that the Christian faith truly does not doubt, recalling among them almost entirely things we too proclaim and consider beyond doubt. This goes so far that we admit even your statement, “No human sin can exist without an act of free choice,” to be true. For even the sin contracted as original sin would not exist without the act of free choice by which the first man sinned, through whom *sin entered the world and passed into all human beings*[^83]. As for your statement that “one person is not held liable for another's sins,” it matters how far this can rightly be understood. I am not now discussing David's sin, for which so many thousands of people fell[^84], or how, because one man took something devoted to destruction against the prohibition, vengeance fell upon those who neither did it nor knew it had been done[^85]. That is another discussion, and this kind of sins or punishments should not detain us now. But parents' sins are in one sense called another's and in another sense found to be ours too. They are another's in the particularity of the action, but ours through the contagion of propagation. If this were false, the heavy yoke upon the children of Adam from the day they come forth from their mother's womb[^86] would assuredly in no way be just.

**10. 29.** As for your citation of the Apostle, *We must all be manifested before Christ's tribunal, so that each may receive what belongs to his own body according to what he has done, whether good or evil*[^87], how do you understand this regarding little children? Will they too be manifested before Christ's tribunal or not? If not, how does this statement help you, since it does not concern those whose case is now at issue? If they will, how does each receive what he has done when he has done nothing, unless because it pertains to them that through the hearts and mouths of those carrying them they either believe or do not believe? He said *what belongs to his own body*, referring to what belongs to each person now living in himself. For how does he receive good, to enter God's kingdom, if each receives what he has done, unless what he has done through another—that is, believed—also pertains to the little child? Therefore, just as his believing pertains to him so that he receives good, namely God's kingdom, so his not believing also pertains to him so that he receives the judgment of condemnation; for this too is a Gospel statement: *Whoever does not believe will be condemned*[^88]. And when the Apostle said, *We must all be manifested, so that each may receive according to what he has done, whether good or evil*[^89], he intended no middle state to be understood. See how unreasonably you refuse to let a little child receive evil from another's sin, yet want him to receive good from another's right action, not just any good but God's kingdom. For it is another's work when he believes through another, just as it was another's work when he sinned in another. Nor do we doubt that every offense is cleansed by baptism, but each person is cleansed by being reborn. Therefore what only regeneration removes, generation does not cease to transmit.

**10. 30.** Certainly, by saying that “concupiscence is not altogether rebellious against the mind,” you undoubtedly confess that it is rebellious. Yet do you not acknowledge the punishment by which you wage war against yourself? “Let God be called the Creator of little children such as are worthy of God's hands,” you say, adding, “that is, innocent.” Does someone not seem to surpass you in piety and God's praise if he says that beautiful and healthy works too befit God's hands? Yet many are born deformed, many sick, many hideous and monstrous. Nevertheless, no one but the good and true God could create that whole substance, all its parts, and whatever substantially exists and lives in it.

**10. 31.** You command me to explain “how the devil dares claim for himself little children created in Christ, that is, in his power.” Explain yourself, if you can, how he claims for himself, not obscurely but openly, little children tormented by unclean spirits. If you say they were handed over to him, we both see the punishment: state the desert. We both perceive the penalty; you who say no evil merits are contracted from parents, while we both confess a just God, show if you can in infants a fault deserving this penalty. Or do you not recognize that this too belongs to that heavy yoke upon the children of Adam, from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until the day of burial in the mother of all? Under this yoke the human race is so crushed by diverse disasters that it is evident that people's becoming, from children of wrath, children of mercy, as though receiving a pledge, is being prepared for the age to come. In this age, however, from birth to death, even they are crushed under the same heavy yoke. For even baptized little children sometimes suffer these assaults of demons among the other evils of this life, though rescued from the power of darkness so that they are not dragged by them into eternal punishment.

**10. 32.** Again you say something you have already said and I have answered[^90], but which I must not pass over even now: “When God grants the glory of regeneration to little children who have earned nothing of their own, either good or evil, by this very fact he teaches that they belong to his care, his right, and his dominion, because he anticipates their will with the generosity of an ineffable benefit.” How, then, have they offended him whom, equally innocent, clean, and created by him in his image, he excludes in countless numbers from this gift, not anticipating their will with the generosity of this ineffable benefit, separating so many images of his from his kingdom? If this will be no evil for them, then so many innocent images of God will not love God's kingdom. But if they love it, and love it as much as the innocent ought to love the kingdom of him who creates them in his image, will they suffer no evil from this very separation? Finally, wherever and however they may be under God the judge, who is neither constrained by fate nor corrupted by partiality, they will not be in the happiness of that kingdom where others who have equally earned nothing good or evil will be. Yet if they had earned no evil, they would never, with a common case, be deprived of sharing so great a good. In them, therefore, as we have often said, the vessels of wrath, God makes known according to the Apostle the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy[^91], so that they may not boast as though of the merits of their own life, when they recognize that what they see rendered to their equal partners in death could most justly have been rendered to themselves.

**10. 33.** If, then, you wish to understand rightly, understand even of little children what the Apostle says of God the Father: *He rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love*[^92], and also, *For we too were once children of wrath by nature, like the rest*[^93]. For all who die to sin are rescued from the power of darkness and were children of wrath. All who are baptized into Christ's death die to sin so that they may live to God. Furthermore, everyone baptized in Christ is baptized into his death. Therefore, since little children too are baptized in Christ, they die to sin and are rescued from the power of darkness, where they had been children of wrath by nature. As for your statement that “where the Apostle says *children of wrath by nature*, it can be understood as children of wrath outright,” should this not have reminded you that the ancient Catholic faith is being defended against you? For scarcely any Latin manuscript is found, unless you are now beginning to correct it—or rather change it into error—in which *by nature* is not written. The ancient translators certainly should have avoided this unless it were also the antiquity of the faith that your novelty has begun to resist.

**11. 34.** But, outstanding man, you do not wish to be in the common flock. Once again you reject the populace's judgment, after so many statements with which, by rendering an explanation, you had aroused it against me more violently than it had been aroused against you. Evidently, considering your arguments, you realized that such reasoning had accomplished and could accomplish nothing among people grounded in the truth and antiquity of the Catholic faith. Hence you again turn your insolent mouth to despising them and run through, describing and ridiculing one by one, the groups composing that multitude of Christians most justly hostile to you. Among them you touch upon “students in the lecture halls” and say they will cry against me, “What times! What morals!”[^94] Yet you shrink from the populace's judgment, where you could find such noisy supporters to terrify me with Cicero's words because “I think the genital part of the body comes from somewhere other than the whole”! When I answer them, “I do not say this; he is lying. I blame lust, not the members; I mark out a defect, not nature. But this man who slanders me before you dares in Christ's Church, before the Teacher seated in heaven, to recite the praise of lust, something no teacher would assign him to recite if he studied with you, lest he offend the modesty of you all,” will they not turn against you other words of Cicero truly fitting you, saying, “On this side modesty fights, on yours impudence; here continence, there lust”[^95]?

**11. 35.** You also accuse some unnamed people as deserters from your doctrine who have converted or returned to the Catholic faith. Yet you made it clear that you fear them so much that you dared not state their names, lest on hearing your false charges against them they immediately return charges against you that, if not true, would at least be more credible. Whoever these people are, if they think seriously, they would not do this, but would spare you according to the Apostle, *not returning insult for insult*[^96]. But at least do not disdain to hear the admonition of the man from whose writings you chose to exclaim, “What times! What morals!” Hear at least him, I say: “As far as you are from shameful deeds”—if indeed you are—“so far keep yourself from freedom of speech, and do not say things against others that you would blush to have falsely charged against yourself.” Let readers know that you have said things against these unnamed people such as we do not know at all in any of those we know to have withdrawn from the Pelagian heretics while professing continence. What your men or women are like is no concern of mine, whom you deceive by saying that I define it thus: “Lust cannot be restrained even in a decaying body.” Rather, precisely because I maintain that it can and must be restrained, I understand it to be evil. Whoever denies it is evil should see how he possesses a good to which, willing or unwilling, he confesses that opposing lust must be restrained. I therefore say lust can be restrained not only by the old but by the young. But I greatly marvel that lust can be praised by the continent.

**11. 36.** Which of us says that “this evil contracted by little children at their origin can exist, or ever existed, without the substance in which it is”? You seek dialecticians as judges as though we said this, and ridicule the populace as though I were bringing you before it as a judge incapable of judging such matters. Had you not learned them, the machinery of Pelagian doctrine would have been left without its indispensable architect. If you wish to live, do not love the wisdom of words by which *Christ's Cross is emptied*[^97]. How qualities, good or evil, pass from one substance to another, not by migration but by affecting them, we already discussed in the preceding book. If you despise the populace's judgment, look at those judges I presented to you in my first two books, endowed with the most illustrious authority in Christ's Church.

**12. 37.** What is this, that to persist in your perversity you accuse Zosimus of blessed memory, bishop of the Apostolic See, of betrayal? He did not depart from his predecessor Innocent, whom you were afraid to name. You preferred Zosimus because at first he dealt more gently with Celestius, since Celestius had said he was ready to be corrected if anything in these opinions of yours displeased him, and had promised to agree with Innocent's letter.

**12. 38.** Remember how insultingly you throw at us the Roman people's dissension over the appointment of a bishop. I ask you whether people did this by their own will. If you deny it, how do you defend free choice? If you admit it, how do you call it “God's vengeance,” departing from your doctrine in your desire to be thought divinely avenged? Or do you at last concede what you denied with the most obstinate contention: that by God's hidden judgment something can be found in human wills themselves that is both sin and the punishment of sin? Unless you understood this in that statement of yours, you would never have called a human deed God's vengeance. Yet when something similar happened there many years ago concerning blessed Damasus and Ursicinus, the Roman Church had not yet condemned the Pelagians.

**12. 39.** You say that “I myself have changed my opinions, and at the beginning of my conversion thought as you do.” But you deceive or are deceived, either by slandering what I now say or by failing to understand, or rather to read, what I said then. For from the beginning of my conversion I have always held, as I hold now, that *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death, and so it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^98]. Books remain that I wrote while still a layman, very shortly after my conversion. Although not yet instructed in the Sacred Scriptures as I was afterward, even then I thought nothing on this matter, and where the course of the discussion required it said nothing, except what the whole Church has learned and taught from antiquity: namely, that the human race fell, by the desert of original sin, into these great and manifest miseries in which *a human being has become like vanity, so that his days pass like a shadow*[^99], and *every living human being is utter vanity*[^100]. No one delivers from this except him who said, *The truth will set you free*[^101], and, *I am the truth*[^102], and, *If the Son sets you free, then you will truly be free*[^103]. For only truth delivers from vanity, but according to grace, not debt; through mercy, not merit. Just as it belonged to judgment that we were subjected to vanity, so it belongs to mercy that we are delivered by truth and confess that our good merits themselves are nothing but God's gifts.

**13. 40.** Now let us examine that slander by which you accuse me of saying that “the baptized are cleansed only in part.” You say “this appears more clearly from my words,” and set out in your discussion for consideration the words of mine in which you claim it appears. I thank you. They are these: “Carnal concupiscence,” I say, “must not be imputed to marriage, but tolerated. It is not a good arising from natural wedlock but an evil resulting from ancient sin. Because of it, even from the just and lawful marriages of God's children there are generated not children of God but children of the world. For even those who generate, if already regenerated, generate not from that by which they are God's children but from that by which they are still children of the world. For the Lord's statement is: *The children of this world generate and are generated*[^104]. Accordingly, from that by which we are still children of this world our outer human being decays, and from this children of this world are generated; from that by which we are God's children the inner human being is renewed day by day[^105]. Although the outer human being too has been sanctified through the washing and received the hope of future incorruption, on account of which it is rightly called God's temple[^106], this is said not only because of present sanctification but because of that hope of which it is said, *We ourselves, having the firstfruits of the spirit, groan, awaiting adoption, the redemption of our body*[^107]. If, therefore, according to the Apostle, the redemption of our body is awaited, what is awaited is certainly still hoped for, not yet possessed”[^108]. These words of mine contain nothing that a baptized person does not recognize in himself when he says with the Apostle, *We ourselves groan within ourselves*. Hence he says elsewhere, *For we who are in this dwelling groan, burdened*[^109]. To this belongs also what we read in the book of *Wisdom*: *The corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling burdens the mind that thinks many things*[^110]. But you, as though already dwelling immortally among the angels in heaven, ridicule words of weakness and mortality. Expounding them according to your deceit rather than my meaning, you assert that I said “grace does not make a person perfectly new.” I do not say this; attend to what I say. Grace makes a person perfectly new, since it also leads to bodily immortality and complete happiness. Even now it perfectly renews a person as regards deliverance from absolutely all sins, not as regards deliverance from all evils and all the corruption of mortality by which the body now burdens the soul. Hence the groaning that the Apostle confesses as his own too, saying, *We ourselves groan within ourselves.* Yet even that perfection we hope for is reached through the same baptism received here. Not all children of the world are children of the devil, though all children of the devil are children of the world. For there are also children of God who are nevertheless still children of the world, and therefore also joined in marriage. But they do not generate children of God by the flesh, because they themselves, to become children of God, were born not of the flesh, *not of blood, not of the will of a man, nor of the will of the flesh, but of God*[^111]. Therefore sanctification through baptism is bestowed even now upon the body, yet its corruption, which burdens the soul itself, is not now removed. Thus although bodies are chaste when their members do not serve sinful desires, on account of which they have begun to belong to God's temple, there is still something for grace to perfect in this whole *building, as long as the flesh desires against the spirit*, so that it incites evil movements needing restraint, *and the spirit against the flesh*[^112], so that holiness may persevere.

**14. 41.** Who does not know what you, outstanding teacher, impress upon us: “The flesh is said to desire because the soul desires carnally”? No concupiscence of the flesh can exist without the soul. Desiring certainly belongs to a living and sentient nature, so that there is also concupiscence for the chastity of eunuchs to restrain. Their chastity is indeed less laborious, because lust rises less strongly against it where it finds no material with which to work. Yet it exists and is chastely suppressed, lest the very attempt at intercourse, though fruitless, result in the shamefulness for which we know Calligonus, the younger Valentinian's eunuch, was punished by the avenging sword after being convicted by a prostitute's confession. Nor would the book of Ecclesiasticus draw a comparison from this and say, *Seeing with his eyes and groaning, like a eunuch embracing a virgin and sighing*[^113], unless even they were moved by the affections of carnal concupiscence, though deprived of the flesh's effects. Thus with its movements according to the spirit the soul opposes its other movements according to the flesh; and conversely, with its movements according to the flesh it opposes its other movements according to the spirit. Therefore *the flesh is said to desire against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh.* But for this reason it is also said of the soul itself, *It is renewed day by* *day*[^114]. Surely it progresses in holiness when it increasingly diminishes the carnal desires to which it does not consent. For the Apostle said to people already baptized, *Put to death your members that are upon the earth*[^115]. There he names fornication, evil concupiscence, and avarice. How, then, does someone already baptized put to death fornication, which he no longer commits, and according to you “has nothing left to put to death”? How, I ask, does he obey the Apostle's saying, *Put fornication to death*, except by fighting its desires, to which he does not consent? In those making good progress, who commit no fornication either by consent or deed, these desires, though not absent, diminish daily. This takes place in God's temple when, with God's help, what God commanded is done. The spirit's works are raised up; the flesh's works are put to death. *For if you live according to the flesh*, he says, *you will die; but if by the spirit you put the deeds of the flesh to death, you will live*[^116]. And so that they might know they do this only by God's grace, he immediately added, *For all who are led by God's Spirit are children of God*[^117]. Accordingly, all who are led by God's Spirit themselves put the deeds of the flesh to death by the spirit.

**14. 42.** The baptized therefore still have something to do within themselves, that is, in God's temple, which is being built in this time to be dedicated at the end. It is built after captivity, as the title of the psalm indicates, the enemy who had taken it captive having been cast out. In the order of the Psalms, surprisingly, the psalm of the house's dedication comes first and that of its building later. The dedication comes first because it sings of that house of which its architect said, *Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up*[^118]. The later one, however, when the house was being built after captivity, prophesied the Church. Indeed, it begins thus: *Sing to the Lord* *a new song; sing to the Lord, all the earth*[^119]. Let no one, then, be so foolish as to consider each baptized person already perfect because it is said, *God's temple is holy, which you are*[^120], and, *Do you not know that your bodies are the temple within you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God?*[^121], and elsewhere, *For we are the temple of the living God*[^122], and other such statements. It is already called this, and as it is being built, our members upon the earth are put to death here. Although, already dead to sin, we live to God, there is still something in ourselves to put to death, so that sin may not reign in our mortal body, making us obey its concupiscences[^123]. Full and perfect forgiveness of sins released us from them so that we would not be liable to them, yet they remained within us for the chaste to wage war against. Among them is that which a chaste married person uses well; but when this is done well, good is born from evil, not without evil, and therefore is reborn to be delivered from evil. For what God creates and a human being generates is certainly good insofar as it is human. Yet it is not without evil because regeneration alone releases from the evil that generation contracts from the first great sin.

**14. 43.** You want it to seem incredible that “in the womb of a baptized woman, whose body is God's temple, a human being is formed who will be under the devil unless reborn to God from God,” as though it were not more marvelous that God works even where he does not dwell. For he does not dwell in a body subject to sins[^124], yet he forms a human being in a prostitute's womb. He reaches everywhere because of his purity, and nothing defiled enters him[^125]. And far more marvelous, he sometimes adopts as a son one whom he forms in the womb of a most unclean woman, and sometimes does not will as his son one whom he forms in his daughter's womb. For the former reaches baptism by some provision I know not, while the latter through sudden death does not. Thus God, in whose power all things are, brings into Christ's fellowship one whom he formed in the devil's dwelling, and does not will in his kingdom one whom he formed in his temple. Or if he wills it, why does he not do what he wills? You cannot say here what you customarily say about adults: God wills it and the little child does not. Surely here, where there is no immutability of fate, no randomness of fortune, no dignity of person, what remains except the depth of mercy and truth? Thus let us know and comprehend even from this incomprehensibility that, according to the two men, one through whom sin entered the world, the other who takes away the world's sin, all children of carnal concupiscence, wherever born, belong by merit to the heavy yoke of Adam's children; and all among them who are children of spiritual grace, wherever born, come without merit to the easy yoke of God's children. Accordingly, a child bears his own condition when formed within another's body, which is God's temple, so that he is not himself God's temple merely because he is built within God's temple. That his mother's body is God's temple is a benefit of grace, not of nature; this grace is bestowed not by conception but by regeneration. For if what is conceived in a mother belonged to her body so as to be considered a part of it, an infant would not be baptized if his mother had been baptized under some imminent danger of death while carrying him in her womb. As it is, when he too is baptized, he certainly will not be considered baptized twice. He therefore did not belong to his mother's body when in her womb; yet within God's temple something not God's temple was being created. Thus an unbeliever was created in a believing woman, and his parents transmitted to him unbelief that they did not have when he was born from them but had when they themselves were similarly born. They therefore transmitted what was no longer in them because of the spiritual seed by which they were regenerated, but was in their carnal seed by which they generated him.

**14. 44.** Although the body too is sanctified by holy baptism, it is sanctified so that through the forgiveness of sins it is no longer liable not only to all past sins but even to the carnal concupiscence within it. Every human being must be born subject to it, and will die subject to it too unless reborn. Where, then, have you heard or read me saying that “people are not renewed through baptism, but as though renewed; not delivered, but as though delivered; not saved, but as though saved”? Far be it from me to call empty the grace of that washing in which I was reborn of water and the Spirit; by which I was freed from the guilt of all sins, whether contracted at birth or acquired through evil living; by which I am freed so that I know not to enter temptation, drawn away and enticed by my own concupiscence, and so that I am heard when saying with my companions, *Forgive us our debts*[^126]; by which I shall be delivered, as I hope, for eternity, where no law in my members will fight against the law of my mind[^127]. Therefore I do not make God's grace empty; rather you, its enemy, seem to have sought empty boasting so as to introduce into your discussion Epicurus, *who denied that the gods have a body, but as it were a body, or blood, but as it were blood*[^128], and on this occasion rave from philosophers' writings irrelevant to our question, the more ineptly the more learnedly you seem to speak. Which of us says that “whatever is done in the present age is blameworthy,” when Christ himself did so many good things here, though in order to rescue us from this present evil age?

**15. 45.** It is worth examining how carefully and appropriately you explain the Apostle's testimony, where he says, *We have been saved in hope*, and the words including *the redemption of our body*[^129]. For you say that “that resurrection does not forgive anyone's sins but sifts each person's merits. For it renders to each,” you say, “according to his works,” yet you do not say according to which works it renders God's kingdom to little children. Certainly no sins are forgiven in the kingdom; but if none were forgiven at that last judgment, I think the Lord would not have said of a certain sin, *It will not be forgiven either in this age or in the age to come*[^130]. That robber hoped for forgiveness then when he said, *Remember me when you come into your kingdom*[^131]. Since this is a most profound question, judgment should not now be rushed. Why does God forgive no sins to his children in the kingdom, except because he finds nothing to forgive? For sins cannot exist where the spirit, I do not say does not consent to carnal concupiscence, but does not even desire against the flesh, because neither does the flesh against it. That ineffable health is perfected there which is not now accomplished in baptism, where all sins are indeed forgiven but the evils of carnal concupiscences remain. Against them, after baptism, if they are making progress, married people engage in glorious struggles and the continent in more glorious ones, as you yourself confess[^132]. But through some wretchedness I cannot understand, when you speak for the truth you do not hear yourself.

**15. 46.** Describing that supreme happiness of the resurrection, you say that “none of the righteous will then bruise his body and subject it to slavery; none will humble his soul through hard beds and the body's filth.” Answer, then, why someone freed of all evil in baptism does these things here. Why does he dare to bruise God's temple? Are not his members certainly the members of God's temple? Why, then, does he invite God's presence, obtain his mercy, or appease his anger not through a sweet fragrance but through the filth of his temple? Is he chastising, taming, conquering, and suppressing no evil in God's temple, even through the bruising and filth of God's temple itself? Do you not attend, do you not see, that one who so violently persecutes his own body, if he persecutes in it nothing displeasing to God, greatly insults God by persecuting his temple in vain? Why evade, why hesitate to confess more openly? It is certainly that, precisely that, which this man whose bruising and filth you commend persecutes in his flesh, concerning which the Apostle said, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^133]. Why deny this is a baptized person's voice when you recognize the works of this voice in bruising the body and dirtying the members? For the saints do not endure these things by suffering under God's scourges or enemies, but inflict them on themselves through continence. Why, unless by desiring in spirit against carnal concupiscence? You yourself certainly recognized this. For when, describing the happiness of the future life, you added, “No one will subject blessed shamelessness to insults, his cheeks to blows, or his back to lashes; nor will he strive to forge strength from weakness; nor will frugality struggle with want or magnanimity with griefs,” why did you refuse to say, “Nor chastity with carnal concupiscence”? Instead you hastened to conclude the thought, saying, “Nor patience with pain,” recalling nothing except what rushes in from outside and is borne with fortitude, rather than what stirs within and is suppressed by chastity. Or perhaps you accuse us of dullness for not understanding that you had already signified this above when speaking of bruising the body and the toil and filth of the members? For when a brave person is afflicted not by an enemy but by himself, the enemy to be conquered is within himself.

**15. 47.** Remember that you have not explained why the Apostle said *awaiting adoption*[^134], though he had already been adopted in the washing of regeneration. Again you say, “No one hates his own flesh.” Who denies it? Yet you assert that it must be crushed by strict discipline. Again, therefore, you speak for the truth and refuse to hear yourself. Why is the flesh crushed by the faithful if nothing remained in baptism to desire against the spirit? Why, I ask, does God's temple crush itself if nothing in it resists God's Spirit? It would not only exist within but also gravely harm us unless the guilt by which it bound us had been dissolved through forgiveness of sins. Thus it is dissolved through pardon because it held us in guilt; it is crushed through continence lest it win in the struggle. The aim is to prevent its harm until it is so healed that it ceases to exist. Accordingly, all sins are forgiven in baptism, both those contracted at origin and those added ignorantly or knowingly. But when the Apostle James says, *Each is tempted, drawn away and enticed by his own concupiscence; then concupiscence, when it has conceived, gives birth to sin*[^135], these words certainly distinguish the offspring from its parent. Concupiscence is the parent, sin the offspring. But concupiscence does not give birth unless it conceives, nor conceives unless it entices, that is, gains the willing person's assent to perpetrate evil. Therefore the fight against it aims to prevent its conceiving and bearing sin. Accordingly, with all sins—that is, all concupiscence's offspring—forgiven in baptism, if concupiscence itself was also destroyed there, how do saints fight against it lest it conceive, through “bruising the body, dirtying the members, crushing the flesh,” to use your words? How, I ask, do saints wage war against it by bruising, dirtying, and crushing God's temple if it too was removed by baptism? It therefore remains, and we did not lose it in the washing of regeneration, unless we lost there the sense by which we perceive that it remains.

**15. 48.** Who could be so imprudent and shameless, so insolent, obstinate, and stubborn, finally so insane and senseless, as to confess sins are evil yet deny that concupiscence for sins is evil, even if the spirit's desire against it prevents it from conceiving and bearing sins? How would so great an evil, merely by being present, fail to hold us in death and drag us into the final death, unless its bond too were dissolved in the forgiveness of all sins that takes place in baptism? Because of this bond contracted from the first Adam and dissolved only in the second Adam—because, I say, of this bond of death—infants are found dead, not by the familiar death that separates soul from body, but by that death in which all were held for whom Christ died. *For we know*, says the Apostle—and we must repeat this often—*that one died for all; therefore all died. And he died for all, so that those who live may no longer live for themselves, but for him who died for all and rose again*[^136]. Those, then, live for whom the living one died so that they might live. To say it more clearly: those are free from death's bond for whom he who was free among the dead died[^137]. Or, more clearly still: those are delivered from sin for whom he who had never been in sin died. Although he died once, he nevertheless dies for each person when that person, whatever his age, is baptized into his death. That is, the death of him who was without sin will then benefit him when, baptized into his death, he who had been dead in sin has himself died to sin.

**16. 49.** You bring forward the Apostle's testimony: *Do not be deceived: neither fornicators nor idolaters*[^138], and the others he lists, concluding that *they will not possess God's kingdom*. But those who do such things consent to the movements of the concupiscence you praise toward every evil and shameful act. Therefore when he says, *And these things you indeed were, but you were washed, but you were sanctified*[^139], he says they were changed for the better, not so as to be without concupiscence, which is impossible in this life, but so as not to obey it, which is possible in a good life, and to recognize that they were freed from the bond by which they were subject to it, which is impossible except through regeneration. But you, thinking that “if concupiscence were evil, one who is baptized would be without it,” greatly err. He is without every sin, not every evil. More plainly: he is without all guilt for all evils, not without all evils. Is he without bodily corruption? Is what burdens the soul not evil? Was the one who said, *The corruptible body weighs down the soul*[^140], mistaken? Is he without the evil of ignorance, through which countless evils are committed by those who do not know? Is it a small evil through which a person does not perceive what belongs to God's Spirit? Surely the Apostle was speaking of the baptized when he said, *The natural human being does not perceive what belongs to God's Spirit, for it is foolishness to him, and he cannot know it, because it is judged spiritually*[^141]. A little later he says, *And I*, *brothers, could not speak to you as spiritual but as carnal. As to little children in Christ I gave you milk to drink, not food, for you were not yet able; indeed you are still not able, for you are still carnal. For while jealousy and contention are among you, are you not carnal and walking according to the human being?*[^142] See what evils he mentions arising from the evil of ignorance. I think he was not saying this of catechumens. For how would they be little children in Christ unless already reborn? If you still do not believe, attend to what he says to them shortly afterward: *Do you not know that you are God's temple and God's Spirit dwells in you?*[^143] Will you still doubt or deny that they could not be God's temple, in whom God's Spirit dwelt, unless baptized? At least consider what he said to them: *Were you baptized in Paul's name?*[^144] They therefore had not lost so great an evil of ignorance in the washing of regeneration, where nevertheless they had undoubtedly lost all sins. Through this evil of ignorance, the things of God's Spirit were foolishness to God's temple, in which God's Spirit dwelt. But if they progressed day by day and walked in what they had attained, this evil would certainly diminish as sound teaching advanced. Let us even believe it can not only diminish but be wholly consumed in this life: yet after baptism, surely not in baptism. But who doubts that concupiscence can be diminished in this life but not consumed?

**16. 50.** Thus all past guilt for these evils is washed away in the holy font. They are therefore forgiven in those reborn and diminished in those making progress. Ignorance diminishes as truth shines ever more brightly; concupiscence diminishes as charity burns ever more fervently. Neither of these goods comes from ourselves. For *we have received not* *the spirit of this* *world, but the Spirit who is from God, so that we may know what God has given us*[^145]. Concupiscence is worse than ignorance in this respect: ignorance without concupiscence sins less, while concupiscence without ignorance sins more gravely. Not knowing evil is not always evil; desiring evil is always evil. Even good itself is sometimes profitably unknown so that it may be known at the right time, but it is altogether impossible to desire human good through carnal concupiscence. Even offspring is desired not through bodily lust but through the mind's will, although it is not sown without bodily lust. We are discussing the concupiscence by which *the flesh desires against the spirit*, not the good desire by which *the spirit desires against the flesh*[^146], and by which continence is desired so that concupiscence may be conquered. Therefore no human good is ever desired by this carnal concupiscence, if fleshly pleasure is not a human good. But if, as you indicated somewhere, “Dinomachus's school pleases you, joining honor and pleasure”[^147]—what even those worldly philosophers who seemed more honorable called a Scylla-like good, composed of human and beastly nature—if, then, you follow this monster in your opinion, it suffices for us that you confess one pleasure unlawful and another lawful. Thus concupiscence is evil because it indiscriminately seeks both, unless restrained from unlawful pleasure by lawful pleasure. This evil is not put away in baptism; but in the baptized, already freed from its bond through the grace of regeneration, it is healthfully conquered lest it drag them toward unlawful things. Its complete absence from the living, painless body at the resurrection is the reward of those who faithfully fought it and, their sickness healed, will be clothed with most blessed immortality. But in those who will not rise to life it will be absent not happily but penally: not because anyone will then be cleansed of it, but because in the wicked it will then not be stirred to pleasures but turned into torments.

**17. 51.** Now let us examine that most excellent acuteness of yours by which you saw how to refute my statement that “carnal concupiscence is forgiven in baptism, not so that it no longer exists but so that it is not imputed as sin; although its guilt has already been dissolved, it nevertheless remains”[^148]. Against these words you argue, most acute man, as though I had said that concupiscence itself is freed from guilt through baptism, because I said “its guilt has already been dissolved,” as though “its” meant the guilt of which it itself is guilty and, once this is dissolved, it remains absolved. Had I meant this, I would certainly say not that it is evil but that it was evil. Thus, according to your marvelous understanding, when you hear that the guilt of homicide has been dissolved in someone, you suppose not the person but the homicide itself has been absolved of guilt. Who would understand it this way except one unashamed to praise what he is compelled to fight? And how you boast and exult in refuting this opinion, plainly not mine but yours! You say the sort of things that should be said against those who affirm that carnal concupiscence is sanctified and made faithful through baptism in those in whom it nevertheless remains after regeneration. But this more appropriately belongs to you who proclaim it good, so that, as you say of infants, “the good of sanctification may be added to its natural good,” and carnal concupiscence may become God's holy daughter. We, however, say it is evil and nevertheless remains in the baptized, though its guilt has been forgiven and annulled—not that by which it was itself guilty, since it is no person, but that by which it made the human being guilty at origin. Far be it from us to call sanctified what the regenerated, if they have not received God's grace in vain, must fight as an enemy in an inward war, desiring and praying to be healed of that plague.

**17. 52.** For if you say no evils remain in the baptized lest the evils themselves be thought baptized and sanctified, see what absurdity follows you. If whatever is in a person when baptized must be considered baptized and sanctified, you will say that even what is in the intestines and bladder, to be expelled through bodily digestion, is baptized and sanctified in him. You will say a human being in his mother's womb is baptized and sanctified if necessity compels the pregnant woman to receive this sacrament, and therefore the child once born should not be baptized. Finally, you will say that fevers themselves are baptized and sanctified when the sick are baptized, and thus even the devil's works are baptized and sanctified, as if that woman whom Satan had bound in illness for eighteen years were baptized before being healed[^149]. What shall I say of the evils of the mind itself? Consider how great an evil it is to think the things of God's Spirit foolishness; yet such were those whom the Apostle fed with milk, not solid food. Will you say that even so great an evil as that foolishness, because it had not been removed by baptism, was baptized and sanctified? So too concupiscence, which remains to be fought and healed although absolutely all sins have been forgiven in baptism, is not only not sanctified but rather deprived of its power to hold the sanctified liable to eternal death. For even those fed with milk rather than solid food, who were still natural and did not perceive the things of God's Spirit because these were still foolishness to them, if they had departed from the body at that age of mind, not of flesh, in which as new human beings they were called little children in Christ, would have been held by no guilt of that foolishness. Through regeneration this benefit had been bestowed on them: the guilt of all evils, which they were to shed afterward through death or progress, was immediately dissolved through the forgiveness of all sins, though not yet through the healing of all diseases. Yet this guilt must hold one generated according to the flesh, because it is forgiven only to one regenerated according to the spirit. Through the one mediator between God and human beings the human race is delivered from the death of most just condemnation, not merely from bodily death but from the death by which those were dead for whom the one died. And since he died for all, therefore all were dead.

**18. 53.** As for your pleasure in discussing the differences of qualities at such length because I once named quality, saying, “Concupiscence remains not substantially, like some body or spirit, but as a certain affection of an evil quality, such as sickness”[^150], do you not realize how irrelevant it is? First you say that “I changed my opinion and, forgetting my whole book, declared lust to be a substance.” In fact, after examining and analyzing my book you will not find that I called lust a substance even once. Certain philosophers called it a vicious part of the mind, and certainly a part of the mind is a substance because the mind itself is a substance. But I call lust the very defect by which the mind or any part of it is vicious in this way, so that when every defect is healed the whole substance is preserved. For those philosophers too seem to me to have called a vicious part of the mind lust figuratively, the part in which the defect called lust exists, as a house is named for those in the house.

**18. 54.** Next, by practicing too incautiously with the dialecticians' excessively sharp weapons and threatening us too boastfully, you mortally wounded your own sect. Dividing, defining, and even describing the differences of qualities, you say among other things: “The third species of quality is affection and affective quality. Affection is placed under quality because it is the beginning of qualities; to it are also assigned the passions of mind or body that come and go momentarily. Affective quality, however, arising from greater causes, adheres so firmly to all whom it befalls that it can be separated only with great efforts or not at all.” You have explained this sufficiently for those who know, but since readers of our books unfamiliar with this discipline must not be despised, I shall provide what I see lacking by illustrating it with examples. As regards the mind, being afraid is an affection, being timid an affective quality; likewise being angry differs from being irascible, being drunk from being a drunkard: the former are affections, the latter affective qualities. As regards the body, growing pale differs from being pale-complexioned, blushing from being ruddy, and other such things, most of which lack customary names. Since, therefore, you have said that “an affective quality arises from greater causes and adheres so firmly that it can be separated only with great efforts or not at all,” when on its account the mind, or rather the human being, is called evil, do you not fear that a good will either cannot exist there or can have no power? Do you not concede that the unhappy man, whoever he is or was, certainly cried against such a quality: *To will is present with me, but I find no way to accomplish the good*[^151]? At least here confess the necessary groan of these words: *Who will deliver me from this body of death? God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^152].

**18. 55.** Therefore, however much you wrap yourself in a dialectical covering before the eyes of the unlearned, manifest truth will strip you bare. I say that the defect by which the flesh desires against the spirit is inborn in a human being from a corrupted origin, like an evil state of health, and that chaste marriages use this evil well when they use it for generation. But in this good use of evil the user is praised, not the evil itself. Nor is the evil itself innocent, but the person who ensures that his evil, which he uses well, cannot harm him. Thus death, though the sinner's punishment, becomes the martyr's merit through the good use of evil. In Christian baptism perfect newness and perfect health are received from those evils of ours that made us guilty, not from those against which we must still struggle lest we become guilty, though these too are in us and are ours, not another's. For after baptism people resist even the certainly evil habit of drunkenness, which they made for themselves rather than contracted at birth, lest it drag them to their customary evils. Yet an evil is being resisted when through continence concupiscence is denied what habit makes it desire. Hence even against this genital concupiscence, inborn in us through original sin, a widow struggles more fiercely than a virgin, and a prostitute wishing to become chaste more fiercely than one always chaste. The more strength habit has given it, the more the will must labor to overcome it. From this human evil and with it a human being is born. This evil is so great in itself, with so powerful a bond toward condemnation and separation from God's kingdom, that even if contracted from regenerated parents, it is dissolved only by regeneration, as in them, and by this sole remedy the ruler of death is expelled from offspring as he was expelled from parents. But the quality of evil does not migrate from substance to substance as from place to place, leaving where it was so that the same quality is elsewhere. Rather, another of the same kind arises through a certain contagion, as often happens to newborn children from their parents' diseased bodies.

**18. 56.** What did you mean when, as you put it, “closing **-**Aristotle's wrestling school, to return from there to the Sacred Scriptures,” you said, “Concupiscence, then, is sensation and is not an evil quality; therefore when concupiscence diminishes, sensation diminishes”? Does not carnal concupiscence diminish more and more daily through the desire for chastity and continence? I would therefore like you to tell me whether someone is not becoming healthier from the disease of fornication when he takes less and less pleasure in fornicating, although he cut off that evil work from himself by a single conversion and never performed it after receiving the washing of regeneration. Likewise, I would like you to say whether one baptized after habitual drunkenness, who never again gets drunk at all, does not become healthier daily from that disease when he desires the whirlpool of drinking less and less than he used to. Therefore sensation is not concupiscence itself, but rather that by which we perceive that our concupiscence is greater or lesser; just as in bodily afflictions pain is not sensation, but sensation is that by which pain is perceived, and disease is not sensation, but sensation is that by which we perceive ourselves to have a disease. Furthermore, if someone immediately becomes good, certainly through a good quality, by renouncing fornication and drunkenness and abstaining from such works, does he not rightly hear, *Behold, you have been made healthy; sin no more*[^153], and rightly receive the names chaste and sober? Then, if through the advance of good desire, by which he conquers the evil desires of fornication and drinking, he becomes what he was not yet at his recent conversion, so that the desires of those sins are stirred less and less in him and he fights those evils with less struggle than before—not through diminished virtues but diminished enemies, not through a failing fight but a growing victory—will you hesitate to pronounce him better? Whence, I ask, except that a good quality has increased and an evil one diminished? Therefore what made him begin to be good has increased, and what made him evil has diminished; and this he did after baptism, not completed in baptism. Thus, although full forgiveness of sins was completed there, a struggle remained, through which progress toward the better could be made, to be vigilantly undertaken and persistently exercised against the crowds of evil desires raging within ourselves. Because of it even the baptized are told, *Put to death your members that are upon the earth*[^154], and, *If by the spirit you put the deeds of the flesh to death, you will live*[^155], and, *Put off the old human being*[^156]. These things are certainly said with great harmony of truth and no reproach to baptism.

**18. 57.** If, then, you are unwilling to be contentious, I think you now see how rightly we understand what you try to explain otherwise: after the Prophet said, *Who forgives all your iniquities*[^157], which certainly occurs through the forgiveness of all sins, he immediately added, *Who heals all your diseases*[^158]. He meant those evils against which the saints' inward wars do not cease until they are healed or diminished more and more as far as possible in this life. For it is not no disease by which the flesh desires against the spirit, even while the virtue of chastity remains unconquered. If it is no disease, let the spirit not desire against it. For the spirit desires precisely to obtain, if possible, the health of not struggling, or at least that of not consenting. This thing we are now discussing, which we feel resisting us within us, is either an alien nature to be separated or our own nature to be healed. If we say an alien nature must be separated, we favor the Manichaeans. Let us therefore confess that our own nature must be healed, so as to avoid Manichaeans and Pelagians alike.

**19. 58.** This “wound inflicted on the human race by the devil compels whatever is born through it to be under the devil, as though he rightly plucked fruit from his own shrub”[^159]. You set out these words from my book for refutation, attacking them as though “I had called the devil the author of human nature and the creator of the very substance of which a human being consists,” as though you could call a bodily wound a substance. But if you think I called the devil the creator of substance because in my comparison I mentioned a shrub—and no one doubts a shrub is a substance—why do you show or pretend yourself so unlearned as to think comparisons from substances must not be applied to things that are not substances? Unless perhaps you will bring a dialectical accusation against the Lord himself because he said, *A good tree produces good fruits, and an evil tree evil fruits*[^160]. Who would call wickedness or goodness, or good or evil works, which he wished understood as those trees' fruits, substances, unless he did not know what he was saying? Yet who would deny that trees and their fruits are substances if he knew what he was saying? We therefore see comparisons drawn from things that are substances applied to things that are not. If, however, the good or evil tree is to be understood not as a person's goodness or wickedness but as the people themselves, in whom as subjects we understand those qualities to exist—goodness in a good person, wickedness in an evil one—so that the substances themselves, the human beings, are the trees, certainly no one but the unlearned calls their fruits, which mean nothing other than works, substances. Yet no one but the unlearned denies that the fruits of all trees, from which these comparisons are taken, are substances. Accordingly, it is lawful to apply a comparison from substance to something that is not substance. For this reason, although the defect the devil inflicted on the human race like a wound is in no way a substance, I nevertheless rightly applied to it a comparison from substance, calling it a shrub and its fruits even those defects with which human beings are born—though you deny it and truth convicts you—and through which they are eternally lost to God's kingdom unless reborn through truth's deliverance.

**19. 59.** I therefore called the devil the corrupter, not the creator, of substance. He subjects to himself what he did not create through what he inflicted, the just God granting him this power; yet he withdraws from God's power neither what is subject to him nor himself. The second birth was instituted precisely because the first birth is condemned. Yet even to this condemned birth God's goodness is shown, so that rational nature is formed from accursed seed. By his most abundant goodness so great a multitude of evil human beings is visibly nourished and sustained through God's hidden work. If the goodness of his activity were withdrawn from forming and developing seeds and giving life to living things, not only would those yet to be generated not come to be, but those already generated would be wholly reduced to nothing. Since only foolish impiety reproaches God that human beings condemnable through a vicious will live by the life he gives who gives life to all, why think it foreign to his works that those condemnable through a corrupted origin are born by the creation of him who is Creator of all? Regenerated through the mediator, they are freed from deserved condemnation, but by gratuitous, not owed, mercy, those whom he chose before the world's foundation through the election of grace, not from works past, present, or future. *Otherwise grace is no longer grace*[^161]. This is most apparent in little children, of whom neither past works can be spoken, since they did not exist, nor present works, since they do nothing, nor future works, when they die at that age.

**19. 60.** I certainly said, “Just as sins remain in guilt though they have passed away in act, so conversely concupiscence can remain in act and pass away in guilt”[^162]. Your error calls this false, but truth proves it true. Unable to refute it, you first try to cast dialectical darkness over the unlearned, saying that “you cannot imagine in what dialectic I found the reciprocity of all contraries.” If I wished to explain your statement and bring it within the understanding of those who have never learned these things, perhaps a whole volume would be needed. For now it suffices that you yourself said, “The reciprocity of all contraries can be found in no dialectic.” You thereby showed that it can exist in some, not all. Among these some I found this too. Had you said that no contraries admit reciprocity, and thus shown that the contraries I set out cannot be reciprocal because none can, I should have had to show that some can, and then that what I set out belongs among them. But you yourself already concede there are some, since you do not say no contraries admit reciprocity, but not all. Therefore some do. It remains to show whether what I set out is among these: whether, just as sins truly remain in guilt though passed away in act, concupiscence truly remains in act though passed away in guilt. Trying to show this impossible, you said what I did not say. I spoke of concupiscence present in the members, fighting against the law of the mind[^163], though its guilt has passed away in the forgiveness of all sins; just as, conversely, a sacrifice made to idols, if not repeated, has passed away in act but remains in guilt unless forgiven through pardon. Sacrificing to idols is such that the work itself passes away as it is done, and after its passing its guilt remains, to be dissolved by pardon. But carnal concupiscence is such that it remains in a human being who struggles against it through continence, though its guilt, contracted through generation, has already been discharged through regeneration. It remains in act, not indeed by drawing away and enticing the mind and through its consent conceiving and bearing sins, but by stirring evil desires that the mind resists. Its very movement is its act, though without the mind's consent the effect is absent. For evil exists in a human being even apart from this act, that is, apart from this movement: from that evil this movement arises, which we call desire. There is not always a desire for us to fight; but if none is present when nothing desirable occurs either to the thinking mind or the body's senses, an evil quality can exist without being stirred by any temptation, as timidity exists in a timid person even when he is not afraid. But when something desirable occurs and evil desires are not stirred even against our will, health is complete. Thus this defect could only hold a human being in guilt, though procreated from chaste spouses through the good use of that same evil. This guilt, even while the evil remains, is dissolved in the forgiveness of all sins through God's grace, by which we are delivered from every evil when the Lord not only forgives all our iniquities but also heals all our diseases. Recall what the deliverer and Savior himself answered those who told him to leave Jerusalem: *Behold*, he said, *I cast out demons and complete healings today and tomorrow, and on the third day I am perfected*[^164]. Read the Gospel and see how much later he suffered and rose. Did he therefore lie? Certainly not. He signified something at issue in this question between us. The expulsion of demons is the forgiveness of sins; the completion of healings is accomplished through progress after baptism; the third perfection, which he also showed in the immortality of his flesh, is the blessedness of incorruptible joys.

**19. 61.** You, however, gave a sacrilegious sacrifice as an example of the matter you were discussing, saying that “whatever belongs to this kind can be shown from this one case, since if someone has once sacrificed to idols, he can be burdened by the impiety committed until he obtains pardon, and guilt remains when the action is finished. But it is altogether impossible,” you say, “for the action to remain and guilt to depart, that is, for him not to stop sacrificing to demons and yet appear free from profanation.” You say this most truly of sacrifice offered to idols: it is an act completed in the deed itself and then no longer existing; if done again, it is another act. But the impiety itself by which these things are done remains until idols are renounced and God believed in. Thus sacrifice to idols is a passing deed, not an abiding vice; but the impiety through which the sacrifice was made, because it remains after the deed is completed, seems like the concupiscence through which adultery was committed. Yet when the error is removed by which that impiety was thought piety, does anyone take pleasure in sacrificing to idols, and is a desire of this kind stirred in him? Thus what you thought to present as similar is in no way similar. A passing sacrifice, I say, is in no way similar to abiding concupiscence. It never ceases to disturb with the goads of unlawful desires, which chastity resists, a person who no longer commits what he used to commit by consenting to it and already holds with full faith and knowledge that such things must not be done. It is not ended by knowledge so as to cease existing, but restrained by continence so as not to reach what it seeks. Therefore, just as sacrifice to idols is no longer in action because it has passed, nor in the will because the error by which it occurred has been destroyed, yet its guilt remains until dissolved through the forgiveness of all sins in the washing of regeneration, so conversely, although evil concupiscence's guilt is dissolved through that same baptism, concupiscence itself remains until healed by a perfecting medicine from him who, after casting out demons, completes healings.

**19. 62.** But tell me, please, since you too concede that the guilt of a sin done and past remains unless washed away in the holy font, what is that guilt, and where does it remain in a person already corrected and living rightly, yet not freed by the forgiveness of sins? Is this guilt a subject, that is, a substance like spirit or body, or is it in a subject, like fever or a wound in the body, or avarice or error in the mind? You will say it is in a subject, for you will not assert guilt is a substance. In what subject, then, do you think it exists? Why ask what you would answer instead of quoting your words? “When the act passes,” you say, “its guilt remains in the conscience of the offender until it is forgiven.” Therefore it is in a subject, namely the mind of someone who remembers his offense and is tormented by a scruple of conscience until forgiveness makes him secure. What if he forgets his offense and his conscience is not goaded: where will that guilt be which you concede remains after the sin passes until forgiven? Certainly not in the body, since it is not among the accidents affecting the body; not in the mind, since forgetfulness has erased its memory. Yet it exists. Where, then, is it, when the person now lives well and commits no such thing? Nor can it be said that the guilt of sins he remembers remains, but that of forgotten sins does not: it all remains until forgiven. Where, then, does it remain except in God's hidden laws, written in a certain way in the minds of angels, so that no iniquity goes unpunished except what the mediator's blood has expiated? By the sign of his Cross baptism's water is consecrated, so that guilt may be washed away by it, as though written in a bond of debt in the knowledge of spiritual powers through whom the punishment of sins is exacted. All born carnally in flesh from flesh are born liable to this bond[^165], to be freed from this debt by the blood of him who was indeed born in flesh and from flesh, yet not carnally but spiritually. For he was born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary: of the Holy Spirit, so that there would be in him no sinful flesh; of the Virgin Mary, so that there would be in him the likeness of sinful flesh. Therefore he came not liable to that bond and releases those liable to it. For it is not no iniquity when, within one person, either higher things shamefully serve lower things or lower things stubbornly resist higher things, even if not allowed to conquer. If a person suffered this iniquity from another person opposing him from outside, since it would not be within him, it would certainly be punished apart from him. But because it is within him, it will either be punished with him, or, once he is freed from its guilt, persist in the struggle against the spirit in such a way as to consign him, no longer guilty, to no torments after death, alienate him from no kingdom of God, and cause him to be held by no condemnation. Nor, that we may be entirely rid of it, is it separated from us as though an alien nature; rather, because it is the sickness of our nature, it is healed within us.

**20. 63.** Because of this defect, then, as I wrote in the book you oppose, “human nature is condemned; and because it is condemned, it is also subjected to the condemnable devil. For the devil himself is an unclean spirit: good insofar as a spirit, evil insofar as unclean, since he is spirit by nature and unclean by defect. Of these two things the former comes from God, the latter from himself. He therefore holds human beings, whether adults or infants, not because they are human but because they are unclean”[^166]. Having quoted these words from my book, you thought you should resist them by saying that “the same pattern observed in the devil should also have been observed in an evil human being, so that no one is condemned except for the defects of his own will, and therefore there is no original sin; otherwise the work of him who created even the devil good cannot be approved.” You fail to notice that God did not create the devil from another devil, nor from some angel, even a good one, who nevertheless had in his members a law fighting against the law of his mind, through which and with which every human being is born from a human being. This argument might perhaps help you if the devil, like a human being, begot children and we denied that they were liable to their father's sin. As it is, one thing is he who was a murderer from the beginning, because from the beginning when humanity was established he killed the human being by seducing the woman, and by free choice did not stand in the truth[^167], and falling cast another down. Another is that *through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death; and it passed into all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^168]. Here original sin common to all human beings, apart from each person's own sins, is clearly expressed.

**20. 64.** As for my statement, “Let anyone who marvels that God's creature is subjected to the devil not marvel, for God's creature is subjected to God's creature, the lesser to the greater”[^169], why did you quote it without adding my following words, in which I showed how I meant “the lesser to the greater,” namely human nature to angelic? Surely to make my meaning less understood and create room to spread, as usual, fog from Aristotle's categories before the unlearned, so that, hidden within it, you might seem to them to be saying something when they did not know what you said. Your heresy has been reduced to this: your followers groan that they cannot find dialecticians as judges in the Church, from the schools of the Peripatetics or Stoics, who could acquit you. What is the relevance, what the meaning, what the purpose of your saying, “Greater and lesser belong to the defined species of quantity. But quantity not only does not admit contraries, which it shares with quality and other categories, but has no contrary, which it shares with the definition of substance; good and evil, however, are contraries”? You would certainly never say this if you thought the readers or hearers of your books would understand what you said. Was an unclean human being therefore not to be subjected to an unclean angel because the quantity by which an angel is greater than a human being not only does not admit contraries but has no contrary? As though the human being should have been subjected to the devil only if found contrary to him, and evil things should not be subjected to evil things because good things appear contrary to evil, not evil to evil! What emptier thought, what more inept statement is possible? Is not a slave subjected to a master, good to good, evil to evil, evil to good, and good to evil? Is not a wife subjected to a husband, a good wife to a good husband, an evil to an evil, an evil to a good, and a good to an evil? What bearing, then, does whether this or that thing can admit or have a contrary have on the force or reason by which something must be subjected to something else? Yet you would certainly not pour out these things so thoughtlessly if you considered wisdom contrary to the foolishness that suggests them to you.

**20. 65.** Furthermore, what sort of argument is this? “If something fittingly ordered belongs to God,” you say, “and something belonging to God is good, it is therefore good to be subjected to the devil, because thereby the order instituted by God is preserved. And it follows that it is evil to rebel against the devil, because resistance disturbs the order instituted by God.” You might say that farmers resist God and disturb his order by clearing fields of the thorns and thistles he commanded to grow as punishment for sinners[^170]. Moreover, by this reasoning of yours one could say: if something fittingly ordered belongs to God and something belonging to God is good, it is good for the wicked to be in hell, because thereby the order instituted by God is preserved. But why add, “It follows that it is evil to rebel against the devil because resistance disturbs God's instituted order”? Who rebels against the devil except someone freed from his power through the mediator's blood? It would therefore have been better not to have an enemy than to defeat one. But because human nature was subjected to the enemy by the desert of sin, a person is first rescued from his power so that he can fight against him. Then, if life in this flesh is prolonged, he is helped in the struggle to overcome. Finally, victorious, he is blessed so that he may reign, saying at the end, *Where, death, is your contention?*[^171] Or, as the Apostle says, *Where, death, is your victory? Where, death, is your sting?*[^172]

**21. 66.** You even thought some passages from Mani's books should be quoted to compare with my opinion, although I not only detest and condemn in faith and word the mingling of two natures, good and evil, from which all their fabulous madness flows, but also expose it by resisting and refuting you yourself as their supporter. For while truth cries against them that evils arose only from goods, do you not cry against the truth for them and with them: “The devil's work is not allowed to pass through God's work. A root of evil cannot be placed in God's gift. The order of things does not allow evil to be produced from good, or anything unjust from the just. Sins are not born from what is free from sin. From this work that has no fault, no fault can be born”[^173]? All these statements of yours establish that evils did not arise from goods, leaving, according to the Manichaeans, that evils arose only from evils. How, then, do you accuse anyone under the name of Manichaeans, as though their adversary, when you stand so firmly on their side that they cannot possibly be defeated unless you too are defeated with them? Let it suffice that we treated this rather fully in the first book of this work, more briefly in the fifth, and thus far here.

**21. 67.** How greatly the heresy common to you all helps the Manichaeans I have often shown, but must not omit even here. The Manichaeans enumerate the evils they point to in little children, which Cicero too recalls in his books *The Republic*, from which I have already quoted his very words[^174]. Recalling these evils he says that “the human being has been cast into these miseries by nature not as a mother but as a stepmother.” To these are added the many and varied evils that we see not all little children, but very many, suffer, even assaults of demons. They conclude: “Since God is just and almighty, whence does his image suffer these evils in little children, unless our assertion of the mingling of two natures, good and evil, is true?” Catholic truth refutes them by confessing original sin, through which the human race became the plaything of demons and the offspring of mortals was destined for laborious misery. This would not be so if human nature through free choice had persisted in the state in which it was first created. But you, denying original sin, will certainly be compelled either to call God weak or unjust, under whose power his image in little children is afflicted with such evils without any desert of personal or original sin—for their virtue is not exercised through them, as is rightly said of good adults who have the use of reason—or, since you cannot call God powerless or unjust, the Manichaeans will establish against you their wicked error about the intermingling of two mutually hostile substances. Therefore it is not that “no fuller's herb cleanses me from Manichaean infection,” as you say. With these insolent words you insult the washing of regeneration that I received in the bosom of the Catholic mother. Rather, so malicious a poison of the ancient dragon has crept into you that you both defame Catholics with the horror of the Manichaean name and help Manichaeans through the perversity of your doctrine.

**22. 68.** As for the passage from another book of mine, where, writing to Marcellinus, I said, “The children of the woman who believed the serpent so as to be corrupted by lust are delivered only through the Son of the Virgin who believed the angel so as to conceive without lust”[^175], you quoted it and wanted it understood as though I had said that “the serpent had bodily intercourse with Eve,” as the Manichaeans rave that the prince of darkness, that woman's father, had intercourse with her. I did not say this of the serpent. But do you deny, against the Apostle, that the woman's mind was corrupted by the serpent? Do you not hear him say, *I fear that, as the serpent deceived Eve by his cunning, your minds too may be corrupted from the simplicity and chastity that is in Christ*[^176]? Surely from this corruption by the serpent, such as occurs also when evil conversations corrupt good morals[^177], the lust to sin entered the woman's mind. Thus, after her husband too was corrupted through transgression, there followed in the flesh that lust of which they were ashamed and for which they covered their private parts, not through the addition of the devil's bodily intercourse but through the withdrawal of God's spiritual grace.

**22. 69.** You therefore have not “crushed through your whole discussion,” as you boast, “my assertion concerning the evil of carnal concupiscence and original sin,” while the praise of marriage remains, since it uses well an evil it did not produce but found. Nor have you crushed the Manichaeans themselves, whom you have rather helped, you especially, and in common all sharers in the Pelagian novelty and error, as I have already shown. Regarding the testimonies of the Catholic commentators, holy Basil of Caesarea and holy John of Constantinople, whose meaning you said agreed with your opinions, I answered you in the first book of this work with most sufficient and certain truth[^178], showing how, by not understanding some of their words, you fight with astonishing blindness against their doctrine, which is Catholic doctrine. In the second book too we sufficiently demonstrated that this is not, as you insultingly say, “a conspiracy of the lost”[^179], but the pious and faithful agreement of the holy and learned fathers of the Catholic Church, who resist your heretical novelty on behalf of the most ancient Catholic truth. Thus even “the people's murmur,” which you say is all we oppose to you, is not alone, because it rests on the authority of so many great teachers; and it is just, because the people refuse to let you overturn the salvation of little children in Christ, which they too know most well.

**23. 70.** As for the Apostle's words, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^180], and what follows as far as *Unhappy man that I am, who will deliver me from this body of death?*[^181], you assert that “I understand them differently from how the whole passage should be understood,” unknowingly giving me far too much credit. For I am neither the only nor the first to understand this passage, which overturns your heresy, as it truly should be understood. Indeed, I previously understood it otherwise, or rather did not understand it, as certain writings of mine from that time testify[^182]. It did not seem to me that the Apostle could say of himself, *But I am carnal*[^183], since he was spiritual, or that he was being led captive under the law of sin in his members. I thought these things could be said only of those so subjected to carnal concupiscence that they did whatever it compelled, which it would be madness to believe of the Apostle, since even a countless multitude of saints desire in spirit against the flesh so as not to fulfill its concupiscences. But afterward I yielded to better and more understanding men, or rather, as must be confessed, to truth itself, so that I saw in those apostolic words the groaning of saints struggling against carnal concupiscences. Though spiritual in mind, they are rightly understood to be still carnal in this corruptible body that weighs down the soul[^184], because they will be spiritual in body too *when, sown a natural body, it rises a spiritual body*[^185]. They are also rightly understood to be still captive under sin's law in that part subject to movements of desires to which they do not consent. Thus I came to understand these things as Hilary, Gregory, Ambrose, and the other holy and renowned teachers of the Church understood them: they held that the Apostle himself vigorously struggled against carnal concupiscences that he did not want to have yet had, and that in those words he testified to this very struggle of his[^186]. Against these movements, which must first be defeated lest they rule and then healed so that they are utterly extinguished, you too confessed that saints engage in glorious struggles[^187]. Let us therefore recognize together the words of those fighting, if we fight. For thus it is not we who live, but Christ lives in us, if for both fighting concupiscences and gaining victory through the destruction of those enemies we trust in him, not ourselves. For he *has become for us wisdom from God, and righteousness, sanctification, and redemption, so that, as it is written, whoever glories may glory in the Lord*[^188].

**23. 71.** It is therefore not “contradictory,” as you think, “for the one who says, *It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me*[^189], also to say, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^190].” For insofar as Christ lives in him, he fights and overcomes what dwells in his flesh as evil, not good. No one's spirit would rightly desire against his flesh unless Christ's Spirit dwelt in him. Far be it, then, from us to say what you charge us with saying, that “the Apostle spoke thus as though he wished to be understood as dragged unwillingly to prostitutes by some hand of deadly pleasure,” when he says, *It is not I who do it*[^191], showing that carnal concupiscences produce only lust's impulse, without consent to sin.

**23. 72.** Why do you vainly try to “transfer these words to the pride of the Jews, as though the Apostle represented in himself those who despised Christ's gifts as unnecessary to them”? Such is your supposition. If only you understood Christ's gifts at least enough to believe they have some power to conquer concupiscence! But you say the Jews despised them “because he forgave sins that they had avoided through the law's admonition.” As though forgiveness of sins conferred on a person that the flesh no longer desires against the spirit[^192], whence came the words, *I know that good does not dwell in me, that is, in my flesh*[^193], and other such statements. But you do not depart from your doctrine, by which you think God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord is so confined to the forgiveness of sins that it does not help us avoid sins and conquer carnal desires by pouring charity into our hearts through the Holy Spirit whom he has given us[^194]. Nor do you consider that the one who says, *I see another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind*[^195], and cries that he is delivered from this evil only by God's grace through Jesus Christ our Lord, is neither a Jew nor struggling because he has sinned, but rather lest he sin.

**23. 73.** “The Apostle emphasizes the power of habit,” you say. Answer, then, whether a baptized person does not fight against that power. If you deny it, you contradict every Christian understanding; if he fights, why do you not recognize in the Apostle's words the voice of a fighter? “Through the good law and holy commandment,” you say, “the minds of the wicked had become fiercer, because without one's own will no instruction could inspire virtue.” What an acute interpreter! What an outstanding expositor of divine utterances! What do you do with the words of one saying, *I do not do what I will*[^196], and, *To will is present with me*[^197], and, *What I do not will, I do*[^198], and, *I delight in God's law according to the inner human being*[^199]? You hear this and say virtue was absent because will was absent. What of the fact that not only will but virtue too was present, preventing consent to carnal concupiscence, which served the law of sin through those very wicked movements? Not yielding to them or presenting his members to sin as weapons of iniquity[^200], yet feeling what he did not will in the flesh desiring against the spirit, and in turn desiring in spirit against it, he said with the truest voice of chastity, *Therefore I myself serve God's law with the mind, but sin's law with the flesh*[^201]. As for the words you quoted, where the Apostle says, *The law is holy, and the commandment holy, just, and good. Did what is good, then, become death to me? Certainly not; but sin, so that it might appear sin, worked death in me through the good, so that sin might become exceedingly sinful through the commandment*[^202], they are rightly understood as spoken of his past life, when he was under the law, not yet under grace. For he uses past-tense words: *I did not know sin except through the law*[^203], and, *I did not know concupiscence*[^204], and, *It worked every concupiscence in me*[^205], and, *I once lived without the law*[^206], certainly when he could not yet have the use of reason; and, *When the commandment came, sin revived, but I died*[^207], and, *Sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me*[^208], and, *Through the good it worked death in me*[^209]. In all these he is understood to signify the time when he lived under the law and, not yet helped by grace, was conquered by carnal concupiscences. But where he says, *The law is spiritual, but I am carnal*[^210], he shows what he was already suffering in the struggle. He did not say, “I have been” or “I was *carnal*,” but, *I am.* He distinguishes the times even more clearly when he says, *But now it is no longer I who do it, but the sin dwelling in me*[^211]. For he was no longer himself producing the movements of evil desires, to which he did not consent to perpetrate sins. By the name of sin dwelling in him he designated concupiscence itself, because it was produced by sin and, if it draws away and entices a consenting person, conceives and bears sin. The rest too, as far as *Therefore I myself serve God's law with the mind, but sin's law with the flesh*[^212], consists of words of one already established under grace but still fighting his concupiscence, not consenting to it so as to sin, yet suffering desires of sin that he resists.

**23. 74.** None of us accuses the substance of the body; none accuses the nature of the flesh. You defend in vain what we do not blame. We do not deny, however, that evil desires are within us, although, if we live well, we do not consent to them. They must be disciplined, restrained, fought against, and overcome; yet they exist, and they are not foreign to us. These things within us are evils, not goods. Nor will they be separated and exist outside us, as Manichaean folly said; rather, as Catholic truth says, they will be healed and cease to exist.

**24. 75.** As for those apostolic words in which, with astonishing shamelessness, indeed madness, you resist the most firmly established faith, where he says: *Through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^213], you labor in vain to carve out another meaning, new, distorted, and alien to the truth. You assert that "the expression *In whom all sinned* was used as if it meant: Because of which all sinned, just as it was said: *By what does a young man correct his way?*[^214]" Thus, according to you, we should not understand that all human beings sinned in one man originally and, as it were, collectively in the unity of the mass, but that they sinned because the first man sinned, that is, when they imitate him, not when they are generated from him. The expression does not, then, fit this meaning, as though *in whom* meant "because of which." For each person sins because of something he sets before himself in sinning, or something that in any way causes him to sin. But who could be so far removed from all human sense as to say: This man committed murder because Adam ate food from the forbidden tree in paradise, when this robber killed a man without thinking at all about Adam, but in order to steal the gold he was carrying? In the same way, all the other sins each person commits as his own have causes for which they are committed, even though no one thinks about what the first man committed or sets that example before himself in sinning. Therefore not even Cain, who knew that same man as his father, should be said to have sinned for this reason, that is, because Adam sinned. For the reason he killed his brother is known: it was not because of what Adam had done, but because he envied his brother's goodness.

**24. 76.** Indeed, not even the testimonies you quoted support this interpretation of yours. For it is right to understand *By what does a young man correct his way?* as "For what reason does he correct it?" since there follows: *By keeping your words.* He corrects his way for this reason: he thinks about God's words as they ought to be thought about, and by thinking keeps them, and by keeping them lives rightly. This, then, is his reason for correcting his way: that he keeps God's words. And what the most blessed Stephen says, *Moses fled at this word*[^215], is rightly understood as "because of this word," because he heard it, feared it, and thought about it so as to flee; this was his reason for fleeing. Is anything said in these expressions that concerns an imitation in which one person imitates another without thinking about him at all? In that case it cannot possibly be said that he sinned because the other sinned, when he neither existed originally in that other nor thought anything about him in his own sin.

**24. 77.** You say: "But if he was speaking of the transmission of sin, nowhere could he have said more appropriately: Sin passed through because all were generated from the pleasure of spouses; and he would have added: It passes inasmuch as they flowed from the corrupted flesh of the first man." You do not see that the same kind of reply can be made to you: If the Apostle was speaking of the imitation of sin, nowhere could he have said more appropriately: Sin passed through because the first man's example came before; and he would have added: It passes inasmuch as all sinned by imitating that one man. For the Apostle would have spoken here in one of these two ways if he were speaking at your discretion or mine. Since, then, he said neither what you proposed nor what I proposed, do you want us to believe that in these words of his we should understand neither original sin according to the Catholics nor imitated sin according to the Pelagians? I think you do not. Set aside, then, these things that can be said on either side with equal force. If you are willing to consider without contention what the Apostle said, attend to what he was doing when he said it. You will find God's wrath upon the human race through one man, and through one man the reconciliation to God of those who are freely delivered from the condemnation of the whole race. The former is the first Adam, made from earth; the latter is the second Adam, made from a woman. But in the former case flesh was made through the Word; in the latter the Word himself was made flesh, so that through his death we might live, having died by abandoning him. He says: *God commends* *his love toward us, because while we were still sinners Christ died for us. Much more, then, having now been justified in his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him*[^216].

**24. 78.** Of this wrath he says: *We too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^217]. Of this wrath the prophet Jeremiah says: *Cursed be the day on which I was born*[^218]. Of this wrath holy Job says: *Let the day perish on which I was born*[^219]. Of this wrath the same man also says: *A human being born of a woman, short-lived and full of wrath, falls like the flower of grass; he flees like a shadow and will not stand. Have you not taken account even of him and brought him into judgment before you? For who will be clean from filth? Not even one, though his life on earth be but one day*[^220]. Of this wrath the book of Ecclesiasticus says: *All flesh grows old like a garment; for the covenant from the beginning is: You shall surely die*[^221]. And again it says: *From a woman* *came the beginning of sin, and because of her we all die*[^222]. And elsewhere: *Great toil has been created for every human being, and a heavy yoke upon the children of Adam, from the day they come forth from their mother's womb until the day of their burial in the mother of all*[^223]. Of this wrath Ecclesiastes says: *Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. What profit has a human being in all the toil at which he labors under the sun?*[^224] Of this wrath the Apostle's voice says: *All creation has been subjected to vanity*[^225]. Of this wrath the Psalm laments: *Behold, you have made my days old, and my substance is as nothing before you; truly, every living human being is altogether vanity*[^226]. Of this wrath another Psalm also laments: *Their years shall be things accounted as nothing. In the morning let him pass away like grass; in the morning let him flourish and pass away; in the evening let him fall, grow hard, and wither. For we have failed in your wrath, and in your indignation we have been troubled. You have set our iniquities before you, our age in the illumination of your face. For all our days have failed, and in your wrath we have failed. Our years shall be spent in thought like a spider*[^227].

**24. 79.** No one is freed from this wrath of God unless he is reconciled to God through the Mediator. Hence the Mediator himself also says: *Whoever does not believe the Son will not have life, but the wrath of God remains upon him*[^228]. He did not say: It will come, but: *It remains upon him.* Therefore adults believe and confess through their own heart and mouth, and little children through those of others, so that they may be reconciled to God through the death of his Son, lest God's wrath remain upon those whom even their corrupted origin makes guilty. Speaking of this, then, the Apostle says: *While we were still* *sinners, Christ died for us. Much more, then, having now been justified in his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him. For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, having been reconciled, shall we be saved in his life. And not only so, but we also glory in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation. Therefore, just as through one man sin entered this world, and through sin death, and so it passed to all human beings, in whom all sinned*[^229]. Surely it is clear what the Apostle was doing when he said this. Go on, then, and withdraw little children from this reconciliation accomplished through the death of the Son of God, who came into the world without sin, so that, because of the man through whom sin entered the world, God's wrath may remain upon them! What place is there here for imitation, I ask you, when you hear: *For judgment came from one offense unto condemnation, but grace from many offenses unto justification*[^230]? How does grace come from many offenses unto justification, unless because, besides that one original offense, it found many added offenses to wipe away together? Otherwise, condemnation would come from the many offenses that people had committed by imitating one man, just as justification came from those same many offenses, through whose forgiveness they breathed freely in grace. But because that one offense was sufficient to bring condemnation even by itself, whereas it was not sufficient for grace to erase that one alone, but also the many added offenses, so that justification might come through the forgiveness of all, it was therefore said: *Judgment came from one offense unto condemnation, but grace from many offenses unto justification.* For just as little children do not imitate Christ, because they cannot yet do so, and nevertheless can belong to his spiritual grace, so, without imitating the first man, they are nevertheless bound by the contagion of carnal generation from him. But if you want them to be strangers to the first man's sin because they have not imitated him by their own will, by the same reasoning you will make them strangers also to Christ's righteousness, because they have not imitated him by their own will either.

**24. 80.** You are unwilling, however, to understand the *many* he mentioned afterward as the *all* he had mentioned before, thinking that "many was said precisely so that all would not be understood." You could say the same thing about Abraham's seed, to whom all nations were promised [^231]: that not all nations were promised to him, because elsewhere it says: *I have made you the father of many nations*[^232]. But the sound understanding is that Scripture spoke this way because there can be a totality that is not numerous, as when we say all the Gospels, although they are contained in the small number of four. Again, there can be many without being all, as when we say that many believe in Christ, although not all believe. For the Apostle says: *Faith does not belong to all*[^233]. But when it was said: *In your seed all nations will be blessed*, and: *He has made you the father of many nations*, it was shown that the same all are many and the same many are all. So too, when it was said that sin passed through one man to all, and afterward that through the disobedience of one man many were made sinners, those many are the very same as all. Similarly, when it was said: *Through the justification of one man unto all human beings, unto justification of life*, and again: *Through the obedience of one man many will be made righteous*[^234], we ought to understand no exceptions, but the same many as all, not because all human beings are justified in Christ, but because all who are justified can be justified only in Christ. In the same way we can say that everyone enters a certain house through one door, not because all human beings enter that house, but because no one enters except through that door. All, then, come to death through Adam, and all to life through Christ. For *as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive*[^235]. That is, from the first origin of the human race no one comes to death except through Adam, and no one comes through Adam except to death; no one comes to life except through Christ, and no one comes through Christ except to life.

**24. 81.** But in wanting us to understand that not all but many are either condemned through Adam or delivered through Christ, you attack the Christian religion with horrible perversity. For if some will be saved without Christ and some are justified without Christ, then Christ died in vain. For there was also another way, as you maintain, in nature, in free choice, in natural or written law, by which those who wished could be saved and righteous. And who but someone unjust would exclude God's righteous images from God's kingdom? Here perhaps you say: But it is easier through Christ. Could not the same be said even of the law: Righteousness comes through the law, but more easily through Christ? Yet the Apostle says: *If righteousness comes through the law, then Christ died in vain*[^236]. Therefore, apart from the one Mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus [^237], there is no other name under heaven in which we must be saved [^238]. And that is why it was said: *In Christ all will be made alive,* because in him God has established assurance for all by raising him from the dead [^239]. Your teaching, however, by praising a supposedly blameless nature, the power of free choice, and the law, whether natural or given through Moses, tries to persuade us that, even if there is reason to do so, it is nevertheless not necessary to come to Christ for eternal salvation. According to this, through the sacrament of his death and resurrection there is a more convenient way, if you even think that much, but not an exclusive way beyond which no other can exist. Consider, therefore, how greatly Christians ought to detest you, and renounce your own position even if we remain silent.

**25. 82.** You wanted the prophetic testimony to be the final and, as it were, strongest support of your case, where we read in Ezekiel that there will no longer be the proverb in which people said that the parents had eaten sour grapes and the children's teeth were set on edge; neither will the son die in the father's sin nor the father in the son's sin, but the soul that sins will itself die [^240]. You do not understand that this is a promise of the New Testament and of the spiritual inheritance belonging to the world to come. For the Redeemer's grace accomplishes this: it erases the ancestral bond of debt [^241], and each person gives an account for himself. Otherwise, who could count how many testimonies of the divine Scriptures bind children by their parents' sins? For why did Ham sin, and vengeance fall upon his son Canaan [^242]? Why was Solomon's son punished for his father's sin by the reduction of his kingdom [^243]? Why was the punishment for the sins of Ahab, king of Israel, deferred to his descendants [^244]? Why do we read in the holy books: *Repaying the sins of the fathers into the bosom of their children after them*[^245], and: *Repaying the sins of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generation*[^246]? This number can also be understood as signifying all generations. Are these things false? Who would say so except the most open enemy of the divine words? But carnal generation, even among God's people, belonging to the Old Testament, which *generates unto slavery*[^247], binds children by their parents' sins. Spiritual generation, however, has changed the threats and promises of punishments and rewards, just as it has changed the inheritance. Foreseeing this in the Spirit, the prophets said these things, but Jeremiah spoke more clearly: *In those days*, he says, *they will not say: The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth have been set on edge; but each will die in his own sin, and the teeth of whoever eats sour grapes will themselves be set on edge*[^248]. Surely it is clear that this is proclaimed prophetically in the same way as the New Testament itself, which was previously hidden and afterward revealed through Christ. Indeed, lest the passages I have recalled and the very many others like them about repaying parents' sins upon their children, all truthfully written, should trouble us and be thought contrary to this prophecy, he immediately resolved this most troubling question by adding: *Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, and I will establish a new testament with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not according to the testament I arranged with their fathers*[^249]. In this New Testament, then, when the ancestral bond of debt has been erased by the testator's blood, a human being through rebirth begins to be free from the ancestral debts by which he had been bound through birth. The Mediator himself says: *Call no one your father on earth*[^250], precisely because we have found another birth by which we do not succeed our Father, but live forever with our Father.

**26. 83.** Julian, I think you can see, unless you are obstinate, that I have answered everything you said and refuted all the arguments by which, in four books, you thought and argued that original sin should not be believed and that the concupiscence of the flesh cannot be blamed without condemning marriage. For it has been shown that only the person who has changed his inheritance and his father is not bound by the ancient ancestral debt: there, adopted by grace, he finds as his coheir the one who is the only Son by nature. It has also been shown that carnal concupiscence does not bring death after death only to the person who has found in Christ's death a death by which to die to sin and has escaped the death in which he had been born in sin. For one died for all; therefore all were dead [^251]. And he died for all; nor can any live for whom he did not die, he who, though alive, died for the dead. Denying these things, fighting against them, striving to tear down these defenses of the Catholic faith and to break the very sinews of Christian religion and true piety, you dare moreover to say that you have undertaken wars against the impious, when you put on the weapons of impiety against the mother who gave you spiritual birth. You dare to mingle with the host of holy patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs, and priests, although the patriarchs tell you that sacrifices for sins were offered even for newborn children [^252], because not even an infant one day old upon the earth is clean from filth [^253]; although the prophets tell you: *We were conceived in iniquities*[^254]; although the apostles tell you: *All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death*[^255], so that we may reckon ourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus [^256]; although the martyrs tell you that those born carnally according to Adam contract the contagion of the ancient death in their first birth, so that in baptism little children are forgiven not their own sins but another's [^257]; although the priests tell you that those formed through carnal pleasure undergo the contagion of sins before they draw in the gift of this life [^258]. You presume to associate yourself with those whose faith you strive to overthrow. You dare to say that you are being defeated by a company of Manichaeans, when you make the Manichaeans invincible unless you too are defeated along with them. You are mistaken, my son, miserably mistaken, or even detestably mistaken. When you have overcome the contentiousness that holds you, then you will be able to hold the truth by which you are overcome.
