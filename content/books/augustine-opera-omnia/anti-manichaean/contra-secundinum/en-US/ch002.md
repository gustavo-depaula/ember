# The letter of Secundinus the Manichean to Saint Augustine

To the lord deservedly honorable and praiseworthy and to be revered above all, Augustine, from Secundinus.

## What Paul attests, Manichaeus himself attests.

**1**. I have, and I give, thanks to the ineffable and most sacred Majesty, and to his firstborn, the king of all lights, Jesus Christ; I have thanks, and as a suppliant I render them, to the Holy Spirit, because they have given and provided the occasion on which I might safely greet your excellent Holiness, lord deservedly praiseworthy and to be revered above all. And no wonder, for they are most fit to bestow all good things and to ward off all evils, and they defend your good will with their bulwarks and snatch it away from that evil which is not nothing, nor is begotten by the doing and the suffering of mortals, but which stands ready to come. And woe to him who offers himself to it as an occasion.

For you are worthy to obtain such gifts from them, and they are made the nurturers of your truth—truly a lamp, which the right hand of truth has set upon the lampstand of your heart, lest at the coming of a thief[^1] the inheritance of your treasure be squandered; and they may command that house to stand without falling, which you have set not upon the sand of error but upon the rock of knowledge[^2]; and may they drive away from us that savage spirit which casts fear and faithlessness upon men, so as to turn souls aside from the narrow path[^3] of the Savior—whose whole assault is poured out through those princes against whom the Apostle confesses, in the Letter to the Ephesians, that he has entered into combat.

For he says that his combat is *not against flesh and blood, but against the principalities and the powers, against the spiritual hosts of wickedness, which are in the heavens*[^4]. And rightly so; for who would take up arms against weapons, and not against the armed man, against the one who is moved to act? For just as the bodies of men are the weapons of sin, so the precepts of salvation are the weapons of justice[^5]. This Paul attests, this Manichaeus himself attests.

## It is not a battle of weapons, but of spirits, who do battle for the sake of souls.

**2**. It is not, then, a battle of weapons, but of spirits, who make use of those weapons. And they do battle for the sake of souls. In the midst of them is placed the soul, to which from the beginning its own nature gave the victory. If the soul acts together with the spirit of the virtues, it will have everlasting life with it and will possess that kingdom to which our Lord invites us; but if it begins to be drawn by the spirit of the vices, and consents, and after consenting does penance, it will have a fountain of pardon for these defilements. For it is led by the commingling of the flesh, not by its own will. But if, after it has come to know itself, it consents to evil and does not arm itself against the enemy, then it has sinned by its own will.

And if it is ashamed again of having erred, it will find ready the author of mercies. For it is punished not because it sinned, but because it did not grieve over its sin. But if it departs along with that same sin, without pardon, then it will be shut out, then it will be likened to the foolish virgin[^6], then it will be an heir of the left hand, then it will be driven by the Lord from the wedding feast[^7] on account of its black garments, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth[^8], and it will go with the devil to the fire[^9] of its own origin—a devil whom your remarkable prudence reports to have been made out of an archangel, or else admits to be nothing. Why, then, will the just reign? Why will the apostles and martyrs be crowned? All of it for this reason, that they conquered nothing? Oh, how much the power of the conqueror is brought to nothing, when the adversary is proclaimed to be of no strength at all! Change your view, I beg you, lay aside the faithlessness of the Punic race, and turn your withdrawal—made out of fear—back to the truth; do not excuse yourself with these lies.

## Your reverend Dignity is angry at the truth as Hortensius was at philosophy.

**3**. For my own slight and ordinary talent, the talent of a Roman, has read a good deal of the writings of your reverend Worship, in which you are angry at the truth as Hortensius was at philosophy. And so, when with suspended mind and quick eye I had gone over them again and again, I found everywhere a consummate orator and almost a god of the whole of eloquence, but nowhere did I find a Christian: armed indeed against everything, yet affirming nothing—whereas you ought to have shown yourself skilled rather in knowledge than in speech. This indeed I cannot keep silent from your most patient Holiness: for it seemed to me, and most certainly it is so, that you never were a Manichean, and that you were never able to come to know the hidden things of its unknown secret, and that under the name of Manichaeus you are persecuting a Hannibal and a Mithridates. For I confess that the marbles of the house of the Anicii do not gleam with such care and such labor as your writings shine with eloquence. Had you been willing to bring this into accord with the truth, it would surely have stood as a great ornament for us. Do not, I beg, go against your own nature; do not be the lance of error, by which the side of the Savior is pierced.

For you see that he is crucified both in the whole world and in every soul, which soul never had a nature given to anger. And you, therefore, who are from that very soul, give up, I beg, these now empty accusations, leave aside these superfluous controversies. For so long a time, set in the midst of the darkness with your parent, you never mocked; in the midst of the sun and the moon you have been found an accuser. Who, then, will be your advocate before the just tribunal of the Judge, when you will begin to be convicted, by your own testimony, both of word and of deed? The Persian whom you have accused will not be there. This case apart, who will console you as you weep? Who will save the Carthaginian? Or has it been amended in the Gospel, that the broad way does not lead down to destruction[^10]? Or is it false in Paul, that each will have to render an account of his own works[^11]?

Oh, that in withdrawing from Manichaeus you had sought the Academy, or had set yourself to expound the wars of the Romans, who conquered all things: how great, how excellent the things you would have found there! And then, a man wholly chaste in his modesty and his poverty, you would not have gone over to the nations of the Jews, barbarous in their morals—since you weave fables into your precepts, and bring forward, *Take a wife of harlotry;* and, *You will beget children of a harlot;* and, *The land has committed great harlotry, departing from the Lord*[^12]; and, *You shall not wash your hands after intercourse with your wife;* and, *Put your hand under my thigh*[^13]; and, *Kill and eat*[^14]; and, *Increase and multiply*[^15].

Or did the lions in the den please you[^16], because there were no cages? Or did the barrenness of Sarah grieve you, whose husband, the betrayer of her modesty, had stood forth pretending she was his sister[^17]? But perhaps, after the contest of Dares and Entellus[^18], you had wished to wait for the all-out wrestling match of Jacob himself[^19]? Or had you resolved to behold the multitude of the Amorites[^20], or the medley of beasts in Noah's ark[^21]? I know that you always held these things in hatred; I know that you always loved great things—things that would forsake the earth, that would seek the heavens, that would mortify bodies, that would give life to souls. Who, then, is the one who has so suddenly changed you?

## For you yourself are not unaware how very evil the devil is, and how malign, and how with such cunning he makes war against the faithful and against the greatest of men.

**4**. And yet to say these things to your Holiness is quite absurd. For you yourself are not unaware how very evil he is, and how malign, and how with such cunning he makes war against the faithful and against the greatest of men, so that he compelled even Peter to deny the Lord three times in a single night, and did not permit Thomas to believe the same Lord when he had risen—wounds that nonetheless were healed by the medicine of pardon. But consider how boldly he contrived, when the Lord was sowing the best seed, to mingle in his weeds, and to snatch away the Iscariot from so great a shepherd; and, that it might come at last to the punishment of the cross, he inflamed the scribes and Pharisees to the Lord's destruction, so that they cried out for Barabbas to be released and Jesus to be crucified. We escaped, therefore, because we have followed the spiritual Savior. For his boldness broke out so far that, had our Lord been a creature of flesh, all our hope would have been cut off. And yet not even by the very reproach of the cross could he be satisfied; rather, raging, he forced him on the one hand to be crowned with thorns, on the other to be given vinegar to drink; here to be struck by a soldier's lance, there to be blasphemed by the mouth of the thief on the left[^22].

But afterward his iniquity grew so great that, when the Lord himself and his apostles had ascended on high, he framed various questions and, what is worse, divided up under their name the dignity of the catholic title among all the superstitious. For I pass over how he armed each one of the disciples against the masters, how he deceived Hymenaeus, how Alexander[^23], what he did at Antioch, at Smyrna, at Iconium; I add now only the things that the present multitude is busy about—a multitude from which virtue is as far off as it is closed to the crowd. For virtue is not something the crowd attains, and a crowd especially of women. But I am afraid to make their secret doings public, lest crimes begun by others be doubled. And yet it is the part of the wise both to bear with each and to laugh at each, and to strive only after that which procures blessedness, which brings life to birth.

## As a suppliant I beg, I pray, and again and again I beseech you with all my ardor: I do not want you to be torn away from our flock.

**5**. And yet, again and again, as a suppliant I beg, I pray, and yet again I beseech you—first of all, that you would deign to grant pardon if your golden breast has been stung by any word of mine; for I have done this out of too great an ardor, because I do not want you to be torn away from our flock, from which I myself, straying, very nearly perished, had I not quickly removed myself from the wicked nature of their communion. And next, that you would reconcile yourself to her against whom you have committed no offense; that you would return to her who, if you return, will not be angry at you on account of your fault. For she knows how to forgive not seven times only; rather, she has the power both to bind and to loose.

Do not pretend to flatter, you who long ago saw the truth; do not wish to learn, you who are able to teach. Let go the glory of men, if you wish to please Christ. In our times renew Paul, who, though he was a teacher of the Jewish law, having obtained from the Lord the grace of apostleship, counted the things he had thought advantages as dung, that he might gain Christ[^24]. Come to the aid of your own soul, so full of light, for you do not know at what hour the thief will come. Do not adorn the dead, for you are the adornment of the living. Do not be a companion of the broad road, for it awaits the Amorite; but hasten to the narrow way, that you may obtain eternal life. Cease, I beg, to shut Christ up in a womb, lest you yourself be shut up again in a womb. Cease to make two natures into one, for the judgment of the Lord draws near. Woe to those who will receive it, who turn what is sweet into bitterness.

## The divine reason exceeds the breasts of mortals—as, for instance, this very point: how there are two natures, or why he fought who could suffer nothing.

**6**. But if you are in doubt about the beginning, if you waver about the origin of the battle, an account can be rendered in a daytime discussion and a peaceful conversation. Yet this I make known to your most sagacious goodness, that there are certain matters which cannot be set forth in such a way that they are understood; for the divine reason exceeds the breasts of mortals—as, for instance, this very point: how there are two natures, or why he fought who could suffer nothing; and likewise concerning the new age, which the same man records, that it is to be built up once the very great upheavals of that earth have been cut short. But who would admit that there is any cutting short among divine things—unless, that is, you make it a figure on the part of one who interprets to a hearer, since the words are cut short by the one and composed in the other?

And although the expounder may have said many things which the hearer retains within himself, yet they have not departed from the expounder—unless you think in such a way both about that age, and that what is said is foolish enough and inept.

So too concerning the battle: unless you first conjecture that God is wholly justice, and that to invade what belongs to another is the ultimate crime; but when the contrary nature came against this, he indeed could suffer nothing, because he had foreknowledge, yet he would have seemed to have consented to the crime had he not fought.

And therefore he set a great strength against the one who came, so that his justice might be defiled by no consenting to sacrilege. For he was so defined by it as just, that he neither ever sins himself, nor ever consents to one who sins. This too: that God in his own kingdom was mighty in nature, as almighty and as judge.

These things, indeed, have been said in this way not as he came to be, but as I was not able to attain to it: as yet they do not satisfy faithlessness, nor has the sun risen for the blind, nor has the voice been heard by the deaf, nor have the banquets been prepared for the dead. But that places cannot be assigned to natures—this is what the human condition calls unutterable and ineffable. The Savior, however, for whom all things are easy, calls these two the right and the left, the within and the without: *Come* and *Depart*[^25]. But you turn it about and set your foot down, as it is: World, life, salvation, light, law, order, power; if you say a vowel and a mute, you call long what is short—things which by nature do not sound this way, yet for certain they signify two things, and things separated from one another.

## Conclusion.

**7**. But when such things are set forth by me to your admirable and lofty prudence, it is as if the Jordan should lend water to the Ocean, or a lamp light to the sun, or the people holiness to the bishop. For this reason one must bear with whatever this letter contains. For I too, had I not known your divine patience, which readily pardons anyone, would never have written in such a way—though you may see that I have touched the most excellent thoughts only in summary, and have taken great care not to seem long-winded to you. Therefore let these things find acceptance with your Holiness, and how we are to be saved; otherwise you will be able to generate thousands of volumes from this, lord deservedly praiseworthy and to be revered above all. Farewell.
