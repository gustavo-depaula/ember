# Book I: On the Catholic Way of Life

## How he will expose the deceptions of the Manicheans.

**1.** 1. In other books I think we have dealt sufficiently with how we can meet the invectives of the Manicheans, by which they are carried, ignorantly and impiously, against the Law that is called the Old Testament, and how, amid the applause of the unlearned, they fan themselves with empty boasting; and this can also be briefly recalled here by me.

For who of moderately sound mind would not easily understand that the exposition of the Scriptures is to be sought from those who profess to be teachers of them, and that it can happen, indeed that it always happens, that many things seem absurd to the unlearned which, when they are expounded by the teachers, seem all the more worthy of praise the more abjectly they seemed worthy of scorn, and are received all the more sweetly when opened the more difficult they were to open when closed?

This is what generally happens with the holy books of the Old Testament, if only the one who is offended by them seeks out a devout teacher of them rather than an impious mangler, and is first steeped in the zeal of one who inquires rather than the rashness of one who finds fault. Nor, if in his desire to learn these things he should chance to fall in with certain bishops or presbyters or ministers and prelates of the Catholic Church of whatever sort who either everywhere take care not to lay bare the mysteries or, content with simple faith, have not cared to know deeper things, should he despair that the knowledge of the truth is there, where neither all of whom it is asked are able to teach nor all who ask are worthy to learn.

Both diligence and devotion, therefore, must be applied: by the one it will come about that we find those who know; by the other, that we deserve to know.

## Two things by which the Manicheans deceive.

**1.** 2. But since there are two especially great enticements of the Manicheans, by which the incautious are deceived into wanting to have them as teachers—one when they find fault with the Scriptures, whether those they understand badly or those they want to be understood badly; the other when they hold out a semblance of a chaste and remarkable life of continence—this book will contain our view, in keeping with Catholic teaching, concerning life and morals, in which it will perhaps be understood both how easy it is to feign virtue and how difficult to have it.

I will indeed keep to this manner, if I can, that I will not inveigh against those diseases of theirs, which are very well known to me, so gravely as they inveigh against the things they do not know; for I wish them to be healed rather, if it can be done, than to be attacked. And I will take from the Scriptures those testimonies which they must of necessity believe, namely from the New Testament, from which, however, I will bring forward none of those things which they are accustomed to say have been interpolated when they are hard pressed in great straits; rather I will say those things which they are compelled both to approve and to praise. Nor will I leave any statement brought forward as a witness from the apostolic teaching for which I do not match a similar one from the Old Testament, so that, if at last, laying aside the stubbornness of their dreams, they should wish to wake up and aspire to the light of the Christian faith, they may notice both how the life they parade is not Christian and how the Scripture they mangle is Christ's.

## He proceeds first by reasoning, yielding to the faulty custom of the Manicheans.

**2.** 3. Where, then, shall I begin? From authority or from reason? The order of nature is indeed such that, when we learn something, authority comes before reason. For reason can seem weak which, once it has been given, afterward takes up an authority by which it may be confirmed. But because the minds of human beings, darkened by the habit of the shadows in which they are veiled in the night of sins and vices, cannot direct a fit gaze upon the clearness and purity of reason, it has been most wholesomely arranged that authority, shaded as it were by the branches of humanity, should lead the wavering vision into the light of truth. But since we are dealing with people who feel and speak and act in all things contrary to order, and who say nothing more insistently than that reason must be given first, I will indulge them and take up what I confess to be faulty in disputation. For it delights me to imitate, as far as I am able, the gentleness of my Lord Jesus Christ, who was even clothed in the evil of death itself, by which he willed to strip us of it.

## Blessed is the one who enjoys what is the best for a human being.

**3.** 4. Let us inquire, then, by reason how a human being ought to live. Certainly we all wish to live happily, and there is no one in the human race who does not assent to this statement even before it has been fully uttered. But a person, in my estimation, can be called happy neither if he does not have what he loves, whatever it may be, nor if he has what he loves, if it is harmful, nor if he does not love what he has, even if it is the best. For the one who craves what he cannot obtain is tormented; the one who has obtained what ought not to be craved is deceived; and the one who does not crave what ought to be obtained is sick. Now none of these befalls the mind without misery; and misery and happiness are not accustomed to dwell together in one and the same person; therefore none of those is happy.

A fourth case remains, as I see it, where the happy life can be found: when what is the best for a human being is both loved and possessed. For what else is it that we call enjoying, if not having at hand what one loves? Nor is anyone happy who does not enjoy what is the best for a human being, nor is anyone who enjoys it not happy. Our best, therefore, ought to be at hand for us, if we mean to live happily.

## What is the best for a human being. Two conditions of the highest good: (1) that nothing be better than it; (2) that it be such that one does not lose it unwillingly.

**3.** 5. It follows that we should inquire what is the best for a human being, which surely cannot be worse than the human being himself. For whoever follows what is worse than himself becomes himself worse. But every human being ought to follow what is best. Therefore the best for a human being is not worse than the human being.

Perhaps it will be something such as the human being himself is? Let it be so, by all means, if there is nothing better than the human being that he could enjoy. But if we find something that is both more excellent than the human being and can be at hand for the human being who loves it, who would doubt that, in order to be happy, the human being must strive toward that which it is plain is more excellent than the very one who strives? For if to be happy is to have arrived at such a good than which there can be nothing greater—and this is what we call the best—how then can he be included in that definition who has not yet arrived at his highest good? Or how is it the highest, if there is something better that we can reach?

If, then, this is so, it must be such that one does not lose it unwillingly. For no one can have confidence in such a good which he perceives can be snatched from him, even if he should wish to hold and embrace it. But whoever has no confidence in the good he enjoys, how can he be happy amid so great a fear of losing it?

## What a human being is.

**4.** 6. Let us inquire, then, what is better than a human being. This is surely difficult to find unless we first consider and discuss what the human being himself is. Nor do I now think a definition of a human being should be demanded of me. What rather seems to me to need inquiry in this place is this: since it is agreed among almost everyone—or at any rate, what is enough, since it is agreed between me and those with whom I am now dealing—that we are composed of soul and body, what the human being himself is: both of these that I have named, or the body only, or the soul only. For although soul and body are two, and neither would be called a human being if the other did not exist—for the body would not be a human being if the soul did not exist, nor again would the soul be a human being if it did not animate a body—it can nevertheless happen that one of these is both held to be and called a human being.

What, then, do we call a human being? Soul and body, as a two-horse team or a centaur? Or the body only, which is in the use of the soul that governs it, just as we call a lamp not the fire and the earthenware together but the earthenware alone, yet on account of the fire? Or do we call a human being nothing other than the soul, but on account of the body it governs, just as we call a horseman not the horse and the man together but the man alone, yet from the fact that he is suited to governing the horse? It is difficult to decide this controversy, or, if it is easy by reason, it is long in the telling; and there is no need to take up and undergo that labor and delay. For whether both, or the body alone, or the soul alone holds the name of human being, the best for a human being is not what is best for the body, but what is best either for the body and the soul together or for the soul alone; that is the best for a human being.

## The best for a human being is not what is best for the body alone, but what is best for the soul.

**5.** 7. But if we ask what is best for the body, sure reason compels us to admit that it is that through which it comes about that the body is in the best condition. Now there is nothing among all the things that give vigor to the body better and more excellent than the soul. Therefore the highest good of the body is not its pleasure, not freedom from pain, not strength, not beauty, not swiftness, nor anything else that is wont to be counted among the goods of the body, but the soul altogether. For the soul also furnishes to the body, by its presence, those things that have been mentioned, and—what surpasses all of them—life. Hence it does not seem to me that the soul is the highest good of the human being, whether we call a human being soul and body together or the soul alone. For just as reason finds that the highest good of the body is what is better than the body and by which vigor and life are provided to it, so, whether a human being is body and soul or the soul itself by itself, it must be found whether something precedes the soul itself which, when the soul follows it, becomes, in its own kind, the best it can be. And if we can find this, it will surely be what, with all evasions removed, must by right and merit be named the highest good of the human being.

**5.** 8. But if the human being is body, I cannot refuse to admit that the best for the human being is the soul itself. Yet surely, when morals are at issue, when we inquire what manner of life must be kept in order that we may be able to attain happiness, precepts are not given to the body, nor is the discipline of the body to be investigated. In short, good morals will be the work of that part of us which inquires and learns, and these belong properly to the soul; therefore the question is not about the body when we are busy with the obtaining of virtue.

And if it is a consequence—as it is—that the body itself, when it is governed by a soul possessed of virtue, is governed much better and more honorably, and is in the best condition by the degree to which that soul is best which rules over it by a just law, then that will be the best for a human being which makes the soul best, even if we call the human being a body. Or indeed, if a charioteer who obeys me nourishes and governs most suitably the horses over which he is set, and himself, the more obedient he is to me, enjoys my generosity, can anyone deny that not only the charioteer's being in the best condition, but also the horses' being so, is owed to me? And so, whether the human being is the body only, or the soul only, or both, it does not seem to me that I must especially inquire into anything except what makes the soul best; for once that is grasped, the human being cannot but be in the best condition, or at any rate in a much better one than if this one thing had been lacking.

## Virtue makes the soul best; the soul gains virtue by following God; and the attainment of God is the happy life.

**6.** 9. No one, however, would doubt that virtue makes the soul best. But it can quite rightly be asked whether this virtue can exist even through itself or cannot exist except in a soul. There arises again a most profound question that needs a very long discourse, but perhaps I shall make good use of this shortcut. I hope God will be present, so that, as far as our weakness allows, we may teach about such great matters not only clearly but also briefly. For whichever it may be—whether virtue can exist even through itself without a soul, or whether it cannot exist except in a soul—without doubt the soul follows something in order to attain virtue; this will be either the soul itself, or virtue, or some third thing.

But if it follows itself in order to gain virtue, it follows something foolish, I know not what; for it is foolish before it has gained virtue. Now the highest wish of those who follow is to gain what we follow. Either, then, the soul will wish not to gain what it follows—than which nothing more absurd and perverse can be said—or, when, being foolish, it follows itself, it gains the very foolishness that it shuns.

But if it follows virtue in its desire to gain it, how does it follow what does not exist? Or how does it desire to gain what it has? Either, then, virtue exists apart from the soul, or, if it is not acceptable to call it virtue unless one means the very disposition and, as it were, the quality of a wise soul, which cannot exist except in a soul, then the soul must follow something else in order that virtue can be born in it, since neither by following nothing nor by following foolishness can it, as far as my reason carries me, arrive at wisdom.

**6.** 10. This other thing, then, by following which the soul becomes possessed of virtue and wisdom, is either a wise human being or God. But it was said above that it must be something such that we cannot lose it unwillingly. And who would think it should be doubted that a wise human being, if we should think it enough to follow him, can be taken from us not only when we are unwilling but even when we resist? God, therefore, remains, whom if we follow, we live well, and if we attain, we live not only well but also happily. And if any deny that he exists, what thought shall I conceive by which to persuade in speech those with whom I do not even know whether I should speak at all? But if it should seem right, a far different beginning, a different method, a different approach must be entered upon than the one we have taken up for the present. Now, therefore, I am dealing with those who do not deny that God exists, and not only that, but also confess that human affairs are not neglected by him. For I think that no one is held by any name of religion who does not at least believe that divine providence has care for our minds.

## God is to be tracked down by the authority of the Scriptures. The plan of the divine dispensations toward our salvation, and its chief mysteries.

**7.** 11. But in what way do we follow him whom we do not see, or how do we see him, we who are not only human beings but even foolish human beings? For although he is discerned not by the eyes but by the mind, what mind, after all, can be found fit which, when it is covered over by the cloud of folly, has the strength to drink in, or even attempts to drink in, that light? We must take refuge, therefore, in the precepts of those whom it is probable to have been wise. Thus far reason could be led. For it was occupied with human affairs, not more certain through truth but more secure through habit. But when it has come to divine things, it turns itself away; it cannot gaze, it throbs, it burns, it gapes with love, it is beaten back by the light of truth, and it turns back to the familiarity of its own shadows not by choice but by exhaustion. How greatly here it is to be feared, how greatly to be dreaded, lest the soul, weary, conceive from that source a greater weakness where it seeks rest. Therefore, for those who desire to flee back into the shadows, let that shadiness of authority meet us through the dispensation of ineffable wisdom, and let it soothe us with the marvelous voices of things and of books as with the more temperate signs and shadows of truth.

## The sum of the faith.

**7.** 12. What more could have been done for our salvation? What more beneficent, what more generous can be said of divine providence, which did not utterly forsake the human being who had fallen from its laws and who, because of the desire for mortal things, by right and merit propagates mortal offspring? For that most just power has, in marvelous and incomprehensible ways, through certain most secret successions of the things that serve it, which it created, both the severity to punish and the clemency to set free. How beautiful this is, how great, how worthy of God, and finally how true is what is sought, we shall by no means be able to understand unless we begin from things human and near at hand. If, keeping the faith and the precepts of true religion, we do not forsake the way that God has fortified for us by the setting apart of the Patriarchs, by the bond of the Law, by the foretelling of the Prophets, by the mystery of the Man he took up, by the testimony of the Apostles, by the blood of the martyrs, and by the bringing in of the gentiles. Therefore let no one henceforth ask of me my own opinion; let us rather hear the oracles and submit our little reasonings to the divine utterances.

## God is the highest good, toward which we are bidden to strive with the highest love.

**8.** 13. Let us see how the Lord himself in the Gospel has commanded us to live, and how also the apostle Paul; for these Scriptures they do not dare to condemn. Let us hear, then, what end of goods you prescribe for us, O Christ; and there is no doubt that this will be the end toward which you bid us strive with the highest love. *You shall love,* he says, *the Lord your God*[^1]*.* Tell me also, I beg you, what is the measure of loving; for I fear lest I be inflamed with the desire and love of my Lord more or less than I ought. *With your whole* heart, he says. That is not enough. *With your whole soul.* Not even that is enough. *With your whole mind.* What more do you want? I would want it, perhaps, if I could see what more there could be.

What does Paul say to this? *We know,* he says, *that for those who love God all things work toward good*[^2]*.* Let him too state the measure of love. *Who, then,* he says, *shall separate us from the love of Christ? Tribulation? Or distress? Or persecution? Or famine? Or nakedness? Or peril? Or the sword?*[^3] We have heard what we ought to love and how much we ought to love; toward that we must strive altogether, to that all our counsels must be referred. The sum of goods, for us, is God. The highest good, for us, is God. We must neither remain short of it nor seek beyond it; for the one is dangerous, the other is nothing.

## The harmony of the Old and New Testaments concerning the precepts of love.

**9.** 14. Come now, let us investigate, or rather let us notice—for it is ready at hand and most easily seen—whether the authority of the Old Testament also agrees with these statements brought forward from the Gospel and the Apostle. What shall I say about the former statement, since it is plain to all that it was drawn from the law that *was given through Moses*? For there it is written, *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart and with your whole soul and with your whole mind*[^4]*.* But what I might match from the Old Testament to what was said by the Apostle, he himself, lest I should search any longer, has supplied. For when he had said that by no tribulation, no distress, no persecution, no necessity of bodily want, no peril, no sword are we separated from the love of Christ, he at once added, *As it is written, For your sake we are afflicted all the day long; we are counted as sheep for the slaughter*[^5]*.* These words they are accustomed to say have been interpolated by corrupters of the Scriptures; so utterly do they have nothing to say in reply that the wretches are compelled to give this answer. But who would not understand that there could have been no other final utterance of men who have been convicted?

## In the books of the Old Testament I see all things to be in harmony with the Christian faith.

**9.** 15. Yet I ask them whether they deny that this statement is in the Old Testament, or affirm that it does not agree with the apostolic statement. The former I will teach from the books; in the latter case I will either recall to peace these men who shuffle and flee over the cliffs, if they should be willing to look back a little and consider what was said, or I will assail them by the understanding of the rest, who judge without desire. For what can be more friendly than that these statements should accord with one another? For tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, peril gravely afflict a human being placed in this life. All these words, therefore, are summed up in that one testimony of the Old Law in which it was said, *For your sake we are afflicted.* The sword remained, which does not bring a wretched life but takes away the life it finds. To this, then, he responds, *We are counted as sheep for the slaughter*[^6]*.* But love itself could not have been signified more expressly than by the words, *For your sake.*

Suppose, then, that this testimony were not found in the apostle Paul, but had been brought forward by me. Surely there would be nothing for you to prove, heretic, except either that it is not written in the Old Law or that it does not agree with the Apostle? And if you dare to say neither of these—for you are pressed, since the manuscript is read by which it is plain that it is written, and people understand that nothing could agree more aptly with what the Apostle said—why, then, do you think it carries any weight that you dare to charge the Scriptures with being corrupted?

Finally, what will you reply to one who says to you: I understand it thus, I take it thus, I believe it thus, and I read those books for no other reason than that I see all things in them to be in harmony with the Christian faith? Rather say this, if you dare and intend to say it against me: that it is not to be believed that the Apostles and martyrs are said to have been afflicted with grave hardships for Christ's sake, that they were counted by persecutors as sheep for the slaughter. And if you cannot say this, why do you slander the book in which I should believe what you confess that I ought to believe?

## What the Church teaches about God. The two gods of the Manicheans.

**10.** 16. Or do you say this: that you grant indeed that God is to be loved, but not that God whom those worship who accept the authority of the Old Testament? So you say that the God who *made heaven and earth* is not to be worshiped. For he is proclaimed throughout every part of those volumes; but you confess that this whole world, which is signified by the name of heaven and earth, has God as its author and maker, and a good God. For one must speak with you with a reservation whenever God is named. For you assert that there are two gods, one good, the other evil. And if you say that you worship, and judge that one is to be worshiped, the God by whom the world was made, but not that he is the one whom the authority of the Old Testament commends, you act shamelessly, in that you try to interpret badly—and altogether in vain—another's mind and meaning, which we have received well and usefully.

For your foolish and impious disputations can in no way be compared with the discourses of devout and most learned men, through whom those Scriptures are opened in the Catholic Church to those who are willing and worthy. Far otherwise indeed, far otherwise than you suppose, are the Law and the Prophets understood by us. Cease to err: we do not worship a God who repents, who is envious, who is in need, who is cruel, who seeks pleasure from the blood of human beings or of cattle, who is pleased by outrages and crimes, who limits his possession to some little patch of earth. For against these and trifles of this kind you are accustomed to inveigh gravely and copiously. Therefore your invective does not touch us; rather, with all the more inept the more vehement your discourse, you tear at certain old-womanish or even childish opinions. And whoever is moved by it and crosses over to you does not condemn the teaching of our Church, but shows that he is ignorant of it.

**10.** 17. Therefore, if you carry anything human in your heart, if you yourselves are a care to yourselves, seek rather, diligently and devoutly, how those things are said. Seek, you wretches; for such a faith, by which something unbecoming to God is believed, we accuse more vehemently and more fully; for even in those things that were said, when they are understood as the letter sounds, we both correct the simplicity and deride the stubbornness.

And there are many other things, which you cannot understand, that Catholic teaching forbids those to believe who, advancing beyond a certain childhood of mind not in years but in zeal and understanding, are promoted to the gray hairs of wisdom. For it is taught how foolish it is to believe that God is contained in some place, however infinite, through any spaces whatever of quantity; and to judge that he himself, or any part of him, moves and passes from place to place is held to be a sacrilege. Indeed, if anyone should hold the opinion that anything of his substance and nature can in any way undergo change or alteration, he will be condemned for astonishing madness and impiety. So it comes about that among us there are found certain childish persons who think of God in human form and suspect that he is so—than which opinion nothing is more base; but there are likewise found many elders who behold by that very mind that his majesty remains inviolable and unchangeable not only above the human body but even above the mind itself. And it has already been said that these ages are to be distinguished not by time but by virtue and prudence.

Among you, however, no one indeed is found who describes the substance of God by the shape of a human body, but again no one who sets it apart from the taint of human error. And so those whom the Catholic Church sustains, as it were, wailing at her breasts, if they have not been despoiled by heretics, are each nourished according to his own capacity and strength, and are led—one in this way, another in that—first to the *perfect man*[^7]*,* and then they arrive at the maturity and gray hairs of wisdom[^8], so that they may live as much as they wish, and live most happily.

## God alone is to be loved, and is therefore the highest good of a human being. Nothing is better than God.

**11.** 18. The following of God, then, is the desire for happiness; the attaining of him is happiness itself. But we follow him by loving, and we attain him not when we become altogether what he himself is, but when we are nearest to him and touch him in a marvelous and intelligible way and are inwardly illumined and grasped by his truth and holiness. For he is light itself, but it is granted to us to be illumined by him. The *greatest,* therefore, that leads to the happy life, *and the first commandment is: You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart and soul and mind. For those who love God, all things work toward good*[^9]*.*

Hence, a little later, the same Paul says, *I am certain that neither death nor life nor angel nor power nor things present nor things to come nor height nor depth nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord*[^10]*.*

If, then, *for those who love God all things work toward good,* and no one doubts that the highest good, which is also called the best, must not only be loved but loved in such a way that we ought to love nothing more—and this is signified and expressed by what was said, *With the whole heart and with the whole soul and with the whole mind*—who, I ask, once all these things are established and most firmly believed, would doubt that there is nothing else best for us, toward the obtaining of which, with everything else set aside, we ought to hasten, except God? Likewise, if no thing separates us from his love, what can be not only better, but even more certain, than this good?

## No one loses God unwillingly; what are the two conditions of the highest good.

**11.** 19. But let us attend to the points one by one, briefly. No one separates us from him by threatening death. For that very thing by which we love God cannot die, except while it does not love God; since death itself is not to love God, which is nothing other than to prefer anything to him in loving and following.

No one separates us from him by promising life; for no one separates water from its own spring by making a promise.

An angel does not separate us; for an angel, when we cling to God, is not more powerful than our mind.

A power does not separate us; for if by "power" here is meant that which has some authority in this world, the mind clinging to God is altogether more sublime than the whole world. But if by "power" is meant that which is the most upright affection of our very mind, then, if it is in another, it favors our being joined to God; if it is in us, it itself joins us to him.

Present troubles do not separate us; for we feel them all the lighter the more closely we cling to him from whom they strive to separate us.

The promise of things to come does not separate us; for whatever good is to come, God promises more certainly, and there is nothing better than God himself, who is surely already present to those who cling well to him.

Neither *height nor depth* separates us; for if these words perhaps signify the height or depth of knowledge, I shall not be inquisitive, lest I be sundered from God, nor does anyone's teaching separate me from him so as to dispel, as it were, an error, from which no one at all would stray except one separated from him. But if by height and depth are signified the things above and below in this world, who would promise me heaven so that I should be sundered from the maker of heaven? Or what hell would terrify me, so that I should forsake God—whom, had I never forsaken him, I would not know the things of hell? Finally, what place will tear me away from his love, who would not be everywhere wholly present if he were contained by any place?

## We are bound to God by love, in that we are subject to him.

**12.** 20. No *other creature,* he says, separates us[^11]. O the man of the most profound mysteries! He was not content to say *creature,* but he said *other creature,* reminding us that the very thing by which we love God and by which we cling to God—that is, the mind—is a creature. Another creature, then, is the body; and if the mind is a certain intelligible thing, that is, one that becomes known only by being understood, another creature is everything sensible, that is, what offers, as it were, a certain knowledge of itself through the eyes or ears or smell or taste or touch; and this must of necessity be lower than what is grasped by understanding alone.

Therefore, since even God can be known by worthy minds only through understanding, while nevertheless he is more excellent than the very mind by which he is understood—for he is its creator and author—it was to be feared lest the human mind, because it is numbered among invisible and intelligible things, should judge itself to be of the same nature as the one who created it, and so fall away through pride from him to whom it ought to be joined by love. For it is made like to God, as far as is granted, when it subjects itself to be enlightened and illumined by him. And if it most closely approaches him by that subjection by which it is made like to him, it must of necessity be made far from him by the audacity by which it wants to be more like him. This is the audacity by which it refuses to obey the laws of God, in its desire to be in its own power as God is.

**12.** 21. The farther, then, it departs from God—not in place but in affection and desire—toward things lower than itself, the more it is filled with foolishness and misery. By love, therefore, it returns to God, by which it strives not to set itself alongside him but to set itself beneath him. And the more insistently and zealously it does this, the happier and more sublime it will be, and, with him alone ruling, the most free. Therefore it ought to know that it is a creature. For it ought to believe that its creator *as he is* always remains in the inviolable and unchangeable nature of truth and wisdom, but to confess that into itself there can fall foolishness and falsity, on account of the errors of which it desires to be stripped. But again it ought to beware lest from the love of God himself, by which it is sanctified so that it may remain most happy, it be separated by the love of another creature, that is, of this sensible world. Therefore no other creature separates us—since we ourselves too are a creature—*from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.*

## Through Christ and his Spirit we are inseparably joined to God.

**13.** 22. Let the same Paul tell us who this Christ Jesus our Lord is: *To those who are called,* he says, *we preach Christ, the power of God and the wisdom of God*[^12]*.* What? Does not Christ himself say, *I am the truth*[^13]? If, then, we ask what it is to live well, that is, by living to strive toward happiness, that will surely be to love virtue, to love wisdom, to love truth, and to love *with the whole heart and with the whole soul and with the whole mind* the virtue that is inviolable and unconquered, the wisdom that foolishness does not succeed, the truth that does not know how to be changed or to be otherwise than it always is. Through this, the Father himself is seen; for it was said, *No one comes to the Father except through me*[^14]*.* To him we cling through sanctification. For when we are sanctified we burn with full and entire love, by which alone it is brought about that we are not turned away from God and are conformed to him rather than to this world. *For he predestined us,* as the same Apostle says, *to be conformed to the image of his Son*[^15]*.*

**13.** 23. It will come about, then, through love, that we are conformed to God, and, conformed and shaped by him and circumcised from this world, we are not confounded with those things that ought to be subject to us. But this comes about through the Holy Spirit. *For hope,* he says, *does not confound, because the love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^16]*.* But we could in no way be made whole again through the Holy Spirit unless he himself always remained whole and unchangeable. And this surely he could not do unless he were of the nature and substance of God, to whom alone unchangeability and, so to speak, unconvertibility always belong. *For the creature*—and this is not I but the same Paul who cries out—*is subjected to vanity*[^17]*.* And what is subjected to vanity cannot separate us from vanity and bind us to truth. And this the Holy Spirit furnishes us; therefore he is not a creature, for everything that exists is either God or a creature.

## We cling by love to the Trinity, the highest good.

**14.** 24. We ought, then, to love God, a certain threefold unity, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, which I shall say is nothing other than being itself. For he is truly and supremely God, *from whom are all things, through whom are all things, in whom are all things;* these are Paul's words. What did he then add? *To him be glory*[^18]*.* Most exactly indeed. For he does not say "to them," for there is one God. And what is *to him be glory* but to him be the best and highest and widely spread renown? For the better and more widely his fame is spread, the more ardently he is loved and cherished. And when this happens, the human race proceeds by nothing other than a sure and steady step into the best and most happy life.

I do not think that, when the question concerns morals and life, anything more need be sought as to what is the highest good of a human being, to which all things are to be referred. For it has appeared, both by reason as far as we were able and by the divine authority that surpasses our reason, that it is nothing other than God himself. For what else will be the best for a human being than that to which to cling is most happy? But this is God alone, to whom we surely cannot cling except by love, affection, charity.

## He defines the four virtues in a Christian way.

**15.** 25. And if virtue leads us to the happy life, I would affirm that virtue is nothing whatever but the highest love of God. For that which is called fourfold virtue is so called, as far as I understand, from a certain varied affection of love itself. And so those four virtues—whose force I wish were as much in our minds as their names are on everyone's lips—I would not hesitate to define thus: that temperance is love offering itself whole to what is loved; fortitude is love readily bearing all things for the sake of what is loved; justice is love serving the beloved alone and on that account ruling rightly; prudence is love sagaciously choosing the things by which it is helped from those by which it is hindered. But we have said that this love is the love not of just anything, but of God, that is, of the highest good, the highest wisdom, and the highest harmony. Therefore it is also permitted to define them thus: that we call temperance love keeping itself whole and uncorrupted for God; fortitude love readily enduring all things for the sake of God; justice love serving God alone and on this account ruling well over the other things that are subject to a human being; prudence love rightly discerning the things by which it is helped toward God from those by which it can be hindered.

## The harmony of the Old and New Testaments.

**16.** 26. I will explain in a few words what manner of living is to be drawn from these virtues individually, if I first match from the Old Testament, as I promised, statements equal to the testimonies of the New Testament, which I have been using for a long time now. For does Paul alone say that we ought to be joined to God in such a way that there is nothing in between to separate us[^19]? Does not the prophet too signify this most aptly and most briefly when he says, *But for me it is good to cling to God*[^20]? Is not what was said there most fully about love contained here in one word, when he says, *To cling*? Likewise, when he added, *It is good,* does he not look back to what was placed there, *For those who love God all things work toward good*[^21]? So that in one little sentence and two words the prophet shows both the force and the fruit of love.

**16.** 27. And since it was said there that the Son of God is the power of God and the wisdom of God[^22], and since virtue is understood to pertain to activity, while wisdom pertains to teaching—whence in the Gospel these two are marked when it is said, *All things were made through him*[^23]*,* for this belongs to activity and power, and then, as to what pertains to teaching and the knowledge of the true, *and the life,* he says, *was the light of men*[^24] *—,* could anything be more in harmony with these testimonies of the New Testament than what was said in the Old concerning wisdom: *It reaches mightily from end to end, and orders all things sweetly*[^25]? For to reach mightily signifies rather power, while to order sweetly signifies, as it were, the very art and reason.

But if this seems obscure, see what follows: *And the Lord of all,* he says, *loved her; for she is the teacher of the knowledge of God, and the chooser of his works*[^26]*.* Here nothing as yet seems said about activity; for to choose works is not the same as to work; therefore these things pertain to teaching; the work of virtue is owed, so that the statement we wish to demonstrate may be complete.

Read, then, next what is appended: *And if a possession that is desired in life is honorable,* he says, *what is more honorable than wisdom, which works all things?*[^27] Can anything more splendid or more plain, or indeed even more full, be brought forward?

Hear another, if you think it too little, that has the same sound: *For wisdom teaches sobriety and justice and virtue*[^28]*.* Sobriety seems to me to pertain to the very knowledge of the true, that is, to teaching, while justice and virtue pertain to action and activity. With these two—that is, with effectiveness in acting and sobriety in contemplating—which the power of God and the wisdom of God, that is, the Son of God, bestows on those who love him, I do not know what is to be compared, since the prophet himself at once says how highly these are to be valued; for it is set down thus: *For wisdom teaches sobriety and justice and virtue, than which nothing is more useful to human beings in life*[^29]*.*

**16.** 28. Perhaps someone may think these things were not said of the Son of God. What else, then, does this show that was said, *She magnifies her noble birth by having companionship with God*[^30]? Or does "noble birth" usually signify anything other than parents? And does not "companionship" cry out and assert equality with the Father himself? Then, since Paul says that the Son of God is *the wisdom of God*[^31]*,* and the Lord himself says, *No one knows the Father except the only-begotten Son*[^32]*,* what could be said by the prophet more in keeping than what was said, *And with you is Wisdom, who knows your works, who was present then when you made the world, and knew what would be pleasing in your eyes*[^33]? And as for Christ being the truth, the same is shown when he is called *the splendor of the Father*[^34]—is there now anything around the sun but the very splendor it begets? What, then, could from the Old Testament accord with this statement more openly and clearly than what was said, *Your truth is round about you*[^35]? Finally, wisdom herself says in the Gospel, *No one comes to the Father except through me*[^36]*;* the prophet says, *Who, then, knows your meaning, unless you give wisdom?* And a little later, *And the things that please you, men have learned and have been saved*[^37]*.*

**16.** 29. Paul says, *The love of God is poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^38]*;* the prophet says, *For the Holy Spirit of teaching will flee deceit*[^39]*.* For where there is deceit, there is no love. Paul says, *that we are made conformed to the image of the Son of God*[^40]; the prophet says, *The light of your countenance is sealed upon us, O Lord*[^41]*.* Paul shows that the Holy Spirit is God and therefore is not a creature; the prophet says, *And you sent your Holy Spirit from the highest*[^42]*.* For God alone is the highest, than whom nothing is higher. Paul shows that this Trinity is one God when he says, *To him be glory*[^43]*;* it is said in the Old Testament, *Hear, O Israel, the Lord your God is one God*[^44]*.*

## An apostrophe to the Manicheans, that they may come to their senses.

**17.** 30. What more do you want? Why do you rage, ignorantly and impiously? Why do you pervert untaught souls with harmful persuasion? The God of both Testaments is one. For just as the things we have set down from each agree with one another, so too do the rest, if you are willing to attend diligently and with fair judgment.

But because many things are said more humbly and more suited to souls creeping along the ground, that through things human they may rise up into things divine, and many things also figuratively, that the studious mind may be both more usefully exercised by what it seeks and more abundantly gladdened by what it finds, you, by a marvelous arrangement of the Holy Spirit, abuse these things to deceive and ensnare your hearers.

Why divine providence allows you to do this very thing, and how most truly the Apostle said, *There must be many heresies, that those who are approved may be made manifest among you*[^45]*,* it would take long to discuss; and what must be said to you, it is not for you to understand these things. You are not little known to me. You bring minds altogether gross, and sickened by the pestilent feeding on bodily images, to the judging of divine things, which are far higher than you suppose.

**17.** 31. Therefore we must not now deal with you in such a way that you should already understand these things—which cannot happen—but that you should at some time desire to understand. For this is brought about by the simple and pure love of God, which is chiefly seen in morals, of which we have already said much, and which, breathed in by the Holy Spirit, leads to the Son, that is, to the Wisdom of God, through whom the Father himself is known. For if wisdom and truth are not desired with all the strength of the mind, they can in no way be found. But if they are sought as is fitting, they cannot withdraw and hide themselves from those who love them.

Hence comes that saying which even you are accustomed to have on your lips, where he says, *Ask and you shall receive, seek and you shall find, knock and it shall be opened to you*[^46]*. There is nothing hidden that shall not be revealed*[^47]*.* By love it is asked, by love it is sought, by love it is knocked at, by love it is revealed, and by love, finally, one remains in what has been revealed. From this love of wisdom and this diligence in seeking we are not deterred by the Old Testament, as you most falsely keep saying, but are most vehemently stirred up to these things.

**17.** 32. Hear, then, at last, and notice, I beg you, without stubbornness, what is said through the prophet: *Wisdom is bright,* he says, *and never fades, and is easily seen by those who love her, and is found by those who seek her; she anticipates those who desire her, so as to show herself to them. Whoever has watched for her will not labor, for he will find her sitting at his doors. For to think about her is perfect understanding, and whoever has watched for her sake will soon be free of care; because she goes about seeking those worthy of herself, and shows herself to them cheerfully in their ways, and meets them in every thought. For the truest beginning of her is the desire for instruction. The care for instruction, then, is love, and love is the keeping of her laws; and the keeping of the laws is the assurance of incorruption; and incorruption makes one near to God. The desire for wisdom, therefore, leads to a kingdom*[^48]*.* Will you then still bark against these things? Do not these words, set down thus and not yet understood, signify to anyone that they contain something deep and ineffable? Oh, that you could understand what has been said! At once you would cast away all the inanities of fables and the most empty imaginings of bodies, and would lodge your whole selves, with great eagerness, sincere love, and most firm faith, in the most holy bosom of the Catholic Church.

## In the Catholic Church alone is there perfect truth, from the agreement of both Testaments.

**18.** 33. I could, according to my mediocrity, examine each point and dig out and demonstrate what I have received—in the excellence and loftiness of which words for the most part fail—but so long as you bark, it must not be done. For it was not said in vain, *Do not give what is holy to dogs*[^49]*.* Do not be angry. I too barked and was a dog, when I was treated, rightly, not with the food of teaching but with the cudgels of refutation. But if there were in you the love of which we are now treating, or if there had ever been in you as much as the greatness of knowing the truth requires, God would be present, to show you that the Christian faith—which leads to the highest peak of wisdom and truth, to enjoy which is nothing other than to live most happily—is not among the Manicheans, and is nowhere except in Catholic teaching.

For what else does the apostle Paul seem to wish, when he says, *For this reason I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named, that he may grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with might through his Spirit in the inner man, that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts, so that, rooted and grounded in love, you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the height and the length and the breadth and the depth, and to know also the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled unto all the fullness of God*[^50]? Can anything be said more plainly?

**18.** 34. I beseech you, wake up a little; see the concord of both Testaments, sufficiently disclosing and teaching what is the measure of life in morals and to what all things are to be referred. The Gospels stir up the love of God, when it is said, *Ask, seek, knock*[^51]; Paul stirs it up by saying, *that, rooted and grounded in love, you may be able to comprehend*[^52]*;* the prophet too stirs it up, when he says that wisdom can be easily known by those who love her, seek her, desire her, watch, think, and care. The salvation of the mind and the way of happiness is shown by the harmony of both Scriptures, and you choose rather to bark against these things than to obey them. I will say briefly what I think: hear the learned men of the Catholic Church with as much peace of mind and with the same eager wish with which I heard you; there will be no need of the nine years in which you made sport of me. Far, far in a shorter time you will discern what difference there is between truth and emptiness.

## He describes the offices of temperance from the sacred Letters.

**19.** 35. But it is time to return to those four virtues and to dig out and draw from each of them a manner of living. And so let us first look at temperance, which promises us a certain integrity and incorruption of that love by which we are bound to God. For its task is in restraining and quieting the desires by which we gape after the things that turn us away from the laws of God and from the fruit of his goodness, which is, to explain it briefly, the happy life. For there is the faith of the true, by enjoying the contemplation of which and inwardly clinging to it we are without doubt happy; but those who fall away from it are entangled in great errors and griefs. For, as the Apostle says, *the root of all evils is desire, which some following have made shipwreck of the faith and entangled themselves in many griefs*[^53]*.*

This sin of the soul is signified in the Old Testament plainly enough, to those who understand well, in the transgression of the very man who was in paradise. For *in Adam* indeed *we all die,* as the same Apostle says, *and in Christ we shall all rise again*[^54]*.* O the deep mysteries! But I shall restrain myself; for I have not undertaken now to teach you what is right, but to unteach you what is depraved, if I shall be able, that is, if God assents to my purpose toward you.

Paul says, then, that the root of all evils is desire, through which the Old Law too signifies the fall of the first man. Paul warns us to put off the old man and put on the new[^55]. And he wishes Adam, who sinned, to be understood as the old man, but the one whom the Son of God took up in the mystery to set us free, as the new man.

For he says in another place, *The first man, of the earth, is earthly; the second man, from heaven, is heavenly. As is the earthly, such also are the earthly; as is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly. As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of the heavenly*[^56]; that is, put off the old and put on the new. The whole office of temperance, therefore, is to put off the old man and be renewed in God; that is, to despise all bodily enticements and popular praise, and to direct one's whole love toward things invisible and divine.

Hence follows what was marvelously said, *And though our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^57]*.* Hear too the prophet singing, *Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew an upright spirit within me*[^58]*.* What can be said against this agreement except by blind barkers?

## We are bidden to despise all sensible things, and to love God alone.

**20.** 37. The enticements of the body are situated in all those things that the bodily sense touches, which by some are also named sensible things; among which this common light especially excels, because among our very senses, which the soul uses through the body, nothing is to be preferred to the eyes; and therefore in the holy Scriptures all sensible things are denoted by the name of visible things.

And so in the New Testament we are forbidden the love of these things thus: *Not looking,* he says, *at the things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen. For the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal*[^59]*.* From which it can be understood how far from Christian are those who think the sun and moon are not only to be loved but even to be worshiped. For what do we see, if we do not see the sun and moon? But we have been forbidden to turn to the things that are seen. Therefore even these things are not to be loved by one who intends to offer to God that incorrupt love.

But there will be another place for me where these things will be inquired into more diligently. For I have set out now to speak not of faith but of life, through which we deserve to know what we believe. God alone, therefore, is to be loved; but all this world, that is, all sensible things, are to be despised, though to be used for the necessity of this life.

## Popular glory and curiosity condemned by the sacred Letters.

**21.** 38. Popular glory is thus cast away and despised in the New Testament: *If I wished,* he says, *to please men, I would not be the servant of Christ*[^60]*.* There is likewise another thing that the soul conceives about bodies through certain imaginings, and calls it the knowledge of things. For which reason we are rightly forbidden also to be inquisitive, which is a great office of temperance. Hence comes that saying, *Beware lest anyone lead you astray through philosophy.* And because the very name of philosophy, if it be considered, signifies a great thing, to be desired with the whole mind—since philosophy is the love and pursuit of wisdom—the Apostle most cautiously, lest he seem to deter us from the love of wisdom, added, *and the elements of this world*[^61]*.*

For there are those who, having deserted the virtues and not knowing what God is and how great is the majesty of the nature that always remains in the same way, think they are doing something great if they investigate, most curiously and most intently, this whole mass of body that we call the world. Whence is begotten so great a pride that they seem to themselves to dwell in the very heaven about which they often dispute. Let the soul, then, restrain itself from the desire of vain thinking of this kind, if it has resolved to keep itself chaste for God. For by such love it is for the most part deceived, so that either it thinks nothing exists but body, or, even if, moved by authority, it confesses that something incorporeal exists, it nevertheless cannot think about it except through bodily images, and believes it to be something such as the deceptive sense of the body forces upon it. To this also pertains what is enjoined, that we must beware *of idols.*

**21.** 39. To this authority of the New Testament, then, by which we are bidden to love nothing of this world[^62], that statement especially belongs in which it was said, *Do not be conformed to this world*[^63]; for it must at the same time be shown that everyone is conformed to the thing he loves; to this authority, then, if I should seek what to match from the Old Testament, I find many things indeed, but one book of Solomon, that which is called *Ecclesiastes*, most copiously brings all these things into the utmost contempt. For it begins thus: *Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. What abundance has a man in all his labor with which he labors under the sun?*[^64] If all these words are attended to, weighed, and examined, many things are found most necessary for those who desire to flee this world and to flee back to God; but it is long, and my discourse hastens to other things.

Yet, this beginning having been established, he goes on to set forth all of it: that those are vain who are deceived by things of this kind; and he calls the very thing by which they are deceived vanity, not because God did not create these things, but because human beings wish to subject themselves through sins to these things, which by the divine law were made subject to them through right deeds. For what else is it to be mocked and deceived by false goods than to judge that things lower than yourself are to be admired and desired?

The temperate man, then, has in mortal and fleeting things of this kind a rule of life confirmed by both Testaments: to love none of them, to think none of them to be desired for its own sake, but to make use of them, so far as is enough, for the necessity of this life and its offices, with the moderation of one who uses, not with the affection of one who loves. These things have been said about temperance briefly, considering the greatness of the matters, yet, considering the work undertaken, perhaps more copiously than was fitting.

## The love of God furnishes fortitude.

**22.** 40. About fortitude, however, not much need be said. For that love of which we speak, which ought to be inflamed toward God with all holiness, is called temperance in not desiring these things, and fortitude in losing them. But among all the things that are possessed in this life, the body is for a human being, by the most just laws of God, the heaviest bond, because of the ancient sin, than which nothing is better known for proclaiming, nothing more hidden for understanding.

This bond, then, lest it be shaken and harassed, shakes the soul with the terror of toil and pain, and lest it be taken away and destroyed, with the terror of death. For the soul loves it by the force of habit, not understanding that, if it used the body well and knowingly, its resurrection and reformation would, by divine help and law, be subjected to the soul's authority without any trouble; but when, with this love, the soul has turned itself wholly to God, once these things are known it will not only despise death but even desire it.

**22.** 41. But there remains a great struggle with pain. Yet nothing is so hard and iron-like that it is not conquered by the fire of love. When the soul snatches itself up into God by this love, it will fly above all torture, free and admirable, on the most beautiful and most whole wings on which chaste love rests for the embrace of God—unless indeed God will allow lovers of gold, lovers of praise, lovers of women to be stronger than his own lovers, though that be called not love but, more fittingly, desire or lust. In which, nevertheless, it appears how great is the impulse of the mind that strives toward the things it loves, bending its course through all manner of monstrous things; and it is an argument to us how all things are to be endured, lest we forsake God, if men endure so much for those things, only to forsake him.

## Counsels and examples of fortitude from the Scriptures.

**23.** 42. Why, then, should I here gather together the authorities of the New Testament, where it was said, *Tribulation works patience, patience proof, proof hope*[^65]*:* and not only said, but also proved and confirmed by the examples of those who said it? Rather from the Old Testament, against which they rage madly, I will call up an example of patience.

Nor will I call to mind that man by whom, amid great torments of the body and a horrible wasting of the limbs, not only are human things borne but divine things are even discussed. In whose every utterance it shines out clearly enough, if anyone attend with a fair mind, how lightly those things are to be valued which, when men wish to hold them by mastery, they themselves are rather held by through desire, and become slaves of mortal things when, ignorantly, they desire to be their masters[^66].

For he lost all his riches, and, made suddenly most poor, he kept a mind so unshaken and fixed in God that he sufficiently showed that those things had not been great to him, but he to them, while to him God was great. And if the men of our time could be of such a mind, we would not be greatly forbidden in the New Testament the possession of those things, in order that we might be perfect. For it is much more admirable not to cling to those things, although you possess them, than not to possess them at all.

**23.** 43. But since we are now treating of the bearing of pain and bodily torments, I leave that man, though great, though unconquered, a man nevertheless. For those Scriptures offer me a woman of astonishing fortitude, and now compel me to pass over to her. She, with her seven children, spent all her inward parts upon the tyrant and executioner sooner than one sacrilegious word, while by her exhortation her sons were strengthened, in whose limbs she herself was tortured—she, moreover, destined to die in her own turn, what she had bidden them to bear[^67].

What, I ask, can be added to so great a patience? Yet what wonder if the love of God, conceived in her every marrow, resisted both tyrant and executioner and pain and body and sex and affection? Had she not heard, *Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints*[^68]? Had she not heard, *Better is a patient man than the mightiest*[^69]? Had she not heard, *Whatever is laid upon you, accept, and in pain endure, and in your humiliation have patience; for gold and silver are tested in fire*[^70]? Had she not heard, *The furnace tests the potter's vessels, and the trial of tribulation just men*[^71]? Indeed she had taken in both these and many other things, which by the one Holy Spirit of God were written down—divine precepts of fortitude—in those books, which alone as yet existed, just as in these of the New Testament.

## On justice and prudence.

**24.** 44. What of justice, which pertains to God? Since both the Lord says, *You cannot serve two masters*[^72]*,* and the Apostle reproves those who serve *the creature rather than the Creator*[^73], was it not said before in the Old Testament, *You shall adore the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve*[^74]? But what need is there to say more on this point, since all things there are full of such statements? Justice, then, will give this rule of life to the lover of whom we are speaking: that he most willingly serve God, whom he loves, that is, the highest good, the highest wisdom, the highest peace; and that he govern all other things, partly as subject to himself, partly as to be made subject. This norm of living, as we have taught, is strengthened by the authority of both Testaments.

**24.** 45. Nor need we discuss prudence at any length, to which belongs the discerning of things to be desired and things to be avoided. If this is lacking, none of the things already spoken of can be accomplished. To it belong the watch and the most diligent vigilance, lest by an evil persuasion creeping up little by little we be deceived,

whence often the Lord cries out, *Watch*[^75]*,* and, *Walk,* he says, *lest the darkness overtake you*[^76]*.* And likewise it is said, *Do you not know that a little leaven corrupts the whole lump?*[^77]

But what can be brought forward more plainly from the Old Testament against that drowsiness of the mind, through which it comes about that we do not perceive ruin creeping, as it were, by little bits, than what was said by the prophet, *He who despises small things will fall away little by little*[^78]?

About which statement, if it were opportune for those who are hastening, I would discuss it most copiously, and the office now undertaken by us would demand it; perhaps we would demonstrate how deep are the mysteries which most ignorant and sacrilegious men, by deriding them, do not indeed fall away little by little, but go headlong with a great fall.

## The offices of the four virtues in relation to the love of God . . .

**25.** 46. What more should I discuss about morals? For if God is the highest good of a human being, which you cannot deny, it surely follows—since to desire the highest good is to live well—that to live well is nothing other than to love God with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind, from whom it comes about that the love of him is kept incorrupt and entire, which belongs to temperance; that it be broken by no troubles, which belongs to fortitude; that it serve no other, which belongs to justice; that it watch in discerning things, lest deceit or guile creep up little by little, which belongs to prudence. This is the one perfection of a human being, by which alone he obtains that he may enjoy the sincerity of truth; this is sung to us by both Testaments; this is urged upon us on this side and on that. Why do you still weave slander against Scriptures that you do not know? With how great ignorance do you assail books which only those find fault with who do not understand, and which only those are unable to understand who find fault! For no enemy is allowed to know them, nor can anyone, except a friend, know them.

## . . . the reward of which love is eternal life and the knowledge of truth.

**25.** 47. Let us love God, then, with the whole heart, with the whole soul, with the whole mind, whoever of us have purposed to arrive at eternal life. For eternal life is the whole reward, in the promise of which we rejoice, nor can the reward precede the merits and be given to a person before he is worthy. For what is more unjust than this, and what more just than God? We ought not, then, to demand the reward before we deserve to receive it. Here perhaps it is not unfitting to ask what eternal life itself is. But let us rather hear its bestower: *This is,* he says, *eternal life, that they know you the true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent*[^79]*.* Eternal life, therefore, is the very knowledge of truth. For which reason, see how perverse and preposterous are those who think they hand on the knowledge of God in order that we may be perfect, when it is itself the reward of the perfect. What, then, is to be done—what, I ask, except that we first love with full love the very one whom we wish to know? Whence arises that which we treated of sufficiently from the beginning, that nothing is done more wholesomely in the Catholic Church than that authority should precede reason.

## Love of self and of neighbor.

**26.** 48. But let us look at the rest, for it seems that nothing has been treated about the human being himself, that is, about the lover himself; but whoever judges so understands too little clearly. For it cannot happen that one who loves God does not love himself; indeed, only the one who loves God knows how to love himself.

For he loves himself well enough who diligently acts so as to enjoy the highest and true good; and if this is nothing other than God, as the things that have been said have taught, who can doubt that the lover of God loves himself? What? Among human beings themselves, ought there to be no bond of love? Indeed there ought, so much so that no surer step toward the love of God is believed possible than the love of one human being toward another.

**26.** 49. Let the Lord himself, then, bring forth for us the second precept, when asked about the precepts of life; for he was not content with one, knowing that God is one thing and the human being another, and that there is as much difference as between him who created and that which was created to the likeness of the Creator. He says, then, that the second precept is, *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^80]*.* But you love yourself wholesomely if you love God more than yourself.

What, then, you do for yourself, that is to be done for your neighbor; that is, that he too may love God with perfect love. For you do not love him as yourself if you do not lead him to that good toward which you yourself strive. For that is the one good which does not become cramped for all who strive toward it with you. From this precept are born the offices of human society, in which it is difficult not to err. But we must act, above all, that we be benevolent, that is, that we use no malice, no evil guile against another. For what is nearer to a human being than a human being?

**26.** 50. Hear also what Paul says: *The love of neighbor,* he says, *works no evil*[^81]*.* I use the briefest testimonies, but, unless I am mistaken, fitting ones and such as satisfy the matter undertaken; for who does not know how many words, and of how great weight, are spread everywhere in those books concerning the love of neighbor? But since one sins against another in two ways—in one, if he harms him; in the other, if, when he can, he does not help him—and since these very things are those by which men are called evil, neither of which the one who loves does, this statement, I think, sufficiently demonstrates what we wish: *The love of neighbor works no evil*[^82]*.*

And if we cannot arrive at good things unless we cease to work evil, these are, as it were, the cradle of the love of God, by which we love our neighbor, so that, since *the love of neighbor works no evil,* from this we may ascend to what was said, *We know that for those who love God all things work toward good*[^83]*.*

**26.** 51. But somehow these either rise together into fullness and perfection, or the love of God is begun first and the love of neighbor perfected first. For to begin, perhaps the divine love snatches us more quickly into itself, but we more easily perfect the lesser things. However the matter stands, this above all must be held: that no one believe he will come to happiness, and to the God he loves, with his neighbor despised[^84]. And would that, as it is easy for a well-instructed and kindly person to love his neighbor, so it were easy either to look out for him or to do him no harm. For a good will is not enough for these things, but there is need of a certain great reason and prudence, which no one can use unless God, that fountain of all goods, has bestowed it. About which most difficult matter, as I judge, we have tried to say, in keeping with the work undertaken, only a few things, placing all our hope in him whose gifts alone these are.

## Beneficence toward the body of one's neighbor.

**27.** 52. A human being, then, as he appears to a human being, is a rational soul using a mortal and earthly body. Partly to the body, then, and partly to the soul of a person does the one who loves his neighbor do good. What pertains to the body is named *medicine,* what to the soul, *instruction.* But by medicine I now mean whatever at all either guards or restores the health of the body. To this, then, pertain not only those things that the art of those properly called physicians provides, but also food and drink, clothing and shelter, and finally all defense and protection by which our body is preserved even against external blows and accidents; for hunger and thirst and cold and heat and whatever is gravely inflicted from outside do not allow the health of which we now speak to remain.

**27.** 53. Therefore all those who dutifully and humbly provide the things by which evils or troubles of this kind are resisted are called merciful, even if they should be so wise as to be no longer disturbed by any grief of mind. For who would not know that mercy is so named from the fact that it makes the heart wretched (*miserum cor*) in one who grieves over another's evil? And who would not grant that the wise person ought to be free from all wretchedness, when he comes to the aid of the needy, when he gives food to the hungry and drink to the thirsty, when he clothes the naked, when he receives the stranger under his roof, when he frees the oppressed, when finally he extends his humanity even to the burial of the dead? Even if he does this with a tranquil mind, prompted by no stings of grief, but led by the office of goodness, he is nonetheless to be called merciful. For the name does him no harm, since wretchedness is absent.

**27.** 54. But the foolish, when they shun mercy as if it were a vice, because they cannot be sufficiently moved by duty if they are not also admonished by a disturbance of feeling, freeze over rather with the rigidity of inhumanity than are made serene by the tranquillity of reason. And so, much more prudently, even God himself is called merciful[^85]; in what way he is so called it remains for those to understand who have shown themselves fit by religion and zeal, lest, by using the words of the learned ineptly, we cause the souls of the unlearned to harden first by avoiding mercy rather than to grow gentle by seeking kindness. And as mercy bids us drive these troubles away from another person, so innocence forbids us to inflict them.

## Beneficence toward the soul of one's neighbor. The two parts of instruction: restraint and teaching. Through good morals the recognition of truth comes to us.

**28.** 55. But as for what pertains to instruction, through which health is restored to the soul itself—without which that health of the body avails nothing for driving away miseries—it is altogether a most difficult matter. And as we were saying in the case of the body, that it is one thing to cure diseases and wounds, which few people can do well, but another thing to relieve hunger and thirst and the rest, in which it is commonly and everywhere granted that one person help another, so in the soul there are certain things in which that excellent and rare expertise is not much needed, as when we exhort and admonish that these very things be provided to the needy which we said are to be provided to the body. For when we do these things, we help the body by our means; but when we teach that they be done, we help the soul by instruction.

But there are other things by which the manifold and varied diseases of souls are healed by a certain great and altogether ineffable method; which medicine, unless it were sent to the peoples from God, there would be no hope of health for those sinning with so unrestrained an advance—although even that medicine of the body, if you trace the origin of things more deeply, is not found to have been able to flow to human beings except from God, to whom the standing and health of all things is to be attributed.

**28.** 56. Yet this instruction of which we now treat, which is the medicine of the soul, is, so far as may be gathered from the divine Scriptures themselves, divided into two: restraint and teaching. Restraint is accomplished by fear, teaching by love—I mean the love of the one who is helped by the instruction; for the one who helps has none of these two except to love. In these two, God himself, by whose goodness and clemency it comes about that we are anything at all, has given us a rule of instruction in the two Testaments, the Old and the New. For although each is in each, fear nevertheless prevails in the Old, love in the New; what there is slavery, here is proclaimed by the Apostles as freedom.

Of the admirable order and divine harmony of these Testaments it would take very long to speak, and many religious and learned men have spoken of it. This matter demands many books, that it may be set forth and proclaimed according to its worth, so far as a human being can. The one, then, who *loves his neighbor* acts, as far as he can, that he may be sound in body and sound in soul; but the care of the body is to be referred to the health of the soul. He acts, then, by these steps, as to what pertains to the soul: that he first fear, then love, God. These are the best morals, through which there comes to us also that *recognition of truth* to which we are snatched by all our zeal.

## The precepts of love.

**28.** 57. And in these two I agree with the Manicheans, that is, that we love God and neighbor; but they deny that this is contained in the Old Testament; and how greatly they err in this appears sufficiently, I think, from the statements concerning each that we brought forward above.

But to say something brief, yet such that to resist it would be astonishing madness: do they not notice that these very two things, which they are compelled to praise—although they deny that they were most aptly brought forward by the Lord in the Gospel from the Old Testament—are written there: *You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart and with your whole soul and with your whole mind*[^86], and that other, *You shall love your neighbor as yourself*[^87]?

Or if they do not dare to deny these things—for they are pressed by the light of truth—let them dare to deny that these precepts are wholesome; let them deny, if they can, that the best morals are contained in them, and say that God ought not to be loved, that the neighbor ought not to be loved, and that for those who love God not all things work toward good[^88], and that the love of neighbor does not work no evil[^89]; by which two things human life is most wholesomely and best ordered.

If they say these things, there is no reasoning to be had with them, not only as with Christians, but not even as with human beings. But if they do not dare to say these things and are compelled to confess that they are divine, why do they not cease, with wicked impiety, to assail and condemn the books from which these things were brought forward?

**28.** 58. Or will they say that it is not a consequence that all things there should be good, where we were able to find these? For this they are accustomed to say. To which evasion what I should reply, how I should meet it, I do not easily see. Should I examine the individual words of the Old Testament so as to demonstrate to the obstinate and unlearned that in them there is the highest concord with the Gospel? But when will that be? When will either I suffice or will they have the patience? What, then, shall I do? Shall I desert the cause and allow them to lie hidden in an opinion that, though wicked and false, is nevertheless difficult to refute? I will not do so; God himself, whose those precepts are, will be present from close at hand, and will not allow me, in such straits, to be helpless and forsaken.

## On the authority of the Scriptures.

**29.** 59. Therefore be present in mind, Manicheans, if any of you are perhaps so held by that superstition that you can at some time escape. Be present, I say, and without stubbornness, without zeal for resisting; for otherwise it is most ruinous for you to judge. For surely it is doubtful to no one—nor are you so turned away from the truth as not to understand—that, if to love God and neighbor is good, which no one can deny, whatever hangs upon these two precepts cannot rightly be blamed. And what hangs upon them, it is ridiculous if you think it should be asked of me; hear Christ himself, hear, I say, *Christ*, hear *the Wisdom of God*: *On these two precepts,* he says, *the whole law hangs, and all the prophets*[^90]*.*

**29.** 60. What can you say at this point, most shameless stubbornness? That Christ did not say this? In the Gospel these words of his are written down. That what is written is false? What more impious than this sacrilege can be found? What more shameless than this utterance? What more audacious? What more wicked? The worshipers of idols, who hate even the name of Christ, have never dared to say this against those Scriptures. For there will follow the utter overthrow of all literature and the abolition of all the books committed to memory, if what has been strengthened by so great a religion of peoples, confirmed by so great an agreement of men and ages, is brought into this doubt, so that it cannot retain even the credit and weight of common history. Finally, what will you be able to bring forward from any Scriptures, where I may not be allowed to use this utterance, if it is brought forward against my own reasoning and intention?

**29.** 61. But who could bear this, that they forbid us to believe books most well known and now in everyone's hands, and command us to believe the things they themselves bring forward?

If we must doubt about a writing, about which one more than about that which has not deserved to be defamed, which could truly have lied wholly under another name?

If you press that writing on me against my will, and by the reasoning of an exaggerated authority force me to faith, shall I, wretched, doubt about that Scripture which I see constantly and most widely spread abroad and fortified by the witness of the Churches scattered throughout the whole world—and, what is more wretched, shall I doubt at your instigation?

Whereas, if you were to bring forward other copies, I ought not to hold any but those that are commended by the agreement of the greater number; now, with you bringing forward nothing to compare except a most empty utterance, most full of rashness, will you think the human race so perverse and forsaken by the help of divine providence as to prefer to those Scriptures, not other ones brought forward by you by which they may be refuted, but your words alone?

For you must bring forward another manuscript containing the same things, but nevertheless uncorrupted and more accurate, in which there are lacking only those things that you charge to have been interpolated here. So that, if, for example, you contend that Paul's epistle written to the Romans is corrupted, you should bring forward another, uncorrupted, or rather another manuscript, in which the same epistle of the same Apostle is written down pure and uncorrupted. I will not do so, you say, lest I myself be believed to have corrupted it; for this you are accustomed to say, and you say it truly. Nothing else at all will even moderately sensible people suspect, if you do this.

See, then, you yourself, what you have judged about your own authority, and understand whether they ought to believe your words against those Scriptures, if to believe a manuscript merely because it is brought forward by you is the mark of great rashness.

## An apostrophe to the Church, teacher of all wisdom. The teaching of the Catholic Church.

**30.** 62. But why more on this point? For who would not see that those who dare to say these things against the Christian Scriptures, even if they are not what people suspect, are certainly at any rate not Christians? For to Christians this form of living has been given: that we love the Lord our God with our whole heart, with our whole soul, with our whole mind[^91], and then our neighbor as ourselves[^92]. For *on these two precepts the whole law hangs, and all the prophets*[^93]*.* Rightly, O Catholic Church, most true mother of Christians, do you proclaim that not only God himself, the attaining of whom is the most happy life, is to be worshiped most purely and chastely, bringing in no creature to be adored by us, to whom we should be bidden to be in servitude—and excluding from that incorrupt and inviolable eternity, to which alone a human being is to be made subject, to which alone the rational soul by cleaving is not wretched, everything that has been made, that is liable to change, that is subject to time; neither confounding what eternity, what truth, what finally peace itself distinguishes, nor again separating what the one majesty joins—but also do you embrace the love and charity of neighbor in such a way that with you there prevails every medicine for the varied diseases by which souls are sick for their sins.

**30.** 63. You train and teach children childishly, young people strongly, the old quietly, according to the age not only of each one's body but of his soul.

You make wives subject to their husbands, by a chaste and faithful obedience, not for the satisfying of lust but for the propagating of offspring and for the fellowship of the household.

You set husbands over their wives, by the laws of sincere love, not for mocking the weaker sex[^94].

You join children to their parents by a certain free servitude, and set parents over their children by a devoted rule.

You bind brother to brother by the bond of religion, stronger and tighter than that of blood.

You bind together every nearness of kindred and bond of affinity, with the ties of nature and of will preserved, by mutual love.

You teach servants to cling to their masters not so much by the necessity of their condition as by the delight of duty.

You make masters gentle toward their servants, by consideration of the most high God, their common Lord, and more inclined to look out for them than to coerce them.

You join citizens to citizens, nations to nations, and human beings to human beings altogether—by the remembrance of our first parents—not only in fellowship but in a certain brotherhood as well.

You teach kings to provide for their peoples; you admonish peoples to submit to their kings. To whom honor is owed, to whom affection, to whom reverence, to whom fear, to whom consolation, to whom admonition, to whom exhortation, to whom instruction, to whom rebuke, to whom punishment, you diligently teach, showing how not all things are owed to all, but love to all, and injury to none.

**30.** 64. But now, when this human love has nourished and strengthened the soul that clings to your breasts, and it has been made fit to follow God, when his majesty has begun to disclose itself so far as suffices for a human being while he dwells on this earth, there is born so great an ardor of love and there rises so great a blaze of divine love that, with all the vices burned away and the person purified and sanctified, it appears well enough how divinely it was said, *I am a consuming fire,* and, *I came to cast fire upon the world*[^95]*.*

These two utterances of the one God, marked in the two Testaments, declare by their concordant witness the sanctification of the soul, so that at some time that may come to pass which likewise was taken up into the New Scripture from the Old: *Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O death, is your striving?*[^96] If only these heretics had the strength to understand this one thing, they would surely nowhere venerate God except with you and in your bosom, not at all proud and well at peace.

Rightly with you are the divine precepts kept widely and broadly.

Rightly with you is it well understood how much graver it is to sin against a known than against an unknown law. *For the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law*[^97]*,* by how much more gravely the consciousness of a despised precept strikes and slays.

Rightly with you has it been seen how vain is action under the law, when lust lays waste the soul and is held in check by the fear of punishment, not overwhelmed by the love of virtue.

Rightly with you are there so many hospitable, many merciful, many dutiful, many learned, many chaste, many holy, many so burning with the love of God that even solitude delights them, in the highest continence and an incredible contempt of this world.

## He sets against the continence of the Manicheans the life of the anchorites and cenobites.

**31.** 65. What is it, I ask, that those see who cannot help loving a human being and yet can help seeing a human being? Surely, whatever that is, it is more excellent than human things, by the contemplation of which a human being can live without a human being. For now hear, Manicheans, the morals and singular continence of perfect Christians, to whom the highest chastity has seemed not only to be praised but even to be taken up, lest you dare to boast shamelessly before the minds of the unlearned, as of an abstinence most difficult of all things, if there is any shame in you. Nor will I tell the things you do not know, but the things you hide from us. For who does not know that the multitude of Christian men of the highest continence is spread day by day more and more throughout the whole world, and especially in the East and in Egypt, which can in no way be hidden from you?

## Anchorites.

**31.** 66. I will say nothing about those whom I mentioned a little before, who, most secluded, utterly removed from all sight of human beings, content with bread alone—which is brought to them at spaced intervals of time—and with water, inhabit the most deserted lands, enjoying the conversation of God, to whom they have clung with pure minds, and most happy in the contemplation of his beauty, which can be perceived only by the understanding of the holy. Nothing, I say, will I speak about these; for to some they seem to have deserted human affairs more than they ought, not understanding how much their mind profits us in their prayers and their life as an example, though we are not permitted to see their bodies.

But to discuss this I think long and superfluous; for who can make this so excellent height of holiness, which to anyone seems of its own accord worthy of wonder and honor, seem so by our discourse? Only these men are to be admonished, who vainly boast of themselves, that the temperance and continence of the most holy Christians of the Catholic faith has advanced so far that it seems to some to need restraining and, as it were, to be recalled to human limits; so far have those men's souls escaped above the level of human beings that they are so judged even by those to whom this is displeasing.

## Cenobites.

**31.** 67. But if this exceeds our endurance, who would not admire and proclaim those who, having despised and deserted the enticements of this world, gathered into a common life most holy and most chaste, spend their time together, living in prayers, in readings, in discussions, swollen by no pride, troubled by no obstinacy, made livid by no envy, but modest, reverent, peaceable, offering a most concordant life, most intent upon God, as a most welcome gift to him from whom they have deserved to be able to do these things? No one possesses anything of his own; no one is a burden to anyone. They work with their hands at things by which the body can be fed and the mind not hindered from God.

They hand over their work to those whom they call deans, because they are set over ten, so that the care of his own body may touch none of them, neither in food nor in clothing nor if anything else is needed either for daily necessity or for changed health, as happens. But those deans, arranging all things with great solicitude and providing at hand whatever that life requires on account of the weakness of the body, themselves nevertheless also render account to one, whom they call father. These fathers, however, not only most holy in morals but also most excellent in divine doctrine, lofty in all things, look out with no pride for those whom they call sons, with great authority of their own in commanding, with great willingness of the others in obeying. At the end of the day they come together, each from his own dwelling, while still fasting, to hear that father; and there gather to each father at least three thousand men, for there are even far more numerous ones living under one. They listen with incredible zeal, in the deepest silence, signifying the affections of their minds, as the discourse of the speaker has moved them, by groaning or by weeping or by a modest joy free of all clamor.

Then the body is refreshed, as much as is enough for soundness and health, each one restraining his desire lest he pour himself out even on the very things at hand, which are sparing and most cheap. Thus they abstain not only from flesh and from wine, for the sufficient taming of lusts, but also from those things that the more keenly provoke the appetite of belly and throat the cleaner they seem to some; under which name the base craving for choice foods, which is foreign to flesh meats, is wont to be ridiculously and basely defended.

Indeed whatever is left over from necessary sustenance—for very much is left over from the works of their hands and from the restriction of meals—is distributed to the needy with greater care than it was acquired by those who distribute it. For in no way do they busy themselves that these things should abound for them, but in every way they act that nothing of what has been left over should remain with them, so much so that they even send laden ships to those places that needy people inhabit. There is no need to say more about a most well-known matter.

## Holy women.

**31.** 68. This too is the life of women serving God solicitously and chastely, who, separated in their dwellings and removed from men as far as is fitting, are joined to them only by devout love and by the imitation of virtue; to whom there is no access of young men, nor even of the gravest and most approved old men, except as far as the vestibule, for the sake of providing the necessities they need. For by wool-working they exercise and sustain the body, and hand over the very garments to the brothers, taking from them in turn what is needed for sustenance. These morals, this life, this order, this institution, if I should wish to praise it, I am not worthily able to, and I fear lest I should seem to judge that what is set forth merely by itself cannot please, if I should think that, beyond the simplicity of the narrator, the buskin of the praiser too must be added. These things, Manicheans, find fault with, if you can. Do not parade our weeds before blind men, unable to discern.

## Praise of the clergy.

**32.** 69. Yet the best morals of the Catholic Church are not so narrowly confined that I should think only the life of those whom I have mentioned is to be praised. For how many bishops, most excellent and most holy men, have I known; how many presbyters, how many deacons and ministers of every kind of the divine sacraments, whose virtue seems to me the more admirable and worthy of greater proclamation the more difficult it is to preserve it in the manifold kind of human beings and in this more turbulent life. For they preside over people not so much already healed as still to be healed. The vices of the multitude must be borne with, that they may be cured, and the pestilence must be tolerated before it is quieted. It is most difficult here to keep the best measure of life and a mind peaceable and tranquil. For, to put it briefly, these act where life is learned, those where it is lived.

## Another kind of those living together in the city. Three-day fasts.

**33.** 70. Nor for that reason, however, would I despise that praiseworthy kind of Christians, namely those who dwell in cities most removed from the common way of life. I myself saw at Milan a lodging of saints, of not a few men, over whom one presbyter presided, a man most excellent and most learned. At Rome too I knew several, in which individuals excelling in gravity and prudence and divine knowledge preside over the rest dwelling with them, who live in Christian love, holiness, and freedom; nor are they themselves a burden to anyone, but, after the manner of the East and by the authority of the apostle Paul, they support themselves by their own hands. I have learned too that many practice fasts altogether incredible—not refreshing the body once daily toward nightfall, which is everywhere most customary, but very often passing a continuous three days or more without food and drink. And this not only among men but even among women; over whom likewise, many widows and virgins living together and seeking their living by wool and weaving, individual women preside, most grave and most approved, skilled and ready not only in instructing and ordering morals but also in instructing minds.

**33.** 71. And amid these things no one is pressed into harsh things that he cannot bear, nothing he refuses is imposed on him, nor is he for that reason condemned by the rest because he confesses himself unable to imitate them in these things; for they remember how greatly love is commended in all the Scriptures; they remember, *All things are clean to the clean*[^98] and, *Not what enters into your mouth defiles you, but what comes out*[^99]*.* And so all their industry watches not over the rejecting of kinds of food as if polluted, but over the thorough taming of desire and the keeping of the love of the brothers.

They remember, *Food is for the belly and the belly for food; but God will destroy both this and these*[^100]; and elsewhere, *Neither if we eat shall we abound, nor if we do not eat shall we be in want*[^101]; and that above the rest, *It is good, brothers, not to eat flesh nor to drink wine nor anything by which your brother is offended*[^102]*.*

For here he shows how all these things are to be directed to the end of love. *For one believes he may eat all things; but he who is weak,* he says, *let him eat vegetables. Let him who eats not despise him who does not eat; and let him who does not eat not judge him who eats, for God receives him. Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own Lord he stands or falls; but he will stand, for God is able to make him stand*[^103]*.*

And a little later, *He who eats, eats unto the Lord and gives thanks to God; and he who does not eat, does not eat unto the Lord, and gives thanks to God*[^104]*.*

And likewise, in what follows, *So then each one of us will render account for himself. Let us therefore no longer judge one another; but judge this rather, that you place no offense or stumbling block before a brother. I know and am confident in the Lord Jesus that nothing is common of itself; but to him who reckons anything to be common, to him it is common*[^105]*.*

Could he have shown more clearly that there is a certain power, not in the very things we eat, but in the mind, which avails to defile it; and that therefore, even to those who are fit to despise these things and know for certain that they are not polluted if they take any foods without base craving, with loftiness of mind, love must nevertheless be kept in view?

See what follows: *For if your brother is grieved on account of food, you no longer walk according to love*[^106]*.*

**33.** 72. Read the rest—for it is long to insert it all—and you will find a precept that those who can despise these things, that is, the stronger and more secure, ought nevertheless to be temperate, lest those be offended who, on account of their own weakness, still need temperance of this kind. These things those of whom I was speaking know and hold, for they are Christians, not heretics; they understand the Scriptures according to the apostolic teaching, not according to a proud and fabricated name of an apostle.

No one despises the one who does not eat, no one judges the one who eats; he who is weak eats vegetables. Yet many of the strong do the same thing for the sake of the weak; for many there is not this reason for doing it, but that it pleases them to live on a cheaper diet and to pass a most tranquil time with the least costly sustenance of the body. *For all things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any*[^107]*.* So many neither eat flesh nor yet think it unclean by superstition. And so these same who, when well, are temperate, if a reason of health compels, when sick accept it without any dread.

Many do not drink wine, nor yet think themselves defiled by it; for they cause it to be provided, most humanely and most modestly, to certain feebler persons and to all who cannot without it obtain the health of the body. And they fraternally admonish some who foolishly refuse it, lest by a vain superstition they become weaker rather than holier. They read to them the Apostle enjoining his disciple to take a little wine on account of his various infirmities[^108]. So they diligently practice piety, but they know that bodily exercise, as the same Apostle says, pertains to a brief time[^109].

**33.** 73. Those, then, who can—and they are innumerable—restrain themselves both from flesh and from wine for two reasons: either on account of the weakness of the brothers or on account of their own freedom. Love above all is kept; to love is the food fitted, to love the speech, to love the dress, to love the countenance; they come together into one love and conspire toward it; to violate this is held a sacrilege, like violating God; if anything resists it, it is overcome and cast out; if anything offends it, it is not allowed to last a single day. They know that this was commended by Christ and the Apostles, so that if this one thing is lacking, all things are empty, and if this is present, all things are full.

## The Church is not to be blamed for the morals of bad Christians.

**34.** 74. Withstand these men, Manicheans, if you can; look upon them; name them, if you dare, without lying and with reproach; compare your fasts with their fasts, chastity with their chastity, your clothing with their clothing, your meals with their meals, modesty with their modesty, love finally with their love, and—what the matter most demands—precepts with their precepts. Now you will see what difference there is between ostentation and sincerity, between the right way and error, between faith and deceit, between strength and swelling, between happiness and misery, between unity and division, finally between the sirens of superstition and the harbor of religion.

## Worshipers of tombs and of pictures.

**34.** 75. Do not gather up for me professors of the Christian name who neither know nor display the force of their profession. Do not pursue the crowds of the unlearned, who either are superstitious even in true religion or are so given to lusts that they have forgotten what they promised to God. I know there are many worshipers of tombs and of pictures. I know there are many who drink most luxuriously over the dead, and, offering banquets to corpses, bury themselves over the buried, and credit their gluttonies and drunkennesses to religion. I know there are many who have renounced this world in words and yet wish to be weighed down by all the masses of this world, and rejoice to be weighed down.

Nor is it any wonder, in so great an abundance of peoples, that there are not lacking those by whose blamed life you deceive the incautious and turn them away from Catholic salvation, since in your fewness you suffer great straits when even one of those whom you call the elect is demanded of you, who keeps those very precepts that you defend with irrational superstition. But how vain those precepts are, how harmful, how sacrilegious, and how they are not observed by a great part of you, and indeed almost by all of you, I have set out to show in another volume.

**34.** 76. Now I admonish you this: that you at last cease to revile the Catholic Church by blaming the morals of people whom she herself also condemns, and whom she daily strives to correct as bad sons. But whoever of them are corrected by a good will and by the help of God recover, by repenting, what they had lost by sinning. But those who, by an evil will, persevere in their former vices, or even add graver ones to the former, are indeed allowed to be in the field of the Lord and to grow with the good seed, but a time will come when the weeds are separated. Or if, on account of the Christian name itself, they are now to be judged to be rather among the chaff than among the thorns, there will also come one who cleanses the threshing floor, and separates the chaff from the grain, and distributes to each part, with the highest fairness, what is fitting according to each one's merit[^110].

## Marriage and possessions are granted by the Apostle even to the baptized.

**35.** 77. Meanwhile, why do you rage, why are you blinded by partisan zeal? Why are you entangled in the long defense of so great an error? Seek the fruits in the field, the grain on the threshing floor; they will easily appear and offer themselves to those who seek. Why do you fix your eye too much upon the refuse? Why do you frighten away unlearned people, by the roughness of the hedges, from the richness of a fertile garden? There is a sure entrance, though known to fewer, by which one can enter, which you either do not believe to exist or do not wish to find.

There are in the Catholic Church innumerable faithful who do not use this world, there are those who use it as though not using it[^111], as is said by the Apostle; and this was already proved in those times in which Christians were forced to the worship of idols. For how many moneyed men then, how many heads of country households, how many merchants, how many soldiers, how many leading men of their own cities, how many senators finally, of both sexes, leaving all these vain and temporal things—by which, although they used them, they were not held fast—underwent death for the sake of a wholesome faith and religion, and showed unbelievers that all those things were possessed by them rather than that they were possessed by them.

**35.** 78. Why do you slanderously charge that the faithful, now renewed by baptism, ought not to beget children and possess fields and houses and any money? Paul permits this. For—and this cannot be denied—he wrote to the faithful; indeed, after the enumeration of many vicious persons who will not possess the kingdom of God, he says, *And these things indeed you were, but you have been washed, but you have been sanctified, but you have been justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God*[^112]*.* No one will dare to understand the washed and sanctified to be any but the faithful, and those who have renounced this world. But since he has shown to whom he wrote, let us see whether he permits them those things.

For it follows thus: *All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient; all things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any. Food is for the belly and the belly for food; but God will destroy both these and this. But the body is not for fornication but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. And God raised up the Lord and will also raise us up by his power. Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? Shall I then take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot? God forbid. Do you not know that he who clings to a harlot becomes one body? For the two, he says, shall be in one flesh. But he who clings to the Lord is one spirit. Flee fornication. Every sin that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body. Do you not know that your members are a temple of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought with a great price. Glorify and bear God in your body*[^113]*.*

*But concerning the things about which you wrote to me: it is good for a man not to touch a woman; but on account of incontinence let each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband. Let the husband render the debt to his wife, and likewise also the wife to her husband. The woman does not have power over her own body, but the husband. And likewise the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife. Do not defraud one another, except perhaps by consent for a time, that you may be free for prayer; and again return to the same, lest Satan tempt you on account of your incontinence. But this I say by way of indulgence, not by way of command. I wish that all men were as I myself; but each one has his own gift from God, one in this way, another in that*[^114]*.*

**35.** 79. Does the Apostle seem to you to have sufficiently both shown the strong what is highest and permitted the weaker what is next to it? For not to touch a woman, he shows to be highest, when he says, *I would that all men were as I myself.* But next to this highest is conjugal chastity, lest a man be laid waste by fornication. Did he therefore say that these are not yet faithful, because they use their spouses? Since by this chastity of marriage he says that even those who are joined are sanctified by each other, if one of them be an unbeliever, and that the offspring born of it is sanctified: *For the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the believing wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified through the believing husband; otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy*[^115]*.* Why do you clamor with stubbornness against so great a truth? Why do you try to cloud the light of the Scriptures with empty shadows?

**35.** 80. Do not say any longer that it is lawful for catechumens to use their spouses, but not lawful for the faithful; that it is lawful for catechumens to have money, but not lawful for the faithful. For there are many too who use as though not using. And by that sacrosanct bath the renewal of the new man is begun, so that, by making progress, it is brought to completion in some more quickly, in others more slowly; yet by many the advance into the new life is made, if anyone attends not with hostility but diligently.

For the Apostle himself says thus: *Though our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day*[^116]*.* The Apostle says that the inner man is renewed from day to day so that he may be brought to completion, and you want him to begin from completion. Would that you wished this; but you seek rather, not whence to raise up the weak, but whence to deceive the incautious. For you ought not to have said these things with such audacity, not even if it were established that you yourselves are perfect in those very trifling commandments of yours.

But since your conscience knows that those whom you introduce into your sect, when they have begun to be more familiarly joined to you, will find many things which, while you were accusing others, no one suspected to be in you—what so great shamelessness is this, to seek perfection in the weaker Catholics, in order to turn the unlearned away from there, and in no way to display it, with you, to those whom you have turned away? But lest we now seem to pour anything out against you rashly, let this be the limit of this volume, that we may at last come to setting forth the precepts of your life and your memorable morals.
