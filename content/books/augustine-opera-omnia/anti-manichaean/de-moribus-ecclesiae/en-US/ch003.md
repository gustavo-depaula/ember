# Book II: On the Manichean Way of Life

## The highest good is that to which it belongs to be in the highest degree.

**1.** 1. I think it is doubtful to no one that, when the question concerns goods and evils, this kind of question pertains to the moral discipline in which we are engaged in this discourse. For which reason I would indeed wish that people should bring to the investigating of these things a mental gaze so serene that they could see that highest good, than which there is nothing better and higher, to which the rational soul, pure and perfect, is made subject. For once this is understood and discerned, they would at the same time see that it is what is most rightly said to be in the highest degree and primordially. For that is to be said to be in the highest degree which always holds itself in the same way, which is in every way like itself, which can in no part be corrupted and changed, which is not subject to time, which cannot hold itself otherwise now than it held itself before. For that is what is most truly said to be. For there underlies this word the signification of a nature remaining in itself and holding itself unchangeably. This we can call nothing other than God, to whom, if you rightly seek a contrary, there is nothing at all. For being has no contrary except not-being. There is therefore no nature contrary to God.

But since to the contemplating of these things we bring a gaze of mind wounded and dulled by trifling opinions and by the depravity of the will, let us try as much as we can to arrive at some sort of knowledge of so great a matter, step by step and cautiously, seeking not as those who see are wont, but as those who grope.

## The Manicheans say most truly that evil is what is contrary to nature . . .

**2.** 2. Often, and indeed almost always, Manicheans, you ask of those to whom you strive to persuade your heresy whence evil is. Suppose that I have now fallen in with you for the first time; let me obtain something from you, if you please: that you too, laying aside for a little while the opinion by which you suppose yourselves to know these things, should try, like beginners, to track down so great a matter with me.

You ask me whence evil is; but I in turn ask you what evil is. Whose inquiry is the juster? That of those who ask whence is the thing of which they do not know what it is, or that of one who thinks it must first be asked what it is, lest the origin of an unknown thing be sought—which is absurd? Most truly, you say; for who is so blind in mind as not to see that to each kind that is evil which is contrary to its nature? But once this is established, your heresy is overturned; for no nature is evil, if that which is contrary to nature is evil. But you assert that a certain nature and substance is evil. There is added also this, that whatever is contrary to nature certainly opposes nature and strives to destroy it. It tends, then, to make what exists not to exist. For nature itself is nothing other than that which is understood to be something in its own kind. And so, just as we, by a new word, from that which is to be (*esse*), call essence—which we for the most part also name substance—so the ancients, who did not have these words, called nature in place of essence and substance. The very evil itself, then—if, apart from stubbornness, you are willing to attend—is to fall away from essence and to tend toward not-being.

## . . . but from this their heresy is subverted.

**2.** 3. For which reason, when in the Catholic Church it is said that God is the author of all natures and substances, it is at the same time understood, by those who can understand these things, that God is not the author of evil. For how can he who is the cause that all things which exist do exist, be in turn the cause that they do not exist, that is, that they fall away from essence and tend toward not-being? For most true reason cries out that this is evil in general. But that race of evil of yours, which you wish to be the highest evil, how will it be contrary to nature, that is, contrary to substance, when you say that it is itself a nature and substance? For if it acts against itself, it takes away its own being from itself; and if it should accomplish this, then at last it would arrive at the highest evil. But it will not accomplish it, because you wish it not only to exist but even to be everlasting. The highest evil, therefore, cannot be that which is declared to be a substance.

**2.** 4. But what shall I do? I know that there are many among you who are altogether unable to understand these things. I know again that there are certain ones who, although by a good natural ability they see these things somehow, nevertheless, by an evil will—by which they are also going to lose that very natural ability—act stubbornly and seek rather what they may say against these things, something that is easily persuasive to the slow and weak, than consent that they are true. Yet I will not regret having written, since either someone among you will at last, by no unfair judgment, consider it and leave your error, or, when those who are gifted and subject to God and as yet untouched by your pursuit have read it, they will not be able to be deceived by your discourses.

## If evil is defined as that which harms, from this too the Manicheans are refuted.

**3.** 5. Let us therefore inquire into these things more diligently and, as far as can be done, more plainly. I ask you again what evil is. If you say that it is that which harms, here too you will not be lying. But I beg you, notice; I beg you, be watchful; I beg you, lay aside partisan zeal, and seek the truth for the sake not of conquering but of finding. For whatever harms deprives the thing it harms of some good; for if it takes away no good, it does no harm at all. What is plainer than this, I beseech you? What more obvious? What so open to any moderate intellect, provided it is not stubborn?

But once this is laid down, it is now seen, I think, what follows. For in that race which you suspect to be the highest evil, no thing can be harmed, where there is nothing of good. But if there are two natures, as you affirm, the kingdom of light and the kingdom of darkness—since you confess that the kingdom of light is God, to whom you grant a certain simple nature, such that in him there is not one thing worse than another—you must of necessity confess (which is indeed strongly against you, but nevertheless you must of necessity confess) that this nature, which you not only do not deny to be the highest good but even strongly try to persuade us is so, is unchangeable and impenetrable and incorruptible and inviolable; for otherwise it will not be the highest good, since that is the highest good than which there is nothing better; but to such a nature harm can in no way be done. But if to harm is to deprive of good, as I have shown, the kingdom of darkness cannot be harmed, because there is nothing of good there; the kingdom of light cannot be harmed, because it is inviolable. Whom, then, will what you call evil harm?

## The difference between what is good of itself and what is good by participation.

**4.** 6. For which reason, since you cannot extricate yourselves, see how unencumbered is the position of Catholic teaching, which says that one thing is the good that is good in the highest degree and of itself, not by participation in any good, but by its own nature and essence; and another the good that is good by participating and by having; and it has from that highest good its being good, while that highest good nevertheless remains in itself and loses nothing. But this good that we spoke of afterward, it calls a creature, which can be harmed through defect; of whose defect God is not the author, because he is the author of existing and, so to speak, of being.

Thus it is also shown in what way evil is spoken of; for it is most truly spoken of not according to essence but according to privation; and the nature that can be harmed appears. For neither is it itself the highest evil, that from which good is taken away when it is harmed, nor the highest good, that which can fall away from good precisely because it is called good not by existing as good but by having good. Nor is a thing good by nature which, when it is said to have been made, certainly received that it might be good. Thus both God is the highest good, and the things he made are all good, although they are not so good as he himself who made them is. For who is so insane as to demand that the works be equal to the craftsman, and the things founded to the founder? What more do you want? Or do you want something even plainer?

## If evil is defined as corruption, from this too their heresy is utterly overturned.

**5.** 7. I will ask, then, a third time, what evil is. You will perhaps answer: corruption. Who would deny that this too is evil in general? For this is contrary to nature, this is what harms. But corruption is not in itself but in some substance that it corrupts; for corruption is not itself a substance. The thing, then, that corruption corrupts is not evil; for what is corrupted is deprived of integrity and purity. What therefore has no purity of which it may be deprived cannot be corrupted; but what has it is surely good by participation in purity. Likewise, what is corrupted is surely perverted; but what is perverted is deprived of order; and order is good. Therefore what is corrupted is not lacking in good; for by the very thing it is not lacking in, it can be widowed when it is corrupted. That race of darkness, then, if it lacked all good, as you say, could not be corrupted; for it had nothing that corruption could take from it, and if corruption takes away nothing, it does not corrupt. Now dare to say, if you can, that God and the kingdom of God could be corrupted, since you cannot find how the kingdom of the devil, such as you describe it, could be corrupted.

## What thing corruption affects, and what it is.

**6.** 8. What, then, does the Catholic light say on this point? What do you think, except what the truth holds—that he made a substance that can be corrupted, but that the unmade substance, which is the highest good, is incorruptible, and that corruption itself, which is the highest evil, cannot be corrupted, but that this is not a substance?

But if you ask what it is, see whither it tries to lead the things it corrupts. For from itself it affects the things that are corrupted. But all things, through corruption, fall away from what they were and are forced not to remain, are forced not to be. For being is referred to remaining. And so what is said to be in the highest and greatest degree is so said by remaining in itself. For what is changed for the better is changed not because it was remaining, but because it was being perverted toward the worse, that is, was falling away from essence; of which falling-away he is not the author who is the author of essence. Certain things, then, are changed toward the better and therefore tend to be, and by this change are said not to be perverted but to be turned back and converted. For perversion is contrary to ordering. But these things that tend to be tend toward order; and when they have attained it, they attain being itself, as far as a creature can attain it. For ordering brings what it orders back to a certain fittingness.

But to be is nothing other than to be one. And so, insofar as anything attains unity, to that extent it is. For fittingness and concord are the work of unity, by which those things that are composite are, insofar as they are; for simple things are of themselves, because they are one; but the things that are not simple imitate unity by the concord of their parts, and are to the extent that they attain it. For which reason ordering forces being, disordering therefore not-being; and disordering is also named perversion and corruption. Whatever, then, is corrupted tends toward not-being. Now it is for you to consider whither corruption forces, that you may be able to find the highest evil; for that is what corruption strives to lead to.

## The goodness of God does not allow any thing to be led by corruption to the point of not-being. How "to create" and "to found" differ.

**7.** 9. But the goodness of God does not allow a thing to be led to that point, and it so orders all defective things that they may be where they can most fittingly be, until by ordered motions they return to that from which they fell away. And so it also orders the rational souls, in which free choice is most powerful, when they fall away from him, in the lower grades of creation, where it befits such to be. They become wretched, then, by the divine judgment, while they are fittingly ordered according to their merits.

From which that was most excellently said, which you are especially accustomed to attack: *I make good things and create evils*[^1]*.* For "to create" is said for "to found" and "to order." And so in very many copies it is written thus: *I make good things and found evils.* For "to make" is, altogether, what was not; but "to found" is to order what already was in some way, so that it may be better and more fully. For God founds, that is, orders, these things when he says, *I found evils*—those that are defective, that is, that tend toward not-being—not those that have arrived at that toward which they tend. For it has been said: Nothing is permitted by divine providence to arrive at the point of not-being.

**7.** 10. These things are treated more broadly and more fully, but while we are dealing with you, it is enough. For the door had to be shown you, of which you despair and which you make others despair of. For no one introduces you except a good will, which the divine clemency makes peaceable, as it is sung in the Gospel: *Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will*[^2]*.* It is enough, I say, that you may see that there is no exit from a religious discussion about good and evil, except that whatever is, insofar as it is, is from God, but insofar as it falls away from essence, is not from God, and yet is always ordered by divine providence, as befits the universe. But if you do not yet see this, what more I should now do I do not know, except to treat even more minutely the things that have been said. For nothing but piety and purity leads the mind to greater things.

## Evil is not any substance, but a disagreement hostile to substance.

**8.** 11. For what else, when I ask what evil is, will you answer except either what is contrary to nature, or what harms, or corruption, or something of this kind? But in these I have shown your shipwrecks—unless perhaps, as you are accustomed to deal childishly with children, you will answer that evil is fire, poison, a wild beast, and other things of this kind. For even about someone who said that no substance is evil, one of the leading men of this heresy, whom we used to hear more familiarly and more often, would say: I would like to put a scorpion in a man's hand and see whether he would not draw his hand back; and if he did, he would be convinced, not by words but by the thing itself, that some substance is evil, since he would not deny that that animal is a substance.

And he said these things not in the man's presence, but when we, moved, reported to him what the other had said; he answered, then, as I said, childishly to children. For who, even moderately instructed and educated, would not see that these things harm through the disagreement of bodily temperament, and again through agreement do not harm, and often even confer no small advantages? For if that poison were evil through itself, it would kill that same scorpion more, and sooner. But on the contrary, if it were somehow entirely drawn out of it, it would without doubt perish. Therefore it is evil for that creature's body to lose what it is evil for our body to receive; likewise it is good for that creature to have what it is good for us to lack.

Will the same thing, then, be both good and evil? By no means; but evil is what is contrary to nature; for this is evil both to that beast and to us, that is, the very disagreement, which without doubt is not a substance, but rather is hostile to substance. Whence, then, is it? Attend to whither it forces, and you will learn, if indeed something of the inner light lives in you. For everything that destroys forces toward not-being. But God is the author of essence, nor can anything be seen to be an essence which, in whatever it has been, forces it not to be. Therefore something can be said as to whence disagreement is not, for as to whence it is, nothing can be said.

**8.** 12. A certain criminal Athenian woman, as history relates, by gradually drinking the poison that the condemned drank in a fixed measure in order to die, brought it about that she could drink it without any harm, even the slightest, to her health. And so, when she had at some time been condemned, she received that lawful quantity of poison which she had overcome by habit, but was not, like the rest, killed by it. And since this was a great marvel, she was sent into exile. What do we suppose? If poison is evil, did she bring it about that it was not evil to her? What is more absurd than this? But because disagreement is evil, she brought it about, rather, that through moderate habit that body agreed with her body. For when could she, by any cunning whatever, bring it about that disagreement should not harm her?

Why so? Because what is truly and generally evil harms always and all. Oil is suitable to our bodies, but vehemently hostile to many animals that have six feet. Is not hellebore in one way food, in another a medicine, in another a poison? Salt taken too immoderately, who would not cry out that it is poison? But how many and how great advantages to the body come from it, who can count? Seawater, when drunk, is harmful to land animals, but to the bodies of many it is, when they are moistened by it, most suitable and useful; and to fishes, in both respects, it is health and pleasure. Bread nourishes a human being, kills a hawk. Mud itself, which both swallowed and smelled gravely offends and harms, does it not both, touched in summer, cool, and serve as a medicine for wounds that have come from fire? What is more contemptible than dung? What more abject than ashes? Yet these bring such great advantages to fields that the Romans thought divine honors should be paid to their discoverer, Stercutius, from whom indeed dung (*stercus*) received its name.

**8.** 13. But why should I gather up small things, which are innumerable? Who would doubt that the four elements themselves, which are at hand, profit through agreement, but, applied unsuitably, are vehemently hostile to nature? We who live by air are killed when buried by earth and water, but innumerable animals creep vitally through sand and looser earth, while fishes die in this air of ours. Fire corrupts our bodies, but, suitably applied, both restores us from cold and drives away innumerable diseases. That sun to which you bend the knee, than which nothing more beautiful is indeed found among visible things, strengthens the eyes of eagles, but wounds and darkens our gaze; yet it comes about through habit that we too may fix our gaze on it without harm. Will you, then, allow us to compare it to that poison which habit made harmless to the Athenian woman? Look back, then, at last, and notice: if any substance is evil because it harms someone, the light you worship cannot be defended from this charge. Consider rather that this disagreement is the universal evil, through which a ray of the sun makes the eyes darken, though nothing is more pleasant to them than light.

## That the fables of the Manicheans about goods and evils cannot even stand.

**9.** 14. I have said these things so that, if it can be done, you may at last cease to say that the earth, immeasurably deep and long, is evil; that the mind wandering through the earth is evil; that the five caverns of the elements—one full of darkness, another of waters, another of winds, another of fire, another of smoke—are evil; that the animals born in those several elements—creeping things in the darkness, swimming things in the waters, flying things in the winds, four-footed things in the fire, two-footed things in the smoke—are evil. For these things, as they are described by you, can in no way exist; since whatever is such, insofar as it is, must of necessity be from the most high God, because insofar as it is, it is certainly good. For if pain and weakness are evil, there were there animals of such firmness of body that you say their miscarried offspring, after the world was fashioned out of them according to your sect, fell from heaven to earth and could not die. If blindness is evil, they saw; if deafness, they heard. If to become dumb or to be mute is evil, voices were there so distinct and articulate that, as you assert, they resolved in an assembly, at the persuasion of one, to wage war against God. If barrenness is evil, there was there a great fecundity for begetting children. If exile is evil, they were in their own land and inhabited their own regions. If slavery is evil, there were there even those who reigned. If death is evil, they lived, and lived in such a way that you proclaim that the mind itself can in no way ever die, not even after the victory of God.

**9.** 15. Why, I ask, do I find in the highest evil such great goods, contrary to those evils which I have mentioned? Or, if these are not evils, will any substance at all, insofar as it is a substance, be evil? If weakness is not evil, will an infirm body be evil? If blindness is not evil, will darkness be evil? If deafness is not evil, will a deaf creature be evil? If to be mute is not evil, will a fish be evil? If barrenness is not evil, how is a barren animal evil? If exile is not evil, how is an exiled animal evil, or an animal sending someone into exile? If slavery is not evil, how is a serving animal evil, or one forcing someone to serve? If death is not evil, how is a mortal animal evil, or one bringing death? But if these are evils, how will not firmness of body, sight, hearing, persuasive speech, fecundity, native soil, freedom, life be goods—all of which you declare to have been in that kingdom of evil, and dare to assert it to be the highest evil?

**9.** 16. Finally, if—which no one at all has ever denied—disagreement is evil, what is more agreeable than those elements to their several animals: darkness to creeping things, waters to swimming things, winds to flying things, fire to the more voracious, smoke to the loftier? For so great a concord is described by you in the race of discord, and so great an order in the seat of perversity. If what harms is evil, I leave aside that most powerful point made above, that no harm could be done where there was no good; but if that is obscure, this at least stands out and appears to all, that, as I said and as all agree, what harms is evil; smoke in that race did not harm the two-footed animals, it begot them and nourished them and sustained them without ruin as they were born, grew, and reigned. But now, after good was mingled with evil, smoke has been made more harmful; it cannot be endured by us, who are certainly two-footed; it blinds, oppresses, kills. Did so great a monstrousness come to evil elements by the mingling of good? So great a perversity, with God reigning?

**9.** 17. Surely, why in the rest do we see that congruity which deceived your author and lured him into composing lies? Why, I say, do darkness suit creeping things, waters swimming things, winds flying things, but fire burns the four-footed, and smoke suffocates us? What of the fact that serpents too see most sharply and exult in the presence of the sun, and are more abundant where the calmer air more difficultly and rarely gathers a cloud? What is more absurd than that the dwellers in and lovers of darkness should be more suited and fitted there where the clearness of light is enjoyed? But if you say that they are delighted rather by heat than by light, you would say far more agreeably that lively serpents were born in fire than a sluggish ass; and yet who would deny that the asp is a friend to this light, since its eyes are compared to the eyes of the eagle? But about beasts I shall see later. Let us consider ourselves, I beseech you, without stubbornness, and let us at last strip our mind of vain and ruinous fables. For who would bear so great a perversity, by which it is said that in the race of darkness, to which nothing of light was mingled, two-footed animals had so firm, so vigorous, so finally incredible a force in the keenness of their eyes that they both saw in their own darkness and beheld and considered and were delighted with and desired that most pure light of the kingdoms of God which is commended by you (since indeed you wish it to have been visible even to such creatures); but that our eyes, by the mingling of light, by the mingling of the highest good, by the mingling, finally, of God, have been rendered so infirm and weak that we neither see anything in darkness nor can in any way bear the sight of the sun, and, turned away from it, seek even the things that were being seen by us?

**9.** 18. These things can be said even if corruption is evil, which equally no one doubts; for then smoke did not corrupt the kind of animals that it now corrupts. And, not to go through each point, which is long and unnecessary, the living creatures that you pretend were there were so much less liable to corruption that their miscarried offspring, not yet fit for being born, when hurled headlong from heaven to earth, were able both to live and to beget, and again to conspire, having of course their former firmness, because they were already conceived before the mingling of good and evil; for after that combining, the things born of them, you say, are the animals which we now see most infirm and easily yielding to corruption. Who could endure this error any longer, except one who either does not see these things, or has, by some incredible habit and familiarity with you, hardened himself against all the weight of reason?

## The three seals of morals wrongly devised by the Manicheans.

**10.** 19. But since I have shown, as I judge, in how great darkness and in how great falsity you are engaged concerning goods and evils in general, now let us look at those three seals which you boast of in your morals with great praise and proclamation. What, after all, are these seals? Of the mouth, certainly, and of the hands, and of the breast. What is this? That, he says, a man may be chaste and innocent in mouth and hands and breast.

What if he sins with eyes, ears, nostrils? What if he afflicts a man with his heels, or even kills him? How shall we hold him guilty, who has sinned neither with mouth nor with hands nor with breast?

But when I name the mouth, he says, I wish all the senses that are in the head to be understood; when I name the hand, all activity; when the breast, all seminal lust.

To which, then, do you want blasphemies to pertain? To the mouth or to the hand? For that activity is through the tongue. And so, if you include all activity under one head, why do you join the activity of the feet to the hands, but separate it from the tongue?

Or, because the tongue signifies something by words, do you want to set it apart from that activity which is not done for the sake of signifying, so that the seal of the hands may thus be defined as continence from evil activity which is not for the sake of signifying? But what will you do if someone sins by signifying something with the hands, as happens when we write, or show by a gesture something that is understood? For this you cannot attribute to the mouth and tongue, because it is done with the hands. For what is more absurd than that, although three seals are spoken of—of the mouth, of the hands, and of the breast—certain sins detected in the hands should be attributed to the mouth? But if general activity is given to the hands, what reason, after all, is there for adding the activity of the feet to this, but not adding that of the tongue?

Do you see how the appetite for novelty, with error as its companion, is led into great straits? For with those three seals, which you proclaim by a certain new division, you cannot find how to include the purgation of all sins.

## What the seal of the mouth is like among the Manicheans, who are convicted of being guilty of blasphemy against God.

**11.** 20. But divide as you wish, pass over whatever you wish; let us treat the things you are especially accustomed to commend. For you say that to the seal of the mouth it pertains to refrain from all blasphemy. But there is blasphemy when some evil things are said about good things. And so now, in common usage, blasphemy is taken to be nothing other than to say evil words about God; for about human beings there can be doubt, but God without controversy is good. If, then, reason convinces us that no one says worse things about God than you, where will that memorable seal of the mouth be? For reason teaches—not indeed a hidden reason, but one set at hand and open to the understanding of all, yet unconquered, and the more unconquered because no one is permitted to be ignorant of it—that God is incorruptible, unchangeable, inviolable, into whom no neediness, no weakness, no misery can fall. And so far does every rational soul commonly perceive this, that even you nod assent when these things are said.

**11.** 21. But when you begin to tell your fables, you, possessed by an astonishing blindness, persuade—and to others possessed by an astonishing blindness you even persuade—that God is corruptible and changeable and violable and liable to neediness and admitting weakness and not safe from misery. And this is too little; for you say not only that God is corruptible but that he is corrupted; not changeable but changed; not violable but violated; not one who can suffer neediness but needy; not one into whom weakness may fall but into whom it has fallen; not one who can be wretched but wretched.

For you say that the soul is God, or a part of God. Nor do I see how that is not God which is said to be a part of God; for a part of gold is gold, and of silver silver, and of stone stone; and, to come to greater things, a part of earth is earth, and a part of water water, and of air air; and if you take anything from fire, you will not deny that it is fire; and any part whatever of light can be nothing other than light. Why, then, will a part of God not be God? Or has God an articulated shape, as a human being and the other living creatures have? For a part of a human being is not a human being.

**11.** 22. But I come down to either of these opinions and consider each separately. For if you want God to be as light is, you cannot refuse that a part of God is some God. For which reason, since you say that the soul is a part of God, which you do not deny to have been both corrupted (it which was foolish) and changed (it which was wise) and violated (in that it does not have its proper perfection) and needy (it which begs for help) and weak (it which needs medicine) and wretched (it which desires to be happy), all these things you heap upon God by a sacrilegious opinion. Or, if you do not grant these things about the soul, then neither is an apostle necessary to lead the soul into truth, because it is not foolish; nor is the soul renewed through true religion, because it is not changed; nor is it perfected by your seals, because it is perfect; nor does God bring it help, because it is not needy; nor is Christ a physician, because it is sound; nor is the happy life rightly promised to it. What of the fact that Jesus is called liberator, which he himself cries out in the Gospel, *If the Son frees you, then you will be truly free*[^3]? And the apostle Paul says, *You have been called to freedom*[^4]*.* Therefore the soul that has not yet attained this freedom is in servitude. God, then, on your authority—since a part of God is God—is corrupted by foolishness, and by falling is changed, and by the loss of perfection is violated, and is in need of help, and is feeble with disease, and oppressed with misery, and disgraced with servitude.

**11.** 23. But if a part of God is not God, then neither can he be uncorrupted in whose part there is corruption, nor unchanged who has been changed in some part, nor unviolated who is not in every part perfect, nor not needy who diligently acts to restore his part to himself, nor altogether sound who is in some part weak, nor most happy who has some part subjected to misery, nor altogether free a part of whom is pressed by servitude. All these things you are forced to say, when you declare that the soul, which you behold overwhelmed by such great calamities, is a part of God. Remove these and many things of this kind from your sect, if you can; then at last say that your mouth is free from blasphemies. Rather, desert that sect; for if you cease to believe and say what that man wrote, you will certainly not be Manicheans.

**11.** 24. God is altogether to be understood or believed to be the highest good, than which nothing better can be or be thought, if we mean to be free from blasphemies. A certain ratio of numbers can in no way be violated and changed, nor could any nature, by any violence whatever, bring it about that, after one, the number that follows should not accord with it in double. This can in no way be changed, and yet God is called by you changeable. This ratio keeps its inviolable integrity, and you do not wish God to be even equal to it. Let any race of darkness whatever bring it about that the intelligible number three, in which there is a one that is so one that it lacks parts—let that race of darkness, then, bring it about that this number three be divided into two equal parts. Your mind surely sees that this can come about by no one's ill will. What, then, could not violate the ratio of number—could it violate God? But if it could not, what, I beseech you, was the necessity that a part of him should be mingled with evil and thrust into such great miseries?

## He shuts off the evasions of the Manicheans.

**12.** 25. For from this arose that which, even when we were zealously hearing you, used to press us with great straits, nor did we find any exit, asking what the race of darkness was going to do to God if he had been unwilling to fight with it at so great a calamity to his own part. For if it was not going to harm him while he was at rest, we complained that we had been cruelly treated, we who were sent to these hardships; but if it was going to harm him, then that nature was not incorruptible, such as the nature of God ought to be.

On this question there was no lack of one who said that God had not wished to be free of evil, nor taken care lest he be harmed, but had, on account of his natural goodness, wished to be of profit to the restless and perverse nature, that it might be ordered. The books of Mani do not so sound; that God took care lest he be invaded by enemies is very often signified there, very often said. But let us grant that Mani thought these things, as that man said who could find nothing else to say: is God, by this reasoning, defended from cruelty or from weakness? For this goodness of his toward the hostile race turned out to be great destruction to his own. To this is added that, if his nature could not be corrupted and changed, neither would any plague change and corrupt us; and that order which had to be furnished to the foreign nature could be furnished without our perversion.

**12.** 26. But that had not yet been said which I recently heard at Carthage. For when a certain man, whom I especially desire to be freed from that error, was being driven by this question into the same straits, he dared to say, namely, that the kingdom had certain borders of its own, which could be invaded by the contrary race, but that God himself could in no way be violated. But he said what not even that author of yours would in any way be forced to say; for perhaps he saw the consequent ruins of his sect to be much easier through this opinion than through the other. And in truth it is so; for if anyone of moderate heart hears that in that nature there was one part violable, another inviolable, he easily understands that there are now not two but three natures: one inviolable, the second violable, the third the violator.

## Deeds are judged not from the things themselves, but from the intention: hence judgment must be passed on the abstinence of the Manicheans.

**13.** 27. Since, then, these blasphemies, having proceeded from the heart, dwell daily in your mouth, cease at last to proclaim the seal of your mouth, as something great, for the enticement of the unlearned. Unless perhaps, because you do not eat flesh and do not drink wine, you think the seal of the mouth is to be wondered at and praised. About which I ask you with what end you do it. For the end to which the things we do are referred, that is, the purpose for which we do whatever we do, if it is not only blameless but even praiseworthy, then at last our deeds too are worthy of some praise; but if that which we look to and regard, when we are engaged in some action, is rightly and deservedly blamed, no one would doubt that that action too is to be disapproved and blamed.

**13.** 28. Of Catiline it has been handed down to memory that he could bear cold, thirst, hunger[^5]. These things were common to him even with our Apostles. Whence, then, is this parricide distinguished from the Apostles, except by that end which he pursued, most different from theirs? For he endured these things in order to satisfy most immoderate and most monstrous desires; they, on the contrary, in order to restrain them and force them to serve ruling reason. You too are accustomed, when the multitude of Catholic virgins is proclaimed to you, to say: A she-mule too is a virgin. Rashly indeed, on account of ignorance of Catholic teaching, but yet signifying that this continence is vain unless it is referred, by sure reason, to some most upright end. Catholic Christians too can compare your abstinence from wine and flesh[^6] to beasts of burden and to *many sparrows*[^7]*,* and finally to innumerable kinds of worms[^8].

But lest I fall into your rashness, I will not do this too hastily, but will first examine with what end you do these things. For it is now agreed, I think, between us, that nothing else is to be sought in morals of this kind. If, then, you abstain for the sake of frugality and of restraining the lust by which we are delighted and captivated by such foods and drink, I hear you and approve; but it is not so.

**13.** 29. For I ask you: suppose there exists—as can happen—a man so sparing and frugal that, moderating the appetite of belly and throat, he does not feast twice in one day, and to him as he dines there are set little vegetables with a bit of bacon, smeared and seasoned with that same bacon, as much as is enough to suppress hunger, and he waters his thirst, for the sake of attending to his health, with two or three draughts that are by no means undiluted, and this is his daily fare;

but another, on the other side, tasting nothing of flesh, nothing of wine, gladly takes at the ninth hour choice and exotic produce, varied in many dishes and sprinkled with abundant pepper, and is going to dine on such things even at the beginning of the night; and he drinks honeyed wine, boiled-down must, raisin wine, and the juices of certain pressed fruits, sufficiently imitating the appearance of wine and even surpassing it in sweetness; and he drinks not as much as he thirsts but as much as he pleases, and takes care to have this provided for him daily, and enjoys such fare and delicacies, with no necessity but with great pleasure.

Which, then, of these two do you judge to lead a more abstinent life, as regards eating and drinking? I do not think you are so blind as not to prefer that man of the sparing bacon and wine to this glutton.

**13.** 30. So indeed the truth compels; but your error sings far otherwise. For your elect, distinguished by three seals, if he lives daily like this man whom we described later, can perhaps be reproved by one or two of the graver sort, but cannot at all be condemned as a violator of the seal. But if he should once dine with that former man, and anoint his lips with a morsel of ham, even rancid, and wet them with flat wine, he will be judged, by the sentence of your author, a breaker of the seal and at once destined for Gehenna, with you wondering but nevertheless consenting.

I beg you, leave your error; I beg you, attend to reason; I beg you, resist habit a little. For what is more perverse than this depravity? What more deranged? What, moreover, can be said or thought more insane than that a man belching with gratification, his belly distended with mushrooms, rice, truffles, cakes, boiled-down must, pepper, laserwort, and daily craving such things, is found in no way to have fallen away from the three seals, that is, from the rule of holiness; but that another, seasoning the cheapest produce with smoky sauce and taking only so much from it as suffices for the refreshment of the body, and sipping three cups of wine for the sake of sustaining his health, and passing from that fare to this one, is prepared for sure punishment?

## For three reasons one praiseworthily abstains from certain kinds of food.

**14.** 31. But the Apostle says, *It is good, brothers, not to eat flesh nor to drink wine*[^9]*.* As if indeed any of us denied that this is good; but either for that end which I mentioned above, according to which it is said, *And make no provision for the flesh in its desires*[^10]*,* or for those ends which the same Paul again shows: that is, either for the sake of bridling gluttony, which is wont to be more madly and immoderately carried away by these things, or lest a brother be offended, or lest there be communion in an idol's offering on the part of the weak.

For at the time when the Apostle wrote these things, much sacrificial flesh was sold in the market. And because wine too was poured out as a libation to the gods of the gentiles, many weaker brothers, who also used these things that were for sale, preferred to restrain themselves entirely from flesh and wine rather than to fall, even unknowingly, into that which they thought was communion with idols.

But on account of these, even those who were stronger and had judged that these things were to be despised with greater faith, knowing that nothing is unclean except through an evil conscience, and holding that statement of the Lord, *Not what enters into your mouth defiles you, but what comes out*[^11]*,* nevertheless ought, on account of these weaker ones, lest they be offended, to abstain from these things.

Nor is this gathered by surmise, but is plainly found in the Apostle's own epistles. For you are accustomed to say to us only this: *It is good, brothers, not to eat flesh nor to drink wine,* but not to add what follows: *nor anything by which your brother is offended or scandalized or made weak.* For from this it shines out with what end the Apostle enjoined these things.

**14.** 32. The preceding and following words indicate this more plainly, which it is indeed long to recount, but on account of those who are lazy at reading and handling the divine Scriptures, we are compelled to set forth this whole passage.

*Receive the one weak in faith,* he says, *not in disputes over thoughts. For one believes he may eat all things; but he who is weak, let him eat vegetables. Let him who eats not despise him who does not eat, and let him who does not eat not judge him who eats; for God has received him. Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own Lord he stands or falls; but he will stand, for God is able to make him stand. For one judges between day and day, another judges every day; let each abound in his own understanding. He who regards the day, regards it unto the Lord; and he who eats, eats unto the Lord, for he gives thanks to God. And he who does not eat, does not eat unto the Lord, and gives thanks to God. For none of us lives to himself, and none dies to himself. For whether we live, we live unto the Lord, or whether we die, we die unto the Lord. For whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's. For to this end Christ both lived and died and rose again, that he might be Lord both of the living and of the dead.*

*But why do you judge your brother? Or why do you despise your brother? For we shall all stand before the judgment seat of God. For it is written: As I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God*[^12]*. So then each one of us will render account for himself to God. Let us therefore no longer judge one another; but judge this rather, that you place no offense or stumbling block before a brother. I know and am confident in the Lord Jesus that nothing is common of itself; but to him who reckons anything to be common, to him it is common. For if your brother is grieved on account of food, you no longer walk according to love. Do not by your food destroy him for whom Christ died. Let not our good then be blasphemed. For the kingdom of God is not food and drink, but justice and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit. For he who in this serves Christ is pleasing to God and approved by men.*

*So then let us pursue the things that are of peace and the things that are of mutual edification. Do not, on account of food, destroy the work of God. All things indeed are clean, but it is evil for a man who eats through giving offense. It is good not to eat flesh and not to drink wine, nor anything by which your brother is offended or scandalized or made weak. You have faith; keep it to yourself before God. Blessed is he who does not judge himself in what he approves. But he who discerns, if he eats, is condemned, because it is not of faith. And everything that is not of faith is sin. But we who are stronger ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak and not to please ourselves. Let each of us please his neighbor unto good edification. For Christ did not please himself*[^13]*.*

**14.** 33. Does it appear plainly enough that the Apostle enjoined those who were stronger not to eat flesh nor drink wine, because they were offending the weak by not according with them, and were making them suppose that those who in faith judged all things clean were unwilling, in deference to the idols, to abstain from such feasts and drink?

This he signifies also when he writes to the Corinthians in this way: *But concerning the foods of sacrifices that are offered to idols, we know that an idol is nothing in the world, and that there is no God but one. For although there are those that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, yet for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things and we in him, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and we through him. But not in all is there knowledge. But some, in their conscience, even until now eat it as a thing offered to an idol in the idol's temple, and their conscience, being weak, is defiled. But food does not commend us to God; for neither if we eat shall we abound, nor if we do not eat shall we fall short. But see lest perhaps this liberty of yours become an offense to the weak. For if anyone sees one who has knowledge reclining at table in an idol's temple, will not his conscience, being weak, be built up to eat things offered to idols, and the weak brother, for whom Christ died, will perish in your knowledge? But sinning thus against the brothers and striking their weak conscience, you sin against Christ. Wherefore, if food scandalizes my brother, I will not eat flesh forever, lest I scandalize my brother*[^14]*.*

**14.** 34. Likewise in another place: *What then? That a thing offered to idols is anything? Or that an idol is anything? But the things that the gentiles offer, they offer to demons and not to God. But I do not wish you to become partners of demons. You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot share in the table of the Lord and the table of demons. Or do we provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than he? All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient; all things are lawful for me, but not all things build up.*

*Let no one seek what is his own, but what is another's. Eat everything that is sold in the market, asking no question for conscience' sake. But if anyone says, This was offered to idols, do not eat, on account of him who pointed it out and on account of conscience; I mean conscience not your own, but the other's. For why is my liberty judged by another's conscience? If I partake with thanksgiving, why am I blasphemed for that for which I give thanks?*

*Whether, then, you eat or drink or do anything, do all to the glory of God. Be without offense to Jews and Greeks and to the Church of God, just as I too please all in all things, not seeking what is useful to me but what is useful to many, that they may be saved. Be imitators of me, as I too am of Christ*[^15]*.*

**14.** 35. It appears, then, I think, with what end one must abstain from flesh and wine. That end is threefold. To suppress the delight that is wont to be had especially in these foods and, in such drink, to reach even to drunkenness. To guard against weakness, on account of the things that are sacrificed and poured out as libation. And—what is especially to be commended—on account of love, lest the weakness of those who are weaker than those who abstain from these things be offended.

But you say that sauces are unclean, although the Apostle says that all things are clean, but that it is evil for the one *who eats through giving offense*[^16]*.* And indeed I believe that you are defiled by these feasts by the very fact that you judge them to be unclean. For he says, *I believe and am confident in the Lord Jesus that nothing is common of itself; but to him who reckons anything to be common, to him it is common*[^17]*.* And to whom is it doubtful that he called the common unclean and defiled? But it is foolish to deal with you about the Scriptures, since you both deceive by promising reason and say that those books, in which there is the great authority of religion, are corrupted by false headings inserted into them. Persuade me, then, by reason how flesh defiles the one who eats it, if it is taken without any offense, without any weak opinion, without any lust.

## Why the Manicheans forbid the eating of flesh.

**15.** 36. It is worth the trouble to know the whole rationale of this superstitious abstinence, which is given thus: since, they say, a member of God was mingled with the substance of evils in order to bridle it and restrain it from its utmost frenzy—for so you say—out of both natures mingled, that is, of good and evil, the world was fashioned. But that divine part is daily purged out of every part of the world and taken back into its own kingdoms; but it, exhaling through the earth and tending toward heaven, runs into the plants, since they are fixed by their roots to the earth, and so makes fruitful and vivifies all herbs and all shrubs.

From these the animals take food, which, if they couple, bind in the flesh that divine member, and, turned away from its sure path and hindered, entangle it in errors and hardships. And so the foods that are prepared from produce and fruits, if they come to the saints, that is, to the Manicheans, through their chastity and prayers and psalms, whatever in them is bright and divine is purged, that is, is in every part perfected, so that it may be carried back to its own kingdoms without any difficulty of filth. Hence it is that you forbid giving to a begging man who is not a Manichean bread or anything of produce or even water itself, which is cheap to all, lest the member of God which is mingled with these things, soiled by his sins, be hindered from its return.

**15.** 37. But flesh you say is now congealed from the very filth. For something of that divine part flees, as you declare, while produce and fruits are gathered; it flees when they are crushed by grinding or milling or cooking or even by biting and chewing. It flees too in all the motions of animals, whether when they frolic or when they exercise or when they labor or when they do anything at all. It flees too in our very rest, while in the body that which is called digestion is accomplished by inner heat. And so, with the divine nature fleeing on so many occasions, something most filthy remains, from which by coupling flesh is formed, while the soul, of the good kind—since, though it is for the most part good, it is yet not wholly good—flies away by those motions mentioned. For which reason, when the soul has also deserted the flesh, the remaining filth becomes excessive, and therefore the soul of those who eat flesh is defiled.

## He lays open the monstrous mysteries of the Manicheans.

**16.** 38. O obscurity of the nature of things, how great a covering it is for falsity! Who, hearing these things—one who has not learned the causes of things, who, not yet sprinkled with even the slightest light of truth, is deceived by bodily images—would not, by the very fact that these things by no means appear and are thought of through certain likenesses of visible things and can be spoken eloquently, judge them to be true? But great throngs and herds of such people wander about, whom a religious fear, rather than reason, keeps safe from these deceits. For which reason I will try to refute these things, as far as God will deign to help me, in such a way that it may shine out sufficiently, not only to the judgment of the prudent, by which they are disapproved as soon as they are said, but even to common understanding itself, how false and absurd they are.

**16.** 39. For first I ask, whence you teach that there is in grains and pulse and vegetables and flowers and fruits that part of God, whatever it is. From the very brightness of color, they say, and the pleasantness of smell and the sweetness of taste it is manifest; and when these things do not have these qualities, by being rotten, they signify that they have been deserted by that same good. Are you not ashamed to think God is found by nose and palate? But I leave this aside. For I will speak Latin to you, and, as is wont to be said, it is much for you. This rather had to be understood by any mind whatever: if the presence of good appears in bodies by color, then the dung of animals, which is also the refuse of those very flesh meats, shines with various colors—now white, often golden, and others of this kind, which in fruits and flowers you take as witnesses of God being present and inhering.

What, after all, is the reason that you declare the redness in a rose to be an index of abundant good, and condemn the same in blood? Why do you embrace the same color in a violet that you spurn in bile, in the disease of the jaundiced, and finally in an infant's excrement? Why do you think the brightness and gleam of oil cries out an abundance of mingled good, and prepare your throat and belly to purge it, yet dread to touch your lips with drops of a most similar gleam dripping from fat flesh? Why do you think a melon to be golden, from the treasures of God, and not the rancid fat of a ham or the yolk of an egg? Why does whiteness in lettuces proclaim God to you, but in milk does not proclaim him? For I am still speaking of colors, in which—to leave the rest aside—you can compare no meadows clothed with flowers to the feathers and plumage of a single peacock, which surely are born of coupling and flesh.

**16.** 40. For if this good is also found by smell, ointments of wondrous smell are made from the flesh of certain animals. Finally, the very foods that are wont to be cooked with somewhat better flesh smell far more pleasantly than if the flesh had been lacking to them. Lastly, if you judge the sweeter-smelling things to be cleaner, you ought to have eaten a certain mud more eagerly than to drink cistern water; because earth, somewhat drier, drenched by a shower, strokes the nostrils with a wondrous smell, and such mud smells better than if a purer rainwater were caught. But if taste must bear witness, that we may know that something of God dwells in a body, it dwells more in dates and honey than in pork, but more in pork than in a bean, more in a fig than in a fig-fed liver—behold, I grant it; but you too grant that it is more in the fig-fed liver than in a beet. What of the fact that by this reasoning you are forced to confess that certain plants, which you certainly want all to be cleaner than flesh, receive God from flesh itself, if God mingled in is recognized by taste? For vegetables too become more savory cooked with flesh, and the herbs on which cattle feed we cannot taste; but turned into the juice of milk, we judge them more excellent in color and most suitable in taste.

**16.** 41. Or do you think that where three goods are present together, that is, good color and smell and taste, there is the greater part of good? Do not, then, so greatly admire and praise flowers, which you cannot admit to be judged at the tribunal of the palate. Do not prefer even purslane to flesh, which is surpassed by it, when cooked, in color and smell and taste. A roast suckling pig—for you compel us to discuss good and evil with you, not with writers and scribes, but rather with cooks and confectioners' servants—a roast suckling pig, then, is bright in color, soft in smell, pleasant in taste; you have a perfect index of the divine substance dwelling within; by a threefold testimony it invites you and desires to be purged by your holiness. Fall upon it; why do you delay? Why do you prepare to contradict?

In color alone an infant's dung surpasses a lentil; in smell alone a roast morsel surpasses a mild and green fig; in taste alone a slain kid surpasses the grass it fed on while alive; flesh has even been found whose cause is helped by all three of these witnesses together. What more do you seek? Or what will you say?

Why does the pulp of flesh make you unclean when you feast, but these monstrosities do not make you unclean when you discuss them—especially since the ray of this sun, which you certainly prefer to all flesh and all produce, neither smells nor tastes, but only stands out among other bodies by the excellence of its most gleaming color, which greatly exhorts you, and indeed forces you against your will, to prefer nothing, among the proofs of mingled good, to brightness of color?

**16.** 42. You will therefore be brought back to those straits, so as to confess that a part of God dwells more in blood, and in those things that, most foul but brightly colored, are voided from the flesh of animals in the streets, than in the pale leaves of an olive. But if you say—for you say this too—that the leaves of an olive, when burned, emit a fire in which the presence of light appears, but that flesh when burned does not do the same, what will you answer about fat, which lights nearly all the lamps of Italy? What about ox dung, which is certainly more filthy than the flesh of an ox, which, when dried, country people so use for the hearth that nothing is easier to kindle by its fire and nothing more purified by its smoke? What of the fact that, if brightness and gleam show the greater presence of the divine part, you do not purge it, do not seal it, do not free it? Since it is especially in flowers—to leave aside blood and the innumerable most similar things in flesh or from flesh—which flowers you certainly cannot have at your feasts; just as, even if you did eat flesh, you would certainly not add to your dishes the scales of fishes and certain little worms and flies, all of which sparkle even in darkness with their own light.

**16.** 43. What, then, remains except that you cease to say that you have fit indices—eyes, nostrils, palate—by which you may prove the presence of the divine part in bodies? But once these are removed, whence will you teach not only that a greater part of God is in plants than in flesh, but that there is anything of him at all in plants? Or does beauty move you—not that which is in the sweetness of color, but that which is in the congruence of parts? Would that it were so! For when would you dare to compare twisted logs to the bodies of living creatures, in whose form like members answer to like? But if you are delighted by the testimonies of the bodily senses, which is necessary for those who cannot see by the mind the force of essence, how do you prove that, through lapse of time and through certain crushings, the substance of good flees from bodies, except that thence God departs, as you assert, and migrates from place to place?

It is full of madness. Yet no signs and indices, as far as I can estimate, led you to this opinion. For many things, plucked from trees or pulled from the earth, before they come to our food, are rendered better by some interval of time, as leeks and endives, lettuces, grapes, apples, figs, and certain pears, and many things besides, which both are better colored when they are not consumed at once after being picked, and are taken into the body more healthfully, and taste more savory in the mouth; which so great suitability and sweetness ought by no means to be in these things, according to your opinion, if they became the more deserted by good the longer they are stored, after they have been separated from the earth as from a mother. The flesh itself of animals slain the day before is surely more pleasant and more suitable; but it ought not to be so, if, as you assert, it had more good on the day the living creature was killed than on the day after, when a greater flight of the divine substance had been made.

**16.** 44. But who would not know that wine becomes purer and better with age? And not, as you suppose, more fragrant for perverting the senses, but more useful for invigorating the body, provided there be a measure, which it befits to be master in every matter. For with newer must perversion of the senses is wont to happen sooner, so that if it has sometimes remained in the vat and fermented a little, it strikes the brain of those looking down on it and drives them headlong, and unless help is somehow given, kills them. Now as to what pertains to health, who would deny that bodies are inflated by it and ruinously distended? Are so great inconveniences present, then, because it has more good, while these are not present in aged wine, because a great part of the divine substance has departed?

It is absurd to say—especially for you who indicate the presence of a part of God by eyes, nostrils, palate, when these senses are well affected. And now, what so great perversity is it to think wine is the gall of the princes of darkness and yet not to spare grapes for eating? Will that gall be more present when it has been in the cask, or when it has been in the berries? But if, with the good departing, the evil remains as it were more undiluted, and this happens through lapse of time, then those same grapes, hung up and preserved, ought not to become milder, sweeter, more healthful; nor ought the wine itself, which was spoken of above, to become both clearer and brighter with the loss of light, and more healthful with the departure of the health-giving substance.

**16.** 45. What shall I say of wood and leaves, which dry up over time and yet cannot for that reason be said by you to become worse? For they lose that from which smoke is begotten, but retain that from which a bright flame rises, and by that brightness which you greatly love, it bears witness that the good is purer in the drier than in the greener. From which it comes about that either you deny that the part of God is greater in pure than in smoky light, and so throw all your proofs into confusion, or you confess that it can happen that from plants cut or pulled up, if they are laid by for a longer time, the nature of evil flees more copiously than that of good. And once this is confessed, we shall hold that from gathered produce a greater evil can depart, and thus in flesh a greater good can remain. And let this be said about time.

**16.** 46. For if that divine nature finds occasion for flight by the agitation and kneading and rubbing of these things, many similar cases refute you, which become better by being moved. Some imitate wine with the juice of barley, which by being moved becomes excellent. Indeed—what is by no means to be passed over—this kind of drink most quickly intoxicates, and yet you have never said that the juice of barley is the gall of the princes. Flour is skillfully drawn together with sparer water, a little harder, so that by kneading it becomes better and—than which nothing more perverse can be said—whiter as the light flees. The pastry-maker kneads honey a long time, so that it may reach that whiteness and a less harmful and milder sweetness; explain how this comes about with the good departing.

But if it pleases you to prove the presence of God not by sight and smell and taste but by the hearing too being delighted, flesh furnishes strings for harps and bones for flutes, which, dried and rubbed and twisted, are rendered sonorous. So the sweetness of music, which you contend has come from the divine kingdoms, is furnished to us by the filth of dead flesh, both dried by time and thinned by rubbing and stretched by twisting—by which afflictions you proclaim that the divine substance flees even from living things, which you say also happens by their being boiled. Why, then, do boiled thistles by no means harm the health? Is it, then, to be supposed that from them, while they are thus cooked, God departs, or the plague?

**16.** 47. Why should I pursue the rest, all of which it is neither easy nor necessary to say? For to whom does it not occur how many things are more pleasant and more healthful when cooked? Which ought not to be so, if, as you suppose, by agitations of this kind they are deserted by good. I think you can find nothing at all by which to prove, by these bodily senses, that flesh is therefore unclean and defiles the souls of those who eat it because gathered produce, after many agitations, is turned into flesh; especially since you think the age and corruption of vinegar to be cleaner than wine, and we see that the boiled-down must you drink is nothing other than cooked wine, which ought to be more filthy than wine, if by motions and cookings the divine members depart from corporeal things. But if it is not so, there is no reason why you should suppose that produce, when it is gathered, stored, handled, cooked, digested, is deserted by good fleeing, and therefore furnishes a most filthy matter for the creating of bodies.

**16.** 48. But if you are not led by color and form and smell and taste to judge that good is in these things, what else can you bring forward? Or is a certain firmness and vigor an argument to you, which seems to be taken away from these things while they are separated from the earth and handled? But if this moved you—although it can quickly be noticed that it is false, on account of the increased firmness of some after they are taken from the earth, as has already been mentioned about wine, which becomes stronger with age—yet if this firmness moved you, as I said, you would prove that in no food is the part of God more copious than in flesh. For athletes, to whom that strength and vigor are especially necessary, do not feed on vegetables and fruits and not on flesh.

**16.** 49. Or, because flesh is nourished by trees, but trees are not nourished by flesh, do you therefore judge that their bodies are better than ours? You do not consider a matter so much at hand: that shrubs grow more luxuriant and fruitful, and crops fatter, by the nourishment of dung, while you, in your accusation of flesh, seem to say nothing graver of it than when you say that it is the home of dung. From this, then, are nourished the things that are clean to you, which you proclaim to be much more unclean in that thing which is unclean to you. But if you spurn flesh because it is born after coupling, let the flesh of worms delight you, which are born so many and so great in fruits, in wood, in the earth itself, without any coupling. But this is some pretense, I know not what. For if flesh displeased you for this reason, that it is formed by the mingling of father and mother, you would not say that those princes of darkness were born from the fruits of their trees—princes whom you certainly spurn more than the flesh you are unwilling to taste.

**16.** 50. For as for your opinion that all the souls of animals indeed come from the food of their parents, and that you glory in freeing from these prisons the divine substance that is held in your foods, this very much presses you against yourselves and most urgently impels you to eat flesh. For why do you not free the souls which those who feast on flesh are going to bind to a body, by anticipating them and eating? But, one says, nothing of the good part is bound by them from flesh, but from the produce that they take with the flesh. What, then, must be answered about the souls of lions, whose food is flesh alone? They drink, he says, and therefore that soul has been drawn from the water and entangled in the flesh. What can be said about the innumerable birds? What about eagles themselves, which are fed on nothing but flesh and need no drink? Here surely you fail, and find nothing that can be answered. For if the soul comes from food, and there are animals that beget offspring whose only food is flesh and which have no drink, then there is in flesh a soul which, that it may be purged, you ought, after your manner, to help by eating flesh. Unless perhaps you think the pig, which is fed on produce and drinks water, has a soul of light, but you maintain that in the eagle, to which the sun is especially congenial, a soul of darkness dwells, because it lives on flesh alone.

**16.** 51. O the straits of the matter, O the incredible absurdities! Into which you would surely not have fallen, if, being strangers to most empty fables, you followed, as to the abstinence of foods, what truth approves: that you should judge delicious foods to be rejected for the sake of restraining desire, not of avoiding an uncleanness that does not exist. For if anyone, even less attentive to the nature of things and to the force of soul and body, should grant you that the soul is defiled by sauce, you nevertheless grant that it is made far more unclean by lust.

What reason, then—or rather what madness—is it to expel from the number of the elect a man who has perhaps tasted flesh for the sake of his health, with no lust, but, if he has desired to eat peppered truffles greedily, to detect perhaps only intemperance and not to condemn him as a corrupter of the seal? So it comes about that one cannot be among your elect who has been betrayed as having dined on some part of a hen, not out of lust but for the sake of healing, but one can be among them who has himself betrayed that he vehemently desired pastries and other things lacking flesh.

You hold guilty, then, the one whom lust plunges into filth; but the one whom the food itself, as you suppose, defiles, you do not hold guilty—although you confess that the defilement from desire is far greater than that from sauce—yet punishing the one who hovers, gaping, over most sweetly seasoned produce and does not restrain himself, but acquitting the one who, for the sake of suppressing hunger, prepared to take and prepared to lose whatever human foods, takes them indifferently, without any lust. Behold marvelous morals, behold a distinguished discipline, behold a memorable temperance!

**16.** 52. Now, as to the fact that you think it a sacrilege if anyone besides the elect touches for eating the things offered to you, as it were, to be purged at your feasts—of how great baseness, and sometimes of crime, is this full? Since often so many things are given that they cannot easily be consumed by a few. And because it is thought a sacrilege either to give to others what is left over or certainly to throw it away, you are thrust into great surfeits, desiring to purge, as it were, all that has been given. And now, distended and almost bursting, you compel the children who are under your discipline to devour the remainder by a cruel domination, so that it was charged against a certain man at Rome that, by forcing wretched little ones to eat, he killed them by such superstition. Which I would not believe, did I not know how great a sacrilege you judge it either to give these things to others who are not elect or certainly to see to their being thrown away. Whence there remains that necessity of eating, which can come almost daily to a most base surfeit, and sometimes even to homicide.

**16.** 53. Since these things are so, you even forbid giving bread to a beggar, yet you judge that money should be given, out of mercy, or rather out of envy. What shall I here first reprove—cruelty or madness? For what if the matter takes place where purchasable food cannot be found? That needy man is going to die of hunger, while you, a wise and kindly man, pity a cucumber more than a man. This is surely—for what could I say more fittingly and plainly?—false mercy and true cruelty. Now let us look at the madness. For what if with that money you give him he buys himself bread? Will not that divine part of yours, in the man who takes it from the seller, suffer in this what it was going to suffer if he had taken it from your giving? That sinful beggar, then, has wrapped in filth the part of God that desired to fly back, helped to so great a crime by your money; and yet you most prudent men think it makes a difference if you do not give a man to be killed to one about to commit murder, but, knowing it, do give money from which he may procure the one to be killed.

What can be added to this insanity? For so it comes about that either a man dies, if he does not find purchasable food, or the food itself dies, if he finds it; of which the one is true homicide, the other yours, but to be so attributed to you as if both were true. For as to the fact that you do not forbid your hearers to eat flesh, but forbid them to kill animals, what more foolish and perverse can be done? For if such food does not contaminate, take it yourselves; if it contaminates, what madness, after all, is it to think it a greater sacrilege to loose a pig's soul from the body than to defile a human soul with a pig's body?

## What the seal of the hands is like among the Manicheans is laid open.

**17.** 54. But let us now come to considering and treating the seal of the hands. And first indeed, that you abstain from the killing of animals and the tearing of plants, Christ showed to be most superstitious; for, indicating that we have no fellowship of right with beasts and trees, he both sent demons into a herd of swine[^18] and made a tree, on which he had found no fruit, dry by a curse[^19]. Certainly the swine had sinned in nothing, the tree in nothing. For we are not so mad as to think that a tree is fruitful or barren of its own accord. Nor is it to be said by you here that by these deeds our Lord wished to signify certain other things—for who would not know it? But surely a sign should not have been given by the Son of God through homicide, if to kill a tree, as you say, is homicide, or to kill animals. For of human beings too, with whom we are certainly joined in a fellowship of right, he gave certain signs, but by healing people, not by killing them[^20]. Which he would have done with beasts and trees too, if he had judged us to be joined to them by the same fellowship that you suppose.

**17.** 55. At this point it seemed to me that authority should be brought in, for this reason: that about the soul of cattle and about a certain life by which trees are said to live, it cannot be subtly disputed with you. But since you protect yourselves by a certain privilege, so as not to be overcome from the Scriptures, by saying that they have been falsified—although those things I mentioned about the tree[^21] and about the herd of swine[^22] you have never said were interpolated by corrupters—yet, lest, considering how much it is against you, you should at some time wish to say this same thing even about these, I will keep to my proposal, so as to ask of you, who are altogether great promisers of reason and truth, first what harm is done to a tree—I do not say if you pluck a fruit or leaf from it, which indeed, among you, if anyone do it not by imprudence but knowingly, he will without any doubt be condemned as a corrupter of the seal—but altogether if you uproot it from the root. For that soul which you judge to be rationally present in trees is, when the tree is cut down, loosed from a bond—for you say this—and indeed from that bond in which it was held with great misery and no advantage. For it is known to you that your own author is wont to threaten the transmigration of a man into a tree as a huge punishment, though not the highest; and a soul cannot become wiser in a tree, as in a man. For the most certain reason for not killing a man is lest you either kill one whose wisdom and virtue profit others most, or one who could perhaps have arrived at wisdom, whether admonished from outside by someone or divinely illumined by inner thoughts.

But the soul of a man, the wiser it is when it leaves the body, the more usefully it leaves it—truth teaches this, both by most subtle reason and by an authority most widely spread. For which reason, the one who fells a tree frees from that body a soul making no progress in wisdom. And so you holy men, you especially, ought to be cutting down trees and leading their souls, stripped of that bond, by prayers and psalms to better things. Or can this be done for those souls, not which you have helped with your mind, but which you have received into your belly?

**17.** 56. Although this very thing—that the souls of trees, so long as they are in trees, make no progress toward wisdom—the greatest straits, as I judge, compel you to confess, when you are asked why an apostle teacher is not sent to trees too, or why the one who is sent to human beings does not preach the truth to trees as well. Here you are forced to answer that those souls cannot receive the divine precepts in such bodies. But you are more vehemently pressed from the other side, since you declare that they both hear our voices and understand our words and behold bodies and the motions of bodies, and finally discern our very thoughts. If these things are true, why can they learn nothing from the apostle of light? Or why can they not even much more easily than we, since they look even into the inner things of the mind? For thus that teacher who, as you say, scarcely teaches you by speaking, could instruct by thinking those souls which would discern his thoughts in his mind before his speech. But if these things are false, see at last in what error you lie.

**17.** 57. Now as to the fact that you yourselves do not pluck fruits and do not pull up grass, but nevertheless bid your hearers to pluck and pull up and offer them to you, so that you may be able to profit not only those who bring them but even the things that are brought—who, considering it well, would in any way tolerate it?

First, because it makes no difference whether you yourself commit a crime or wish it to be committed by another on your account. You say you do not wish it. How, then, is that divine part helped, which lies in lettuces and in leeks, if no one pulls them up and brings them to the saints to be purged?

Then, you yourself, passing through a field in which, by the right of friendship, you are given power to pluck whatever you please, if you see a crow hovering over a fig, what will you do? Does it not, by your opinion, seem that the fig itself speaks with you and pitiably begs that you yourself pluck it and bury it to be purified and revived by your holy belly, rather than that that crow, having devoured it, mingle it with its deadly body and pass it on to be bound and tortured in other forms? What is crueler than you, if it is true? What more inept, if it is false? What more contrary to your discipline, if you loose the seal? What more hostile to you, O member of God, if you keep it?

**17.** 58. But this follows from your false and trifling opinion; for a sure and manifest cruelty is proved to be in you, flowing from that same error. For if anyone, his body dissolved by disease, weary from a journey and half-alive from a plague, lie on the road, able to do no more than somehow utter words, and to him it would profit, for binding his body together, to be given a pear, and he should beg you as you pass that you help, and beseech you to bring the man a fruit from the nearest tree—from which you are forbidden by no human, no, finally, true right—the man being about to die after a little while unless you do it: will you, a Christian and holy man, rather pass by and desert a man so afflicted and begging, lest the tree weep while its fruit is taken down and you, a loosener of the seal, be destined for Manichean punishments? O morals and singular innocence!

**17.** 59. But now, about the killing of animals, I will inquire what moves you, and many things indeed of this kind can be said in this matter too. For what harm will be done to the soul of a wolf by one who kills a wolf, when that wolf, as long as it lives, is going to be a wolf and obeys no preacher so as to refrain a little from the blood of sheep, and the rational soul, according to you, is, when the beast is killed, freed from that bond of the body? And from this killing too you forbid even your hearers, for it seems greater than that in trees.

Here I do not much disapprove of your senses, the bodily ones, that is. For we both see and perceive by their cries that living creatures die with pain, which indeed a human being disregards in a beast, with which—since it does not have a rational soul—he is coupled by no fellowship of law. But I inquire after these same senses of yours in beholding trees, and I find you altogether blind. For, to leave aside that no sense of pain appears in wood by any motions, what is more manifest than that a tree is then in the best condition when it flourishes, when it is leafy, when it is glad with flowers, rich with fruits? But this is for the most part, and especially, furnished to it by pruning. And if iron felt it, as you wish, the tree would rather waste away, afflicted by so many and so great wounds, than, sprouting from those very places, grow green again with so sure an exultation.

**17.** 60. Yet why do you think it a greater sacrilege to cut down animals than plants, since the latter seem to you to have a purer soul than flesh? There is made, one says, a certain compensation, when some part of the things that are taken from the fields is given to the elect and the saints to be purged. These things have already been frustrated above, and it has been sufficiently shown, as I judge, that by no reason can it be said that there is more of that good part in produce than in flesh. But if anyone supports his living by selling flesh and spends all the profit of such trade in buying foods for your elect, and this man brings more foods to the saints than a farmer and rustic, will he not cry out that by the same compensation it is lawful for him to kill living creatures? There is, one says, a certain other most secret reason. For a clever man does not lack a refuge against the unlearned in the obscurity of nature. For the heavenly princes, he says, who, captured and bound from the race of darkness, were ordered by the founder of the world in those places, each possess their own animals on their own earth, coming, namely, from their own kind and stock; and they hold the killers of these guilty and do not permit them to go out of this world, and wear them down with such punishments and tortures as they can. Who among the unlearned would not dread these things, and who, seeing nothing in such great obscurity, would not suppose that it is as it is said? But I will not leave my proposal, in which God will be present to me, that obscure lies may be refuted by the most open truth.

**17.** 61. For I ask: if the animals that are on land and in the waters come from that race of princes through the succession of offspring and the activity of coupling, since the origin of those being born is traced back to those miscarried fetuses—I ask, I say, if it is so, is it not a sacrilege to kill bees and frogs and many other things that are begotten without coupling? You say it is a sacrilege. Therefore it is not on account of the kinship of certain princes, whoever they are, that you forbid your hearers from the killing of animals. Or if you say that there is a general kinship of all bodies, then trees too will without doubt pertain to that same offense of the princes, which trees the hearers are not commanded to spare. We return, then, to that weak point: that the things in plants that the hearers harm are expiated through the fruits that they bring to your church. For it was said in this way that those who in the market butcher animals and sell flesh, if they are your hearers and bring their profits to you in produce that has been bought, can, by the same reckoning, hold that daily occupation of theirs lawful for themselves, and that whatever of sin is in it is abolished by your feasts.

**17.** 62. But if you say that, just as in the case of fruits and vegetables there had to be a contribution that that killing might deserve pardon—which, because it cannot be done (for the elect do not eat flesh), the hearers must abstain from the killing of animals—what will you answer about thorns and useless weeds, which farmers, by pulling them up in clearing the fields, kill, and from which they can furnish you no foods? How will so great a devastation, from which there is no food for the saints, pertain to pardon? Or do you perhaps dissolve whatever sin there was that produce and fruits might thrive, by eating something of those very produce and fruits? What, then, if locusts or mice and shrews devastate the fields, which it is manifest often happens? Will they be killed with impunity by your hearer the farmer, because he sins to the end that the fruits may thrive? Here certainly you are pressed. For either you grant the hearers the killing of animals which your author was unwilling to grant, or you will forbid them even the cultivation of the field, which he granted. Although you often even dare to say that a moneylender is more innocent than a peasant, so much more friendly are you to melons than to men. Since, lest they be harmed, you judge it better that a man be slaughtered by usury. Is this a justice to be desired and proclaimed, or rather a deceit to be execrated and condemned? Is this a memorable mercy, or rather an execrable monstrousness?

**17.** 63. What of the fact that from the killing of animals not even you yourselves abstain in the case of lice and fleas and bedbugs? And you think this a great defense of the matter, that you say these are the filth of our bodies. Which first is plainly false as said of the flea and the bedbug. For to whom is it not manifest that these animals do not come into existence from our body? Then, if you execrate coupling more vehemently, which you wish to seem excessive, why do those animals not seem cleaner to you which are born from our flesh without coupling? For although they afterward give birth by mating, they are not, when we mate, first born from our body. But now, if whatever things are begotten of living bodies are to be thought most filthy, much more whatever are begotten of dead ones. With more impunity, then, is a shrew or a snake or a scorpion killed, which especially we are wont to hear from you are born of human corpses.

But I leave aside obscure and uncertain things. About bees there is certainly a more celebrated report[^23], that they arise from the carcasses of oxen. Therefore they are killed with impunity. But if this too is doubtful, scarcely anyone doubts about beetles that they come into existence from dung rolled into a ball by them and buried[^24]. These living creatures, then, and others which it is long to pursue, you ought certainly to think more filthy than your lice; and yet to kill those seems to you a sacrilege, but to spare these foolish—unless perhaps you despise these animals because they are small. Indeed, if it is so, that an animal ought to be the more contemptible the smaller it is, you must of necessity prefer a camel to a human being.

**17.** 64. To this is added that gradation which, when I heard you, often troubled us. For there is no reason why, on account of a small measure of body, a flea should be killed, but not also a fly that is begotten in a bean. And if this one, why not also that one a little larger, whose offspring is certainly smaller than the fly? Now this follows: that a bee too is killed without fault, whose young is scarcely equal to this fly. Thence to the young of a locust and a locust, thence to the young of a mouse and a mouse. And, not to make it long, do you not see that by these steps one arrives at an elephant, so that whoever thinks the killing of a flea is not a sin on account of its tiny body cannot at all refuse to kill that huge beast without fault? But I think enough has now been said even about trifles of this kind.

## On the seal of the breast and the abominable mysteries of the Manicheans.

**18.** 65. There remains the seal of the breast, in which your chastity is much unchaste. For you forbid not coupling, but, as was long ago said by the Apostle[^25], truly marriage, which is the one honorable defense of such a work. Here I do not doubt that you will cry out and stir up resentment by saying that you vehemently commend and praise perfect chastity, but do not forbid marriage; since your hearers, whose grade among you is the second, are not forbidden to take and have wives. And when you have said this with a loud voice and great indignation, I will ask you more gently in this way: Are you not the ones who think that to beget children, because souls are bound in the flesh, is a graver sin than coupling itself? Are you not the ones who are accustomed to admonish us to observe, as far as could be done, the time at which a woman is, after the purgation of her genital inward parts, fit for conception, and at that time to abstain from coupling, lest a soul be entangled in flesh? From which it follows that you judge one should have a spouse not for the sake of procreating children but of satisfying lust. But marriage, as the very marriage tablets cry out, joins male and female for the sake of procreating children; whoever, then, says that to procreate children is a graver sin than to couple, certainly forbids marriage, and makes the woman no longer a wife but a harlot, who, with certain things given to her, is joined to a man to satisfy his lust. For if she is a wife, it is matrimony. But it is not matrimony where care is taken that she not be a mother; therefore she is not a wife. For which reason you forbid marriage, nor do you by any reason defend yourselves from this charge, which was long ago foretold of you by the Holy Spirit.

**18.** 66. Now, when you vehemently busy yourselves that a soul not be bound in the flesh through coupling, and vehemently assert that through the food of the saints the soul is freed from seeds, do you not confirm, O wretches, what people suspect about you? For why is it believed that you wish to free the soul from wheat and bean and lentil and other seeds, when you eat them, but not believed concerning the seed of animals? For just as you say that the very flesh of a dead living creature is unclean because it does not have a soul, you cannot say the same of the seed of a living creature, in which you judge that the soul which will appear in the offspring is bound, and in which you confess that the soul of Mani himself was entangled. And because such seeds cannot be brought to you by your hearers to be purged, who would not suspect that a secret purgation of this kind takes place among you about yourselves, and is therefore hidden from them, lest they desert you? If you do not do these things—which I would it were so—you see nevertheless to how great a suspicion your superstition lies open, and how people are not to be blamed for supposing what is gathered from your profession, since you proclaim that you free souls from bodies and senses through food and drink. I do not wish to dwell here longer; you see how great is the occasion for invective. But because the matter is such that discourse dreads rather than assails it, and because that proposal of mine can be noticed throughout my whole discourse, by which I resolved to exaggerate nothing but to deal, as it were, with bare facts and reasons, let us pass to another matter.

## The outrages of the Manicheans.

**19.** 67. For it now appears sufficiently of what sort your three seals are. These are your morals, this is the end of your admirable precepts, where there is nothing certain, nothing constant, nothing reasonable, nothing blameless, but all things doubtful, indeed without doubt most false, all things conflicting, all things absurd.

In short, so many and so grave sins are detected in these morals that, if anyone wished to accuse them all, a man of some ability could treat each one, at the least, in a single volume. If, then, you kept these and fulfilled your profession, nothing would be more inept than you, nothing more foolish, nothing more ignorant; but since you praise and teach these things and do not do them, what more deceitful, what more treacherous, what more malicious can be said or found than you?

## What Augustine heard and saw in the school of the Manicheans.

**19.** 68. For nine whole years I heard you with great care and diligence; no one of the elect could become known to me who, according to these precepts, was not either caught in sin or certainly subject to suspicion. Many were found amid wine and flesh, many bathing in the baths. But these things we only heard. Some were proved to have seduced other men's wives, so that on this point I plainly cannot doubt.

But let even this be report rather than truth. I myself saw—not alone, but with those who have partly already been freed from that superstition, and whom I partly still wish to be freed—we saw, then, at a crossroads of Carthage, in a most frequented square, not one but more than three of the elect together, behind some women passing by, neighing with so wanton a gesture that they surpassed the lewdness and shamelessness of all the loungers of the streets.

That this came from a great habit, and that they so lived among themselves, was sufficiently evident, since no one, fearing the presence of his companion, indicated that all, or certainly almost all, were held by the same plague. For these were not from one house, but dwelling quite separately; they had perhaps come down together from the place where the assembly of all had been held. But we, gravely moved, gravely also complained. Who, after all, thought this should be punished—I do not say by separation from the church, but, considering the greatness of the outrage, by at least a vehement rebuke?

**19.** 69. And this was the whole excuse for their impunity: that, at the time when their conventicles were forbidden by public law, it was feared lest some, being injured, should betray them. Where, then, is what they proclaim, that a perpetual persecution will be theirs in this world, and on this account wish themselves to be held more commendable, interpreting from this that this world hates them[^26], and asserting that therefore the truth is to be sought with them, because in the promise of the Holy Spirit the Paraclete it was said that this world cannot receive him[^27]? About which matter this is not the place to discuss. But certainly, if a perpetual persecution is to be yours, even unto the end of the age, perpetual also will be this dissolution and the unpunished contagion of so great a baseness, while you dread to injure such men.

**19.** 70. This too was answered us, when we had reported to the leading men themselves that a woman had complained to us that, in a chamber where she was with other women—secure, namely, in the holiness of those men—when several of the elect had entered and the lamp had been put out by one of them, it was uncertain by which of them in the darkness she had been assailed with an embrace and forced into outrage, had she not escaped by the help of her outcry. This sacrilege too, most well known to us, from how great a habit must it be supposed to have come? And this was done on that night when the festal vigils are celebrated among you. But truly, even if there were no fear of betrayal, who could offer to the bishop one to be condemned, who had so taken precaution lest he be recognized? As if indeed the same crime did not hold guilty all who had entered together. For all of them, jesting wantonly, had been pleased that the lamp be put out.

**19.** 71. But how great doors were opened to suspicions, when we found them envious, when avaricious, when most greedy for choice feasts, when most frequent in quarrels, when most easily stirred over trifling matters? We did not at all suppose that those could restrain themselves from things they professed to restrain themselves from, when they should find hiding places and darkness.

There were two of fairly good reputation, of ready ability and leaders in those disputations of theirs, joined to us more and more familiarly than the rest. One of them, who clung to us more closely on account of liberal studies too, is now said to be a presbyter there. These two gravely envied each other, and the one charged the other—not by an open accusation, but by talk among those he could and by whispers—with having violently assailed the wife of a certain hearer.

But that man, clearing himself, meanwhile accused before us another elect of the same crime, who dwelt with that same hearer as a most faithful friend; and because, suddenly entering, he had caught him with the woman, he said that counsel had been given to the woman and the adulterer by that hostile and envious man, that this slander should be forged against him, lest, if he should betray anything, he be believed. We were anguished and bore it most grievously that, although about the woman who was assailed it was uncertain, yet the spite in those two, than whom we found none better there, appeared most keen and forced us to conjecture such things.

**19.** 72. Finally, in the theaters we very often found elect grave both in age and, as it seemed, in morals, together with an old presbyter. I leave aside the young men, whom we used to catch even brawling on behalf of stage-players and charioteers—which thing is no slight argument of how they can restrain themselves from hidden things, when they cannot overcome that desire which displays and offers them to the eyes of their own hearers, blushing and fleeing. Or indeed would that great outrage of even that holy man, to whose disputations we used to come in the street of the fig-sellers, have been betrayed, had he been able only to make a consecrated virgin a woman, and not also pregnant?

But the hidden and incredible evil, as the womb grew, did not allow itself to lie hidden. And when the mother had betrayed this to her young brother, grieving most keenly, he was nevertheless, by the name of religion, recalled from a public accusation; and he brought it about that the man—for no one could bear this—was driven out of that church; and lest the matter go altogether unpunished, he took counsel, with friends joined to him, to beat the man down with fists and heels. But that man, while he was being gravely beaten, cried out that he should be spared on the authority of Mani, that Adam, the first hero, had sinned, and after his sin had been holier[^28].

**19.** 73. For such is the opinion among you about Adam and Eve; it is a long fable, but from it I will touch on what suffices for the present. You say that Adam was so begotten by his parents, those miscarried princes of darkness, that he had the greatest part of light in his soul and a very small part of the hostile race. And although he lived holily on account of the surpassing abundance of good, yet that hostile part was stirred in him, so that he was bent toward coupling; thus he fell and sinned, but afterward lived holier.

Here I do not so much complain of a wicked man, who by abominable defilement led another's household, under the dress of an elect and holy man, into so great disgrace and infamy. I do not charge this to you. Let this have been the deed of a most abandoned man rather than of your habit. I do not, then, charge so great an outrage to you but to him. Yet that one thing, how it can be borne and tolerated in all of you, I do not know: that, although you say the soul is a part of God, you nevertheless assert that even by a small admixture of evil its greater abundance and richness is overcome. For who, having believed this, when lust has assailed him, would not flee to such a defense rather than to the bridling and suppressing of that lust?

## The outrages of the same men detected at Rome.

**20.** 74. What more shall I say about your morals? I have said the things I myself learned, when I was in that city where these things were committed. But what was done at Rome in my absence, it is long to set forth in full. Yet I will say it briefly.

For the matter broke out to such a point that it could not be hidden from those absent; and I myself, afterward, when I was at Rome, confirmed that I had heard all to be true; although one so familiar and approved by me, who was present, had reported the matter to me, that I could not doubt it at all.

For a certain hearer of yours, in no way yielding to the elect in that memorable abstinence, who had also been liberally educated and was both willing and accustomed to defend your sect copiously, bore it most grievously that, when he often disputed, the most abandoned morals of elect who lived wandering, most wickedly, and lived everywhere at random, were cast in his teeth. He desired, therefore, if it could be done, to gather into his house all who were prepared to lead a life according to those precepts, and to support them at his own expense. For he was both no slight despiser of money and not slightly moneyed. But he complained that his so great efforts were hindered by the dissoluteness of the bishops, by whose help he ought to have fulfilled them.

Meanwhile a certain bishop of yours, a man plainly, as I myself experienced, rustic and unpolished, but somehow, by that very harshness, seeming more severe in keeping good morals—this man, very long desired and at length present, that hearer seizes upon; he sets forth his wish to the man: the bishop praises it and consents; it is agreed that the bishop himself should first begin to dwell in his house. When this was done, all the elect who could be at Rome were gathered there. A rule of living was set forth from Mani's epistle; to many it seemed intolerable, and they went away; yet not a few remained out of shame. They began to live as had been agreed, and as so great an authority prescribed, while meanwhile that hearer vehemently forced all to all things, yet no one to anything that he himself did not first undertake.

Meanwhile most frequent quarrels arose among the elect; crimes were cast at one another, all of which that hearer heard with groaning, and he took pains that they should, in quarreling, most incautiously betray themselves; they betrayed abominable and monstrous things. There it was learned what sort they were who nevertheless, among the rest, had thought that the force of those precepts must be undergone by them. Now about the rest, what was to be suspected, or rather what to be judged? Why more? At length, being compelled, they murmured that those commandments could not be borne; thence into sedition.

The hearer was pleading his cause with a most brief summary: either all those things must be kept, or the one who had enjoined such things under such a condition, which no one could fulfill, must be thought most foolish. Yet the most unbridled clamor of the many overcame the opinion of one—for it could not be otherwise. Afterward the bishop himself too gave way and fled with great disgrace; whose foods, indeed, taken secretly contrary to the rule and often found, were reported, although abundant money was supplied to him from his own little purse, carefully hidden.

**20.** 75. If you say these things are false, you withstand things too open and widely spread. But would that you would say it. For since these things are manifest and most easy to learn for those who have wished to know, it is understood how truly those are wont to speak who have denied that these things are true. But you use other defenses, which I do not disapprove. For you either say that there are some who keep your precepts and that they ought not to be drenched with the crimes of others, or that one ought not at all to ask of what sort the people are who profess your sect, but of what sort the profession itself is.

Both of which, although I grant them—although you can neither show those to be faithful observers of the commandments nor purge the heresy itself from so many and so great trifles and crimes—yet this I will especially ask of you: why you assail with curses Christians of the Catholic name, looking at the abandoned life of certain ones, while concerning your own people you either shamelessly refuse that a question be raised, or more shamelessly do not refuse, and wish it to be understood that in your so great fewness there lie hidden certain ones, whoever they are, who keep their precepts, but in so great a multitude of Catholics are unwilling to have it understood?
