# Revisions I

## Chapter 7 (6): The Catholic Way of Life and the Manichean Way of Life, in Two Books

**1.** After I had been baptized, while I was at Rome, I could not bear in silence the boasting of the Manicheans about a false and deceptive continence or abstinence by which, in order to deceive the unlearned, they even put themselves ahead of true Christians, with whom they are not to be compared. And so I wrote two books, one *On the Catholic Way of Life* and the other *On the Manichean Way of Life.*

**2.** In the book, then, that is *On the Catholic Way of Life*, where I set down the testimony in which one reads, *For your sake we are afflicted all the day long; we are counted as sheep for the slaughter*[^1]*,* the faultiness of our manuscript misled me, since I was less mindful of the Scriptures, in which I was not yet practiced. For other manuscripts of the same translation do not have *for your sake we are afflicted,* but *for your sake we are put to death*, which others expressed in a single word: *we are slain*.

That this is more accurate the Greek books show, from which language the translation of the ancient divine Scriptures was made into Latin according to the *Septuagint* translators. And yet, following these words, that is, *for your sake we are afflicted,* I said much in the course of my discussion that I do not, as regards the things themselves, reject as false. Nevertheless, the agreement of the Old and New Scriptures, which I was eager to demonstrate, I certainly did not demonstrate from these words alone. But I have said how the error crept up on me; from other testimonies, however, I showed that same agreement sufficiently.

**3.** Likewise a little later I set down a testimony from the book of *Wisdom* according to our manuscript, in which it was written, *For wisdom teaches sobriety and justice and virtue*[^2]*.* And following these words I discussed things that were indeed true, but found by the occasion of a faulty text. For what is truer than that wisdom teaches the truth of contemplation, which I supposed to be signified by the name of sobriety, and the uprightness of action, which I wanted understood by the two others, by justice and virtue, although the more accurate manuscripts of the same translation have, *For it teaches sobriety and wisdom and justice and virtue*? For by these names the Latin translator named those four virtues that are especially wont to be on the lips of the philosophers, calling temperance sobriety, imposing on prudence the name of wisdom, declaring fortitude by the word virtue, and rendering justice alone by its own name. But these four virtues, named in the same book of *Wisdom* by their own names, as they are called by the Greeks, we discovered much later in the Greek manuscripts.

**4.** Again, what I set down from the book of Solomon, *Vanity of vanitizers, said Ecclesiastes,* I did indeed read in many manuscripts; but the Greek does not have this, rather it has *vanity of vanities*[^3], which I saw afterward, and I found that those Latin texts are more accurate which have *of vanities* and not *of vanitizers*. Yet whatever I discussed on the occasion of this faulty text appears to be true in the things themselves.

**5.** As for my saying that *him whom we wish to know,* that is, God, *let us first love with full love*[^4]*,* it would be better said "sincere" than *full*, lest perhaps it be thought that the love of God will not be greater when we shall see him *face to face*[^5]. And so let this be taken in such a way that "full" is said of the love than which there can be none greater so long as we walk by faith; for it will be fuller, indeed fullest, but by sight.

**6.** Likewise, what I said about those who come to the aid of the needy, that *they are called merciful, even if they should be so wise as to be no longer disturbed by any grief of mind*[^6]*,* is not to be taken as though I had laid down that such wise persons exist in this life; for I did not say "since they are so," but I said *even if they should be so*.

**7.** In another place, where I said, *But now, when this human love has nourished and strengthened the mind that clings to your breasts, and made it fit to follow God, when his majesty has begun to disclose itself so far as suffices for a human being while he dwells on this earth, there is born so great an ardor of love and there rises so great a blaze of divine love that, with all the vices burned away and the person sanctified and purified, it appears well enough how divinely it was said, 'I am a consuming fire'*[^7]*,* the Pelagians can suppose that I said this perfection can be attained in this mortal life.

But let them not suppose this. For the ardor of love, made fit by following God and so great that it consumes all vices, can be born and can grow in this life; but to accomplish that for which it is born, namely that no vice should be in the person, it cannot consequently do here, although this great thing is brought to completion by that same ardor of love where it can be completed and when it can be completed. So just as the bath of regeneration purifies from the guilt of all the sins that human birth drew along and iniquity contracted, so that perfection purifies from the defilement of all the vices without which human weakness cannot exist in this world;

as is also to be understood what the Apostle says: *Christ loved the Church and handed himself over for her, cleansing her by the bath of water in the word, that he might present to himself a glorious Church, having no spot or wrinkle or any such thing*[^8]. For this is the *bath of water in the word* by which the Church is cleansed. But since the whole Church says, so long as it is here, *Forgive us our debts*[^9], it is certainly not here *without spot or wrinkle or any such thing*; yet from what it here receives, it is brought to that glory, which is not here, and to perfection.

**8.** In the other book, whose title is *On the Manichean Way of Life,* what I said, that *the goodness of God so orders all defective things that they may be where they can most fittingly be, until by ordered motions they return to that from which they fell away*[^10], is not to be taken as though all things *return to that from which they fell away*, as it seemed to Origen[^11], but as referring to all those things that do return. For those who will be punished by everlasting fire do not return to God from whom they fell away, although all defective things are so ordered that they may be where they can most fittingly be, since even those who do not return are most fittingly in punishment.

In another place I say, *Scarcely anyone doubts about beetles that they come into existence from dung rolled into a ball by them and buried*[^12]*,* although many doubt whether this is true, and many have not even heard of it.

This work begins thus: *In other books I think we have dealt sufficiently.*
