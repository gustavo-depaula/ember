# Revisions I

## Chapter 16 (15): The Proceedings against Fortunatus the Manichean, One Book

**1.** During the same period of my priesthood I held a debate against a certain Fortunatus, a Manichean priest who had lived for a very long time at Hippo and had led astray so many people that it pleased him to make his home there on their account. This debate of ours, as we disputed, was taken down by stenographers, just as official records are drawn up; for it bears both a date and the name of a consul. We have taken care to gather it into a book, to be committed to memory. The question dealt with there is where evil comes from, I asserting that man's evil arose from the free choice of the will, while he labored to make the case that the nature of evil is coeternal with God. But on the following day he finally confessed that he could find nothing to say against us. Nor, to be sure, did he become a Catholic, yet he did depart from Hippo.

**2.** In this book what I said—*I say that the soul was made by God, like all the other things that were made by God, and that among those things that almighty God made the soul was given the foremost place*[^1]—I said in such a way that I meant it to be taken in a general sense, of the whole of rational creation, although in the holy Scriptures it cannot be found either at all or at least not easily that the souls of the angels are called souls, as we have already said above[^2].

Likewise in another place: *I say,* I declared, *that there is no sin unless one sins by one's own will*[^3]*.* There I meant that sin to be understood which is not also the punishment of sin; for of such a punishment I said elsewhere in the same debate what had to be said[^4].

And likewise I said: *So that afterward this same flesh, which tormented us with punishments while we remained in our sins, may be made subject to us in the resurrection and may shake us with no adversity, so as to keep us from observing the law and the divine precepts*[^5]*.* This is not to be taken as though even in that kingdom of God, where we will have an incorruptible and immortal body, the law and the precepts must be drawn from the divine Scriptures; rather, it means that there the eternal law will be most perfectly kept, and that we will hold those two precepts about loving God and neighbor[^6] not by reading them but in that perfect and everlasting love itself.

This work begins thus: *On the fifth day before the Kalends of September, in the consulship of Arcadius Augustus for the second time and of Rufinus, men of the highest rank.*

[^1]: Acta c. Fortun. 1, 13.
[^2]: Retract. 1, 11, 4.
[^3]: Acta c. Fortun. 2, 21.
[^4]: Acta c. Fortun. 1, 15; Retract. 1, 15, 2.
[^5]: Acta c. Fortun. 2, 29.
[^6]: See Mark 12:30; Luke 10:27.
