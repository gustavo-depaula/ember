# Book 10

**1.** Faustus said: Why do you not accept the Old Testament?

Because both from it, and from the New, we have learned not to covet what is another's. But what, you say, does the Old Testament have that is another's? Rather, what does it have that is not another's? It promises riches, and fullness of the belly, and sons and grandsons, and a long life, and with these the kingdom of Canaan: but all these to the circumcised, and to those who observe sabbaths, and sacrifice to themselves, and abstain from pork, and the rest of this kind. Since I neglect these, and so does every Christian, as silly, of course, and in no way pertaining to the salvation of the soul, I therefore recognize that what it promises no longer regards me:

and mindful that there is a commandment, *You shall not covet what is another's*[^1], I have willingly and gladly allowed the Jews to keep their own goods, content, of course, with the Gospel alone and the splendid inheritance of the kingdom of heaven. For just as I would rightly rebuke a Jew, if he usurped the Gospel for himself, saying: Shameless man, what have you to do with that whose precepts you do not keep?—so I fear lest a Jew should rebuke me with the same words, for holding the Old Testament whose commandments I despise.

**2.** Augustine replied: He is not ashamed often to repeat the same vain things, but it irks me often to reply with the same true things. So whoever seeks a reply even to these, let him read what we said above. But to a Jew saying to me: Why do you hold the Old Testament, whose precepts you do not keep?—I reply that the precepts for living life are kept by Christians even from those very books; but the precepts for signifying life were rightly observed then, when those things were being foretold which have now been revealed.

Therefore I do not observe even these for religion, yet I hold them for testimony: just as I hold the carnal promises too, which are contained there, whence properly it is called the Old Testament. For although eternal things to be hoped for have been revealed to me, I yet read those things as bearing witness, which happened to them in figure; but were written for our sake, upon whom the end of the ages has come[^2]. And so, if you have heard what we reply to the Jews, hear also what we set against the Manicheans.

**3.** Faustus certainly said that we can be disturbed, if the Jews say to us: Why do you hold the Old Testament, whose precepts you do not observe? To whom we reply, with the authority of that same Scripture venerated and kept. What do you reply, when it is said to you: Why do you hold the evangelical books, whose followers you feign to be, in order to deceive the unskilled; and the things written there, you not only do not believe, but even assail with all the strength you can? Surely you see that you can answer the things set against you from the New Testament even less than we can answer the things set against us from the Old.

For all the things written in the Old, we say to be both true, and divinely commanded, and distributed at suitable times. But you, when the things written in the books of the New Testament are set against you, and are not accepted by you, failing to reply, and with your throat choked by manifest truth, say with wounded breath that they have been falsified. What else could the choked mouths of liars breathe out? Or rather, what else could the pierced corpses of the dead stink of?

And yet Faustus confessed that he had learned not to covet what is another's, not only from the New Testament but also from the Old: which surely he could not learn from his own God. For that God, if he did not covet what was another's, why did he construct new ages upon the land of darkness, where they had never been? Or will he say: That race of darkness first coveted my kingdom, which was another's to it? Then he imitated the race of darkness, so that he too coveted what was another's? Or had the realm of light been too narrow before? War, then, was to be desired, that breadth of ruling might be acquired from victory. But if that is good, it could have been coveted before; only it was being waited for that the hostile race should first break out into war, by which it might be assailed, as it were, more justly. But if it is not good, why, when the enemy was conquered, did he wish his kingdom to grow upon another's land, when before, content with his own bounds, he had lived in full felicity?

But would that these men were truly willing to learn from those writings these very precepts for living life, one of which is that we not covet what is another's: surely they would grow gentle, and, made mild, would understand that those precepts for signifying life too, against which their offense barks, were fitting to be observed at that time. But how do we covet the Old Testament as though it were another's, when we read the things that happened to them in figure, but were written for our sake, upon whom the end of the ages has come[^3]? I think that he does not covet what is another's, who reads what was written for his own sake.

[^1]: Exod 20:17; Rom 7:7.
[^2]: 1 Cor 10:11.
[^3]: 1 Cor 10:11.
