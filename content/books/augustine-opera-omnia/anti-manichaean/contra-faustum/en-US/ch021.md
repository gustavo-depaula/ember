# Book XX

**1.** FAUSTUS said: Why do you worship the sun, unless because you are pagans and a schism of the gentiles, not a sect? Therefore it will not be beside the point to inquire into this very thing too, so that we may see more clearly which of us ought to be called by this name. And indeed, if I now report my faith to you simply, as among friends, I may perhaps seem to feign it for the sake of an excuse, or to blush, far be it, at the worship of the divine luminaries. But take it however you wish: I, at any rate, will not regret having spoken—if only for the sake of some who henceforth will know that our religion has nothing in common with the gentiles.

**2.** We, then, worship the one and the same divinity of God the Father almighty, and of Christ his Son, and of the Holy Spirit, under a threefold appellation: but the Father himself we believe to inhabit the highest and principal light, which Paul elsewhere calls inaccessible[^1]; the Son to dwell in this second and visible light; who, since he too is twofold—as the Apostle knew him, calling Christ the Power of God and the Wisdom of God[^2]—his power, indeed, we believe to dwell in the sun, but his wisdom in the moon. And of the Holy Spirit, who is the third majesty, we confess this whole compass of air to be the seat and lodging; from whose forces and spiritual outpouring the earth too, conceiving, brings forth a passible Jesus, who is the life and salvation of men, suspended from every tree. Wherefore both we, with respect to the universe, and you likewise, with respect to the bread and the cup, have an equal religion, although you most fiercely hate its authors. This is our faith: about which, if you think it should be inquired of elsewhere, you shall hear; though even this is no less firm an argument for the present, that either you, or anyone else, when asked where he believes his God to dwell, will not hesitate to answer: In the light—from which this worship of mine is confirmed by the testimony of almost all.

**3.** But now to that point, that you called us not a sect but a schism of the gentiles. A schism, unless I am mistaken, is for one who holds the same opinions and worships with the same rite as the rest to take pleasure only in a separation of the assembly. But a sect is for one who holds far other opinions than the rest, and has instituted for himself a worship of the divinity by another and far dissimilar rite. If these things are so, my opinion and worship, at any rate, are far other than the pagans'. About yours we shall see later. The pagans dogmatize that good and evil things, foul and splendid, perpetual and perishable, changeable and certain, corporeal and divine, have one principle. I think things very contrary to these, who confess God to be the principle of all good things, but Hyle of the contrary ones; for so does our theologian name the principle and nature of evil. Likewise the pagans think God is to be worshiped with altars, shrines, images, victims, and incense. From these I walk far different in this too, who think that I myself, if only I am worthy, am the rational temple of God; I receive Christ his Son as the living image of the living majesty; I set up as an altar a mind imbued with good arts and disciplines; I place divine honors too and sacrifices in prayers alone, and those pure and simple: how, then, am I a schism of the pagans?

**4.** For so far you could call me a schism of the Jews too, because I worship the almighty God, which every Jew boldly claims for himself, while you did not consider the diversity of rites by which the Almighty is worshiped by me and by the Jews—if indeed the Jews do worship the Almighty. But for the present we are treating of opinion, which has so deceived the pagans about the worship of the sun, as it has the Jews about the Almighty. But not even if you called me a schism of yours would it be true, although I venerate and worship Christ: because I worship him by another rite and another faith than you. For a schism ought either to change nothing from that whence it was made, or not much: as for instance you, who, falling away from the gentiles, first tore away with you the opinion of monarchy—that is, that you believe all things to be from God; but you turned their sacrifices into love-feasts, their idols into martyrs, whom you worship with like vows; you appease the shades of the dead with wine and feasts; you celebrate the solemn days of the gentiles together with them, such as the kalends and solstices; of your life, certainly, you have changed nothing; you are indeed a schism, having nothing different from its mother but the assembly. And likewise your predecessors the Jews, themselves too separated from the gentiles, abandoned only the graven images; but the temples, and immolations, and altars, and priesthoods, and all the ministry of holy things they practiced by the same rite, and much more superstitiously than the gentiles. And concerning the opinion of monarchy they too dissent in nothing from the pagans: wherefore it is established that you and the Jews are schisms of gentilism; whose faith and rites you hold, somewhat though slightly changed, and yet from the mere division of assemblies you suppose yourselves to be sects. But further, if you ask for sects, there will be no more than two—that is, the gentiles' and ours, who hold far different opinions from them. So opposed are we to each other, indeed, as is truth to falsehood, as day to night, as want to plenty, as disease to health. But you are neither a sect of error nor of truth, but only a schism; and not even that of truth at least, but of error.

**5.** AUGUSTINE replied: O ignorant plague, and shifty vanity! Why do you object to yourself what, if anyone objects it to you, he does not know with whom he is dealing? For we do not call you pagans, or a schism of the pagans, but say that you have a certain likeness to them, in that you worship many gods. But that you are far worse than they, because they worship things that exist, but that are not to be worshiped as gods; for idols do exist, but for salvation they are nothing. And he who worships a tree—not, indeed, by plowing, but by adoring—worships not what is nowhere, but what is not thus to be worshiped. The very demons too, of whom the Apostle says, *What the gentiles immolate, they immolate to demons and not to God*[^3], surely exist—those to whom he says they immolate, and whose associates he does not wish us to be. And further, heaven and earth, sea and air, sun and moon, and the other stars—all these appear manifest to the eyes, and are present to the senses themselves. When the pagans worship these as gods, or as parts of one great god—for some of them suppose the whole universe to be the greatest god—they worship things that exist. When we deal with them, that they may not worship these, we do not say to them that these are nothing, but that they are not to be worshiped; and we persuade them to worship the invisible God, the maker of all these, by participation in whom alone a man can become blessed—which all wish, no one doubts. But since some of them worship an invisible and incorporeal creature, such as the human soul and mind, yet because a man is not made blessed by participation in a creature of this kind either, that God who is not only invisible but also immutable—that is, the true God—is to be worshiped: because he alone is to be worshiped, by enjoying whom alone the worshiper becomes blessed; and by not enjoying whom alone every mind is wretched, however much it enjoys any other thing. But since you worship things that do not exist at all, but are fashioned by the vanity of your deceitful fables, you would be nearer to true piety and religion if you were at least pagans, or in the class of those who worship corporeal things—not, indeed, things to be worshiped, but yet things that are true. Whence I would say more truly that you do not even worship this sun, around whose circuit your prayer turns.

**6.** For even about it you boast things so false and so detestable that, if it avenged its own injuries, you would already be burning alive in its flames. For first you say it is a kind of ship: thus you not only err, as the saying goes, by the whole heaven, but even swim by it. Then, although it shines forth round to the eyes of all, and that shape is perfect for it according to the position of its order, you declare it triangular—that is, that this light radiates upon the world and the lands through a kind of triangular window of heaven. So it comes about that you turn your back and neck to this sun indeed, but adore not the sun itself, conspicuous in such clear roundness, but some ship or other, glittering and shining through a triangular opening, which you imagine fabricated. Which assuredly that craftsman would not make, if, as the timbers are bought from which the planks of ships are joined together, so too were bought the words from which the fables of heretics are fabricated. But these things are more tolerably either laughed at or wept over in you. That is intolerably wicked, that you say beautiful girls and boys are displayed from that very ship, with whose most shapely bodies the princes of darkness burn—males toward the females, and females toward the males—so that, in that flaming lust and gaping concupiscence, from their members, as from foul and squalid fetters, the members of your god may be loosed. And to these most obscene rags of yours you try to stitch the ineffable Trinity, saying that the Father dwells in a certain secret light; that the Son's power is in the sun, his wisdom in the moon; but the Holy Spirit in the air!

**7.** In this threefold—or rather now fourfold—fable of yours, concerning the Father's secret light, what shall I say to you, except that you cannot conceive of light except such as you are accustomed to see? For gazing on this light, conspicuous and most familiar to all flesh—not only of men, but even of beasts and little worms—you are accustomed to enlarge beyond measure the phantasm conceived in your heart from it, and to call that the light where God the Father dwells with the dwellers in his realm. For when have you distinguished the light by which we see from that light by which we understand—since you have never thought that to understand the truth is anything else than to imagine corporeal forms, whether finite or in some parts infinite, which you do not know to be empty phantasms? Accordingly, since there is so great a difference between the thought by which I think of your land of light, which is nowhere at all, and the thought by which I think of Alexandria, which I have never seen, but which nonetheless exists; and again, so great a difference between this by which I think of unknown Alexandria, and that by which I think of known Carthage: from this thought too, by which I think of certain and known bodies, the thought is far and incomparably distant by which I understand justice, chastity, faith, truth, love, goodness, and whatever is of this kind—which thought, tell me, if you can, what light it is, by which all those things which are not this body are both distinguished among themselves, and known by a faithful manifestation how far they are distant from this body; and yet even this light is not that light which God is. For this is a creature, that is the Creator; this is made, that is he who made it; this, finally, is changeable, while it wills what it did not will, and knows what it did not know, and remembers what it had forgotten; but that persists with an immutable will, truth, and eternity. And from it comes to us the beginning of existing, the reason of knowing, the law of loving; from it, to all irrational living things too, the nature by which they live, the vigor by which they perceive, the motion by which they seek; from it, to all bodies too, measure that they may subsist, number that they may be adorned, weight that they may be ordered. And so that light, the inseparable Trinity, is the one God, whom you, with no body added, divide even by places—a substance incorporeal, spiritual, and immutable in itself. Nor even do you give three places to the Trinity, but four: to the Father one, that is, the inaccessible light, which you utterly do not understand; to the Son two, namely the sun and the moon; to the Holy Spirit again one, that is, this whole compass of air. Concerning the Father's inaccessible light, then—because from it the Son and the Holy Spirit are not separated for those who hold the true faith—let this much be said for the present.

**8.** But why has it pleased your vanity to place the power of the Son in the sun and his wisdom in the moon? For since the Son remains inseparable in the Father himself, how can his wisdom be separated from his power, so that the one is in the sun, the other in the moon—when by places of this kind nothing can be divided and separated except bodies? Had you known this, you would never have woven such great fables with a foolish and insane phantasm. But in that very falsity and deceit, how incongruously, how perversely do you say that the seat of wisdom shines less than the seat of power—when it seems to pertain to power to work and effect, but to wisdom to teach and show; and on this account, if heat predominated in the sun, but light in the moon, in some way these figments would have found a fog of verisimilitude for deceiving carnal and animal men, who think nothing exists except what they have imagined corporeal; for the violent operation of heat is for moving, whence it would be attributed to power; but the clear brightness of light is for demonstrating, whence they would give it to wisdom—but since light far excels in the sun, how is power there, while what shines so much less is here wisdom? O sacrilegious folly! And since Christ is one, the Power of God and the Wisdom of God[^4], but the Holy Spirit is not himself Christ; how is Christ separated from himself, when the Holy Spirit is not separated from him? For the air, which your fable assigns as the seat of the Holy Spirit, you declare to fill the whole fabric of the world. Whence the sun and moon, performing their circuits, are always with it. But the moon recedes from the sun, and again approaches the sun: so, on your authorship—or rather your deceiving—through half the part of the circle wisdom recedes from power, and again through the other half approaches it; and when it is full, then wisdom is far from power; for then these two luminaries are disjoined from each other by so long an interval that, when the sun inclines to the west, then the moon rises from the east: whence it comes about that, since all things that are deserted by power are weakened, wisdom is the more feeble the fuller the moon is. But if, as the truth holds, the wisdom of God is always equally strong, and the power of God is always equally wise, why do you so speak of these two as to separate them by seats and intervals of places, when you say that the seats themselves are of the same substance—you men who, with a blind and insane mind, do not depart from the phantasm of bodies, and are so lacking in power and wisdom that you can neither be wise in anything strongly, nor strong wisely? Is it really so, you detestable and anathematizable folly, that Christ is stretched out through the sun and moon, dwelling here by power, there by wisdom; perfect and full neither here, neither wise in the sun nor mighty in the moon, and in both places suborns beautiful boys to be coveted by the female princes of darkness, and girls by the males? These things you read, these you believe, these you teach; by this faith and doctrine you live; and you wonder that you are thus abominated!

**9.** But if in these so eminent and well-known luminaries you so err as to adore in them not what they are, but what you most insanely imagine for yourselves, what shall I say about the rest of your fables? For who is the Splenditenens who suspends the world, and what Atlas supports it with him? These and innumerable things, which you likewise rave about, do not exist at all, and you worship them. Hence we say you are worse than the pagans, like them only in this, that you worship many gods; but unlike them on the worse side, in that they worship as gods things that exist, but are not gods, while you worship things that are neither gods nor anything at all, since they utterly do not exist. They too, indeed, have certain fabulous figments, but they know those to be fables; and either they assert that they were invented by the poets for the sake of pleasure, or they try to interpret them with respect to the nature of things or the morals of men: as Vulcan lame, because the motion of earthly fire is of this kind; and Fortune blind, because the things called fortuitous happen by chance; and the three Fates, with distaff and spindle and fingers twisting a thread from wool, on account of the three times—the past, which is already spun and wound on the spindle; the present, which is passed between the fingers of the spinner; the future in the wool, which is wrapped on the distaff, which has still to be passed through the fingers of the spinner to the spindle, as through the present to the past; and Venus the wife of Vulcan, because pleasure is naturally born from heat, and the adulteress of Mars, because it is unsuited to those waging war; and Cupid a flying and shooting boy, because irrational and unstable love wounds the hearts of the wretched; and very many others in this manner. Wherefore in them we laugh at this, that they so adore, when interpreted, things which, not understood, they would adore—though damnably, yet more excusably. For by their very interpretations they are convicted that they worship not that God by participation in whom alone the mind becomes blessed, but a creature established by him; nor only the powers of the creature itself, like Minerva—whose fable, that she was born from the head of Jove, they interpret with respect to the prudence of counsels, which is proper to reason, to which Plato too gave the seat of the head—but even vices, as we said of Cupid. Whence one of their tragedians says:

*That love is a god, base lust, favoring vice,*

*has feigned*[^5].

For the Romans even consecrated images of bodily vices, such as those of Pallor and Fever. So, to pass over the fact that the worshipers of images have an affection toward the very figures of bodies, so that they fear those very forms—exalted in places of honor, to which they see such great obeisance shown—as gods; those very interpretations, by which these mute and deaf and blind and lifeless things are defended, are more justly accused. Nevertheless, even these things, in whatever way, do exist—although, as I have already said, for salvation or any usefulness they are nothing—and the things interpreted from them are found in reality. But you—the first man warring with the five elements; and the powerful spirit fabricating the world from the captive bodies of the nation of darkness, or rather from the conquered and subjected members of your god; and the Splenditenens, holding the relics of those same members of your god in his hand, and bewailing all the rest, captured, oppressed, defiled; and the greatest Atlas, bearing with him beneath his shoulders, lest the former, wearied, cast the whole away, and so your fable, as it were upon a theatrical tapestry, fail to reach the final winding-up of that last globe—and innumerable other things equally inept and insane: these you demonstrate neither by painting or sculpting, nor by interpreting; and them, though they are nothing at all, you believe and worship; and besides, you insult, as rashly credulous, Christians who cleanse devout minds by faith unfeigned. For—not to seek many things by which it might be shown that these do not exist at all, since to treat more subtly and loftily of the world's fabric, though it would not be difficult for me, is certainly far too long—this I say: if these things are true, the substance of God is changeable, corruptible, defilable. But to believe this is full of sacrilegious madness. All those things, therefore, are vain, are false, are nothing. Accordingly, you are utterly worse than those pagans who are commonly known, and existed of old, and now in their remnants already blush: because they worship things that are not gods, but you things that utterly do not exist.

**10.** And so, if you suppose that you therefore hold the truth because you are far dissimilar to the error of the pagans, while we are therefore in error because we perhaps stand further from you than from the pagans: let a dead man too be called healthy because he is no longer even sick; and let one who is healthy be reproached because he is nearer to a sick man than to a dead one. Or, if very many pagans are to be reckoned not as sick but as dead, let the shapeless ash in the tomb be praised, because it no longer even keeps the form of a corpse; and let living members be blamed, because they are more like a corpse than like ash. So these people too suppose that we are to be blamed, because they say we are more like the funeral of the pagans than like the ash of the Manicheans. And yet, through many differences, each thing is wont to be divided one way and another for the sake of distinguishing, so that what was on this side is, through other differences, found on the other side, where it was not before. As, for example, if someone divides all flesh into birds and those that cannot fly, by this difference four-footed creatures are more like men than birds; for equally they cannot fly. Again, if someone divides by another difference, so as to say that some are rational, others irrational, now four-footed creatures are more like birds than men; for equally they are devoid of reason. Not thinking of this, Faustus says: "But further, if you ask for sects, there will be no more than two, that is, the gentiles' and ours, who hold far different opinions from them." Namely, because he had said that the gentiles differ most from the Manicheans in this, that they say all things are from one principle, which the Manicheans deny, adding the principle of the nation of darkness. In this difference, it must be confessed, very many pagans agree with us; but he did not see that likewise, if someone so divides as to say that, of those who are held by some religion, it pleases some to worship one God, others many: by this difference both the pagans are removed from us, and these people are reckoned with the pagans, while we are reckoned with the Jews. Someone, then, can according to this difference suppose, in this way too, that there are only two sects. Here perhaps you may say that you declare your many gods to be of one substance—as though the pagans do not assert their many to be of one, although they attribute to them diverse duties and works and powers: just as among you too, one conquers the nation of darkness, another fabricates the world from it once captured; one suspends it from above, another carries it beneath; one turns the wheels of fires, winds, and waters in the depths, another, going around in the heavens, gathers even from the sewers the members of your god with his rays. And who could count all the fabulous duties of your gods, manifested by no truth, figured by no riddles? Further, if someone so divides all men as to say that some are those who believe God cares for human affairs, others who do not believe this at all: on this side both the pagans agree with us, and the Jews, and you, and all heretics who are in any way called Christians; but on that side are found the Epicureans, and any others who have thought so. Is this a small difference? Why, then, should not according to this too only two sects be said to exist, so that in one of them you are with us? Or will you dare, in this difference, to depart from us, who preach that God cares for human affairs, and to be with the Epicureans, who deny this? Here surely, repudiating them, you run to us. Thus, through one difference and another, now here, now there, men are found joined on one side, disjoined on another—all by turns with us, and we with all, and again none of them with us, nor we with any of them. And if Faustus considered this, he would not rave so eloquently.

**11.** But what shall I say to this, that he says: "From the forces of the Holy Spirit and his spiritual outpouring the earth too, conceiving, brings forth a passible Jesus, who is the life and salvation of men, suspended from every tree"? O madman—not, for the moment, to discuss your empty talk on this matter of yours—can the earth conceive a passible Jesus from the Holy Spirit, and could the virgin Mary not? Compare, if you dare, virginal entrails sanctified by such great chastity with all the places of the earth where trees and herbs are born. Do you so shudder, in that woman, or pretend to shudder, at a womb dedicated to modesty, and not shudder that Jesus is born in all the gardens around the various cities from the waters of the sewers? For what muck, however filthy, does not produce and nourish innumerable shoots? Thus you preach that a passible Jesus is born, whom you cry out it is unworthy to believe born of a virgin. If you think the flesh unclean, why does it not seem to you more unclean, what the very nature of the flesh purges out from the tempering of its own health? Or is the flesh unclean, and the dung that is voided from the flesh clean? Do you not attend, do you not see, that fields rejoice in dung, by which they may be more fruitfully made fertile? Surely your madness comes to this, that from the Holy Spirit, whom you say disdained the flesh of Mary, the earth conceives the more abundantly and joyfully the more diligently it has been fattened with the filth and squalor of flesh. Or, to defend this, will you say that the Holy Spirit prevails everywhere by an incontaminable presence? You are answered: Why, then, not in the virginal womb too? But, to be silent now about the conception, look next at the birth itself. You say the earth, conceiving from the Holy Spirit, brings forth a passible Jesus, whom yet you declare to hang, thus contaminated, from every tree in fruits and crops, so that he is the more contaminated by innumerable animals feeding on the flesh of animals, to be purified only from that one part which your hunger has succored. And so we believe in our heart, and confess with our mouth, that Christ, the Son of God, the Word of God, was clothed with flesh incontaminably; because that substance, which can be defiled by nothing, cannot be defiled even by flesh: but you, according to your fable, say that Jesus, still hanging on the tree, is already contaminated before he enters the flesh of anyone feeding; or, if he is not contaminated, how do you purify him by eating? Then, since you say all trees are his cross, whence Faustus preaches him "suspended from every tree," why do you not, just as that Joseph of Arimathea, doing a good work, took that true Jesus down from the cross to bury him[^6], so too pluck the fruits, that you may bury in your belly Jesus taken down from his suspension on the tree? Or how is it pious to lay Christ in a tomb, but impious to take him down from the tree? Or, that what the Apostle sets down from the prophet may suit you too: *Their throat is an open tomb*[^7]—do you, with mouth open, await who may bring Christ, as to a most excellent burial, into your jaws? Finally, tell us how many Christs you say there are. Is the one whom the earth, conceiving from the Holy Spirit, brings forth passible—suspended not only from every tree, but even lying in the grass—a different one from him whom the Jews crucified under Pontius Pilate; and a third, that one stretched out through the sun and moon? Or one and the same, in some part of himself bound in the trees, but in some part free, succoring that same bound and captive part? But if it is so, that one whom you concede suffered under Pontius Pilate—since you relate that he was without flesh—I do not yet say how he could undergo such a death without flesh; but I ask to whom he left those ships, in order, descending thence, to suffer such things as could not happen without some body. For according to a spiritual presence he could in no way suffer those things; but according to a corporeal presence he could not be at once in the sun, and in the moon, and on the cross. Accordingly, if he had no body, he was not crucified; but if he had one, I ask whence he had it, since you say all bodies are from the nation of darkness, although you have never been able to conceive of the divine substance except as corporeal. Whence you are forced either to say he was crucified without a body—than which nothing more absurd and insane can be said; or that he was seen to be crucified in phantom rather than in truth—and by this impiety again what is worse?; or that not all bodies are from the nation of darkness, but that there is also a body of the divine substance, which yet is not immortal, but can be fastened to wood and slain—which is no less full of madness; or that Christ had a mortal body from the nation of darkness, and thus, you who fear to believe the virgin Mary the mother of that body, do not fear the nation of demons. Finally, since, according to Faustus's opinion—which indeed he touched, with as much brevity as he could, plucked from that most lengthy fable of yours: "The earth, conceiving from the Holy Spirit, brings forth a passible Jesus, who is the life and salvation of men, suspended from every tree"—why does that Savior fit the one who hangs by hanging, and not fit the one who is born by being born? But if you therefore say there is a Jesus in the trees, and a Jesus crucified under Pontius Pilate, and a Jesus stretched out through the sun and moon, because all this is from one and the same substance, why do you not include the rest of the thousands of your divinities under this appellation? For why should not the Splenditenens too be Jesus, and that Atlas, and that king of honor, and that powerful spirit, and that first man, and whatever else, innumerably, you preach under diverse names and diverse duties?

**12.** Finally, why is the Holy Spirit himself set in the third person, when he is among innumerable ones? Or why is he too not Jesus? And what does the deceitful web of words mean in Faustus's writings, where, trying as it were to agree with true Christians, from whom he is far and altogether shut off, he says: "We, then, worship the one and the same divinity of God the Father almighty, and of Christ his Son, and of the Holy Spirit, under a threefold appellation"? For why under a threefold, and not rather under a manifold—not appellation only, but also reality, if there are as many persons as names? For it is not as in weapons, where one thing is under a threefold appellation—"blade," "point," "sword"—just as you call one thing "moon" and "lesser ship" and "luminary of the night," and whatever other term you assign to the same thing; in this way you can call the same one "first man" whom you call "powerful spirit," whom "Splenditenens," whom "greatest Atlas." But that one is one, and this and that are another and another, and you are not accustomed to call any of these Christ. Or how is there one divinity, if there are diverse works? Or why is not the whole at once one Christ, if on account of one substance there is Christ in the trees, and Christ in the persecution of the Jews, and Christ in the sun and moon? Surely your phantasms have lost all their ways; surely they are nothing else than the visions of madmen.

**13.** But why Faustus should think that our religion concerning the bread and the cup is equal to his, I do not know, since for the Manicheans to taste wine is not religion but sacrilege. For in the grape they recognize their god, in the cask they refuse to, as if its being trodden and shut in had offended them in some way. But our bread and cup—not just any (as if on account of Christ bound in the ears of grain and in the vine-shoots, as those people dote), but made mystical for us by a definite consecration—becomes so for us; it is not so born. Accordingly, what is not so made, although it is bread and cup, is the nourishment of refreshment, not the sacrament of religion: except that we bless and give thanks to the Lord in every gift of his, not only spiritual but also corporeal. But to you, through your fable, Christ is set before you bound in all foods, still to be bound in your entrails, and to be loosed by your belchings. For both when you eat, you refresh yourselves by the defect of your god; and when you digest, you defect by his refreshment. For when he renders you full, your taking-in presses him. This would indeed be reckoned to mercy—when he suffers something for you in you—were it not that he again leaves you empty, that, freed from you, he might escape. How, then, do you compare our bread and cup, and call equal a religion an error far separated from the truth—you who dote worse than some who suppose that, on account of the bread and cup, we worship Ceres and Liber? This I thought worth mentioning, that you may notice from what vanity comes that other notion of yours, that on account of the Sabbath our fathers were dedicated to Saturn. For just as we are far from Ceres and Liber, the gods of the pagans, although we embrace by our rite the sacrament of the bread and cup, which you praised so as to wish to be our equals in it; so our fathers were far from the chains of Saturn, although, for the prophetic season, they observed the rest of the Sabbath.

**14.** But why did you not say that you too have a religion equal to the pagans on account of Hyle, which is frequently mentioned in some books of the pagans? Nay rather, on this account you wished it to be understood as unequal and far dissimilar, because by this name your theologian names the principle and nature of evil. In which, indeed, your great ignorance is caught out: because you neither know what Hyle is, and yet you affect to be puffed up, as though learned, by this term for a thing of which you are utterly ignorant. For the Greeks, when they discuss nature, define Hyle as a certain matter of things, in no way at all formed, but capable of all corporeal forms; which indeed is in some way known in the mutability of bodies, for in itself it can neither be perceived nor understood. But in this certain of the gentiles err, that they join it as coeternal to God, so that it is not from him, although it is formed by him: which is foreign to the truth, as truth itself teaches. Behold, nonetheless, with what pagans you are found to be quite alike concerning this very Hyle, in that you too declare that it has its own principle, and is not from God; and in this you said you were unalike, not knowing what you say. But in that there is no proper form to this Hyle, and it cannot be formed except by God, they agree with our truth, but dissent from your falsehood; for you, not knowing what Hyle is—that is, what the matter of things is—call it the nation of darkness, where you establish not only innumerable corporeal forms distinct in five kinds, but even a mind that forms these bodies; and—what is more ignorant, or rather more insane—you say that the mind itself is Hyle rather than the bodies, the mind which you declare not to be formed but to form. For if there were there a certain mind that forms, and corporeal elements that are formed, those elements would have to be called Hyle—that is, the matter which that same mind formed, which mind you wish to be the principle of evil. If you said this, you would not indeed greatly err concerning what Hyle is—except that the elements themselves too, although to be formed into other forms, yet because they would already be elements and distinguished by their proper appearances, would not be Hyle, since that is utterly formless; nevertheless your ignorance would be tolerable, because you would call Hyle that which is formed, not that which forms. But even so you would still be held vain and sacrilegious, because, not knowing that all measure of natures and number of forms and order of weights cannot exist except from the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, you would attribute so great a good to the principle of evil. But as it is, since you are ignorant both of what Hyle is and of what evil is—oh, if I could persuade you to restrain yourselves from seducing the more ignorant!

**15.** Now as for the fact that you wish to be better than the pagans even in this, that they think God is to be worshiped with altars, shrines, images, victims, and incense, while you do none of these things—who would not laugh? As if it were not better to set up an altar even to a stone, which in some way exists, and to offer a victim, than to adore in the delirium of a phantasm that which utterly does not exist. But you, who said you are the rational temple of God, how will you explain this? Does it please you that God should have a temple, some part of which the devil has fabricated? Or are you not the ones who say that all your members and your whole body were fabricated by the malignant mind which you call Hyle, and that a part of that same fabricatrix dwells there together with a part of your god? Since this is held there, as you declare, chained and shut up, ought you to call yourself the temple of God, or the prison of God? Unless perhaps you call your soul the temple of God, which you have from the land of light. But that you are accustomed to call a part of God, or a member of God, not the temple of God. It remains, then, that you have called yourself the temple of God only with respect to the body, which according to you the devil fabricated. Behold how you blaspheme the temple of God, so as to say it is not only not holy, but even a contrivance of the devil and a workhouse of God. But the Apostle: *For the temple of God is holy,* he says, *which you are*[^8]; and lest you suppose that what was said pertains only to the soul, hear it more expressly: *Do you not know,* he says, *that your bodies are the temple in you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God?*[^9] But you call the fabric of demons the temple of God, and there you place, as Faustus says, "the living image of the living majesty, Christ the Son of God." Plainly let your phantom Christ dwell in such a temple of sacrilegious vanity. For he can be called an image not from likeness but from feigning.

**16.** So you have made your mind an altar too—but see of whom. For it appears from those very arts and disciplines with which you said it was imbued. Those arts and disciplines forbid offering bread to a begging man, so that on your altar you may burn with a sacrifice of cruelty: such an altar the Lord destroys, who recalls from the Law with what odor God is delighted, saying: *I desire mercy rather than sacrifice*[^10]. And notice where the Lord recalled this: namely, when he was passing through the grain, and his hungry disciples plucked the ears—which you say is murder, by your discipline, with which you imbued your mind; an altar surely not of God, but of lying demons, by whose doctrines a malignant conscience is branded and cauterized[^11], calling murder what Truth calls innocence. For thus he says to the Jews, where he struck down and destroyed you too as those to come: *If you knew what this means, I desire mercy rather than sacrifice, you would never have condemned the innocent*[^12].

**17.** But what simple and pure prayers will you be able to have as divine honors and sacrifices, when you hold so unworthy and base opinions about the very nature and substance of the divine, that not only is the true God not propitiated by your sacrifices, but in the sacrifices of the pagans your god is immolated? For you judge that he is bound not only in wood, and in herbs, or in human members, but even in the flesh of cattle, by fetters that contaminate and pollute him. But your very soul—to whom shall it say praise to God, when, crying out that a particle of itself is held captive in the nation of darkness, what else does it do but find fault with God, whom it testifies could not have provided for himself against his enemies in any other way than by so great a corruption and so base a captivity of his own parts? Whence your very prayers to your god could not be religious, but invidious. For what evil did you commit before him, that you should now groan to him in this punishment—him whom you did not desert by sinning of your own will, but to whom you were given over to his enemies, that peace might be procured for his kingdom? And not even as hostages are usually given, to be guarded with honor; nor as a shepherd lays a snare to catch a beast: for he is wont to place his own cattle in that trapping snare, not his own member, and generally so that the beast is caught before the cattle is hurt. But you, members of god, were given over to his enemies, unable to restrain their savagery from your god except by being contaminated by their foulness, having no sin of your own, but wasted by the enemy's poison. Whence you cannot say in your prayers: *For the glory of your name, O Lord, free us; and be merciful to our sins for the sake of your name*[^13]; but you say: "Free us by your art, because, that you may now in your kingdom mourn secure, we here are pressed, torn apart, defiled." This voice is accusatory, not deprecatory. Nor can you say what the Master of truth taught: *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*[^14]. For who are your debtors, who have sinned against you? If it is the nation of darkness, do you forgive its debts—you who shut it up, uprooted forever, in an eternal prison? But what debts can that god of yours forgive you, when he rather sinned against you, in sending you to these things, than you against him, since you obeyed the one sending? Or if he therefore did not sin, because he did this of necessity, your necessity is greater—since you now lie prostrate in the fight—than his was before you fought. For now you suffer evil commingled, while he was suffering nothing such, though he suffered the necessity of sending you. And so either he rather owes you something that you may forgive him; or, if not even he owes you, much less do you owe him. Where, then, are your sacrifices, your simple and pure prayers, when they are deceitful and impure blasphemies?

**18.** And yet I want you to tell me whence you call all those things which you praise in yourselves by these names, so that you say "temple," "altar," "sacrifice." For if these true things are not owed to the true God, why are they laudably proclaimed in the true religion? But if a true sacrifice is rightly owed to the true God, whence too they are rightly called divine honors, the other things that are called sacrifices are done in likeness of a certain true sacrifice. Now these are partly imitations of false and deceitful gods—that is, of demons proudly exacting divine honors for themselves from those whom they have deceived, such as are, or were, all the things in the temples and idols of the gentiles; partly proclamations of the one most true sacrifice to come, which had to be offered for the sins of all believers, such as were the precepts given divinely to our ancient fathers, where there was also that mystical anointing by which Christ was prefigured, whence the very name is drawn from the chrism. Accordingly, the true sacrifice, which is owed to the one true God, whose altar Christ alone fulfilled, the demons, imitating it in the victims of cattle, arrogantly demand for themselves. Whence the Apostle says: *What the gentiles immolate, they immolate to demons and not to God*[^15]—not blaming what was offered, but because it was offered to them. But the Hebrews, in the victims of cattle which they offered to God, celebrated in many and various ways, as was worthy of so great a matter, a prophecy of the future victim which Christ offered. Whence now Christians, with that same sacrifice accomplished, celebrate its memory by the sacrosanct oblation and participation of the body and blood of Christ. But the Manicheans, not knowing what is to be condemned in the sacrifices of the gentiles, and what is to be understood in the sacrifices of the Hebrews, and what is to be held and observed in the sacrifice of Christians, offer their vanity as a holy rite to the devil who has deceived them, departing from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits and doctrines of demons in the hypocrisy of liars.

**19.** Let Faustus, then—or rather those who delight in his writings—learn that we have the opinion of monarchy not from the gentiles; but that the gentiles had not so far fallen to false gods as to lose the opinion of the one true God, from whom is every nature of whatever kind. For their wise men—since, as the Apostle says, *the invisible things of God, from the foundation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made; his eternal power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse*—knowing God, did not honor him as God or give thanks; but *they became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened; for, saying they were wise, they became fools, and changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, and of four-footed beasts, and of serpents*[^16]. For these are the images of the gentiles, in interpreting which they have no way out except to the creature which God established; so that in the very interpretation of images, of which the more skilled among them are accustomed to boast and be puffed up, that may happen in them which the same Apostle says a little after: *They worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever*[^17]. But you, both in that you are unlike them, are vain; and in that you are like them, are worse. For to this end you do not, with them, believe in monarchy, in that they believe what is true—namely, so that you believe the substance of the one God himself to be conquerable and corruptible, which is of impious vanity; but in the worship of many gods, the doctrine of lying demons persuaded them of many idols, you of many phantasms.

**20.** Nor have we turned their sacrifices into love-feasts; but we have understood that sacrifice which I mentioned a little before, the Lord saying: *I desire mercy rather than sacrifice.* For our love-feasts feed the poor, whether with crops or with flesh. For a creature of God is fed from a creature of God that is suited to human meals. But because lying demons persuaded you—not for the governing of the flesh, but for the practice of blasphemy—*to abstain from foods which God created to be received with thanksgiving by the faithful and by those who have known the truth; for every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be rejected that is received with thanksgiving*[^18]—ungrateful to the Creator, and repaying for his liberal benefits sacrilegious injuries, since very often in the love-feasts flesh too is distributed to the poor, you call the mercy of Christians like the sacrifices of the pagans, to some of whom you are alike in this too. For you hold it a sin for cattle to be killed, because you judge that human souls are reincarnated into them—which is found in the books of certain gentile philosophers, although it is said to have been understood otherwise by later ones. But in this too you err much worse: for they feared, in cattle, to slaughter their neighbor; but you, your god, whose members you judge even the souls of cattle to be.

**21.** As for the fact that Faustus slanders us on this score too, that we honor the memorials of the martyrs—saying that in this we have turned idols into martyrs—it moves me not so much to answer this slander as to show that Faustus himself, in his zeal for slandering, wished to swerve even from the vanities of Mani himself, and, I know not how, fell incautiously into the vulgar and poetic opinion of the pagans, from whom he desires to seem most estranged. For when he had said that we turned idols into martyrs, "Whom," he says, "you worship with like vows; you appease the shades of the dead with wine and feasts." Are there, then, shades of the dead? We never heard this in your discourses, never read it in your writings; rather you are accustomed to contradict such opinions, asserting that the souls of the dead that are evil or less purified either go into reincarnations or into certain heavier punishments; but the good are placed in ships, and, sailing in the heavens, pass from here into that phantasm of the land of light, for which they had perished in fighting: so that no souls are detained about the tombs of their bodies. Whence, then, the shades of the dead? What is their substance? What their place? But in his desire for cursing, Faustus forgot what he was professing; or perhaps, dozing, he dictated of shades while dreaming, and did not wake up when he read his own words. But the Christian people celebrate the memorials of the martyrs with religious solemnity, both to arouse imitation, and that they may be associated with their merits and aided by their prayers: yet so that we set up altars to none of the martyrs, but to the very God of the martyrs, though at the memorials of the martyrs. For which of the bishops, standing at the altar in the places of the holy bodies, ever said, "We offer to you, Peter, or Paul, or Cyprian"? But what is offered is offered to God, who crowned the martyrs, at the memorials of those whom he crowned; so that from the admonition of the places themselves a greater affection may arise, to sharpen love both toward those whom we can imitate, and toward him by whose help we can. We honor the martyrs, then, with that worship of love and fellowship by which in this life too holy men of God are honored, whose heart we perceive to be prepared for such suffering for the evangelical truth. But them so much the more devoutly, as more securely, after the contests have been overcome; with so much the more confident praise, too, as they are now victors in a happier life than while still fighting in this one. But with that worship which in Greek is called latreia, which in Latin cannot be said in one word, since it is a certain service properly owed to the divinity, we neither worship nor teach that anything is to be worshiped except the one God. And since the oblation of sacrifice pertains to this worship, whence is called idolatry that of those who exhibit this even to idols, in no way do we offer, or command to be offered, any such thing, either to any martyr, or to any holy soul, or to any angel: and whoever slips into this error is corrected by sound doctrine, whether that he may be corrected, or that others may beware. For even the holy ones themselves, whether men or angels, do not wish exhibited to themselves what they know to be owed to the one God. This appeared in Paul and Barnabas, when the Lycaonians, stirred by the miracles done through them, wished to immolate to them as to gods: for, tearing their garments, confessing and persuading that they were not gods, they forbade these things to be done to themselves[^19]. It appeared also in the angels, as we read in the Apocalypse, an angel forbidding himself to be adored and saying to his adorer: *I am a fellow servant of you and of your brethren*[^20]. These things proud spirits plainly exact for themselves, the devil and his angels, as throughout all the temples and sacred rites of the gentiles. Whose likeness was expressed in certain proud men too: as we hold committed to memory concerning certain kings of Babylon. Whence holy Daniel endured accusers and persecutors, because, when the king's edict had been issued that nothing be asked of any god by anyone except the king alone, he was caught adoring and entreating his God—that is, the one and true God[^21]. But those who get drunk at the memorials of the martyrs—how can they be approved by us, when sound doctrine condemns them, even if they do this in their own homes? But it is one thing what we teach, another what we endure, another what we are commanded to enjoin, another what we are commanded to amend, and, until we amend it, are compelled to tolerate. One is the discipline of Christians, another the excess of the drunken, or the error of the weak. Yet even in this very thing the faults of the drunken and of the sacrilegious differ greatly. For it is a sin far less to return drunk from the martyrs than even to sacrifice to the martyrs while fasting. I said "to sacrifice to the martyrs"; I did not say "to sacrifice to God at the memorials of the martyrs," which we do most frequently, in that rite alone by which he commanded sacrifice to be offered to himself in the manifestation of the New Testament; which pertains to that worship called latria, and is owed to the one God. But what shall I do, and when shall I demonstrate to so great a blindness of these heretics what force that has which is sung in the Psalms: *The sacrifice of praise will glorify me, and there is the way by which I will show him the salvation of God*[^22]? The flesh and blood of this sacrifice, before the coming of Christ, was promised through victims of likenesses; in the passion of Christ it was rendered through the truth itself; after the ascension of Christ it is celebrated through the sacrament of memory: and on this account there is as much difference between the sacrifices of the pagans and of the Hebrews as between an erring imitation and a foretelling prefiguration. But just as the virginity of holy women is not on that account to be despised or detested, because the Vestals too were virgins; so the sacrifices of the fathers are not on that account to be reproached, because there are also sacrifices of the gentiles: for just as among those virginities there is a great difference, although nothing else differs except to whom each is vowed and rendered; so between the sacrifices of the pagans and of the Hebrews there is a great difference, by this very thing, that this alone differs—to whom they are immolated and offered: the former, namely, to the proud impiety of demons arrogating this very thing to themselves for this reason, that they might be held gods, because sacrifice is a divine honor; the latter to the one God, that there might be offered to him a likeness promising the truth of the sacrifice, to whom the rendered truth itself was to be offered in the passion of the body and blood of Christ.

**22.** For it is not, as Faustus said, that our predecessors the Jews, separated from the gentiles, when they had a temple, and immolations, and altars, and priesthoods, abandoned only the graven images—that is, the idols: for they could, like some, even without graven idols, immolate to trees and mountains, and finally even to the sun and moon and the other stars. And if they did this through that worship which is called latria, serving the creature rather than the Creator, and on this account erring in the no small evil of impious superstition, the demons would nonetheless offer themselves to delude them and to receive from them what they had thus offered. For those proud and impious spirits are fed not by reek and smoke, as some vain men suppose, but by the errors of men—not by the refreshment of their own body, but by malevolent delight when in any way they deceive, or by the arrogant pride of a feigned majesty when they glory that divine honors are exhibited to them. Not, therefore, did those fathers of ours abandon only the images of the gentiles; but immolating nothing to earth, nor to any earthly thing, nor to sea, nor to heaven, nor to the host of heaven, they offered victims to the one God, the creator of all, who willed them to be offered to himself, promising through their likeness the true victim, through which he reconciled us to himself by the remission of sins in Christ Jesus our Lord, whose body the faithful, made his body, Paul addresses, saying: *I beseech you, then, brethren, through the mercy of God, that you present your bodies as a living victim, holy, pleasing to God*[^23]. But the Manicheans say that human bodies are the workmanship of the nation of darkness, and the prisons in which the conquered God is shut up: whence Faustus announces one thing, Paul far another. But since *whoever preaches to you a gospel besides what you have received, let him be anathema*[^24]—Christ speaks truth in Paul; let Mani be anathema in Faustus.

**23.** He also says that we have changed nothing of the morals of the gentiles, not knowing what he says. For since *the just lives by faith*, and the end of the commandment is *love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*[^25], and there remain, for the forming of the life of the faithful, these three—faith, hope, charity[^26]—how can it happen that one has morals equal to another, who does not have these three equal with him? For he who believes one thing, hopes another, loves another, must necessarily live otherwise. And if the use of certain things seems to be like ours with the gentiles—such as food and drink, dwellings, garments, baths; and, for those of ours who lead a conjugal life, the marrying and having of wives, the begetting, rearing, and making heirs of children—yet he uses these things far otherwise who refers their use to another end; and otherwise he who gives thanks to God for these things, of whom he believes nothing depraved and false. For just as in your very error, although you feed on the same bread as other men, and live on fruits and springs, and are clothed with wool and linen likewise woven, yet in these things you do not lead an equal life—not by eating, or drinking, or being clothed differently, but by feeling and believing differently, and referring all these things to another end, namely the end of your error and vanity: so we, both in these and in other things which we likewise take, do not live like the gentiles, referring the same things not to the same end, but to the end of the lawful and divine commandment, love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned, from which certain men, straying, were turned to empty talk. In which indeed you hold the primacy, not noticing, not considering, that in having and doing the same things, so great a difference toward a diverse life avails, if the faith is diverse—so that, when your Hearers both have wives, and beget children though unwilling, and amass or guard patrimonies for them, eat flesh, drink wine, bathe, reap, gather the vintage, do business, administer public honors, you nonetheless reckon them with you, not with the gentiles, though their deeds seem more like the gentiles' than yours. For when the deeds of certain gentiles are nearer to you than to certain of your Hearers—for some, in their sacrilegious rites, abstain even from wine and flesh and intercourse—you rather number among Mani's flock your Hearers, using all these things, and unlike you in this, than those gentiles doing the same things you do; and you rather call yours a woman who has believed in Mani, even one bearing children, than a Sibyl not even marrying. But, you say, many are called catholic Christians, and are adulterers, robbers, misers, drunkards, and whatever else is opposed to sound doctrine. But what of you—in your so scanty and almost nonexistent number, are not very many such, while some among the pagans are not such? Do you therefore call the pagans who are not such better than you? And yet, on account of the sacrilegious vanity of your sect, even those among you who are not such are worse than even such pagans. Whence it is manifest that sound doctrine, which alone is catholic, is not on that account disparaged because many wish to be reckoned by its name and are unwilling to be healed by it. For that fewness is to be recognized which the Lord especially commends amid the vast and innumerable multitude spread through the whole world[^27]—which fewness of the saints and faithful, nonetheless (a thing often to be commended), is called fewness as it were of grains in comparison with the multitude of chaff; but in itself it makes so great a mass of wheat that it surpasses by an incomparable multitude all your approved and reprobate, whom truth alike reproves. Behold, we are not a schism of the gentiles, from whom we differ greatly for the better; but neither are you this, because you differ greatly from them for the worse.
