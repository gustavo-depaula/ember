# Book XIX

**1.** FAUSTUS said: *I came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets, but to fulfill them*[^1].

Behold, I now agree that it was said. Yet it must be asked why Jesus said this—whether for the sake of soothing the fury of the Jews, who, seeing their own sacrosanct things trodden underfoot by him, were indignant and reckoned him, as impious and unsound, not even to be heard, much less to be followed; or in order to instruct and form us who from among the gentiles believed in him, to take up patiently and obediently the yoke of the commandments that the Law of the Jews and the Prophets would impose on our necks. But I do not believe that even you yourself suppose that Jesus uttered this word in order to bind us to the Law of the Hebrews and the Prophets. And on this account, if this was not the cause of saying it, that other must be, which I mentioned. For no one is unaware that the Jews always lay in wait, fiercely, against the words and works of Christ. Since from these they gathered that their Law and Prophets were being abolished by him, they were necessarily indignant; and on this account it would not be beside the point that he said, for the sake of repressing their fury, that they should not suppose he had come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it. And in this very thing he neither lied nor deceived: for he named the Law indifferently and absolutely.

**2.** Now there are three kinds of laws: one, indeed, that of the Hebrews, which Paul calls the law of sin and death[^2]. Another, that of the gentiles, which he calls natural: for, he says, *the gentiles by nature do what the Law requires; and not having such a law, they are a law to themselves, who show the work of the Law written in their hearts*[^3]. The third kind of law is truth, which the Apostle, signifying it likewise, expresses when he says: *For the law of the spirit of life in Christ Jesus has freed me from the law of sin and death*[^4]. Since, then, there are three laws, and Jesus asserts to us that he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, it requires no small care and diligence to understand of which of them he spoke.

Likewise the prophets: some are of the Jews, some of the gentiles, some of the truth. But about those of the Jews no one would inquire, for it is well known. And if anyone is in doubt about those of the gentiles, let him hear Paul, who, writing to Titus about the Cretans, says: *One of their own prophets said: The Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy bellies*[^5]. And on this account there is no need to doubt that the gentiles too have their prophets. And that the truth also has its prophets, the same Paul signifies, as does Jesus too. Jesus, indeed, where he says: *Behold, I send to you wise men and prophets, and some of them you will kill in each place*[^6]. And Paul, when he says: *The Lord himself appointed first apostles, then prophets*[^7].

**3.** Since, then, the Law is threefold, and the Prophets threefold, of which of them Jesus spoke is not sufficiently clear; yet it is possible to conjecture from what follows. For if he had at once named circumcision, and the Sabbaths and sacrifices and Hebrew observances, and had brought forward something into them for the sake of fulfilling, there would be no doubt that he had spoken of the Law of the Jews and the Prophets, that he came not to abolish them but to fulfill them. But where he mentions none of these, but reviews only the more ancient precepts—that is, You shall not kill, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not swear falsely—and these were of old among the nations, as is easy to prove, promulgated long ago through Enoch and Seth and the rest of the righteous like them, to whom illustrious angels handed them down for the sake of tempering the savagery in men—to whom would it not seem that he spoke of the law of the truth and its prophets?

Finally, his fulfillment is proved with respect to these very things which he promised. For what does he say? *You have heard that it was said to the ancients: You shall not kill; but I say to you, do not even be angry*—this is fulfillment. *You have heard that it was said: You shall not commit adultery; but I say to you, do not even lust*—this is fulfillment. *It was said: You shall not swear falsely; but I say to you, do not even swear*—this likewise is fulfillment. For in these he both strengthens the earlier things and adds what was lacking.

But where he seemed to name certain things of the Jews, those indeed he did not fulfill, but even uprooted utterly by commanding the contraries. For what follows? *You have heard that it was said: An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth; but I say to you: Whoever strikes you on the cheek, offer him the other also*—this now is destruction. *It was said,* he says, *You shall love your friend and hate your enemy; but I say to you: Love your enemies, and pray for your persecutors*—this likewise is destruction. *It was said: Whoever wishes to put away his wife, let him give her a writ of divorce; but I say: Whoever puts away his wife, except for the cause of fornication, makes her commit adultery, and he will be an adulterer if he afterward marries another*[^8]. These, then, are the precepts manifestly of Moses, and therefore destroyed; those others belong to the ancient righteous, and on this account fulfilled. But if it pleases you too to understand it thus, it will not be beside the point that Jesus said he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. If this exposition of ours displeases you, seek another—only beware lest you be compelled to call Jesus a liar, or it become necessary for you to become a Jew: lest even now you persist in abolishing the Law which he himself did not abolish.

**4.** And yet, if any of the Nazoreans—whom others call Symmachians—were to object this to me, namely, that Jesus said he came not to abolish the Law, I would for a while have hesitated, uncertain what to answer him. And not without reason: for such a man came beset with the Law and the Prophets, in body and mind alike. For men of this sort, of whom I speak, both bear circumcision and observe the Sabbath, and abstain from pork and the rest of such things as the Law commanded, under the profession of the Christian name—deceived themselves too, as may be understood, by this very chapter as you are, because Christ said he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. Wherefore, since with such men I would have no small contest, as I said, until I demolished the difficulty of this chapter, I shall by no means fear to engage with you, who rely on no strength and provoke rather by impudence, so that I think myself more easily tested by you than compelled to believe that Christ said what I do not see that you yourself believed. For you, putting forward none of the things by which the Law and the Prophets might seem not abolished but fulfilled, reproach me as a sluggard and a transgressor, on the ground of this chapter's objection. Or do you too now glory in that obscene little seal of the mutilated groin, like a Jew or a Nazorean? Or do you raise an eyebrow over the observance of the Sabbaths? Or do you rejoice, conscious of yourself, over abstinence from pork? Or, finally, do you exult that you have glutted God with the blood of victims and the reeking smoke of the holocausts of the Jews? But if you have done none of these things, why do you contend that Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it?

**5.** Wherefore I give unceasing thanks to my teacher, who held me back when I was likewise slipping, so that I am today a Christian. For I too, when I read this chapter incautiously, as you do, had almost gone over to the plan of becoming a Jew. And not without reason: for if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, and fulfillment is never spoken of an empty vessel, but of a half-full one, it seemed to me that only an Israelite could become a Christian—one who, filled for the most part with the Law and the Prophets, might come to Christ to be filled up with him, of whom he seemed still more capable; provided that he too did not abolish the earlier things; otherwise this would not be a fulfilling with respect to him, but an emptying. But I, coming from the gentiles, thought I had approached Christ in vain, because I brought nothing of the kind that he could, by his additions, fulfill in me. Seeking, then, what that earlier measure might be, I find Sabbaths, circumcision, sacrifices, new moons, washings, eatings of unleavened bread, distinctions of foods and of drinks and of garments, and others which it is long to run through. I supposed, then, that this was it—nor anything else—which Christ said he came not to abolish but to fulfill.

And not without reason: for what is the Law without commandments? What are the Prophets without prefaces? In addition to these, I also find a bitter curse pronounced there against those who do not continue in all the things written in the book of that Law, to do them[^9]. Fearing, then, the curse from that side, as though God's, and hearing from this side Christ, as though his son, saying that he came not to abolish those things but to fulfill them, see whether anything could now hinder my becoming a Jew. But from this danger the venerable faith of the Manicheans snatched me.

**6.** Yet I ask with what confidence you object these things, or why you suppose that to be against me alone which seems no less contrary to you. If it is not Christ's to abolish the Law and the Prophets, then surely it is not the Christians' either.

Why, then, do you abolish them? Or do you gradually confess that you are not Christians? Why do you, of all the Law and the Prophets, profane with every kind of work that sacrosanct day of the Sabbaths, on which they testify that God, the very maker of the world, rested[^10]—fearing neither the penalty of death which he appointed against its violators, nor the disgrace of the curse? Why do you protect your loins from that disgraceful seal of circumcision, honored by all the Law and the Prophets, and especially by Abraham after his renowned faith—especially since the God of the Jews declares that everyone of his people will perish who is not marked beforehand with this ignominy[^11]? Why do you spurn the legal observances of the sacrifices, which neither Moses and the Prophets under the Law, nor Abraham under his faith, held in second place? Why do you defile your souls with the indifference of foods, if, as you believe, Christ came to abolish none of these things, but to fulfill them? Why do you violate the yearly rite of unleavened bread, and the sacred rite of slaying the lamb, which the Law and the Prophets command to be observed forever? Why, finally, do you fail to avoid breaking in upon the new moons, and washings, and the Feast of Tabernacles, and the rest of such carnal sacraments of the Law and the Prophets, if Christ in no way destroyed them?

Wherefore I would not say without reason that, if you wish your account of this contempt to hold together, you must either deny that you are disciples of Christ, or at last confess that he himself destroyed all these earlier things. And when you have confessed this, then that other thing follows too: that you must either confess it to be falsely written, as though he said he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, or confess that this meant something far other than you suppose.

**7.** AUGUSTINE replied: Since you now agree that Christ said, *I came not to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them*[^12]—for it seems hard to you to come against the evangelical authority; let it seem hard to you too to come against the Apostle, who says, *All these things were figures of us*[^13]; and who likewise says of Christ that in him there was not *yes* and *no*, but in him was *yes*: *for as many as are the promises of God, in him they are yes*[^14]—that is, in him displayed, in him fulfilled: then, without any fog, you will see both what Law he came to fulfill, and in what manner he fulfilled it. Nor will you go on being stretched out over the three kinds of law and the three kinds of prophets, seeking a way out and not finding it. For it is manifest, and clearer than light, and the Scripture of the New Testament too often bears witness, what Law and which Prophets Christ came not to abolish but to fulfill. For it is the very Law which, given through Moses, became grace and truth through Jesus Christ[^15]. It is, I say, the very Law given through Moses, of whom Christ says: *For he wrote about me*[^16].

For surely it is the very Law which *entered in, that the offense might abound*[^17]—which, understanding nothing, you are accustomed to have on your lips to its reproach. There, then, read and see that it is the very one of which it is said: *And so the Law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did then that which is good become death to me? Far be it. But sin, that it might appear as sin, worked death in me through the good*[^18]. For the Law did not command the offense, so that, when it entered in, the offense might abound; but the proud, attributing much to themselves, had been made guilty even of transgression by the addition of the holy and just and good commandment—so that, humbled in this way, they might learn that it belongs to grace, through faith, that they should no longer be subject to the Law through guilt, but associated with the Law through righteousness.

For the same Apostle says: *Before faith came, we were kept guarded under the Law, shut up unto that faith which was afterward revealed. And so the Law,* he says, *was our tutor in Christ Jesus; but after faith came, we are no longer under a tutor*[^19]—because the guilt of the Law does not bind us, now freed through grace. For before, being humbled, we received spiritual grace, the letter did nothing but kill us, commanding what we could not fulfill. Whence he says the same: *The letter kills, but the spirit gives life*[^20]. Again, the same Apostle's words are: *For if a Law had been given that could give life, righteousness would altogether be from the Law; but the Scripture shut up all things under sin, that the promise from faith in Jesus Christ might be given to believers*[^21]. Likewise his own words are: *For what was impossible for the Law, in that it was weakened through the flesh, God, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, that from sin he might condemn sin in the flesh, that the righteousness of the Law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the spirit*[^22]. Behold what it is: *I came not to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*.

For since the Law bound even the proud by the guilt of transgression, increasing sin when it commands what they cannot fulfill, the righteousness of that same Law is fulfilled through the grace of the spirit, in those who learn from Christ to be meek and humble of heart—who came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. Then, since even for those set under grace it is difficult in this mortal life to fulfill in every way what is written in the Law, *You shall not covet*[^23], he, made a priest through the sacrifice of his own flesh, obtains for us pardon, in this way too fulfilling the Law; so that what, through our weakness, we are less able to do, may be recovered through his perfection, whose members we, the head, have been made. Whence John says: *My little children, these things I write to you, that you may not sin; and if anyone sins, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just; he is the expiation for our sins*[^24].

**8.** The prophecies, moreover, he fulfilled thus, when in him the truth of God's promises came to be. This I mentioned a little before from the Apostle, who says: *For as many as are the promises of God, in him: yes.* The same again says: *For I say that Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the sake of the truth of God, to confirm the promises of the fathers*[^25]. Therefore what was promised in the Prophets, whether openly or through figures—of speech or of action—was fulfilled in him, who came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them. But this you do not understand: for if certain deeds and celebrations, which were figures foretelling things to come, were still performed by Christians, nothing would be signified except that the things then foretold by those figures had not yet come. For what is still foretold as to come either has not yet come, or, if it has already come, is foretold superfluously or deceitfully. Wherefore, from the very thing by which it seems to you that Christ did not fulfill the Prophets—namely, that certain things are not done by Christians which through the Prophets were instituted to be done by the Hebrews—from that very thing he is rather proved to have fulfilled them. For whatever was prophesied through figures of this kind has been so far fulfilled that it is no longer prophesied through them.

To this pertains also what the Lord himself says: *The Law and the Prophets were until John*[^26]. For the Law, which shut up transgressors in abundant guilt unto that faith which was afterward revealed, became grace through Jesus Christ, through whom grace superabounded; and on this account it was fulfilled through the grace that frees, which was not being fulfilled through the letter that commands. Likewise, in the Law itself, all prophecy, which promised the coming of the Savior not only by words but also by the figures of certain actions, became truth through Jesus Christ. For *the Law was given through Moses; but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ*[^27]. From his coming the kingdom of God began to be announced: because *the Law and the Prophets were until John*—the Law, to make guilty those who would desire salvation; the Prophets, to promise the Savior.

But that other prophets arose in the Church after Christ's ascension, who is unaware? Of whom Paul says: *And some he appointed in the Church, first apostles, then prophets, third teachers*[^28], and the rest. It was not, then, of those that it was said, *The Law and the Prophets were until John*, but of those who prophesied the first coming of Christ—which coming, once fulfilled, surely could no longer be prophesied.

**9.** Accordingly, when you ask why a Christian is now no longer circumcised in the flesh, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian is now no longer circumcised for this very reason, that what was prophesied by that same circumcision Christ has now fulfilled. For the stripping off of the carnal generation, which was figured in that act, has now been fulfilled in Christ's resurrection—which, as it will be in our resurrection, is commended in the sacrament of Baptism. For neither ought the sacrament of new life to be removed entirely, since there still remains in us the future resurrection of the dead; and yet the same had to be changed for the better, with Baptism succeeding, because that has now happened which had never happened before: that, in the resurrection of Christ, the example of the future eternal life was offered to us.

When you ask why a Christian does not observe the leisure of the Sabbath, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian does not observe it for this reason, that what was prophesied by that figure Christ has now fulfilled. For in him we have the Sabbath, who said: *Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you; take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls*[^29].

**10.** When you ask why a Christian does not observe the distinction of foods which is commanded in the Law, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian does not observe it for this reason, that what was prophesied by those figures Christ has now fulfilled, not admitting to his body—which he has predestined among his saints to eternal life—whatever was signified in the conduct of men by those animals.

When you ask why a Christian does not, by slaying animals, offer to God sacrifices of flesh and blood, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian ought now all the more not to offer these, because the things which by such figures of things were being prophesied to him, Christ has fulfilled by the immolation of his own flesh and blood.

When you ask why a Christian does not observe the unleavened bread as the Jews do, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian all the more does not observe this, because what was prophesied by that figure Christ has fulfilled, showing the new life with the leaven of the old life purged out[^30].

When you ask why a Christian does not celebrate the Passover with the flesh of a lamb, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian no longer celebrates the Passover thus, because what was foretold by that figure, Christ, the unspotted lamb, has fulfilled by his passion.

When you ask for what cause a Christian does not celebrate the new moons commanded in the Law, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply: On the contrary, a Christian now does not celebrate these, because what they were celebrated to foretell, Christ has now fulfilled. For the celebration of the new moon foretold the new creation, of which the Apostle says: *If anyone, then, is a new creation in Christ, the old things have passed away; behold, they have been made new*[^31].

When you ask why a Christian does not observe those washings of each several uncleanness which are commanded in the Law, if Christ came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, I reply that a Christian rather does not observe these because they were figures of things to come, which Christ has fulfilled. For he came to bury us together with himself through Baptism into death, so that, as Christ rose from the dead, so we too might walk in newness of life[^32].

When you ask what the cause is that the Feast of Tabernacles is not a solemnity of Christians, if the Law was fulfilled by Christ and not abolished, I reply that the tabernacle of God is his faithful, in whom, joined and as it were fitted together in love, he deigns to dwell; and that therefore it is rather not observed by Christians, because Christ has now fulfilled in his Church what that figure prophetically promised.

**11.** And now indeed, for the sake of the business undertaken, lest they be passed over in silence, we have touched on these things with as much brevity as we could. But discussed member by member and point by point, they have made great and many books, showing in them nothing other than that Christ was prophesied: so that it comes about that all the things you suppose are not observed by Christians because Christ abolished them are rather found not observed by Christians because Christ fulfilled them. For the very observance of such figures was a foretelling of Christ. Whence what wonder is it, what absurdity—indeed, what is not fitting and consistent—if, after his coming, whatever was being done in order to foretell his coming ceased? The figures of things, then, which were observed for this purpose, that by their very observance the Christ to come might be prophesied, ought so far not to be supposed unfulfilled through Christ's coming because they are not observed when he has come, that, unless they were already fulfilled through Christ's coming, they would still be observed.

But into no name of religion, whether true or false, can men be curdled together, unless they are bound by some fellowship of visible signs or sacraments—the force of which sacraments is inexpressibly great, and therefore, when despised, makes men sacrilegious. For that is impiously despised without which piety cannot be brought to completion.

**12.** Nevertheless, because the visible sacraments of piety can be present even in the impious, as we read that even Simon the magician had holy Baptism[^33], such people become as the Apostle says: *Having a form of piety, but denying its power*[^34]. But the power of piety is the end of the commandment, that is, *love from a pure heart and a good conscience and faith unfeigned*[^35]. Whence the apostle Peter, when he spoke of the sacrament of the ark, in which the house of Noah was freed from the flood, says: *So too Baptism, by a like form, saves you.* And lest they suppose the visible sacrament, through which they had a form of piety, sufficient for themselves, while by living ruinously through evil conduct they denied its power, he immediately added: *not the putting off of the filth of the flesh, but the appeal of a good conscience*[^36].

**13.** Accordingly, the first sacraments, which were observed and celebrated according to the Law, were foretellings of the Christ to come; and when Christ had fulfilled them by his coming, they were removed—and removed for this reason, that they were fulfilled; for he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. And others were instituted, greater in power, better in usefulness, easier in performance, fewer in number, as befits the righteousness of faith revealed, and the yoke of slavery removed from the sons of God called into freedom[^37]—a yoke that was suited to a hard people given over to the flesh.

**14.** Nevertheless, if the ancient righteous, who understood that by those sacraments was foretold the coming revelation of the faith—from which, although still covered and hidden, yet by the gift of piety understood, they too even then lived (for in this life no one can be righteous except one who lives by faith[^38])—if, then, the ancient righteous were prepared to suffer all hard and dreadful things for the sake of those foretelling sacraments and figures of things not yet fulfilled, and very many did suffer them; if we praise the three young men and Daniel, because they were unwilling to be contaminated by the king's table[^39], which was against the sacrament of that time; if we set forth the Maccabees with vast admiration, because they were unwilling to touch foods which Christians now lawfully use[^40], since at that time, for the prophetic season, it was not permitted: how much more now, for the Baptism of Christ, for the Eucharist of Christ, for the sign of Christ, ought a Christian to be readier to endure all things, since those were promises of things to be fulfilled, and these are signs of things fulfilled?

For what is still promised to the Church—that is, to the body of Christ—and is preached in its manifestation, has already surely been fulfilled in the very head of the body, the Savior, that is, in the very Mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus[^41]. For what is promised but eternal life from resurrection from the dead? This has already been fulfilled in that flesh, in that *the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^42]. At that time, then, the faith too was hidden; for all the righteous and holy even of those times believed the same things and hoped the same things; and all those sacraments and all that rite of holy things were promissory. But now the faith has been revealed, in which the people was shut up when it was kept guarded under the Law[^43]; and what is promised to the faithful at the judgment has already been fulfilled by way of example, through him who came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them.

**15.** Wherefore it is indeed inquired among the searchers of the holy Scriptures whether the faith of a Christ who was to suffer and to rise—which they learned either by revelations or understood in the Prophets—profited the ancient righteous as much as the faith of a Christ who has suffered and been raised now profits us; or whether the very shedding of the blood of the Lamb of God, which was done, as he himself says, *for many unto the remission of sins*[^44], gave or added some usefulness and purification even to those who, believing this would happen, had departed from this life before it happened; and whether his death visited even the dead for their liberation. But to discuss this question now by treating it, or to define anything found in it even by confirming it, is both long and not necessary for this work.

**16.** For the present, against the slanderous ignorance of Faustus, it will suffice to demonstrate with how great an error they rave who suppose that, when signs and sacraments are changed, the very things too are different which the prophetic rite foretold as promised, and which the evangelical rite announced as fulfilled; or who judge that, when the things are the same, they ought not to have been announced as fulfilled by sacraments other than those by which they were foretold as still to be fulfilled.

For if the sounds of the words by which we speak are changed according to the time, and the same thing is expressed in one way as to be done, in another as done—just as those very two words I said, "to be done" and "done," sounded neither with equal intervals of duration, nor with the same or as many letters and syllables—what wonder is it if by some seals of the mysteries the passion and resurrection of Christ was promised as future, and by others is announced as now done;

seeing that the very words "future" and "done," "about to suffer" and "having suffered," "about to rise" and "has risen," could neither be drawn out equally nor sound alike?

For what else are the various bodily sacraments but, as it were, certain visible words—sacrosanct indeed, yet changeable and temporal? For God is eternal; yet the water, and all that bodily action which is performed when we baptize, both happens and passes away, and is not eternal: where again even those syllables, swiftly sounding and passing—when "God" is said—unless they are spoken, no consecration takes place. All these things happen and pass away, sound and pass away; yet the power which works through them remains continually, and the spiritual gift which is imparted through them is eternal.

He, therefore, who says, "If Christ had not abolished the Law and the Prophets, those sacraments of the Law and the Prophets would remain in the gatherings and celebrations of Christians," can say: "If Christ had not abolished the Law and the Prophets, one would still be promised as about to be born, to suffer, and to rise"—since he rather did not abolish these things, but fulfilled them, in that no one is now promised as about to be born, to suffer, to rise, which those sacraments once proclaimed, but it is announced that he has been born, has suffered, has risen, which these sacraments performed by Christians now proclaim. He, therefore, who came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them, by that very fulfillment took away the things through which there was still being promised, as to be fulfilled, what now stands fulfilled—as if he were to take away these words, "about to be born, about to suffer, about to rise," which were rightly spoken when these things were future, and were to institute the saying, "He has been born, has suffered, has risen," which, when those things are fulfilled and on that account removed, are rightly spoken.

**17.** As, then, with those words, so with those sacraments of the former people: because, through him who came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them, they have now been fulfilled, therefore they had to be removed and changed. Which, for the first Christians who had believed from among the Jews—until, against so long-standing a custom, they should gradually be persuaded and brought to a perfect understanding, and because they had been so born and reared—the Apostles allowed them to keep the ancestral rite and tradition; and they admonished those for whom this was needful, that they should accommodate themselves to the slowness and manners of those people.

Hence it is that the Apostle even circumcised Timothy, born of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, on account of those to whom he had come with such men[^45]; and he himself kept a custom of this kind among them, not by a deceitful pretense, but by a prudent counsel. For to those so born and so reared these things were not harmful, although they were no longer necessary for signifying things to come. It was rather more harmful to forbid them as harmful in those men among whom they had to last. For Christ, who had come to fulfill all those prophecies, had found them initiated in this way; so that thenceforth those who were held by no such necessity, but came together from the opposite wall as it were—that is, from uncircumcision—to that cornerstone which is Christ[^46], were not compelled to any such things. But if those who had come from circumcision, and were still given to such sacraments, of their own accord wished, as Timothy did, to render this conformity, they were not forbidden.

But if in works of the Law of this kind they supposed that their hope and salvation was contained, they were forbidden as from certain ruin. Hence comes that saying of the Apostle: *Behold, I Paul say to you, that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing*[^47]. Circumcised, that is, as they themselves wished, as had been urged upon them by certain corrupt men that without these works of the Law they could not be saved[^48]. For when the gentiles came to the faith of Christ—chiefly through the preaching of the apostle Paul—as they ought to have come, so that they would be burdened by no observances of this kind (both because these unaccustomed things, especially circumcision, would, by frightening those advanced in age, deter them from the faith; and because, not being so born as to be steeped in such sacraments, if they became proselytes in the old manner, Christ would seem to be still promised, as to come, through those mysteries);

since, then, they came to the faith in the way the gentiles now had to come, those who had come from circumcision—not understanding why those things had been permitted to themselves, and why they were not to be imposed on the gentiles—had begun to disturb the Church with certain carnal seditions, because the gentiles, approaching the people of God, were not solemnly made proselytes by the circumcision of the flesh and by the other observances of the Law of this kind. And among these were some who insisted greatly that this be done, because they feared the Jews among whom they lived. Against these the apostle Paul wrote many things; for he even corrected, with a brotherly rebuke, Peter, who had been drawn into their pretense[^49].

But after the Apostles, gathered together into one, also decreed by their counsel that the gentiles were not to be compelled to works of the Law of this kind[^50], it displeased certain Christians from the circumcision, who could not discern in their minds that those alone were not to be forbidden such observances whom the faith, once revealed, had found already steeped in them—so that in those whom it had already held before the fulfillment of the prophecy, the prophetic action itself might be brought to completion; lest, if it were removed from them too, it should seem disapproved rather than ended; but if it were imposed on the gentiles also, it should be thought either not to have been instituted for the sake of promising Christ, or to be promising Christ still.

Thus the first people of God, before Christ came to fulfill the Law and the Prophets, were commanded to observe all those things which promised him: free in those who understood to what they pertained, but slaves in those who did not understand this. But the later people, coming to the faith by which it was now preached that Christ had come, had suffered, and had risen, in those men whom the same faith had found already instructed in such sacraments, were neither compelled to observe these things nor forbidden; but in those who, empty of such things, held by no necessity of birth, custom, or conformity, had believed, they were even forbidden—so that through them it might now begin to appear that all those things had been instituted for the sake of promising Christ; and that, with him come and fulfilling these promises, they now ought to cease. This tempering and governance of the Holy Spirit, working through the Apostles, having displeased certain believers from the circumcision who did not understand these things, they remained in that perversity, so that they even compelled the gentiles to Judaize. These are they whom Faustus mentioned under the name of Symmachians or Nazoreans, who endure even to our times, now indeed in a small remnant, but still, even in that fewness, persisting.

**18.** What, then, do these people have, by which to slander the Law and the Prophets, that Christ came to abolish them rather than to fulfill them, because Christians do not observe the things commanded there—when they fail to observe only those things by which Christ was being promised, and fail to observe them all the more because Christ has now fulfilled those same promises, and the things already fulfilled are no longer promised; and their promissory signs had to be ended in those whom the faith of a Christ who fulfills these things had found already steeped in them? For do Christians not observe what is in that Scripture: *Hear, O Israel; the Lord your God is one God*[^51]; *You shall not make for yourself an idol*, and the rest of this kind? Do Christians not observe what is said there: *You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain*? Do Christians not observe the very Sabbath, which pertains to the understanding of true rest? Do Christians not pay honor to parents, which is commanded there? Do Christians not refrain from fornications, or murders, or thefts, or false testimonies, or coveting a neighbor's wife, or coveting a neighbor's property—all of which are written in that Law[^52]? These are precepts of morals, those are sacraments of things promised: these are fulfilled through assisting grace, those through truth rendered; both through Christ, who both always bestows that grace, now also revealing it, and both promised that truth then and displays it now: because *the Law was given through Moses; but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ*[^53]. In short, these things, which are preserved in the conscience of one living rightly, are fulfilled by faith working through love[^54]; but those which were engaged in a promissory signification have passed away with the things rendered. So these too were not abolished but fulfilled; for Christ showed them not void nor deceitful, when he displayed what was promised by their signification.

**19.** It is not, then, as Faustus supposes, that the Lord Jesus fulfilled certain things which had already been said by the ancient righteous before the Law of Moses—such as, You shall not kill, which he did not refute by a contrary but rather strengthened, when he recalled men even from anger and from reviling[^55]—but abolished certain things which seemed proper to the law of the Hebrews, such as, An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, which he seems to have taken away rather than confirmed, when he says: *But I say to you, do not resist evil; but if anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the other also*[^56], and the rest. For we say that even these things, which they suppose Christ abolished, by introducing as it were contraries, were both well instituted then for the time, and now not abolished by Christ but fulfilled.

**20.** Accordingly, I first ask these people whether those ancient righteous men, Enoch and Seth (for these especially Faustus mentions), and any others—not only before Moses, but any who lived before Abraham—were angry with a brother without cause, or said to a brother, "You fool." For if they did not say it, why did they not also teach such things? But if they did teach such things, I ask how Christ fulfilled their righteousness and doctrine by adding: *But I say to you, whoever is angry with his brother; or whoever says, Raca; or whoever says, You fool, will be liable—to the judgment, or to the council, or to the fire of Gehenna*[^57]—since they too lived in the same manner and admonished men to live in the same manner? Or did those righteous men not know that anger must be bridled, and that a brother is not to be provoked with insolent reviling? Or did they know it, but were unable to abstain from these things? Then they were liable to Gehenna: how, then, were they righteous? For surely you do not dare to call their righteousness either ignorant of the things pertaining to its duty, or intemperate, to such a degree as to make them liable to Gehenna. Why, then, would Christ fulfill that law, according to which the ancient righteous lived, by adding these things, since their righteousness too could not exist without them? Or will you say that headlong anger and a reckless tongue began, from the time Christ came, to pertain to iniquity, whereas before it was not iniquitous to commit these things in heart or mouth? Just as in certain matters instituted according to the properties of the times, we find that something is now not permitted which before was permitted, or that what before was not permitted is now permitted. You are not so foolish as to say this; but even if you do say it, you will be answered that, according to this understanding, Christ came not to fulfill what was lacking in the ancient law, but to institute a law that did not exist; if saying to a brother, "You fool," not having been unjust among the ancient righteous, Christ now willed it to be so unjust that whoever says this is liable to Gehenna. Accordingly, you have not yet found to what law these things were ever lacking, by whose addition now Christ would fulfill it.

**21.** Or was the law of not committing adultery, perhaps, half-full among those ancient righteous, until it should be fulfilled by the Lord, who added that no one should look at a woman to lust after her? For thus you recalled the very statement: "You have heard that it was said: You shall not commit adultery; but I say to you, do not even lust. This is fulfillment," you say. Set forth plainly the evangelical words themselves; do not weaken by your own words what was said; and see what you have judged about those most ancient righteous men. *You have heard,* he says, *that it was said: You shall not commit adultery; but I say to you: Whoever looks at a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart*[^58]. Did those righteous men, then—Seth or Enoch, or any like them—commit adultery in their hearts; and either was their heart not the temple of God, or did they commit adultery in the temple of God? But if you do not dare to say this, how did Christ, coming now, fulfill the law of theirs concerning this matter too, which among them was already full even then?

**22.** As for not swearing[^59]—since here too you said that their law was fulfilled by Christ—I cannot affirm that the ancient righteous did not swear: for we find that the apostle Paul too swore[^60]. But from your own mouth frequent swearing is not removed, since you swear by the light, which you love along with flies; for that light of minds, which *enlightens every man coming into this world*[^61], you do not know, except that you can think of it in some part as not utterly foreign to these eyes. And you swear by your lord Mani, who in his native tongue was called Manes; but you, to avoid among the Greeks the name of madness, by inflecting and lengthening the name, added as it were "a pouring," where you slipped the further. For thus one of you explained to me why he was called Manichaeus: namely, that in the Greek language he might seem as it were to pour out manna; for "to pour" is in Greek cheô—in which I do not know what you accomplished, except that you dreamed up your madness for yourselves more expressly. For you did not add one letter in the earlier part of the name, so that "manna" might be recognized, but you added in the latter part two syllables, calling him not Mannichaeus but Manichaeus; so that with such prolix and vain discourses of his he might sound to you as pouring out nothing but madness. Very often you also swear by the Paraclete—not, indeed, that one whom Christ promised to his disciples and sent[^62], but by that very one whom, to interpret his name in Latin, I would call the "madness-pourer." Since, then, you never cease to swear, I would like to know how you understand this part of the Law too, which you wish to be understood as most ancient, that the Lord fulfilled it for you—and the more so on account of the Apostle's oaths. For what authority is yours, even for yourselves, much less for me or any man? Whence I think it is now clear how differently must be taken what Christ says: *I came not to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*. Not, namely, by those additions, which pertain either to the exposition of the ancient statements set forth, or to manner of life, not to fulfillment.

**23.** For because they did not understand murder except as the destruction of a human body, by which it is deprived of life, the Lord disclosed that every wicked impulse to harm a brother is reckoned in the category of murder. Whence John too says: *He who hates his brother is a murderer*[^63]. And because they thought that only bodily unlawful union with a woman was called adultery, the Master showed that such lust too is nothing else. Likewise, because to swear falsely is a grave sin, but not to swear is, like swearing truly, no sin; yet he who is not accustomed even to swear is removed further from swearing falsely than he who is prone to swear truly: the Lord preferred that we, even not swearing, should not depart from the truth, rather than that we, swearing truly, should draw near to perjury. And so the Apostle too, in the discourses he is reported to have held, never swore, lest by the habit of swearing he should sometime, even unknowingly, slip into perjury. But in his writings, where there is greater and more deliberate consideration, he is found to have sworn in several places, lest anyone should suppose that one sins even by swearing truly, but should rather understand that the hearts of human frailty are more safely preserved from perjury by not swearing. With these things perceived, we find that not even those things were destroyed, as Faustus supposes, which he wishes to pertain, as it were properly, to Moses.

**24.** For here too I ask these people why they wish it to be proper to the Law of Moses, what was said to the ancients: *You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy*[^64]. Or did not the apostle Paul too call certain men hateful to God[^65]? And surely in this admonition the Lord himself exhorts us to this, that we imitate God. *That you may be,* he says, *sons of your Father who is in heaven, who makes his sun rise upon the good and the bad, and rains upon the just and the unjust.* It must therefore be asked how it is to be understood that, by the example of God, to whom Paul said certain men are hateful, enemies are to be held in hatred; and again, by the example of God, who makes his sun rise upon the good and the bad and rains upon the just and the unjust, enemies are to be loved. Thus it will appear that the Lord wished to bring to those who understood badly what was said—*You shall hate your enemy*—what they did not know at all: that they should love their enemies. But how both are to be observed is long to discuss. But for the present, to these people, who are generally displeased if anyone hates his enemy, we have a word that may press their brow, when we ask them whether their god loves the nation of darkness; or, if enemies are now to be loved because they have a part of good, why we ought not on this account also to hate them, because they have a part of evil. For by that rule this too is resolved, and it is taught that there is no contradiction in what was said in the ancient Scripture, *You shall hate your enemy*, and in the Gospel, *Love your enemies*[^66]: namely, that every wicked man, insofar as he is wicked, is to be hated; but insofar as he is a man, is to be loved; so that what we rightly hate in him, that is, the vice, we may reprove, in order that what we rightly love in him, that is, human nature itself, may be freed by the vice's being amended. This, I say, is the rule by which we both hate the enemy on account of what is evil in him, that is, iniquity, and love the enemy on account of what is good in him, that is, the social and rational creature—except that we convict him of being evil not through nature, whether his own or another's, but through his own will. But they suppose man to be evil through the nature of the nation of darkness, which, according to them, the whole of their god feared before he was in part conquered; and by which he was in part so conquered that he was not even wholly freed. Having heard, then, and not understood, what was said to the ancients, *You shall hate your enemy*, men were carried into hatred of a man, when they ought to have hated nothing but the vice: these the Lord corrects, by saying, *Love your enemies*; so that he who had already said, *I came not to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*, and therefore would not abolish what is written in the Law concerning hatred of an enemy, by commanding us to love our enemies, would compel us to understand in what manner we could both hate one and the same man on account of fault, and love him on account of nature. But this is much for their perverse minds to understand. They are only to be pressed, so that, according to the lost reasoning—or rather the madness—of their slander, they may defend their god, whom they cannot call a lover of the nation of darkness; and therefore by his example they have no way to exhort each man to love his enemy. For they could rather attribute love of an enemy to the very nation of darkness than to their god. For that nation, as they rave, coveted the light near and adjacent to it, and wished to enjoy it, and, in order to enjoy it, planned to invade it. And this is not a fault, when the true and beatifying good is sought. Whence the Lord too says: *The kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and those who do violence seize it*[^67]. Behold, the nation of darkness, according to their vanity, wished to do violence to and seize the good which it had loved, delighted by its brightness and form; and God did not love it in return, but, detesting in hatred the one wishing to enjoy himself, set about to uproot it utterly. If, then, the evil love the good which they may enjoy, but the good hate the evil lest they be polluted, answer, you Manicheans: which of them fulfill what the Lord says, *Love your enemies*? Behold, if you wish these to be separate and conflicting statements, your god did what is written in the law of Moses, *You shall hate your enemy*; and the nation of darkness did what is written in the Gospel, *Love your enemies*. And yet not even by inventing have you been able to find by what agreement you might settle the question between the light-seeking flies and the light-fleeing cockroaches: for you contend that both are offspring of the nation of darkness. Whence, then, do the former love a light foreign to them, while the latter, shunning this light, are delighted rather by their own origin? Or are flies born more cleanly in fetid sewers than cockroaches in dark bedrooms?

**25.** Now as for what was said to the ancients, *An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth*, how is it contrary to what the Lord says, *But I say to you, do not resist evil; but if anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the other also*[^68], and the rest? Since that ancient saying too was commanded for the purpose of repressing the flames of hatred and of bridling the immoderate minds of the raging. For who is easily content to repay only as much in vengeance as he has received in injury? Do we not see men lightly hurt plotting slaughter, thirsting for blood, and scarcely finding in an enemy's misfortunes wherewith to be satisfied? Who, struck with a fist, does not either stir up lawsuits for the condemnation of him who struck, or, if he wishes to strike back himself, pound the whole man with fists and heels—if not even by snatching up some weapon? To this immoderate and therefore unjust vengeance, then, the law, fixing a just measure, instituted the penalty of retaliation, that is, that each should pay such a punishment as the injury he inflicted. Accordingly, *An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth* is not the fuel but the limit of fury—imposed not that what had been quieted might be kindled by it, but lest what was burning might be extended further. For there is a certain just vengeance, and it is justly owed to him who has suffered injury; whence surely, when we forgive, we give away, as it were, something of our own right. Whence too those things are called debts which, in the Lord's Prayer, we are admonished to remit in human fashion, so that ours too may be remitted to us by God[^69]. But what is owed, even if it is kindly remitted, is nonetheless not unjustly demanded back. But as in swearing, even he who swears truly draws near to perjury, from which he is far removed who does not swear at all; and although he who swears truly does not sin, yet he who does not swear is further from sin; whence the admonition not to swear is a preservation from the sin of perjury: so, since he sins who through immoderation wishes to be avenged unjustly, but he does not sin who, applying measure, wishes to be avenged justly, he is further from sin who does not wish to be avenged at all. For he sins who exacts beyond what is owed; but he does not sin who exacts what is owed: yet he is far safer from the sin of an unjust exactor who does not exact the debt at all, especially lest he too be compelled to pay a debt by him who has no debt. I too, then, could set these things forth thus: It was said to the ancients, "You shall not avenge yourself unjustly"; but I say, "Do not even avenge yourselves"—this is fulfillment; just as Faustus says of swearing: "It was said: You shall not swear falsely; but I say, do not even swear; this likewise is fulfillment." I too, then, could speak thus, if by these added words it seemed to me that what was lacking in the Law was added by Christ, and not rather that what the Law wished to effect—that no one should sin by avenging himself unjustly—is more safely preserved if he does not avenge himself at all; just as what it wished to effect—that no one should sin by swearing falsely—is more safely preserved if he does not swear. For if *An eye for an eye* is contrary to *Whoever strikes you on the cheek, offer him the other also*, why is not *You shall render to the Lord your oath* contrary to *Do not swear at all*[^70]? And yet Faustus judges the latter not destruction but fulfillment—which here too he ought to have judged. For if "Swear truly" is fulfilled by saying "Do not swear," why is not "Avenge justly" fulfilled by saying "Do not avenge"? Thus I too judge that in both there is a preservation from the sin by which either a falsehood is sworn or vengeance taken unjustly; although this matter of forgoing vengeance altogether avails also for that other end, that by remitting debts of this kind, we too may deserve to be forgiven. But for a hard people a measure first had to be applied, by which it might learn not to go beyond what is owed: so that, with anger tamed—which carries men off to immoderate vengeance—now he who wished might calmly consider what he himself owed, what he desired to be relaxed to him by the Lord, in order that, by this consideration, he might relax the debt to his fellow servant.

**26.** For that precept too of the Lord, that a wife is not to be put away—although it was said to the ancients, *Whoever puts away his wife, let him give her a writ of divorce*[^71]—if we look at it carefully, we shall see that it is not contrary. For the Lord set forth what the Law willed, when it commanded one who put away his wife at random to give a writ of divorce. For it did not say, "Whoever wishes, let him put away his wife"—to which it would be contrary not to put her away; but it surely did not wish a wife to be put away by her husband, when it interposed this delay, so that a mind rushing headlong into separation, checked by the writing of the document, might desist, and might consider what an evil it is to put away a wife: especially since, as they report, among the Hebrews it was lawful for no one to write Hebrew letters except the scribes alone, who both professed a more excellent wisdom and, if any of them were endowed with equity and piety, not only professed wisdom but even practiced it. To these, then, who ought to be prudent interpreters of the Law and just dissuaders from separation, the Law wished to send the man whom it commanded to give a writ of divorce, if he put away his wife. For the document could not be written for him except by them, who, by this occasion, would in a manner guide back into their own hands one coming under necessity, with good counsel, and, dealing peaceably between the man and his wife, would urge love and concord. But if so great a hatred intervened that it could not be extinguished and amended, then surely the document would be written: for he would not put away in vain a woman whom he so hated that he could be recalled by no persuasion of the prudent to the love owed to marriage. For if a wife is not loved, she is to be put away. Since, then, she is not to be put away, she is to be loved. But love can be composed by admonishing and persuading, not imposed by compelling the unwilling. This the just and wise scribe ought to do, such as one ought to be in that profession; that he might be resorted to, the discordant husband was commanded to write a document, which a good and prudent man would not write, unless in a mind too averse and perverse the counsel of concord availed nothing. But from you, out of the sacrilegious vanity of your error, I ask why it should displease you to put away a wife, whom you judge ought to be had not for the fidelity of matrimony, but for the crime of concupiscence. For matrimony is so named from this, that a woman ought to marry for no other reason than to become a mother (mater); which is hateful to you. For in this way you suppose that a part of your god, conquered and subdued in battle by the nation of darkness, is bound also by fleshly fetters.

**27.** But to set forth rather what is now under discussion: if Christ, where, after certain ancient statements set forth, he added, *But I say to you*, neither fulfilled the law of the first men by this addition of words, nor destroyed that which was given through Moses, as it were by the opposition of contraries, but rather so commended all the things mentioned from the law of the Hebrews that whatever he spoke besides, from his own person, availed either toward seeking the exposition, if that law had set anything down obscurely, or toward more safely preserving what that law had willed: you see how differently must be understood what he says, that he came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it—namely, that it was not made whole by these words as though half-full, but that what, with the letter commanding, could not be done on account of the presumption of the proud, might, with grace persuading, be fulfilled on account of the confession of the humble, by the work of deeds, not by the addition of words. For faith, as the Apostle says, *works through love*[^72]. Whence likewise he says: *For he who loves another has fulfilled the Law*[^73]. This love, because Christ, coming, bestowed it in manifestation through the Holy Spirit whom he sent as promised—by which love alone the righteousness of the Law could be fulfilled—he therefore said: *I came not to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it*. This is the New Testament, by which to this love the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven is promised, which was veiled in the figures of the Old Testament according to the distribution of the times. Whence he says: *A new commandment I give you, that you love one another*[^74].

**28.** And so all, or nearly all, the things he admonished or commanded, where he added *But I say to you*, are found also in those ancient books. There it is said against anger: *My eye is troubled because of anger*[^75]; and, *Better is he who conquers anger than he who takes a city*[^76]. There against a hard word: *The stroke of a whip makes a bruise, but the stroke of the tongue breaks the bones*[^77]. There against adultery of the heart: *You shall not covet your neighbor's wife*[^78]. For it does not say, "You shall not commit adultery," but, "You shall not covet." Whence the Apostle recalls this from the Law, saying: *For I would not have known covetousness, unless the Law had said: You shall not covet*[^79]. There, concerning the patience of not resisting, the man is praised who offers his cheek to the one striking him and is filled with reproaches[^80]. There, concerning loving an enemy, it is said: *If your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he thirsts, give him drink*[^81]. For from here the Apostle recalled this[^82]. And that saying in the Psalm: *With those who hated peace I was peaceable*[^83]; and many other things. And that we imitate God by tempering ourselves from vengeance and by loving even the wicked, you have there a copious passage about God himself doing this; for there it is written: *For your great might is always at hand for you alone, and who will stand against the strength of your arm? For the whole world before you is like a grain that tips the scales, and like a drop of morning dew that comes down upon the earth. But you have mercy on all, because you can do all things, and you overlook the sins of men for the sake of repentance. For you love all things that are, and hate none of the things you have made; for you would not have established anything if you hated it. How could anything endure, if you had not willed it; or what had not been called by you, be preserved? But you spare all things, because they are yours, O Lord, who love souls. For your good spirit is in all things; on account of which you rebuke little by little those who go astray, and admonishing them about the things wherein they sin, you address them, that, leaving wickedness, they may believe in you, O Lord*[^84]. To this kindly patience of God, who makes his sun rise upon the good and the bad and rains upon the just and the unjust, Christ exhorts us to imitate it: that we should neglect to avenge our injuries, and do good to those who hate us, that we may be perfect, as our heavenly Father is perfect[^85]. And that it avails us also toward the remitting of the debts of our sins, that we relax to others these debts of vengeance, and that we must beware lest, if we do not do this, the obligation of sin not be remitted to us either, though we entreat—thus it is written in those ancient books: *He who wishes to be avenged will find vengeance from the Lord, and he will surely establish his sins. Forgive your neighbor who has harmed you, and then, when you pray, your sins will be loosed. Does a man harbor anger against a man, and seek healing from the Lord? He has no mercy on a man like himself, and does he entreat the Lord for his own sins? He himself, being but flesh, harbors anger, and seeks propitiation from the Lord? And who will obtain pardon for his sins?*[^86]

**29.** Now concerning a wife not to be put away, what else, or what more apt, should I recall from those books, than that by which the Lord himself answered the Jews when they questioned him about this matter? For when they asked whether it was lawful to put away a wife for any cause whatever, he said to them: *Have you not read that he who made them from the beginning made them male and female, and said: For this reason a man shall leave father and mother and cleave to his wife; and the two shall be one flesh. And so they are no longer two, but one flesh. What, then, God has joined together, let not man separate.* Behold, the Jews are convicted from the books of Moses that a wife is not to be put away—they who supposed they were acting according to the will of the law of Moses when they put her away. At the same time we here recognize too, with Christ himself bearing witness, that God made and joined male and female—which the Manicheans, by denying, condemn, now resisting not the book of Moses but the Gospel of Christ. Moreover, if what they themselves suppose and preach is true, that the devil made and joined male and female, by what diabolical cunning does Faustus rebuke Moses as one dissolving marriages by a writ of divorce, and praise Christ as one confirming that bond of this kind by the precept of the Gospel—when surely, according to his own foolish and sacrilegious opinion, he ought to praise Moses for separating what the devil had made and joined, and to find fault with Christ for solidifying the devil's contrivance and bond? Now, how the good Master opens up that other point—why Moses himself, from whose book was brought forth, concerning the first joining of male and female, conjugal chastity so holy and to be violated by no separation, afterward permitted a wife to be put away? For when they had answered him: *Why, then, did Moses command to give a writ of divorce and put away?* he said to them: *Because of the hardness of your heart Moses permitted you to put away your wives*[^87]. This is what we expounded a little before[^88]. For how great was the hardness that could not, even by the interposition of a document—where the place for dissuasion was granted to the just and prudent—be loosed and bent to receive or recall the love of marriage? Thus the Lord declared, by the testimony of the same Law, what the Law both commanded to the good and permitted to the hard: when he both admonished, from the joining of male and female recalled from that same Scripture, that a wife is not to be put away, and expounded the divine authority of that joining, and showed that the writ of divorce is to be given on account of the hardness of a heart, whether to be tamed or untamable.

**30.** Wherefore, since all those excellent precepts of the Lord, which Faustus wished to show contrary to the ancient books of the Hebrews, are found in those same books as well, for what did the Lord come—not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it—except that, the figures of things promised being excepted (which, with the truth rendered, have been fulfilled and removed), the precepts themselves, by which that Law is holy and just and good[^89], should be fulfilled in us, not through the oldness of the letter that commands, and that increases the offenses of the proud by the guilt even of transgression, but through the newness of the spirit that assists, and that frees the confession of the humble by the grace of salvation? For in truth, just as all these sublime precepts are not lacking even in those ancient books, so there the end to which they are referred is hidden; although according to that end the saints lived, who saw its future revelation, and, according to the property of the times, either prophetically veiled it, or wisely understood it as prophetically veiled.

**31.** Finally—and I would not say this rashly—I do not know whether anyone has found in those books the name "kingdom of heaven," which the Lord names so often. It is indeed said there: *Love wisdom, that you may reign forever*[^90]. And eternal life itself, if it were not there preached openly, the Lord would not say even to the wicked Jews: *Search the Scriptures, in which you think you have eternal life; they bear witness about me*[^91]. For to what else does it pertain that it is written there: *I shall not die, but live, and recount the works of the Lord*[^92]; and, *Enlighten my eyes, lest I ever sleep in death*[^93]; and, *The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and no torment shall touch them*—and a little after: *But they are in peace; and though before men they suffered torments, their hope is full of immortality; and being chastised in few things, in many they will be well disposed*[^94]; and in another place: *But the just will live forever, and their reward is with the Lord, and the care of them is with the Most High; therefore they will receive the kingdom of glory and the crown of beauty from the hand of the Lord*[^95]? These and many other testimonies of eternal life, whether most open or somewhat obscure, are found there.

And concerning the resurrection of bodies itself the Prophets were not silent. Whence the Pharisees most fiercely contended against the Sadducees, who did not believe it; which appears evidently not only in the canonical Acts of the Apostles—which these people do not accept, lest they be convicted concerning the coming of the true Paraclete whom the Lord promised[^96]—but also in the Gospel, where the Sadducees set before him the question about the woman who had married seven brothers one by one, since each in turn succeeded the other dying in marriage to her, as to whose wife of them she would be in the resurrection[^97].

Accordingly, that Scripture abounds in testimonies of eternal life and of the resurrection of the dead; but this name—that is, "kingdom of heaven"—occurs to me from no passage there; for this pertains properly to the revelation of the New Testament, because those bodies which had been earthly will, by that change which Paul mentions more openly, become spiritual in the resurrection[^98], and on this account heavenly, in which we may possess the kingdom of heaven. Which was reserved also to be named by the mouth of him whom that whole apparatus of the old Instrument was bringing to birth—as one to come, a king to rule and a priest to sanctify his faithful—in generations, deeds, words, sacrifices, observances, festivities, and in all the proclamations of its discourses, and in deeds done, and in figures of things: who, full of grace and truth[^99], both to do the precepts by assisting through grace, and to fulfill the promises by caring through truth, came not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it.
