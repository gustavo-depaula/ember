# Book XXVI

**1.** FAUSTUS: If Jesus was not born, how did he die? This is now plainly a conjecture; and no one resorts to conjectures except someone whom proofs have failed. We will answer this too, however, drawing our examples from no other source than the things you are accustomed to believe: if these are true, they will confirm us as well; if false, they will destroy you as well. You say, then: How did Jesus die, if he was not a man? And I ask of you in turn: How did Elijah not die, when he was a man? Was this mortal allowed, beyond his own condition, to seize the right of immortality, while Christ, being immortal, was not allowed to take to himself something of death, if it was necessary? And if Elijah lives forever against nature, why would you not all the more grant that Jesus could die against nature for only three days—especially since you believe this not only of Elijah but of Moses and Enoch as well, that they are immortal, themselves caught up with their bodies into heaven? Therefore, if by this argument it is rightly concluded that Jesus was a man because he died, it can nonetheless be concluded by the same argument that Elijah was not a man because he did not die. But it is false that Elijah was not a man, although he is believed to be immortal; it will be equally false that Jesus was a man, although he is reckoned to have died. And if you are willing to believe me when I speak the truth, both of them have drawn a false opinion among the Hebrews—Jesus about death, and Elijah about immortality; for neither did this one die, nor did that one not die. But you believe what you wish, and what you do not wish you reduce to nature. Therefore, if it is asked what is proper to nature, nature indeed wills neither that the immortal should die nor that the mortal should not die. But if we inquire about the power in God and in a man to accomplish what they will, I think it more likely that Jesus could die than that Elijah could not die; for there is greater power in Jesus than in Elijah. And if you raise the less powerful one up into heaven against the license of nature and consecrate him to perpetuity, forgetful of his nature and condition, why should I not grant that Jesus could die if he willed—even if I agree that it was truly that death and not a figure of death? For just as from the beginning, having taken on the likeness of a man, he simulated all the affections of the human condition, so it was not beside the point if at the end too, for the sake of sealing the dispensation, he had seemed even to die.

**2.** Besides, this too must be recalled: that if it is asked what each thing is allowed by nature, this ought to be asked about everything that Jesus did, not about his death alone. For nature does not permit one blind from birth to see the light; yet Jesus is seen to have powerfully worked this upon the blind of this kind, so much so that the Jews themselves cried out that from the beginning of the world it had never been seen that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind[^1]; that he healed a withered hand, that he gave back voice and speech to those deprived of these by nature, that he restored the vital breath to the dead and to bodies already dissolved into decay, with their frame given back to them—who would not be brought to amazement by these things, and in a way compelled, if he considers what is or is not allowed by nature, not to believe them at all? Yet all these things we Christians together believe were done by the same one, no longer by consideration of nature, but only of the power and might of God. This too is read: that having once been hurled from the brow of a mountain by the Jews, he went away unharmed. He, then, who was cast down from a high mountain and did not die, because he assuredly did not will it—why could he not also die when he willed? And let this much be answered by us in this way for now, since it pleases you to argue and you try foreign weapons, wishing to dispute dialectically; otherwise, for us neither did Jesus die nor is Elijah immortal.

**3.** AUGUSTINE: Whatever Holy Scripture testifies about Enoch and Elijah and Moses—Scripture set at the highest peak of authority by the sure and great proofs of its trustworthiness—this we believe, not what Faustus suspects us of believing. But what is according to nature and what is against nature, men who err as you do cannot know. That something is said, in human fashion, to be against nature when it is against the use of nature known to mortals, we too do not deny—as in what the Apostle says: *if you were cut from what is by nature a wild olive tree, and grafted, contrary to nature, into a cultivated olive tree*[^2]; he called that against nature which is against the custom of nature that human knowledge grasps, namely, that a wild olive grafted into an olive should bear, not the berries of the wild olive, but the richness of the olive. But God, the creator and founder of all natures, does nothing against nature; for that will be natural to each thing which he has made, from whom comes every measure, number, and order of nature. Nor does man himself do anything against nature, except when he sins—and he too is by punishment reduced to nature. For it belongs to the natural order of justice either that sins should not be committed or that they should not be able to go unpunished; whichever of these it is, the natural order is preserved, if not by the soul, then certainly by God. For sins vex the conscience and harm the mind itself, since by sinning it is deprived of the light of justice, even if no pains follow—pains which are either inflicted to correct, or reserved as the final lot for those who are not corrected. But we say, not inappropriately, that God does something against nature when he does something against what we know in nature. For we also call this "nature," the course of nature known and customary to us, against which, when God does something, the deeds are named mighty works or wonders. But against that highest law of nature, removed from the knowledge of the impious or of those still weak, God does nothing, just as he does nothing against himself. The spiritual and at the same time rational creature, in which class the human soul also belongs, the more fully it becomes a partaker of that unchangeable law and light, the more it sees what can and what cannot be done; but the farther removed from it, the more it marvels at the unusual, the less it discerns what is to come.

**4.** And through this, what was done concerning Elijah we do not know; yet this we believe of him, which truthful Scripture testifies. This indeed we know: that what was done of him is what the will of God holds; but what the will of God does not hold can in no way be done concerning anyone. Accordingly, if it is said to me that it can come about that the flesh, for instance, of this or that man should be changed into a heavenly body, I grant that it can be done, but whether it will be done I do not know; and I do not know it because what the will of God holds in this matter is hidden from me. This, however, is not hidden from me: that without doubt it will come about, if the will of God holds it so. Further, if I should hear that something was going to be, but God brought it about that it should not be, I will answer most faithfully: rather, that was going to be which God did, not that which, if it had been going to be, he would have brought about. For God surely knew what he was going to do, and therefore he at the same time knew that that was not going to be which he was going to bring about should not be; and beyond doubt what God knows is truer than what man supposes. Hence future things can no more fail to come about than past things can fail to have been done; for it is not within God's will that anything should be false by the very thing whereby it is true. Therefore all things that are truly future will without doubt come about; but if they shall not have come about, they were not future. So all things that are truly past have without doubt passed away.

**5.** Whoever, then, says, "If God is omnipotent, let him bring it about that the things which have been done should not have been done," does not see that he is saying this: "If he is omnipotent, let him bring it about that the things which are true should, by the very fact that they are true, be false." For he can bring it about that something not be which was; for he brings it about that it not be when he finds something to exist out of which this may come—as when someone who began to be by being born, he brings it about that he not be by dying; for he finds this done, out of which it may come. But who would say that he should bring it about that what already is not should not be? For whatever is past already is not; and if anything can come about of it, there still is something of which it may come; and if there is, how is it past? So that is not, which we truly say has been; but it is true that that has been, because in our judgment it is true, not in that thing which already is not. For the judgment by which we say that something has been is true precisely because that of which we speak already is not. This judgment God cannot make false, because he is not contrary to truth. And if you ask where this true judgment is: it is first found in our mind, when we know and say it to be true. But if it should also be taken away from our mind, when we have forgotten what we know, it will remain in truth itself. For it will always be true that that has been which was and is not; and there it will be true that that which was has already been, where it was true, before it came to be, that what was not would be future. To this truth God cannot be opposed, in whom is the highest and unchangeable truth itself, by which is illumined unto being whatever of the true is in anyone's soul and mind. But we do not call God omnipotent as if we should also believe that he can die; and because he cannot do this, therefore he is not to be called omnipotent. He alone is plainly and truly called omnipotent who truly is, and from whom alone is whatever in any way exists, whether spiritual or corporeal. For he uses every creature of his as it pleases him; and it pleases him, according to true and unchangeable justice, as he himself is to himself, to change all changeable things, while he himself is unchangeable, according to the merits of their natures or their deeds. Shall we then say that Elijah, being a creature, could not be changed either for the worse or for the better, or could not be changed in a way unusual to the human race according to the will of almighty God? Who, even the most foolish, would say this? Why, then, should we not believe what has been set down about him in most truthful Scripture?—unless we suppose that God can do only what we have been accustomed to see.

**6.** But if Elijah, he says, was a man and could avoid dying, why could Christ, since he was not a man, not be able to die? It is as if someone should say: If the nature of man could be changed into something better, why could the nature of God not be changed into something worse? Foolish man—because the nature of man is changeable, but God's is unchangeable. For someone equally most insane could likewise say: If he can grant to a man to reign forever, why can he not bring it about for himself that he be damned forever? I do not say this, he says, but compare at least the three-day death of God with the eternal life of man. Plainly, if you understood the three-day death of God in such a way that the flesh died in him which he assumed from the race of mortals, you would think rightly; for the gospel truth proclaims that this three-day death of Christ was wrought for the eternal life of men. But since you wish a three-day death, with no mortal creature assumed, to be believed—not absurdly, you say—to be in the divine nature itself, on the ground that human nature can be endowed with immortality, you assuredly are as senseless as one who knows neither God nor the gifts of God. Then, how is it that you do not say and hold what I set down above—that God brought it about for himself that he be damned forever, since that part of your god will be forever fixed in the lump? Or will you say this too, that a part of light is light, but a part of God is not God? Finally, that you may hear from us, without any reasoning and by the plain truth of faith, why we believe that Elijah, a man born, was caught up by God's power from the earth, but that Christ was both truly born of a virgin and truly died on the cross: we believe these things because Holy Scripture testifies both that of Elijah and this of Christ[^3], Scripture which no one pious disbelieves, and no one but the impious fails to believe. But that about Elijah you deny, because you simulate everything; while about Christ, that he could not be born and could die, not even you say; rather, you contend that there was no birth of his from a virgin, and that his death on the cross was false—that is, also no death at all, but simulated to mock human eyes, for no other purpose than that, from those who have believed these things, pardon may be granted to you also, who lie about everything.

**7.** But who would put to you the objection that Faustus puts to himself as if in the person of a Catholic—"If Jesus was not born, how did he die?"—except one who gives too little thought to the fact that Adam himself both was not born and yet died? If, therefore, the Son of God had willed to form for himself true human flesh from the same source from which he formed it for that first man—since all things were made through him[^4]—who would dare to affirm that he could not? If, finally, he willed to take a body from the heavenly or airy or watery creation and change it into the most true quality of human flesh, in which he could both live as a mortal man and die, who would deny that he could do this, the omnipotent Son of the Omnipotent? He would not dare. Lastly, if he willed to take a body from no corporeal elements, which were created through him, but to create for himself altogether from nothing true flesh, just as all things that were not were created through him—who of us would contradict, who of us would contend that it could not be done? Not, therefore, do we believe him born of the Virgin Mary because he could not otherwise exist in true flesh and appear to men; but because so it is written in that Scripture which, unless we believe it, we can be neither Christian nor saved. We believe, then, that Christ was born of the Virgin Mary, because so it is written in the Gospel; we believe that he was crucified and died, because so it is written in the Gospel; and that he was truly born and truly died, because the Gospel is truth. But why he willed to suffer all those things in flesh assumed from a woman's womb, the sum of the counsel lies with him: whether because he judged that both sexes, which he had created, were also in this way to be commended and honored, by assuming the form of a man and being born of a woman; or for some other cause, whatever it may be, I would not rashly say. Yet this I will say confidently: that it was not done otherwise than the gospel truth has taught, nor ought it to have been done otherwise than the wisdom of God judged. We set the faith of the Gospel before all the disputations of heretics; and the counsel of the wisdom of God we praise above all counsel of any creature whatever.

**8.** Yet Faustus urges us to believe him, saying: "And if you are willing to believe me when I speak the truth, both of them have drawn a false opinion among the Hebrews—Jesus about death, and Elijah about immortality"—although a little later he says: "For just as from the beginning, having taken on the likeness of a man, he simulated all the affections of the human condition, so it was not beside the point if at the end too, for the sake of sealing the dispensation, he had seemed even to die." Most wicked and most deceitful man, how am I to believe you, as one speaking the truth, when you say that Christ could lie about death? Was he, then, lying when he said: *The Son of man must be killed, and on the third day rise*[^5]; and you do not lie, and you say we should believe you as one speaking the truth? More truthful, then, was Peter, when he said to him: *God forbid, Lord! This shall not happen*; and thereby deserved to hear: *Get behind me, Satan*[^6]. And he indeed did not hear this without fruit, who afterward, corrected and perfected, preached that very truth of Christ's death even unto his own death. But if he, because he only thought that Christ would not die, deserved to hear "Satan," what do you deserve to hear, who not only deny that he died, but even say that he lied about death? But for this reason, he says, he is to be believed to have simulated death too, because he simulated all the affections of the human condition. For who will grant you this against the Gospel, that he simulated all the affections of the human condition? Indeed, if the evangelist said, *Jesus slept*[^7]; if he said he hungered[^8], thirsted[^9], was grieved[^10], was gladdened, and the like—all are true, which are so narrated that they are written down as not having been simulated by him, but plainly done or displayed, not indeed by the necessity of his condition, but by the will to teach, and also by divine power. For a man, even if he does not will it, grows angry; even if he does not will it, is grieved; even if he does not will it, sleeps; even if he does not will it, hungers and thirsts; but he did all these things because he willed. So too men are born and suffer, not because they will, nor what they will; but he did even these things because he willed. Yet these things are true and have been written down faithfully and truthfully about him, so that whoever believes his Gospel may be instructed by truth, not mocked by lies.
