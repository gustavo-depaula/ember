# Revisions II

## Chapter 7 (34): *Answer to Faustus, a Manichean*, in thirty-three books

**1.** I wrote a large work *Answer to Faustus, a Manichean*[^1], who blasphemed the Law and the Prophets and their God and the incarnation of Christ, and who said that the Scriptures of the New Testament, by which he is refuted, had been falsified. I set out his words and gave my replies to them. There are thirty-three discussions; and why should I not also call them books? For although some of them are very short, they are nonetheless books. But one of them, in which we defend the life of the patriarchs against his accusations, is of such length as almost none of my books.

**2.** In the third book, then, when I was resolving the question of how Joseph could have had two fathers[^2], I said indeed *that he was born of one and adopted by another*[^3]*;* but I ought also to have stated the kind of adoption. For what I said sounds as though some other, living father adopted him. The Law, however, adopted sons even for the dead, commanding that the brother of a man who had died without children should marry his wife and raise up offspring for his deceased brother from her. And surely a readier account is given there of one man's two fathers. Now those in whom this happened were brothers by the same mother, so that one of them, Jacob, married the wife of the other, who was named Heli and had died; and Matthew relates that Joseph was begotten of Jacob[^4]. But Jacob begot him for his brother by the same mother, whose son Luke says Joseph was[^5]—not begotten, of course, but adoptive under the Law.

This is found in the writings of those who, with the memory still fresh after the Lord's ascension, wrote on this matter. For Africanus did not pass over even the name of the same woman who bore Jacob, the father of Joseph, by her first husband Mathan—who according to Matthew was the father of Jacob, the grandfather of Joseph—and who by her later husband Melchi bore Heli, whose adoptive son Joseph was[^6]. This indeed I had not yet read when I was replying to Faustus; but even so I could not doubt that it could have happened through adoption that one man had two fathers.

**3.** In the twelfth and the thirteenth books[^7], regarding the second son of Noah, who was called Ham, the argument was conducted as though he had been cursed by his father in his own person and not, as Scripture shows, in his son Canaan[^8]*.*

In the fourteenth book such things were said about the sun and the moon as though they had perception and therefore put up with their foolish worshipers[^9], although the words there can be taken as transferred from the animate to the inanimate, in the manner of speech called in Greek *metaphor*; just as it is written of the sea: *that it roared in its mother's womb, wishing to come forth*[^10], though of course it has no will.

In the twenty-ninth book I said: *Far be it that there should be any shamefulness in the members of the saints, even the genital ones. They are indeed called dishonorable because they do not have that form of comeliness which the members placed in open view have*[^11]*.* But a more probable account was given afterward in other writings of ours[^12] as to why the Apostle too called them *dishonorable*—namely, on account of *the law in the members warring against the law of the mind*[^13], which arises from sin, not from the first constitution of our nature.

This work begins thus: *There was a certain Faustus.*
