# Book 11

**1.** Faustus said: Do you accept the Apostle?

Indeed, above all.

Why, then, do you not believe that the Son of God was born of the seed of David according to the flesh[^1]?

I would not indeed believe that the Apostle of God could have written things contrary to himself, and held now this, now that opinion about our Lord. But since it so pleases you, who never hear without indignation that anything in the Apostle has been peddled, know that not even this is contrary to us.

Indeed, this would seem to be the old and ancient opinion of Paul about Jesus, when he too thought him, like the rest, the son of David: which, however, when he learned it was false, he interpolates and weakens, and writing to the Corinthians, he says: *We know no one according to the flesh; and even if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him.* Wherefore you ought to consider how great a difference there is between these two passages, of which one declares Jesus to be the son of David according to the flesh, while the other declares that he now knows no one according to the flesh. If both of these are Paul's, either they will be so for the reason I have said, or one of them will not be Paul's.

He goes on, finally: *Therefore*, he says, *if there is in Christ a new creature, the old things have passed away: behold, all things are made new*[^2]. You see, then, that he calls that former faith old and transitory—that is, to have believed Jesus to be of the seed of David according to the flesh; but this second and abiding one new, by which he now knows no one according to the flesh. Therefore elsewhere too: *When I was a child*, he says, *I spoke as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child; but when I became a man, I put away the things of a child*[^3]. And if this is so, what then do we do that is unworthy, if, holding Paul's new and better confession, we cast off that old and worse one? Or if it is dear to your heart to believe according to what he writes to the Romans, why is it not lawful for us to teach as a doctrine according to what he writes to the Corinthians? Although I have answered even this to your hardness in this way. Otherwise, far be it that the Apostle of God should ever destroy what he built, lest he set himself up as a transgressor, as he testified[^4]. But yet, if that former opinion too is his, it has now been corrected; but if it is not right that Paul ever said anything uncorrected, then it is not his.

**2.** Augustine replied: This is what I said a little earlier: that where these men are so choked by manifest truth that, besieged by the clear words of the holy Scriptures, they can find no exit for their deceit in them, they answer that the testimony which was brought forward is false. O voice fugitive from truth, stubborn in madness! So far unconquerable are the things brought against you from the divine books, that there is nothing else for you to say, except that they have been falsified. What authority of writings can now be opened, what sacred book unrolled, what document of any scripture set forth to convict your errors, if this voice is admitted, if it is reckoned of any weight? It is one thing not to accept the books themselves, and to be held by no bond of them—as the pagans do with all our books, as the Jews do with the New Testament, as, finally, we ourselves do with yours and those of other heretics, if they have any of their own and proper to them, or with those that are called apocryphal: not because they are to be held in some secret authority, but because, declared by no light of testimony, they have been brought forth from some secret or other, by the presumption of who knows whom:

it is one thing, then, not to be held by the authority of certain books or men, and another to say: This man indeed, a holy man, wrote all true things, and this epistle is his, but in that very epistle this is his, this is not his. Where, when you hear from the other side: Prove it; you do not take refuge in the more truthful copies, or the greater number of manuscripts, or the older ones, or the preceding language from which this was translated into another language; but you say: I prove that this is his and that is not, on the ground that this sounds for me, that against me.

Are you, then, the rule of truth? Whatever is against you, is it not true? What if another, with similar insanity, but yet one by which your hardness may be shattered, should arise and say: On the contrary, that which sounds for you is false; but this which is against you is true? What will you do, unless perhaps you are going to bring forth another book, where whatever you read can be understood according to your own opinion? If you do this, you will hear one contradicting and crying out, not about some particular part of it, but about the whole: It is false. What will you do? Where will you turn? What origin of the book you bring forth, what antiquity, what series of succession will you cite as witness? For if you try to do this, you will avail nothing: and you see in this matter what the authority of the Catholic Church avails, which is confirmed from the most firmly founded sees of the Apostles down to the present day by the series of bishops succeeding one another, and by the consent of so many peoples.

And so, if a question were raised about the trustworthiness of the copies, as in some few varieties of readings, well known to those zealous for the sacred writings, our doubt would be settled either from the manuscripts of other regions, whence the very teaching came over, or, if the manuscripts there too varied, the more would be preferred to the fewer, or the older to the more recent; and if the variety were still uncertain, the preceding language, from which it was translated, would be consulted. In this way those inquire who wish to find what disturbs them in the holy Scriptures, confirmed by so great authority, so that they may have whence to be instructed, not whence to quarrel.

**3.** But this, which is brought forth against your impiety from the Epistle of the apostle Paul—that the Son of God is of the seed of David according to the flesh[^5]—all the manuscripts, both new and old, have, all the Churches read, all the languages agree on. Strip off, then, the cloak of deceit, clothed in which Faustus, when he had set himself, as it were, asking and saying, "Do you accept the apostle Paul?" replied, "Indeed, above all." For why did he not rather reply, "Indeed, not at all"; except that, being deceitful, he could not answer anything other than what was false? For what does he accept of the apostle Paul? Not the first man, whom Paul calls of the earth, earthy; of whom he likewise says: *The first man Adam was made into a living soul*[^6]. But that other announces some first man or other, neither of the earth, earthy, nor made into a living soul, but of the substance of God, existing as the very thing that God is, who sank his own members, or his garments, or his arms, that is, the five elements—when these too were nothing other than the substance of God—into the race of darkness, that they might, defiled, be captured. Not the second man, whom Paul calls from heaven[^7], whom he also calls the last Adam made into a life-giving spirit[^8], whom he calls made of the seed of David according to the flesh, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law[^9]; of whom he says to Timothy: *Remember that Jesus Christ rose from the dead, of the seed of David, according to my Gospel*[^10]; in whose example he also preaches our resurrection, saying: *For I delivered to you first of all what I also received: that Christ died for our sins, according to the Scriptures*[^11]; and that he was buried, and that he rose on the third day according to the Scriptures. And a little later he brings in why he said this: *But if Christ is preached as risen from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?*[^12]

But this man, who, asked whether he accepts the apostle Paul, replies, "Indeed, above all," denies all these things: he refuses to accept either that Jesus is of the seed of David, or that he was made of a woman—whom Paul calls a "woman," not because she was corrupted by intercourse or childbearing, but speaking after the manner of the Scriptures, which were accustomed to call the sex itself by that word: as in Genesis it is written of Eve: *He formed her into a woman*[^13], when she had not been mixed with a man. Nor does he accept the very death of Christ, and his burial, and his resurrection; since he says that Christ neither had a mortal body, in which that death would be true, nor that those scars were true, which he showed to the disciples after the resurrection, when he appeared alive to them, which Paul also recalls[^14]:

nor that this very flesh of ours will rise, changed into a spiritual body, as the same Apostle says most plainly: *It is sown a natural body, it rises a spiritual body.* Hence, distinguishing between the natural body and the spiritual, he wove in what I have already recalled about the first Adam and the last Adam.

Then he brought in: *But this I say, brethren, that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God.*

And lest anyone should believe that the very form of the flesh, and its very substance, cannot rise, wishing to express what he had now called flesh and blood—since he wished corruption itself to be understood, which then, in the resurrection of the righteous, will not be—he at once wove in: *Nor will corruption possess incorruption*[^15]. And lest anyone should still think that not that which was buried will rise, but that, as it were, one tunic is laid aside and another better one is received, wishing to declare most plainly that this very thing will be changed for the better—just as Christ's garments on the mountain were not laid aside and others taken up, but the very ones that had been there were glorified into something better[^16]—he goes on and weaves in, saying: *Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise, but we shall not all be changed.* And lest it should still be uncertain who they are who will be changed: *In a moment*, he says, *in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet: for the trumpet will sound, and the dead will rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed*[^17].

Still perhaps they may say that we are changed in the resurrection not according to this mortal and corruptible body, but according to the soul: although the Apostle did not propose this as the thing he was speaking of, but spoke of the body from the very beginning of his question, as his proposition itself shows: *But someone will say*, he says, *How do the dead rise? And with what body do they come?* Here now, consequently, he points out, as if with a finger, what he was speaking of, and adds, saying: *For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^18].

Since, then, this man denies these things, and since he preaches that very God to be corruptible, of whom Paul says: *To the immortal, incorruptible, only God be honor and glory, Amen*[^19]—since he dreams that detestable and abominable fable of theirs, that God's substance and nature both feared utterly to be corrupted by the race of darkness, and, that provision might be made for the rest, was in part corrupted—what is it that he tries in this too to deceive the unskilled, and those less learned in the divine Scriptures, so that, asked whether he accepts the apostle Paul, he replies, "Above all," and is found to accept him not at all?

**4.** But there is, he says, a sure reasoning by which I will show that the apostle Paul either, by progressing, changed his opinion, and, writing to the Corinthians, corrected what he had written to the Romans; or did not write at all what is brought forward as his—that the Son of God is of the seed of David according to the flesh. By what reasoning, after all, does he show this? Because, he says, both cannot be true: both what is in the Epistle to the Romans, *Concerning his Son, who was made of the seed of David according to the flesh*[^20]; and what he says to the Corinthians, *Therefore from now on we know no one according to the flesh; and even if we had known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him*[^21]. It remains, then, for us to show how both can be true, and how these two are not contrary to each other. For that one of them is not Paul's we can in no way say, since no authority of the manuscripts varies in it. For although in certain Latin copies one reads not "was made," but "was born of the seed of David"—when the Greek has "was made," whence the Latin translator wished to render not word for word, but according to the sense, by saying "born"—yet that Christ is of the seed of David according to the flesh, the authority of all books and languages agrees on. But that Paul ever erred in this, and by progressing changed his opinion, far be it that we should say. Faustus himself perceived how shamelessly and impiously this would be said; and he preferred to say that Paul's Epistle had been corrupted by the falsification of others, rather than vitiated by his own error.

**5.** For of these books it can be said that they have something not in accord, perhaps, with a more hidden truth that is more difficult to discern—something that has either remained so, or has been corrected in later writings: these books which are written by us not with the authority of commanding, but with the exercise of progressing. For we are among those to whom the same Apostle says: *And if you savor anything otherwise, this too God will reveal to you*[^22]. This kind of writing is to be read not with the necessity of believing, but with the liberty of judging. Yet, lest the place for it be closed off, and the most wholesome labor of tongue and pen for handling and turning over difficult questions be taken from those who come after,

there is distinguished, from the books of later writers, the excellence of the canonical authority of the Old and New Testament, which, confirmed in the times of the Apostles, has, through the successions of bishops and the propagations of the Churches, been established loftily as on a kind of throne, which every faithful and pious understanding ought to serve. There, if anything strikes one as absurd, it is not lawful to say: The author of this book did not hold to the truth; but: Either the manuscript is faulty, or the translator erred, or you do not understand. But in the works of later writers, which are contained in innumerable books, but are in no way to be equated with that most sacred excellence of the canonical Scriptures—even where the same truth is found in any of them, the authority is nonetheless far unequal.

And so in these, if perhaps some things are thought to be discordant with the truth, because they are not understood as they were said, the reader or hearer nonetheless has there a free judgment, by which he may either approve what has pleased him, or disapprove what has offended him: and therefore all things of this kind, unless they are defended either by sure reason or by that canonical authority, so that it may be shown either that the thing argued or narrated there is so altogether, or could have been so—if anyone is displeased by it, or is unwilling to believe it, he is not reproved.

But in that canonical eminence of the sacred writings, even if one prophet, or apostle, or evangelist is declared, by the very confirmation of the canon, to have set something in his writings, it is not lawful to doubt that it is true: otherwise there will be no page by which the weakness of human ignorance may be governed, if the most wholesome authority of the canonical books is either utterly abolished by being despised, or thrown into confusion by being made unbounded.

**6.** Therefore, whoever you are who have been disturbed by these things as though they were contradictory—that it is written in one place, *The Son of God of the seed of David*[^23], and in another, *And even if we had known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him*[^24]—even if both were not brought forth from the writings of one Apostle, but one of them had been said by Paul, the other by Peter, or Isaiah, or any other of the Apostles or Prophets: since all things so agree with one another in canonical authority that, as though said with one mouth, they are believed with the most just and most prudent piety, and found with the most serene understanding, and demonstrated with the most skillful diligence,

it would not be lawful to doubt about either. Accordingly, since both are brought forth from the canonical Epistles of the apostle Paul, that is, the truly Pauline Epistles, and we cannot say either that the manuscript is faulty—for all the corrected Latin copies have it thus—or that the translator erred—for all the corrected Greek copies have it thus—it remains that you do not understand, but that the reasoning be demanded of me, how neither is discordant with the other, but both agree in the same rule of sound faith. For if you too inquired piously, you could find how these things, once searched out, become clear.

**7.** For that the Son of God was made man of the seed of David, the same Apostle says in more than one place, and the other holy Scriptures preach most plainly. But as for what he says, *And even if we had known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him*[^25], the very circumstance of the Scriptural passage shows sufficiently what the Apostle is saying. For after his manner he meditates on our future life—which is already completed in the very man, the mediator Christ Jesus, our head, in his rising—as though it were already present, and held as present: which life surely will not be according to the flesh, just as Christ's life is now not according to the flesh. For by "flesh" in this passage he wishes to be understood, not the very substance of our body, which the Lord even after his resurrection calls flesh, saying: *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have bones and flesh, as you see me to have*[^26], but the corruption and mortality of the flesh, which then will not be in us, just as it is now not in Christ. For he properly named this "flesh," even when he was speaking quite evidently of the resurrection itself, and said, what I have already recalled above: *Flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God, nor will corruption possess incorruption.* When, therefore, that shall have come to pass which he says consequently there: *Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise, but we shall not all be changed. In a moment*, that is, in an instant, *in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet; for the trumpet will sound, and the dead will rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed: for this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^27]: then, in respect of that which he calls "flesh"—not the very substance of the body, but the very corruption of mortality—it will surely not be flesh, because, changed, it will not have the corruption of mortality; but in respect of the origin of its very substance and body it will be the same flesh, because that very flesh will rise and that very flesh will be changed,

because that too is true which the Lord said after he rose: *Handle and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see me to have*; and this is true which the Apostle says: *Flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God.* For the former was said in respect of the very substance, which will exist even then, because it itself will be changed; but the latter was said in respect of the carnal quality of corruption, which then will no longer be, because the flesh, once changed, will not be corrupted. We had known Christ, then, according to the flesh, that is, according to the mortality of the flesh, before he rose: *but now we no longer know him*; because, as the same Apostle says: *Christ, rising from the dead, dies now no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^28].

**7.** For if you hold yourself to the strict propriety of words, he lied when he said: *We had known Christ according to the flesh*, if Christ was never according to the flesh: for no one had known what did not exist. But he did not say: We had supposed Christ to be according to the flesh; but: We had known. Yet, not to press the word, lest anyone affirm that the Apostle spoke by a figure of speech, so that for "we had supposed" he said "we had known," I wonder that blind men do not attend to this—or rather I do not wonder that the blind do not see—that if Christ therefore had no flesh, because the Apostle said that he now no longer knows Christ according to the flesh, then neither did those have flesh of whom in the same passage he says: *Therefore from now on we know no one according to the flesh*[^29].

For if he wished this to be understood of Christ alone, he could not say: We know no one according to the flesh; but because he was meditating on the future life, as though present, of those who will be changed when they rise together with himself, he says: *From now on we know no one according to the flesh*; that is, we hold so sure a hope of our future incorruption and immortality that from now on we already rejoice in that very knowledge. Hence in another place he says: *But if you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God; savor the things that are above, not the things that are on earth*[^30]. Surely we have not yet risen as Christ has; but yet, according to the hope which is ours in him, he testified that we have already risen with him. Hence he also says: *According to his mercy he saved us through the washing of regeneration*[^31]. But who would not understand that in the washing of regeneration the hope of future salvation was given us, not now the salvation itself, which is promised? And yet, because it is a sure hope, as though that same salvation had already been given, he says, *he saved us*.

For in another place he says most lucidly: *We ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption, the redemption of our body. For in hope we have been saved. But hope that is seen is not hope; for what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we await it through patience*[^32]. Therefore, just as here he did not say: We shall be saved; but: From now on we have already been saved—not yet in fact, however, but in hope: *For in hope*, he says, *we have been saved*—so too what was said there: *From now on we know no one according to the flesh*, is understood not yet in fact, but in hope: because our hope is in Christ, because in him there is already completed what we hope is promised to us. For he has already risen, and death will no longer have dominion over him. And even if we had known him according to the flesh, when he was still to die—for there was in his body that mortality which the Apostle properly calls "flesh"—*yet now we no longer know him*; for that mortal part of him has already put on immortality, so that according to his former mortality he can no longer be called flesh.

**8.** And so let us consider the context of the very passage where this sentence is, about which these men raise their slander, so that what I say may shine forth more. *The love of Christ*, he says, *constrains us; judging this, that if one died for all, then all died: and he died for all, that those who live may now live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and rose again for them. Therefore from now on we know no one according to the flesh; and even if we had known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him.* Surely now it is apparent to anyone that the Apostle said this on account of the resurrection of Christ; since these words were preceded by that: *That those who live may now live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and rose again for them.* For what else is it, to live not for themselves but for him, than to live not according to the flesh in the hope of earthly and corruptible goods, but according to the spirit in the hope of the resurrection, which has already taken place from among them in Christ? And so of those for whom Christ died and rose again, and who now live not for themselves but for him, the Apostle knew no one according to the flesh, on account of the hope of future immortality, in the expectation of which they were living,

which in Christ was now not hope but fact: and even if he had known him according to the flesh, when he was still to die, yet now he no longer knew him; because he knew that he had risen, and that death would no longer have dominion over him. And because we all are this in him, even if not yet in fact, yet now in hope, he goes on and says: *If, then, there is in Christ any new creature, the old things have passed away: behold, all things are made new. But all things are from God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ*[^33].

Every new creature, then, that is, the people renewed through faith, that it may have for the time in hope what afterward is to be completed in fact, has already in Christ what it hopes for in itself. And so now the old things have passed away according to hope, because it is now no longer the time of the Old Testament, in which a temporal and carnal kingdom is awaited from God; and all things are made new according to that same hope, that we may hold the promised kingdom of heaven, where there will be no death and no corruption.

But in the resurrection of the dead, now not according to hope but according to fact, both the old things will pass away, when the last enemy, death, is destroyed; and all things will be made new, when this corruptible has put on incorruption, and this mortal has put on immortality[^34]: which has already taken place in Christ, whom, according to fact, Paul now no longer knew according to the flesh; but of those for whom he died and rose again, not yet according to fact, but according to hope, he knew no one according to the flesh; because by his grace, as the same Apostle says to the Ephesians, we have been saved.

For the passage itself bears witness to this sentence: *But God*, he says, *who is rich in mercy, on account of the great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, made us alive together with Christ, by whose grace we have been saved*[^35]. For what he said here, *made us alive together with Christ*, this he said to the Corinthians: *That those who live may now live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and rose again for them.* And what he says here, *By whose grace we have been saved*, he speaks of as already perfected, which he holds in hope. For, as I recalled a little earlier, he says most plainly elsewhere: *For in hope we have been saved.* And therefore here too he goes on and recounts as perfected what is to come: *And he raised us up together*, he says, *and made us sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus.* For surely Christ is already seated in the heavenly places, but not yet we: but because in sure hope we already hold what is to come, he said that we sit together in the heavenly places, not yet in ourselves, but already in him. And indeed, lest you should think it is now already perfected, what is said in hope as though it were perfected, and that you may understand it is still to come, he goes on and says: *That he might show in the ages to come the surpassing riches of his grace, in his kindness toward us in Christ Jesus*[^36].

Hence this too: *For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins, which are through the Law, worked in our members, to bring forth fruit unto death*[^37]. For he said thus, *When we were in the flesh*, as though they were now no longer in the flesh: which is understood thus—when we were in the hope of carnal things; at the time when the Law, which cannot be fulfilled except through spiritual love, was over them to this end, that through transgression the offense might abound; so that afterward, with the New Testament revealed, through indulgence, grace might superabound[^38].

Hence likewise he says elsewhere: *But those who are in the flesh cannot please God.* And lest anyone should think that those who are not yet dead were meant, he at once added: *But you are not in the flesh, but in the spirit*[^39]. That is, those who are in the hope of carnal goods cannot please God; but you are not in the hope of carnal things, but in the hope of spiritual things, that is, of the kingdom of heaven, where the body itself, through that change, will be in a certain manner of its own spiritual, which now is natural. *For a natural body is sown*, as he says likewise to the Corinthians, *a spiritual body will rise*[^40].

If, then, the Apostle now no longer knew according to the flesh any of those who were said to be not in the flesh because they were not in the hope of carnal things, although they still bore corruptible and mortal flesh, how much more expressly would he say of Christ that he now no longer knew him according to the flesh, in whose body even in fact there was now completed what those men held promised in hope?

How much better, then, how much more religiously the divine Scriptures are handled, so that, all things being examined, they are found to agree, than that, when a man fails in a question he cannot solve, they should be accepted in part as true and condemned in part as false? For even though the Apostle was a child, and savored the things that were a child's[^41]—although he said this by way of a likeness—he was nonetheless not yet spiritual, such as he already was when he wrote the things which were to be turned over, for the building up of the Churches, not in the hands of students with the exercise of progressing, but to be read in the ecclesiastical canon with the authority of commanding.
