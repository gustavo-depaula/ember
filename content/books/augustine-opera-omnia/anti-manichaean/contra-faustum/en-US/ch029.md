# Book XXVIII

**1.** FAUSTUS: But he could not die unless he had been born. And I answer: Nor could he be born unless he were not God; or if he could both be God and be born, why could he not both not be born and die? You see, then, that it is not useful enough to seek a consequence in these matters, or to lean on arguments, when one deals with things that pertain to Jesus. Rather, it must be asked what he himself proclaimed about himself, and what his Apostles proclaimed about the same. And the genealogy itself must be examined, and it must be seen whether it agrees with itself; not to seek out the truth of his birth from conjecture about his passion—because he could both suffer though not born, and being born not suffer at all; especially since you yourselves confess that nothing is impossible to God. And this too will be false, if it stands that one not born could not die.

**2.** AUGUSTINE: Again and often you put to yourself what you do not hear from those by whom you are confuted. No one says to you: He could not die unless he had been born—because Adam died, although he had not been born. But it is said to you: He was born, because the holy Gospel, not some heretic or other, says this; he died, because this is read in the holy Gospel, not in the book of some heretic. But you who forbid arguing when one deals with things that pertain to Jesus, and judge that it must be asked what he himself proclaimed about himself, and what his Apostles proclaimed about the same—when I begin to recite the Gospel of his apostle Matthew, where the whole narrative of his birth is woven together, you will at once say that that narrative is not Matthew's, which the whole Church says is Matthew's, brought down by a sure succession from the apostolic sees to the present bishops. What will you read to me against it? Some book of Mani, perhaps, where Jesus is denied to have been born of a virgin. Therefore, just as I believe that book to be Mani's, because from the very time when Mani lived in the flesh, through his disciples by a sure succession of your overseers it has been kept and brought down even to your times; so believe you that this book is Matthew's, which from the time when Matthew himself lived in the flesh, by an unbroken series of times, the Church has brought down by a sure succession of connection even to these times. And tell me, whose book ought we rather to believe: that of the apostle who clung to Christ while he was still on earth, or that of some Persian or other who was born so long afterward? But perhaps you will bring forward another book, which bears the name of some apostle whom it is agreed Christ chose, and there you will read that Christ was not born of Mary. Since, then, one of these books must be lying, to which do you think we ought rather to grant our trust? To the one which that Church—begun from Christ himself, carried forward through the Apostles by a sure series of successions even to these times, spread throughout the whole world—acknowledges and approves as handed down and preserved from the beginning; or to the one which the same Church rejects as unknown, especially when it is brought forward by men so truthful that they praise Christ for having lied?

**3.** Here you will say: Let the genealogy itself of the two books of the Gospel be considered, whether it agrees with itself. We have already said elsewhere in this work what had to be said. For nothing moves you except the question of how Joseph could have had two fathers. But if it had not occurred to you, as you thought it over, that there was one who begot and another who adopted—even so you ought not so easily to have brought forth a hasty verdict against so great an authority. But now, if even when admonished you consider how this could have come about, believe the Gospel simply, and cease rather to argue so badly and perversely.

**4.** But as for what Faustus thinks must be asked—what Jesus himself proclaimed about himself—to whom would it not seem just? But can this be known except by his disciples narrating it? And if they are not believed when they announce that he was born of a virgin, how will they be trusted when they announce what he proclaimed about himself? For if some writings were produced which, with no one else narrating, were said to be properly Christ's own, how could it come about that, if they were truly his, they would not be read, would not be received, would not stand out at the highest peak of authority in his Church—which is propagated and spread from him, through the Apostles, by bishops succeeding one another, even to these times; many things now fulfilled in it which were foretold before, and the things that remain unto the end without doubt to be and to come? For if those writings too were brought forth, surely it would have to be considered by whom they were brought forth. If by him, they could without doubt have been brought forth first by those who then clung to him, and through them have come even to others. And if this had been done, through those successions of overseers and peoples which I have mentioned they would shine with most well-established authority. Who, then, is so mad as to believe today that there is a letter of Christ which Mani has brought forth, and not to believe that the things Matthew wrote were done or said by Christ? Or if he doubts even of Matthew, whether he himself wrote these things, why should he not rather believe of Matthew himself what he finds in the Church, which is declared from the times of Matthew himself even to this time by a sure series of successions; and not believe some man or other coming sideways from Persia after two hundred years or more, and urging that it should rather be believed of him what Christ said and did?—since the apostle Paul himself, after the Lord's ascension, called from heaven[^1], if he had not found in the flesh Apostles, by communicating with whom and by conferring with whom it would appear that the Gospel belonged to the same fellowship, the Church would not have believed him at all. But when it had recognized him as announcing this, which they too announced, and as living in their communion and unity, with such signs coming about through him as they too worked, so, with the Lord commending him, he deserved such authority that his words are heard today in the Church as though, in him, Christ, as he himself most truly said, were heard speaking[^2]. And does Mani think that he ought to be believed by the Church of Christ, speaking against the Scriptures confirmed by so great and so ordered an authority?—Scriptures by which it is especially commended to it that whoever announces to it anything beyond what it has received should be anathema[^3].

**5.** "But I offer," he says, "a reason by which I may show that those Scriptures are not to be believed." Surely you are not arguing? And yet even in your very argumentation you are overcome. For all your argumentation is reduced to this: that in the end the soul should believe that it is wretched in this world because by its wretchedness it came to the aid of its God, lest he be deprived of his kingdom; and that its nature and substance are so far changeable, corruptible, violable, and defilable, that a certain part of it cannot even be cleansed, and is punished by the eternal torment of the lump by the very one who, knowing it to be innocent, drawn from his own bowels and sinning in nothing against him, mingled it with so great a defilement. This is the end of all your argumentations and fables; would that there were an end of them—but in your heart and on your lips, so that at some time you might cease to believe and speak such execrable blasphemies! "But from those very writings," he says, "I prove that they are not everywhere to be believed, since they speak things contrary to themselves." Why, then, do you not rather say that they are nowhere to be believed, as inconstant witnesses attacking themselves? "But this," he says, "I choose, which I see to be consonant with the truth." With what truth? With your fable, of course, which has at its head the war of God, in its middle the defilement of God, at its end the damnation of God. "And nowhere," he says, "are writings believed that are at variance and contrary to themselves." But it seems so to you for this reason, because you do not understand; for whatever you have brought forth that seemed such, it has been shown how you do not understand it; and whatever you shall bring forth, it will be shown. There is, then, no cause why we should not believe those Scriptures endowed with so great an authority; and plainly this is the greatest cause why we should anathematize those who announce something else.

[^1]: See Acts 9.
[^2]: See 2 Cor 13:3.
[^3]: See Gal 1:8–9.
