# Book 6

**1.** Faustus said: Do you accept the Old Testament?

How, when I do not keep its precepts? Indeed, I suppose you do not either: for I spat out circumcision as shameful; and, if I am not mistaken, so did you. The cessation of work on the sabbaths, as superfluous; I believe you did too. The sacrifices, as idolatry; I do not doubt you did too. As for pork, I am certainly not the only one who abstains; and you for your part do not eat it alone. I, indeed, because I reckon all flesh unclean; but you, because nothing is unclean—by both of which, by each of us, the Old Testament is destroyed. The weeks of eating unleavened bread, the Feast of Booths, each of us despised as useless and vain. Not to insert purple into linen garments. To count it among adulteries to put on a garment of mixed linen and wool. To count it among sacrileges, if need be, to yoke an ox and an ass together. Not to appoint as priest a bald man or one with stiff bristling hair or a man of similar mark, because such are unclean before God—both of us despised these, laughed at them, held them neither in second place nor in first: all of which are precepts and ordinances of the Old Testament.

What you charge me with, then, is common to you with me, whether it is to be reckoned a crime or rightly done; for each of us rejects the Old Testament. So if you ask what difference there is between my faith and yours, it is this: that it pleases you to lie, and to act in an ignoble way, so that what you hate in mind you praise in word; whereas I have not learned to deceive, I say what I think, I confess that I hate the teachers of such base things as much as the precepts themselves.

**2.** Augustine replied: How and why the Old Testament is accepted by the heirs of the New Testament we have already said above. But since a little earlier Faustus dealt with its promises, and now wished to deal with its precepts, I answer that these men are wholly ignorant of the difference between precepts for living life and precepts for signifying life. For example: *You shall not covet*[^1] is a precept for living life; *You shall circumcise every male on the eighth day*[^2] is a precept for signifying life.

For from this lack of skill the Manicheans, and all to whom the writings of the Old Testament are displeasing, not understanding that whatever God commanded the earlier people to observe was a shadow of things to come, and noting that these things are not now observed, blame, after the manner of the present time, those things which were certainly suited to that time, in which the things now made manifest were signified as to come.

But what will they say against the Apostle, who says: *All these things happened to them in figure; but they were written for our sake, upon whom the end of the ages has come*[^3]? Behold, he himself has shown why those writings are accepted by us, and why it is no longer necessary that those signs of things be observed by us. For when he says, *They were written for our sake*, he shows beyond doubt with how great care they must be read and understood by us, and in how great authority held, since they were written for our sake. But when he says, *They were figures of us*, and, *They happened to them in figure*[^4], he shows that there is no longer need, when we are dealing with the things themselves now made manifest, to serve the celebration of the foretelling figures. Hence he says in another place: *Let no one, then, judge you in food, or in drink, or in respect of a feast day, or a new moon, or sabbaths, which are a shadow of things to come*[^5]. Here too, when he says, *Let no one judge you in these things*, he declares how unnecessary it now is that these be observed; but when he says, *which are a shadow of things to come*, he shows how fitting it was that they be observed at that time, in which the things that have now shone forth manifest to us were foretold as to come, through such shadows of figures.

**3.** Accordingly, if the Manicheans were justified by the resurrection of the Lord—whose day of resurrection was, from the day of his passion indeed, the third, but after the sabbath day, that is, after the seventh, the eighth—they would surely be stripped of the carnal veil of mortal desires; and rejoicing in the circumcision of the heart, they would not deride it as foreshadowed and figured in the flesh in the time of the Old Testament, although in the time of the New Testament they would not compel it to be done and observed. For in what member is the stripping away of carnal and mortal concupiscence more fittingly figured than where the carnal and mortal offspring arises?

But, as the Apostle says: *All things are clean to the clean; but to the unclean and unbelieving nothing is clean, but both their mind and their conscience are polluted*[^6]. And so these men, who seem all too clean to themselves, because they turn away from those members as unclean, or pretend to turn away, have fallen into such uncleannesses of unbelief and error that, while they detest the circumcision of the flesh, which the Apostle called a seal of the righteousness of faith[^7], they yet believe that the divine members of their God are held bound and defiled in those very carnal members; so that, when they call the flesh unclean, they are compelled to say that God too, in the part by which he is held there, has been made unclean—since they assert that he needs to be cleansed: until which is done, as far as it can be done, they say he meanwhile now suffers all the things that flesh suffers, not only in the labor and pain of afflictions, but also in the pleasure of corruptions.

For they say they spare him by not having intercourse, lest he be more tightly entangled in fleshly knots and more filthily defiled. Since, then, the Apostle says, *All things are clean to the clean*—to men, of course, who can be changed for the worse by perversity of will—how much more are all things clean to God, who always remains unchangeable and incapable of contamination? Of whose divine wisdom it is said in those books which you defile yourselves all the more vehemently by reproaching, that nothing defiled enters into it, and that it reaches everywhere because of its cleanness[^8]. Therefore, O most unclean vanity, does it so displease you that God, to whom all things are clean, commanded the sign of human regeneration to be set in the human member by which the human race is propagated; and yet it pleases you that even in the shameful deeds which are perpetrated with that member by lewd men, your very God, to whom nothing is clean, is in part of his nature stained and corrupted? For what does he suffer in various base corruptions, whom you believe to be defiled even by conjugal intercourse?

To that too which you are wont to say—"Was there, then, no place for God to prefigure the seal of the righteousness of faith except in that member?"—it is answered: Why not there too? First, because all things are clean to the clean, how much more to God? Then, because the Apostle said that this seal of the righteousness of faith was given to Abraham in such circumcision. But do not you blush, if you can, when it is said to you: Was there, then, no place for your God to act, that he should not entangle part of his nature in those members which you so spit upon? These members are indeed called shameful by men on account of the corruptible and penal propagation of our mortality which arises from them: to which the chaste apply modesty, the lewd wantonness, God righteousness.

**4.** The cessation of work on the sabbaths we now indeed reckon superfluous to observe, ever since the hope of our eternal rest was revealed; yet not superfluous to read and understand. For since, in prophetic times, the things which have been laid open to us in this present time had to be prefigured and foretold not only by words but also by deeds, by that sign which we read, this thing which we hold was foreshadowed. But I want you to tell me why you are unwilling to fulfill your own cessation. For the Jews, on their sabbath, which they still savor carnally, not only pick no fruit in the field, but neither cut nor cook in the house. But you, while ceasing, wait for one of your Hearers, for your sake, to dash out into the garden armed with a little knife or sickle, a murderer of gourds, to bring you—wondrous to say—living corpses of them. For if he does not kill them, what do you fear in such a deed? But if they are killed when they are picked, how is there life in them, which you assert you help to purify and renew by eating and belching? You receive, then, living gourds, which, if you could, you ought to swallow whole; so that, after that one wound, in which your Hearer became guilty when he picked them, needing your indulgence to be freed, they might at least thereafter reach intact and whole the workshop of your belly, where you could reform your God shattered in that battle. But as it is, before your teeth fall upon grinding them, they are cut up by you piece by piece, if it pleases the palate so: by such frequent wounds of theirs, how are you not guilty?

See how it would profit you to do daily what the Jews do once in seven days, and to cease even from such household work. Again, what do the gourds suffer in the fire, where surely the life that is in them is not reformed? For a boiling pot cannot be compared to a holy belly; and yet you mock the cessation on the sabbath as superfluous. How much more sound it would be not only not to blame it in the Fathers, when it was not superfluous, but even now, when it is already superfluous, to keep it yourselves, rather than this practice of yours which is not acceptable in its signification but damnable in its error—by not fulfilling which you are guilty according to the opinion of your own vanity, and by fulfilling which you are vain according to the judgment of truth? For you say that fruit feels pain when it is plucked from the tree, feels it when it is cut, when it is ground, when it is cooked, when it is eaten. You ought, then, to feed only on those things which can be swallowed raw and unharmed, so that they might suffer at least the one pain, when they are plucked, not by you but by your Hearers.

4.2. But you say: How do we help so great a life, if we consume only those things which can be swallowed uncooked and soft? If, then, by this compensation you inflict so many pains on your foods, why do you abstain from that one alone to which this necessity most of all compels you? For fruit can be eaten raw too, as some of you have trained yourselves to do, so as to do this not only with fruits but even with all vegetables. But unless it is plucked, or cut down, or in some way taken from the earth or the tree, it can in no way come to be eaten. This, then, ought to have been easily pardonable, without which you could not help him—not those many torments which you do not hesitate to inflict on the members of your God in preparing food.

But the tree weeps, you say, when its fruit is picked: for this you are not ashamed to say. Surely all the life that is there knows and senses beforehand who comes to it. When the Elect come, then, and pick the fruit, it ought to rejoice, not weep, compensating that passing pain with such felicity, and escaping such great misery as it would suffer if it fell to others. Why, then, do you not pluck the fruit, when by plucking it you inflict so many blows and pains? Answer, if you can. Nor do your very fasts suit you: for the furnace ought not to be idle, in which the spiritual gold is purified from its mixture with dung, and the divine members are loosed from their pitiable bonds. Therefore he is the more merciful among you who can so train himself that, with no harm to his health, he often takes raw foods and consumes much. But you both eat cruelly, by inflicting such penalties on your food, and fast cruelly, by ceasing from the purification of the divine members.

**5.** And yet you dare even to execrate the sacrifices of the Old Testament, and to name them idolatry, and to associate us too in such sacrilege. Whence, on our behalf, we answer first that they are no longer in our works, yet we embrace them in the mysteries of the divine Scriptures, for understanding the things foretold by them; because they too were figures of us, and all such things, in many and various ways, signified the one sacrifice whose memory we now celebrate. Hence, once this was revealed and offered in its own time, those things were removed from the celebration of doing, but remained in the authority of signifying. For they were written for our sake, upon whom the end of the ages has come[^9].

But in them, evidently, the killing of animals disturbs you, when this whole creation in a way serves the uses of men conditionally. But you, who do not hold out bread to a hungry beggar, are merciful to cattle, in which you believe human souls reside. The Lord Jesus, however, was in your view cruel, when he permitted the demons to go into the herd of swine, when they asked it of him[^10]. He too, even before the sacrifice of his body was shown forth through the passion, said to a certain leper whom he had cleansed: *Go, show yourself to the priest, and offer your gift, which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them*[^11]. For rather, since God often testifies through the Prophets that he needs no such gift, and since it is easy to perceive by reason that he, who needs nothing, has need of no such thing, the human mind is compelled to inquire what he wished to teach us through these things—he who surely would not have commanded that things he does not need be offered to him for nothing, unless he were showing in them something which it would profit us to know and which had to be prefigured by such signs.

But how much better and more honorable it would be for you to be subject to these sacrifices—though no longer necessary for our time, yet signifying and teaching something—than to command and believe that living victims of food are offered to you by your Hearers! For when the apostle Paul said most fittingly of certain men who preach the Gospel for the sake of banquets: *Whose God is the belly*[^12], with how much more arrogant impiety do you boast, who are not afraid to call your belly not God, but—what is of more wicked audacity—the cleanser of God? Whose madness, moreover, is it to wish to seem pious because they abstain from the killing of animals, when they say that all their foods have those same souls; on which, living, as they think, they inflict so many wounds with hands and teeth?

**6.** But why, if you are unwilling to eat flesh, do you not slaughter the animals themselves as offered to your God, so that those souls, which you think not only human but so divine that you believe them to be the very members of God, may be released from the prison of the flesh, and be commended by your prayers not to return again? Or do you help them more by the belly than by the mind, and is that nature of God rather saved which has deserved to be breathed out through your bowels, than that which is commended by your prayers? Therefore you do not sacrifice cattle to your belly for this reason: because you cannot consume them alive, so as to free their souls by the intercession of your stomach.

O blessed vegetables, which, though plucked by hand, and cut by iron, and tormented by fire, and crushed by the tooth, are nonetheless permitted to arrive alive at the altars of your intestines! And O wretched cattle, which, since they leave their own body more quickly, cannot enter into your bodies! So, still raving, you think we are enemies of the Old Testament because we reckon no flesh unclean, holding the sentence of the Apostle who says: *All things are clean to the clean*[^13]; and that where the Lord says: *Not what enters your mouth defiles you, but what comes out*[^14].

This the Lord did not say to the crowds alone, as your Adimantus—whom Faustus praises above all after Mani—wished it to be understood when he slandered the Old Testament; but, withdrawn from the crowds as well, he spoke this same thing to his disciples more evidently and expressly. For when Adimantus had set this sentence of the Lord against the Old Testament, because in it certain unclean things of flesh are written, from which that people was commanded to abstain, he feared it would be said to him: Why, then, do you reckon not some but all flesh unclean, and refrain from eating any, when you yourself adduce the evangelical testimony that a man is not defiled by the things which enter into the mouth, and pass into the belly, and are cast into the privy? And so, attempting to escape these most narrow straits, which choke his deceit by most manifest truth, he says that the Lord said this to the crowds—as though he spoke true things to the few in secret, but cast false things to the crowds: when both to believe this of the Lord is sacrilegious, and all who read know that he said this more plainly to his disciples even when withdrawn from the crowds.

Therefore, since at the beginning of these writings of his Faustus so admires Adimantus that he prefers to him only Mani, I ask in brief whether this sentence of the Lord, by which he said that a man is not defiled by the things that enter his mouth, is true or false. If they say it is false, why did so great a teacher of theirs, Adimantus, saying that it was uttered by Christ, set it against the Old Testament to assail it? But if it is true, why do they believe themselves to be defiled against it, if they eat any flesh whatever? Unless perhaps they wish to answer truly, and to say that the Apostle did not say: All things are clean to heretics, but: All things are clean to the clean. Why these things are not clean to them, the same Apostle goes on to say: *But to the unclean and unbelieving nothing is clean; but both their mind and their conscience are polluted*[^15]. Hence indeed to the Manicheans nothing at all is clean, since they contend that even the very substance or nature of God could not only be defiled, but has even in part been defiled; and not only defiled, but cannot in every part be recovered and cleansed. Hence it is strange that they say they reckon all flesh unclean, and abstain from it on this account, as though they reckon something to be clean, not only of foods but of all creatures. For even the vegetables themselves, and the fruits, and all crops, and the whole earth, and heaven, they declare to be defiled by their mixture with the race of darkness. Would, then, that they agreed with their error in the other foods as well, and, by abstaining from all the things they call unclean, would rather die of hunger than stubbornly speak such blasphemies! For who would not understand that this is more useful for those unwilling to correct and amend themselves?

**7.** But to the Old Testament, where certain flesh foods are forbidden, why this sentence is not contrary, by which the Apostle says: *All things are clean to the clean*; and: *Every creature of God is good*[^16]—let them understand, if they can, that the Apostle said this of the natures themselves; while those writings, on account of certain prefigurations suited to the time, called certain animals unclean, not by nature, but by signification. And so, for example, if the question is asked about a pig and a lamb, both are clean by nature, because every creature of God is good; but by a certain signification the lamb is clean, the pig unclean: just as if you said "fool" and "wise," each of these words, in the nature of the voice and the letters and syllables of which it consists, is certainly clean; but by signification one of these words, the one called "fool," can be called unclean, not by its own nature, but because it signifies something unclean. And perhaps what the pig is in the figures of things, the fool is in the kind of things; and both that animal, and these two syllables that say "fool," signify one and the same thing.

For that animal was set in the Law as unclean, on the ground that it does not chew the cud: but this is not its fault, but its nature. There are, however, men signified by this animal, unclean by their own fault, not by nature: who, though they gladly hear the words of wisdom, afterward do not think of them at all. For to recall the useful thing you have heard, as it were from the inner part of memory, as if to the mouth of thought, by the sweetness of remembering—what else is this than in a way spiritually to chew the cud? Those who do not do this are figured by the kind of those animals. Hence the very abstinence from such flesh forewarned us to beware such a fault. For since wisdom itself is a desirable treasure, of this cleanness of chewing the cud and uncleanness of not chewing the cud it is written in another place: *A desirable treasure rests in the mouth of the wise; but the foolish man gulps it down*[^17]. These likenesses of things in figured expressions and observances usefully and pleasantly move rational minds, for the exercise of inquiring and comparing. But to the earlier people many such things were enjoined not only to be heard but also to be observed. For it was a time in which the things to be revealed in a later time had to be prophesied not only in words but also in deeds. When these were revealed through Christ and in Christ, the burdens of observances were not imposed on the faith of the nations, yet the authority of the prophecy was commended.

Behold, we have said for what cause, although according to the sentence of the Lord and the Apostle we hold no flesh of animals unclean, we yet are not opposed to the Old Testament, where some were called unclean. Now do you say why you reckon flesh unclean.

**8.** If, according to your error, it is on account of the mixture with the race of darkness, then it is not the flesh, but your very God that is unclean in that part which he sent and mingled, to be absorbed and defiled, as though for warring down and capturing the enemy; and then, on account of that very mixture, whatever else you eat is also unclean. But you say: Flesh is far more unclean. And why flesh should be more unclean, it would be long to recount their ravings on this matter; but I will touch briefly on what is enough, so that the reprovers of the Old Testament may be inspected—men so worn out by such an ancientness of folly that, as accusers of the flesh, they are convicted of savoring flesh alone, with no spiritual truth. For perhaps this somewhat longer reply will so instruct the reader against them that in the other replies so many words will not be required of us.

For these vain talkers and seducers of the mind say that in that battle, when their first man entangled the race of darkness with deceptive elements, with the princes of both sexes captured from there, when the world was being constructed from them, very many of them were bound in the celestial fabrics, among whom were also some pregnant females: who, when the heaven had begun to revolve, being unable to bear that same dizziness, shook out their conceptions by abortion; and these same aborted offspring, both male and female, fell from heaven to earth, lived, grew, had intercourse, begot. Hence, they say, is the origin of all the flesh that moves on earth, in water, in air.

Therefore, if the origin of flesh is from heaven, it is most absurd on that account to think it more unclean: especially since, in the very structure of the world, they say those same princes of darkness are so bound throughout all the joinings, from the highest down to the lowest, that the more of the good mixed in any of them had, the more loftily it deserved to be placed; and by this, flesh, whose origin is from heaven, ought to be cleaner than crops, which arise from the earth.

Then, what can be said so insane as that conceived offspring, before the mixture of life, were so much the more full of life that, both aborted and rushing from heaven to earth, they lived; whereas, with life mixed in, unless brought forth at the mature time, they cannot live, and if they fall from a slightly higher place, they die at once? Surely, if the realm of life waged war against the realm of death, the mixed-in life ought to have made them more full of life, not more corruptible. But if each thing keeps its own incorruption more in its own nature, they ought to have preached not two natures, one good and one evil, but two good ones, of which one is better.

Whence, then, do they assert that flesh is more unclean, which they affirm draws its descent from heaven—at least the flesh known to all? For they suppose that the very first bodies of the princes of darkness arose, like little worms, from the trees born there; and those trees from those five elements. Accordingly, if the bodies of animals have their first origin from trees, their second from heaven, what cause is there that they should be reckoned more unclean than the fruits of trees? If it is because, when they die, they lose their soul, so that whatever remains after life has deserted it is now unclean—why, by the same reasoning, are vegetables or fruits not unclean, which surely, as was said above, die when they are plucked or torn off? For they are unwilling to be guilty of these murders, while they pluck nothing from earth or tree.

Then, since they affirm that there are two souls in one body of a living thing, one good from the race of light, the other evil from the race of darkness—when an animal is killed, does the good soul flee and the evil remain? For if this were so, the slain animal would live as it lived in the race of darkness, when it had only the soul of its own race, by which it had even rebelled against the divine realms. Since, then, in the death of any animal, both souls, good and evil, desert the flesh, why is the flesh called unclean, as though deserted by the good soul alone? For even if some remnants of life remain, they remain from both: for they say that not even the dung itself remains without some scanty remnants of the members of God. They find, then, no cause why they should assert that flesh is more unclean than crops.

But evidently, trying to display their deceptive chastity, they think flesh more unclean because it exists from intercourse: as though they were not all the more vehemently compelled to help that divine member by eating it, the more tightly bound they think it there. Finally, if this is the cause of the greater uncleanness of flesh, let them eat the bodies of those animals which do not arise from intercourse, such as the innumerable kinds of worms, some of which, born from trees, certain regions of Venetia commonly eat. They ought also to take as food the frogs which the earth suddenly generates from a single rain, so as to free the members of their God mixed in such forms, if they detest the flesh that is propagated by intercourse; and they would convict the human race of error, because they eat hens and doves, procreated from the coupling of males and females, and reject the cleaner daughters of heaven and earth, the frogs.

For according to their fable, the first princes of darkness, whose parents were trees, are cleaner than Mani himself, whom father and mother begot by intercourse; cleaner too are their lice, which are born without intercourse, from the sweat of the flesh or the exhalation of the body, than these very wretches themselves, who were born of parents in intercourse. Or if they now think whatever is born of flesh, even without intercourse, to be unclean for this reason, because the flesh itself is from intercourse, then vegetables and fruits will be unclean, which have sprung up more fertilely and abundantly from dung.

Here let them see what they are to do, or what they are to answer, who say that fruits are cleaner than flesh. For what is cast out from flesh more unclean than dung, and what is applied to crops more productive? Surely they themselves say that through the chewings and digestions of foods, life flees from there, and something scanty remains in the dung. Why, then, where scanty life remains, do your foods, that is, the fruits of the earth, arise from dung both better and larger and more numerous? Flesh is fed not on the refuse of the earth, but on its produce; but the earth is made fruitful by the refuse of flesh, not by its produce.

Let them choose which is cleaner; or, now corrected, let them cease to be unclean and unbelieving, to whom nothing is clean, and let them embrace with us the Apostle who says: *All things are clean to the clean*[^18]. *The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof*[^19]. *Every creature of God is good*[^20]. For all things that exist naturally are good in their own order; and no one sins in them, except one who, not keeping his own order in obedience to God, disturbs their order too by using them badly.

9.1. But our fathers who pleased God kept their own order in that obedience, so that whatever God distributed by commanding at the suitable times, they observed it just as he distributed it. And so they did not, at that time, eat certain flesh given for food—though all clean by nature, yet unclean by some signification—when it was commanded not to be eaten, in order that by such significations the future manifestation of things might be prefigured; but they would have been as guilty, the men of that time and that people, if they had despised observing both unleavened bread and the rest of this kind, in which the Apostle says there was a shadow of things to come[^21]—when those things had to be done thus, and these which are now revealed had then to be thus foretold—as we would be senseless if now, with the New Testament made manifest, we thought those foretelling observances profited us anything.

Just as sacrilegious and impious men we would be, if those same books which were written for our sake—so that we, recognizing the things now revealed to us and announced in manifestation as foretold to those men so long before by those figures, might faithfully and firmly hold them—we thought were therefore to be cast off, because the things written there the Lord commands us no longer to observe corporeally, but to understand and do spiritually. For they were written for our sake, upon whom the end of the ages has come, as the same Apostle likewise says[^22]. *For all things that were written before were written for our instruction*[^23]. Therefore, not to eat unleavened bread for the appointed seven days was a sin in the time of the Old Testament; but in the time of the New Testament it is not a sin:

but in the hope of the age to come which we have in Christ, who, both clothing our soul with righteousness and clothing our body with immortality[^24], renews the whole of us, to believe that we shall suffer or do anything from the necessity and want of the old corruption is always a sin, as long as those seven days are revolving by which time runs its course. But this, in the times of the Old Testament hidden in figure, was understood by certain holy ones; in the time of the New Testament, revealed in manifestation, it is preached to the peoples. Hence that Scripture was then a precept, now a testimony.

Not to celebrate the Feast of Booths was once a sin[^25]; now it is not a sin: but not to be joined to the tabernacle of God, which is the Church, is always a sin; but then it was treated under a figured precept, now it is read in a revealed testimony. For that which was then done would not be called the tabernacle of testimony, unless by a certain fitness of signification it bore witness to some truth that had to be declared in its own time.

To mix purple with linen garments, and to be clothed in a garment of mixed linen and wool, was once a sin[^26]; now it is not a sin: but to live in a disordered way, and to wish to mix professions of different kinds—so that either a consecrated woman should have the ornaments of the married, or one who, not being continent, has married should bear the appearance of a virgin—is in every way a sin; and so is anything that is woven together unfittingly out of a different kind in anyone's life. But that which was then figured in garments is now declared in morals. For that was the time of signifying, this of manifesting. Therefore that very Scripture, which was then the exactor of signifying works, is now the witness of the things signified; and what was then observed for foretelling is now recited for confirmation.

To yoke an ox and an ass together for work was then not lawful[^27]; now it is lawful. For it was declared through the Apostle, when he recalled the Scripture about not muzzling the ox that treads out the grain, saying: *Is it for the oxen that God is concerned?* Why, then, is it now read, when what it forbade is now lawful? Because the same Apostle, following on there, says: *For our sake the Scripture says it*[^28]. And surely it is impious that we should not read what was written for our sake: for it was written more for our sake, to whom it is made manifest, than for those in whom it was figured. For anyone, if need be, yokes an ox and an ass without harm to the work; but a wise man and a fool—not so that one should command and the other obey, but equally, from equal power, so as to announce the word of God—no one makes companions without scandal. And so we hold the same Scripture, both then commanding by its power that what would now be opened should be covered in shadows, and now bearing witness by its authority that what was then covered is opened in the light.

9.2. But concerning the bald man and the one with bristling hair[^29], that the Law called them unclean, Faustus had paid too little attention, or had fallen upon a faulty manuscript. But would that he himself had been willing to have a bald forehead, and had not blushed to fix on it the cross of Christ: surely he would have believed that Christ, who cries out, *I am the truth*[^30], neither fell with false wounds nor rose with false scars. Indeed he even says: "I have not learned to deceive; I say what I think." He is therefore not a disciple of his own Christ, whom in his insanity he supposes to have shown false scars to his doubting disciples, and he wants himself to be believed, as though not deceiving, not only about his other vanities, but even about that very deceit of Christ: whether he is better than Christ, by whose deceiving he himself does not deceive; or whether he is the disciple of that very man—not of a truthful Christ, but of a deceitful Mani—since even in this he deceives, in which he glories that he has not learned to deceive?
