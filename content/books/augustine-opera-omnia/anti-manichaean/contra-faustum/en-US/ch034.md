# Book XXXIII

**1.** FAUSTUS: It is written in the Gospel: *that many will come from the east and the west, and will recline with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven*[^1]. Why, then, do you not accept the Patriarchs? Far be it from us indeed to begrudge any mortal whom God, looking upon him perhaps in his mercy, has ever led back from perdition to salvation; but this we surely set down as belonging to the clemency of him who has had mercy, not to the merit of one whose disreputable life you cannot deny. And on this account the fathers of the Jews too—namely Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob—if this testimony of Christ which you bring forward about them is for certain, although they themselves were indeed most shameful, as their great-grandson Moses nearly shows, or whoever else is the author of this history which is called Genesis, who wrote for us their lives most worthy of all hatred and disgust—let them too, nevertheless, now be in the kingdom of heaven, let them be in a place which they had never believed in nor hoped for, as nearly appears from their own books clearly. Provided, however, that it be established—you yourselves also confessing it—that, after a long interval, freed by Christ our Lord from the foul and penal custody of the lower regions, where the merits of their life were confining them, they could have come, through his mystical passion, to this very thing which is written of them, if they have come. For neither, because our same Lord freed a certain robber from the cross, and said that on that very same day he would be with him in the paradise of his Father[^2], would anyone begrudge it, or could anyone be so inhuman that this office of so great kindness should displease him. But nevertheless we will not for that reason say that the lives and morals of robbers ought to be approvable to us, because Jesus gave indulgence to a robber; or because he forgave the sins of tax collectors and harlots, and said that they too would go before into the kingdom of heaven those who bore themselves proudly[^3]. For a certain woman caught in injustice and adultery, the Jews accusing her, he himself absolved, charging her now to cease from sinning[^4]. Therefore, if he did some such thing also for Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, thanks be to him: it befits him to work such things upon souls, who makes his sun to rise upon the good and the evil, and rains upon the just and the unjust[^5]. But this one thing in this opinion of yours is troublesome to me: why you hold this only of the fathers of the Jews, and not also of the other patriarchs of the Gentiles—that they too at some time perceived this grace of our Liberator; especially since the Christian Church is established more from the sons of these than from the seed of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. But you will surely say that the former indeed worshiped idols, but these the omnipotent God, and that therefore Jesus had care of these alone. So the worship of the omnipotent God thrusts down into Tartarus, and he who worshiped the Father needs the help of the Son? But you shall see to it: let us agree, I say, thus far, that they were led back into heaven, not because they deserved it, but because divine clemency conquers the force of sins.

**2.** Nevertheless, whether this too was said by Christ, the very variety of the writers makes us doubtful and uncertain. For when two evangelists, Matthew and Luke, narrate alike about a certain centurion, whose servant was then sick, and on whose account Jesus is seen to have said this very thing—namely that he had not found so great a faith in Israel as in that man, Gentile and pagan though he was, in that the same said that he was not so worthy that Jesus should enter under his roof, but asked only this, that he should command by a word, and his servant would be healed—yet Matthew alone adds this, that Jesus went on to say: *Amen I say to you, that many will come from the east and the west, and will recline with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven; but the sons of the kingdom will be cast into the outer darkness*. Signifying indeed many pagans who were going to come, on account of the centurion, because he too was a Gentile, in whom yet so great a faith had been found; but calling the Jews "sons of the kingdom," in whom no faith had been found. But Luke, although he too thought that this, as necessary and memorable, ought to be inserted among the wondrous deeds of Christ in his Gospel, yet makes no mention there of Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob. And if anyone should say that he passed it over for this reason, because it had already been said enough by Matthew, why then does he mention the deed itself toward the centurion and his servant, which had equally been sufficiently intimated to us by Matthew's diligence? But it is false. For even about the very entreaty of Jesus to come, Matthew indeed says that the centurion came to him through himself to ask for a cure; but Luke not, but that he sent to him elders of the Jews, who, lest perhaps he be spurned by the same as a Gentile (since indeed they wish Jesus to be plainly a Jew), set about persuading him, saying that he was worthy to whom he should grant this, since indeed he loved his nation, and had himself built a synagogue[^6]—as if this too pertained to the Son of God, if the Jews had deserved the building of their synagogue from a pagan centurion. Nor yet did Luke entirely keep silent about this word, thinking, I suppose, that it might perhaps have been true; but he changes its place and applies it to a certain other quite dissimilar occasion—that is, where Jesus says to his disciples: *Strive to enter through the narrow door; for many will come seeking to enter, and will not be able. But when the master of the house has entered*, he says, *and has shut the door, you will begin to stand outside and to knock, saying: Lord, open to us. And answering he will say: I do not know you. Then you will begin to say: We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets and synagogues. And he will say to you: I do not know where you are from; depart from me, all you workers of iniquity. There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth, when you see Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the Prophets entering into the kingdom of God, but yourselves cast outside; and they will come from the east and the west and the south and the north, and will recline in the kingdom of God*[^7]. This very thing indeed—that many will be excluded from the kingdom of God, namely those who have borne only the name of Christ, but have not done his works—Matthew too did not omit to write[^8]; but there he makes no mention at all of Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob. Likewise, about the centurion and his servant Luke too indeed wrote; but he equally testifies nothing at all there about Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob: so that, since it cannot be established for certain where it was said, nothing forbids even not believing that it was said.

**3.** And not without reason do we never, indeed, bring our ears to Scriptures of this kind, so inconsonant and various, without judgment and reason; but, contemplating all things, and comparing some with others, we weigh whether each of them could have been said by Christ or not. For many words have been inserted by your elders into the sayings of our Lord, which, marked with his name, do not agree with his faith—especially because, as has often been proved by us, these were written neither by him nor by his Apostles, but long after their assumption were learned by certain persons, themselves not concordant among themselves, half-Jews, through reports and opinions; who nevertheless, referring all these same things to the names of the Lord's Apostles, or of those who were seen to have followed the Apostles, lied that they wrote their errors and falsehoods according to them. But you shall see to it: about this chapter for the moment, as I say, I would not contend too much with you, since that is enough for my defense which I set down before, and which it is not permitted even to you yourselves to deny—that before the coming of our Lord all the Patriarchs of Israel and the Prophets lay in the Tartarean darkness for their merits; whence, if they were ever freed by Christ and led back to the light, what does this have to do with hatred of their life? For we hate and refuse, not what they were—that is, men—but what they were like—that is, evil; nor what they now are—that is, purged—but what they were—that is, once impure. And on this account, for the moment, in whatever way you wish it to be, this chapter is no obstacle to us: since, if it is true, here the mercy and goodness of Christ is commended; if false, the crime cleaves to its writers; we, either way, are in safety, as always.

**4.** AUGUSTINE: How in safety, wretched one? How in safety, you who say that you hate the Patriarchs as impure, and still mourn your god as impure? Surely you have granted to those Patriarchs purgation bestowed after the coming of the Savior, and blessed rest given them; but your god, even after the coming of the Savior, still lies in darkness, is still plunged in all shameful things, still wallows in all things impure: so that not only was the life of those men better than your god, but even their very death was happier. But in what abodes the just were, who had departed from this life before Christ came in the flesh, and whether the passion of Christ transferred even those to something better, who had not only believed in him as one going to come, to suffer, to rise again, but had also foretold this very thing, as was fitting, by the prophetic Spirit—this is to be searched out in the Holy Scriptures, if in any way it can be searched out clearly; not from this point are the rash opinions of any men whatever to be pursued, much less the perversities of so execrable a heresy of those straying too far from the truth. In vain indeed does Faustus, through this obliquity, promise himself that something can be bestowed on anyone after this life which he has not procured to be bestowed on himself in this life. It is good for you that, while you live here, you abandon that error, and recognize and hold the truth of the catholic faith. Otherwise, far off will be what the unjust man promises himself, when there begins to come about what God has threatened to the unjust.

**5.** But about the life of the Patriarchs I have already, as much as I judged sufficient, answered the slanderer not a little: to whom—not corrected at death, nor justified after his own passion—the Lord bore testimony, when he admonished the Jews that if they were sons of Abraham they should do the deeds of Abraham; and that the same Abraham desired to see his day, and rejoiced when he had seen it[^9]; and that into his bosom—that is, into some great and hidden secret of I know not what restful happiness—the angels carried that poor, afflicted man, despised by the proud rich man[^10]. What shall I say of the apostle Paul? Does he perhaps also praise Abraham as justified after death, because before he was circumcised he believed God, and it was reckoned to him unto righteousness[^11]—which he weighs so heavily that he says we, who are not propagated from his flesh, have been made his sons for no other reason than that we follow the footsteps of his faith?

**6.** But what shall I do with you, whom against the testimonies of the Scriptures iniquity has so deafened that, whatever is brought forward thence against you, you dare to say was not said by the Apostle, but written under his name by some forger or other? So openly foreign to Christian teaching is the doctrine of demons which you preach, that you can in no part defend it under the name of Christian teaching, unless you say that the Scriptures of the Apostles are false. Unhappy enemies of your own soul! What writings will ever have any weight of authority, if the evangelical, if the apostolic, will not have it? About what book will it be certain whose it is, if it is uncertain whether the writings which the Church—propagated from the Apostles themselves and declared throughout all nations with such great eminence—calls and holds to be the Apostles' are the Apostles'; and if it will be certain that the Apostles wrote what the heretics, contrary to this Church, bring forward, called by the names of their own authors, who existed long after the Apostles? As though, even in secular writings, there were not most certain authors, under whose names many things were afterward brought forward, and therefore rejected, either because they in no way agreed with those which were established to be theirs, or because, at the time when those men wrote, they in no way managed to become known and to be handed down and commended to posterity through them or their most intimate friends. Were not—to omit others—certain books brought forward under the name of the most noble physician Hippocrates, and not received into authority by the physicians? Nor did some similarity of matters and words help them; for, compared with those which were established to be truly Hippocrates's, they were judged unequal; and because they did not become known, from that time when his other writings did, as being truly his. But these books, compared with which those that are brought forward sideways are refused—whence is it established that they are Hippocrates's, whence, if anyone deny it, he is not even refuted, but laughed at, except because the series of succession so commended them from the very time of Hippocrates even to this time and onward, that to doubt of it is the part of a madman? The books of Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, Varro, and other authors of this kind—whence have men known that they are theirs, except by the same continuous attestation of times succeeding one another? Many have written many things about the ecclesiastical writings, not indeed with canonical authority, but with some zeal of helping or of learning. Whence is it established what is whose, except because, in those times in which each wrote them, he made them known and published them to whom he could, and thence, by knowledge continued and more widely confirmed to others and others among posterity, they have come even to our times; so that, when asked whose each book is, we do not hesitate what we ought to answer? But why should I go on into things long past? Behold these writings which we have in our hands: if, after some time of this life of ours, someone should deny either that those are Faustus's, or that these are mine, whence is he convicted, except because those who now know these things pass on their knowledge, by continued successions of posterity, even to those who will exist long afterward? Since these things are so, who at last is blinded with so great a madness—unless he be subverted by consenting to the malice and deceit of lying demons—as to say that the Church of the Apostles, so faithful, so numerous a concord of brethren, could not have deserved this, that they should faithfully pass on their writings to posterity, when they have kept their sees by a most certain succession even to the present bishops, since this comes about with such great ease for the writings of men of whatever kind, whether outside the Church or in the Church itself?

**7.** "But," he says, "their writings are found contrary to one another." With malicious ill will you read; foolish, you do not understand; blind, you do not see. For what great thing was it to inspect these things diligently, and to find the great and wholesome agreement of those same writings, if contentiousness did not pervert you, and if piety helped you? For who, ever reading two historians writing about one matter, supposed that either, or either one of them, was deceiving or deceived, if one of them said what the other passed over; or if one embraced something more briefly, yet keeping the same meaning safe and whole, while the other set forth everything as it were limb by limb, so as to intimate not only what was done, but also how it was done? Just as Faustus wished hence to slander the truth of the Gospels, because Matthew said something which Luke, when he narrated the same thing, omitted to say—as if Luke denied that Christ said what Matthew wrote that he said. Hence there was never any question at all, nor can this be objected except by those wholly imprudent and unwilling or unable to consider anything of these matters. That indeed is wont both to be inquired into among the faithful, and to be objected by the unfaithful—but also by those themselves either little learned or too contentious, unless, admonished, they come to their senses—namely that Matthew said: *A centurion came to him, beseeching him, and saying*; but Luke, that he sent to him elders of the Jews, asking this very thing about his servant who was sick, that he would heal him; and that when he was drawing near the house, he sent others through whom he might say that he was not worthy that Jesus should enter into his house, nor was he himself worthy to come to Jesus. How, then, according to Matthew, *did he come to him, beseeching him, and saying: My servant lies at home paralyzed, and is grievously tormented*[^12]? This, then, is understood: that Matthew briefly embraced the very meaning, true and whole, saying that the centurion came to Jesus, and not saying whether he came through himself or through others; and that he said that about his servant to him, not expressing whether he said it through himself or through others. For what? Is not human custom full of such expressions, when we say that someone has come a long way toward something, even one whom we do not yet say has arrived? Do we not also most commonly speak of the very arrival, to which there seems as it were to be nothing that can now be added, as being done through others, often saying: He pleaded his case, he reached the judge; or: He reached this or that powerful man—when for the most part he does this through friends, without seeing at all the one to whom each is said to have reached? Whence even such men, whoever, by the art of ambition, reach the minds of the powerful, inaccessible in a way, whether through themselves or through others, are now even commonly called "Reachers." What, then? When we read, do we forget how we are accustomed to speak? Or was the Scripture of God going to speak with us otherwise than in our manner? And this indeed, from the common custom of speaking, I would answer to the obstinate and turbulent.

**8.** But those who seek these things not litigiously, but peaceably and faithfully, let them come to Jesus, not by flesh, but by heart; not by the presence of the body, but by the power of faith, like that centurion: then they will better perceive what Matthew said. For to such it is said in the Psalm: *Come to him, and be enlightened; and your faces will not blush*[^13]. Whence that centurion had come more to Christ, whose faith he so praised, than those very men through whom he sent his words. Like this matter is also that which the Lord says: *Someone touched me*—when the woman who suffered an issue of blood, touching the fringe of his garment, was made whole. For he seemed wonderfully to say this to his disciples: *Who touched me?* and *Someone touched me*—when the crowd was pressing him. Finally, they answered him this: *The crowds press you, and you say: Who touched me?*[^14] Therefore, just as they were pressing, but she touched; so those men had been sent to Christ, but the centurion came more. So Matthew both kept the manner of a not so very unusual expression, and intimated something mystically; but Luke showed also this very thing, how it was done, so that we might be compelled hence to notice how Matthew said it. I would indeed wish that one of those vain men, who slanderously object little questions of this kind to the Gospel as though great, would himself narrate something twice over, not falsely nor deceitfully, but wholly willing to intimate and set it forth, and that his words should be taken down with the pen and recited to him: whether he would not say something more or less, or in an inverted order, not only of words, but even of matters; or whether he would not say something from his own opinion, as though another had said it, which he had not heard him say, but had plainly recognized that he willed and meant it; or whether he would not briefly embrace the truth of some meaning, of which matter he had before, as it were, more expressly explained the particulars: and whatever else there is that can perhaps be comprehended under fixed rules—how it comes about that, whether in the single narrations of one matter by two, or in the two narrations by one of one and the same matter, many diverse things are found, yet nothing adverse; and many varied, nothing contrary. Thus are solved all the things by which these wretched men tie up their own neck, so as to hold the spirit of their error within, and to admit no salvation from without.

**9.** Therefore, after all the slanders of Faustus have been refuted—at least those of these chapters of his to which I have, in this work, as far as the Lord deigned to help, sufficiently, as I judge, and at length responded—I briefly admonish you who are held by that so wicked and execrable error, that if you wish to follow the authority of the Scriptures, which is to be preferred to all, follow that which, from the very times of Christ's presence, through the dispensations of the Apostles and the sure successions of bishops from their sees, has come down, kept, commended, and made glorious throughout the whole world, even to these times. For there you will see even the obscure things of the Old Testament revealed, and the things foretold fulfilled. But if you are moved as it were by reason, first consider who you are, how much less fit to comprehend the nature—I will not say of God, but of your own soul: to comprehend it, indeed, in the way you say you wish or have wished, by most certain reason, not by most vain credulity; and since you have not been able to do so at all (for without doubt, as long as you are such, you will in no way be able), at least think, or believe, what is naturally implanted in every human mind, if only it be not disturbed by the depravity of a perverse opinion—that the nature and substance of God is altogether unchangeable, altogether incorruptible; and you will at once not be Manicheans, that at some time you may also be able to be Catholics. Amen.
