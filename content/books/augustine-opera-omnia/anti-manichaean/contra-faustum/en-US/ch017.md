# Book 16

**1.** Faustus said: Why do you not accept Moses, since Christ says: *Moses wrote about me*; and: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also*[^1]?

I, for my part, would wish that not only Moses had written about Christ, but all the Prophets too, both of the Jews and of the gentiles. For what would this harm our faith, or what would it not rather profit, if we gathered from everywhere fitting and suitable testimonies to our God? Since it would even then be free for us, with hatred and execration of their superstition remaining, to excerpt from those same men only the prophecies about Christ: so that it cannot be contrary to me, if Moses too, although he is a stranger to Christ, yet seems to have written something about Christ. Or would not any man wish to gather a flower from all thorns, fruit from every herb, honey from all flies: although we use neither flies, nor grass, for food, nor thorns for the ornament of a crown? Or would anyone be unwilling that a pearl should be born in every deep, gems in all lands, fruits in all woods? Or if to eat fish from the sea does not harm, while to drink water harms, and men know how, having taken what is useful, to reject what is hostile; would it not be free for us, with the rite of any religion condemned, if it were useless to us, to accept from it only the prophecies about Christ? Nor would this profit their errors for capturing us and reducing us into servitude of their own: because not even the unclean spirits, when they openly and undissemblingly confessed Jesus to be the Son of God[^2], profited so as to be the less hateful to us. Wherefore, if Moses too, according to this testimony, wrote something about Christ, I will accept it: yet so that it profit him in no way for taking me captive into his own law, which I see to differ in nothing from paganism. Wherefore there is nothing at all in your supposing that, if this is proved, I shall in no way rejoice that every spirit prophesied about Christ.

**2.** To you indeed I will render sufficient thanks, if, just as you show that Christ testified that Moses wrote about him, so you also teach this: what those things are which he wrote. For I, having searched his Scriptures, as I was bidden, found no prophecies about Christ there; either because there are none, or because I myself could not understand. Hence, set in a great heat, I was compelled by reason to one of two things: either to pronounce this passage false, or Jesus a liar. But that indeed was foreign to piety, to reckon God to have lied. It seemed more right, then, to ascribe falsehood to the writers, than a lie to the author of truth. Since I heard him too saying that all who came before him were thieves and robbers[^3]: by which sentence I see Moses struck first of all. To this, when, as he spoke of his majesty, where he calls himself the light of the world, the Jews indignantly cried out against him: *Because you testify about yourself, your testimony is not true*: I do not see him going on, where the place most demanded it, to say that Moses prophesied about him; but, as though truly a stranger, and having no testimony from their fathers, he answered: *Surely in your law it is written that the testimony of two men is true. I am one who testifies about myself; and the Father who sent me testifies about me*[^4]. Recalling to them that which, said from heaven, all had heard: *This is my most beloved Son; believe him*[^5]. Moreover, this too does not seem verisimilar to me, that the Jews could keep silent, when Christ said that Moses wrote about him, without at once, malignant and crafty as they were, asking what that was which he thought had been written about him by Moses. But this their all-around silence too signifies no less that Jesus said no such thing.

**3.** Although, then, these things too seem no small matter for confirming the suspicion of falsehood about that passage, yet I am held more by this, that, having searched, as I said, all the writing of Moses, I found no prophecies about Christ there. Now, however, having found you a reader of better understanding, I believe I shall obtain something, and I confess I shall give thanks, if you frustrate by no ill will the hope of progress and teaching which the confidence of your rebuke promises me; but teach me, if there is anything that has perhaps escaped me in reading, recorded about God and about our Lord in the writing of Moses. And do not say, I beg, as the unskilled are wont, that this very thing ought to be enough for faith, that Christ said Moses wrote about him. For I do not want you now to look at me, whom my profession has made bound to believe, so that I cannot but believe him whom I follow; but suppose we are dealing with a Jew, suppose with a gentile: when we have said to them, Moses wrote about Christ, they will seek proofs—what shall we offer? Shall we be able to say, Christ said this, in whom they still in no way believe? Surely it will be necessary for us to show them what he wrote.

**4.** What, then, shall we show? That which you are wont to, where his God speaks to Moses, saying: *I will raise up for them a Prophet from among their brothers like you*[^6]? But that this in no way regards Christ neither escapes the Jew, nor is it expedient for us to believe so: because Christ is not a prophet, nor a prophet like Moses; since the former was a man, the latter God; the former a sinner, the latter holy; the former born of intercourse, the latter, according to you, of a virgin, but according to me not even of a virgin; the former, with his God offended, is killed on the mountain[^7], the latter, most pleasing to the Father, suffers by his own will[^8]. How, then, will he himself be a prophet like Moses? Surely at once the Jew will either deride us as unskilled, or convict us as liars.

**5.** Or shall we offer him that which you likewise are wont to bring in: *They will see their life hanging, and will not believe their life*[^9]? To which you indeed add, "on wood"; for it is not so written. But to prove that this too in no way pertains to Christ, nothing is so ready to hand. For among the savage curses which he uttered against his own people, if they should fall away from his law, he added this too, saying that they would be in the captivity of their enemies, and would meditate their own end day and night, so that they would not even have confidence in their own life, which had been granted them by the victors: because it would hang from uncertainty, always fearful and anxious under the imminence of swords. Not even this, then, pertains to Christ: other things are to be sought. For that one indeed I would scarcely believe you think said of Christ, that everyone is cursed who hangs on wood[^10]: or that other, that the prophet or prince of the people is to be killed, who would wish to turn them away from their God, or to break any of the commandments[^11]. Which Christ I cannot indeed plainly deny to have done. But you, on the contrary, cannot plainly confess these things to be written of him, lest, if this be so, we begin again to ask in what spirit Moses prophesied, so as either to curse Christ, or to order him killed. For if he had the Spirit of God, he did not say these things of Christ: if he said these things of Christ, he did not have the Spirit of God. For neither would the divine Spirit either curse Christ, or order him to be killed. To clear Moses, then, of this charge, you must confess that he did not write even these things of Christ. But if he did not write these of Christ, either you will give others, or there will be none. If there were none, neither could Christ assert what is nowhere. So if Christ in no way asserted this, that passage will be established to be false.

**6.** But neither is that verisimilar which follows: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also*[^12]. Because the tradition of Moses and of Christ is exceedingly unlike and far otherwise, so that if the Jews believed one of them, they would necessarily resist the other. For Moses indeed teaches, above all else, that one must abstain from all work on the sabbath, and brings in this as the reason of this religion, that God, when he was fashioning the world and all things in it, devoted six days to the work, but on the seventh rested, which is the sabbath: and therefore blessed it, that is, sanctified it, as the harbor of his tranquillity, and gave a law besides, that whoever broke it should be killed[^13]. This, then, the Jews vehemently believed, Moses teaching it: and therefore they thought they ought not even lend their ears to Christ, asserting that God always works, and has set himself no day of rest, because he is a continual and untiring power; and that he himself therefore ought never to rest, not even on the sabbaths: *For my Father*, he says, *always works, and I must work*[^14].

Likewise Moses counts the circumcision of the flesh among things sacred and pleasing to God, and orders every male to be circumcised in the flesh of their foreskin: and teaches that this is a necessary sign of that covenant which his God arranged with Abraham, and affirms that whoever of the men should not bear this, that one would be exterminated from his tribe, and would not come into the fellowship of the inheritance which was promised to Abraham and his seed[^15]. And this too the Jews strongly believed, Moses asserting it: and therefore they could not have faith in Christ, who weakened these things, and besides asserted that he who was circumcised would be made doubly a son of Gehenna[^16].

Likewise Moses makes an anxious distinction of carnal foods, and sits as a disputant among fish and birds and quadrupeds after the manner of a glutton; and orders some indeed to be licked up as clean, but others not even to be touched as unclean: among which, in part, he censures the pig and the hare, and whatever among fish lacks scales, or among quadrupeds does not have a cloven hoof, nor chews the cud[^17]. And these things too the Jews stoutly believed, Moses writing them: and therefore they could not now believe Christ, who taught an indifference of foods, and indeed removed all things entirely from his own disciples, but to the worldly commonly granted all things that could be eaten, and asserted that nothing entering into the mouth defiled them; because the things which proceed shamelessly from the mouth are alone what defile a man[^18]. That Jesus laid down these and many other things contrary to Moses, there is no one who does not know.

**7.** Since to run through these one by one is long, I will show one for many, that is, that the greatest part of the Christian heresies, and—what is obvious—the Catholics take no care to keep any of the things which Moses writes. Which very thing, if it does not descend from some error, but from that true tradition of Christ and of his disciples, you must by all means confess that Jesus and Moses taught things mutually contrary to each other: and therefore that Christ was not believed by the Jews, because they wished to show faith to Moses. To what end, then, will it not be false, that Jesus said to them: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also*[^19]: when it is far manifest that they the more did not believe Jesus, because they believed Moses; but could have had faith in Christ, if they had ceased to believe Moses? Yet you, as I said, teach us, I beg, where Moses wrote anything about Christ.

**8.** At other times you say: If you are a Christian, believe Christ when he says that Moses wrote about him. But if you do not believe, you are not a Christian. This reply is always silly and feeble, of those who have nothing to show. How much better, then, would you have done, if you simply confessed the same? And yet you could indeed say this to me, whom you know to be under necessity to believe for the sake of the religion by which I serve Christ: although this very thing is still in question, whether this too is a testimony of Christ, so that it ought to be believed absolutely; or of a writer, so that it must be examined carefully. Nor, if we do not believe falsehoods, do we hereby offend Christ, but the falsifiers. Yet somehow this will be able to be borne, when set against Christians:

but what shall we do about those whom I mentioned, namely the Jew and the gentile, to whom we cannot say: If you are a Christian, believe; if you do not believe, you are not a Christian? Although you would not most rightly say this even to a Christian, since Christ did not spurn the apostle Thomas doubting about him; but, that he might heal the wounds of his mind, showed the scars of his body; nor did he say: If you are a disciple, believe; if you do not believe, you are not a disciple. You may say this to me, not doubting about Christ, but about the sentence, whether it is his, or introduced. But, you say, he calls more blessed those who have not seen and have believed[^20]. If you think this was said so that we should believe anything without reason and judgment; be you the more blessed without sense, I shall be content to have heard myself blessed with reason.

**9.** Augustine replied: Cunningly indeed you say you are prepared, if you find any prophecies about Christ in the books of Moses, to take them up, as if a fish from the sea, while you reject the very water from which the fish is taken. But because everything Moses wrote is about Christ, that is, pertains wholly to Christ, whether because it foretells him by figures of things either done or said, or because it commends his grace and glory; you, who believed in a fabricated and deceitful Christ from the writings of Mani, so refuse to believe Moses, just as you also refuse to eat a fish. But this is the difference, that you pursue Moses with hostility, but praise the fish deceitfully. For if to eat a fish from the sea does not harm, as you yourself said, why do you so proclaim it harmful, that if no other food turns up, you would be consumed by hunger first, rather than feed on a fish? What? If all flesh is unclean, as you say, and in all water and every herb is held that wretched life of your god, which must be purified through your foods; your detestable superstition compels you both to throw away the fish you praised, and to drink sea water and eat the thorns you blamed.

But as for the fact that you even compared the servant of God to demons, so that, such as they were when they confessed Christ[^21], such also this one is to be accepted if anything can be found in his books which preaches Christ; he indeed does not disdain the reproach of his Lord. For if the householder was called Beelzebul, how much more the members of his household![^22] But do you see from whom you learned these things—surely more wicked than those who so reviled the Lord. For they did not believe him to be Christ, and therefore thought him deceitful: but you do not think any teaching truthful, except that which dares to proclaim Christ deceitful.

**10.** But whence does the law of Moses seem to you to differ in nothing from paganism? Is it because it commends a temple, a sacrifice, an altar, and a priest? But all these names are found in the New Testament too. *Destroy this temple*, he says, *and in three days I will raise it up*[^23]; and: *When you offer your gift at the altar*[^24]; and: *Go, show yourself to the priest, and offer for yourself the sacrifice which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them*[^25]. And of what these were figures, the Lord himself in part shows, when he compares the temple of his body to that temple; in part we learn from apostolic teaching: *For the temple of God is holy*, says the Apostle, *which you are*[^26]; and: *I beseech you, therefore, by the mercy of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, pleasing to God*[^27]; and the rest of this kind. Accordingly all those things were figures of us[^28], as he likewise says, and it must often be recalled: because they were shown forth not to demons, but to the one true God, who made heaven and earth; not as though he needed such things, but as one distinguishing the times, and commanding the present things, through which he might signify the future.

But you, who, in order to seduce and deceive the simple and imperfect Christians, feign that you detest paganism, produce for us the authority of Christian books, in which you are commanded to worship and adore the sun and the moon. Your error, then, is rather like paganism; since you do not even worship Christ, but, under the name of Christ, some thing or other which you have feigned for yourselves by lying, and you adore gods either visible in this conspicuous heaven, or others, innumerable and fabricated. To which phantasms, as though vain and empty images, you have built not little shrines, but made your hearts temples.

**11.** You demand of me that I show what things Moses wrote about Christ. Many indeed have already been demonstrated above; but who could demonstrate all? Especially since, if I recall certain things, this perverse man seems prepared either to try to pervert them into another sense; or, if he be overwhelmed by the evidence of clearer truth, to say that he takes those things as though catching a sweet fish from the salt sea: and not on that account ought he be compelled to the whole Scripture of Moses, as to drinking sea water. Wherefore I think it suffices for this work, that I show that those very things which he plucked out as to be reproached from the Scripture of the Hebrew law pertain to the preaching of Christ, if they are rightly understood: from which it may sufficiently appear that the rest much more, whether pronounced at once, or diligently and truthfully searched out, suit the Christian faith; if the things which the enemy casts as to be derided and condemned, convict him by those very things as to be condemned by Christian truth. Wherefore, O man full of all deceit, since the Lord in the Gospel said: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote about me*[^29]; there is no reason for you to feign yourself set in a great heat, and to seem compelled to one of two things, either to pronounce this passage false, or Jesus a liar. For just as this passage is true, so also Jesus is truthful. "It seemed more right," you say, "to ascribe falsehood to the writers, than a lie to the author of truth."

Do you so believe Christ the author of truth, whom you proclaim a feigner of flesh, and of death, and of wounds, and of scars? I want you to show me whence you learned that Christ is the author of truth, if you dare to ascribe falsehood to those who wrote about him, whose authority, commended and confirmed by recent memory, flowed out to posterity. For you did not see Christ, or how he spoke with you as with the Apostles, or call you from heaven, as he did Saul[^30]. What can we think about him, what believe, except what the Scripture testifies? Further, if the Gospel, spread abroad and known to all the nations, and from the beginning of the preaching of the name of Christ set in all the Churches in so great a height of holiness, is a liar; what Scripture can be brought forth, to which faith about Christ is to be given? What will you be able to bring forth written, which he who is unwilling to believe this would not say is fabricated, if so great a knowledge of the Gospel comes into doubt?

**12.** Then you add that you yourself heard him saying that all who came before him were thieves and robbers[^31]. Whence did you hear him saying this, except from the Gospel? But if this, which you so believe from the Gospel that you say you heard it as though from the mouth of the Lord, another should contend to be false, and deny that Christ said this; where will you go? What will you do? Will you not proclaim the evangelical authority with all the strength you can? Wretched man, there is written what you are unwilling to believe, where you learned what you so believe that you say you heard it from Christ himself. Behold, we believe both, because we believe the holy Gospel, where both are written: both that Moses wrote about Christ, and that all who came before Christ were thieves and robbers. For he wishes "came" to be understood thus, because they were not sent: for those who were sent, like Moses and the holy Prophets, came not before him, but with him; because they did not wish to precede him through pride, but humbly bore him speaking through them.

You, however, who understand these words of the Lord in this way, by your own interpretation admit plainly enough that you have no prophets who prophesied that Christ would come, and so you have fashioned him for yourselves just as you wished. For suppose there are some of yours—who deserve no credence for the very reason that they are produced by none but you—suppose, I say, there are some whom you would dare to say prophesied that Christ would come in false flesh, suffer a false death, and offer false scars to his doubting disciples: I will not dwell on how detestable and to be shunned such people are by this very fact, and how they cannot be truthful, for whom a lying Christ is acceptable. Leaving that aside, then, as I had begun to say, on this interpretation of yours they were thieves and robbers, since they came before Christ, predicting in whatever manner that he would come. Moreover, if that interpretation is true, by which those are said to have come before Christ who refused to come with Christ—that is, with the Word of God—but brought their lies to men when God had not sent them, then you yourselves, even though you were born into this world after Christ's passion and resurrection, are thieves and robbers; for before he himself enlightened you to preach his truth, you wished to come before him in order to flaunt your deceit.

**13.** But in that passage where the Jews said to him, *You bear witness to yourself; your testimony is not true*[^32], it is no wonder that you do not see him going on to say that Moses had prophesied about him, for you do not have the devout eye by which you could see this. For consider his very reply to them, namely: *In your law it is written that the testimony of two men is true. I am one who bears witness to myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness to me*[^33]. To those who understand rightly, what else does this convey but that the number of witnesses was, in the law, consecrated and commended by the prophetic Spirit, so that in this way too there might be foretold the coming revelation of the Father and the Son, whose Spirit, in that inseparable Trinity, is the Holy Spirit? This is why it is written: *On the word of two or three witnesses shall every word stand*[^34]. Otherwise even a single witness very often speaks the truth, and several very often lie; and at the beginning of the faith of the gentiles, one apostle preaching the Gospel was believed rather than the erring peoples by whom he himself was being persecuted. Not in vain, then, was this number of witnesses in a manner consecrated; and when the Lord made this reply, by that very fact too he wished it to be understood that Moses had prophesied about him.

Or do you perhaps raise a quibble from the fact that he said not "in the law of God" but *in your law it is written*? Who would not recognize here the usual idiom of the Scriptures? For he said *in your law* meaning "given to you," just as the Apostle calls it his gospel, which nonetheless he testifies that he received not from man but through the revelation of Jesus Christ[^35]. Or will you say that Christ denied that he had God as Father wherever he said not "our Father" but *your Father*[^36]? And as for that voice you mentioned, brought down from heaven, *This is my most beloved Son; believe him*[^37]—because you did not hear it, do not believe him! But if you believe because you found it in the holy Scriptures, then there too is that other saying which you refuse to believe, that Moses wrote about Christ;

there too are many other things to which you likewise deny credence. And you do not fear, wretches, that in this way some profane person might say that this voice did not sound from heaven at all. Just as you argue, against the salvation of the human race that is conferred on all nations by the authority of the Gospel—and indeed to your own ruin—when you say that Christ's statement that Moses wrote about him is not to be believed, because "if he had said this, the Jews could not have kept silent, but at once, being malicious and crafty as they were, would have asked what that thing might be which he supposed Moses had written about him"—so too that vain and lost person might say: "If that voice had sounded from heaven, would not all the Jews who heard it have believed?" Why then do you not consider, you madmen, that just as it could happen that even after that heavenly voice the hard unbelief of the Jews remained, so it could happen that, when Christ said Moses had written about him, in their malicious cunning they feared to hear that by which they would be refuted, and so did not ask at all what Moses had written about him?

**14.** But Faustus too senses that this line of argument is not only sacrilegious against the holiness of the Gospel but also feeble and weak; and so he directs his effort rather to this point, and says he is the more firmly persuaded because, having searched through all the writing of Moses, he found no prophecies about Christ there. To him I quickly reply that he does not understand; and if anyone asks why he does not understand, I will answer: because he reads with a hostile mind, with an opposed mind; because he does not search in order to know, but thinks he knows what he does not know. This presumption of swollen arrogance either closes the eye of the heart, so that it does not see at all, or distorts it, so that it sees perversely and approves or disapproves one thing for another. "You teach me," he says, "what it is that may perhaps have escaped me in my reading concerning our God and Lord, recorded in the writing of Moses." And here too let me quickly answer: It all escaped you, because he wrote it all about Christ. But since we cannot discuss and treat it all, I will reserve for you in this work, if I can with the Lord's help, what I said above: namely, that the very passages you select to censure I will show to have been written about Christ.

Indeed, you even ask that I not say, "as the unlearned are wont to, that this in itself ought to suffice for faith, namely, that Christ said Moses wrote about him." Now if I do say this, I say it not as one unlearned but as a believer; yet that this does not avail to convince a gentile or a Jew, I too admit. But that against you, who in whatever manner glory in the Christian name, it is sufficiently apt and powerful, you yourself—though you long shuffled about—were nonetheless forced to confess, saying: "For do not now look to me, whom my profession has bound to believe, so that I cannot but believe him whom I follow; but suppose we are dealing with a Jew, suppose with a gentile."

By these words you showed that you, for the present—the one with whom I now have to do—since your profession has bound you to believe, are sufficiently convicted that Moses wrote about Christ; for that Christ himself said this is written in the Gospel, whose so illustrious and holy authority you do not dare to undermine. For even when you do dare to do so indirectly, pressed by the straits of your difficulty and seeing what great ruin overwhelms you when you are told that there is no writing from which you can demand belief about the deeds and words of Christ if you think the Gospel, so holy and so widely and most fully known, is not to be believed—and fearing that, with the cloak of the Christian name lost, your naked emptiness will remain to be spat upon and detested by all—you try again to gather up your wounded self, and you say that by these words of the Gospel your profession has already bound you to believe.

And so, for the present, the one with whom I am now dealing—I hold you, I strike you, I slay you; that is, your error and your deceit—and I compel you to confess that Moses wrote about Christ, since it is read in the Gospel that Christ said this, which gospel your profession has bound you to believe. But if I should have to dispute with a Jew or a gentile, I have already shown above in what ways, according to my own small strength, I think I ought to proceed.

**15.** Nor do I deny that the following was foretold of Christ—the very thing you selected as easy to refute—where God speaks to Moses, saying: *I will raise up for them a prophet from among their brethren, like you*[^38]. Nor do your polished and witty antitheses, with which you wished as it were to color and paint your muddy discourse, deter me in any way from this truth of faith. For in comparing Christ and Moses, and desiring to show them dissimilar, so that on this account what is written—*I will raise up for them a prophet like you*—might seem not to be understood of Christ, you set up against yourself many contraries: that the one was a man, the other God; the one a sinner, the other holy; the one born of sexual union, the other (according to us) of a virgin, but (according to you) not even of a virgin; the one slain on a mountain because he offended God, the other suffering by his own will, well-pleasing to the Father.

As if, when something is said to be similar, it were to be understood as similar in every part and in every way! For not only are things of one and the same nature said to be similar to one another—as twin men, or sons to their parents, or all men to all men, who insofar as they are men are surely similar (something very easy to observe in the other animals too, or in trees, as an olive is said to be similar to an olive, a laurel to a laurel)—but also many things of unlike nature are, and are said to be, similar, as the wild olive to the olive, and spelt to wheat.

I am still speaking of things close at hand and akin. For what is so far distant from the Son of God, *through whom all things were made*[^39], as cattle and stone? And yet in the Gospel one reads, *Behold the Lamb of God*[^40], and in the Apostle, *and the rock was Christ*[^41]—which no one would rightly say if he bore no likeness whatever to these things. What wonder, then, if Christ did not disdain to be made like Moses, when he was made like the sheep which, in foretelling him, God commanded through Moses himself to be eaten by his people, its blood to be applied for the safeguarding of their salvation, and to be called the Passover[^42]—which no one is permitted to pretend has not now been fulfilled in Christ? Therefore from the Scriptures I acknowledge him unlike; from the Scriptures acknowledge him with me also like—not unlike in the respect in which he is like, but the one for one cause, the other for another, provided I demonstrate both. Christ is unlike man, because he is God; for it is written of him: *Who is over all, God blessed forever*[^43]. And Christ is like man, because he is man; for it is likewise written of him: *the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^44]. Christ is unlike the sinner, because he is always holy; and Christ is like the sinner, because *God sent his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, that from sin he might condemn sin in the flesh*[^45]. Christ is unlike the man born of sexual union, insofar as he was born of a virgin; but Christ is like the man who is born, insofar as he too was born of a woman, of whom it was said: *What is born of you, the holy one, shall be called the Son of God*[^46]. Christ is unlike the man who died for his own sin, insofar as he died without sin and by his own power; but again Christ is like the man who dies, insofar as he too died a true death of the body.

**16.** Nor should you disparage Moses, God's servant, because you have said that he was a sinner and was slain on a mountain because he had offended his God[^47]. For he too knew how to glory in the Lord, that he might be saved by him—by whom that man too is saved who says: *Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first*[^48]. For Moses is rebuked by the divine voice because his faith faltered somewhat in bringing water from the rock[^49]—which can be common to him with the sin of Peter, who amid the waves wavered through a like failure of faith[^50]. But far be it that we should believe him, on this account, estranged from the eternal fellowship of the saints—he who, with holy Elijah, as the Gospel relates, was found worthy to stand by the Lord glorified on the mountain[^51]. For, as we read in the ancient books, even after that very sin it appears how great is his merit before God. But what was the cause that God should speak of avenging his sin by such a death? Since I have promised to demonstrate that the very things you have selected as deserving censure pertain to the foretelling of Christ, I will do as I can, with the Lord's help, so that I may teach that even this which you censured in the death of Moses was a prophecy about Christ to those who understand rightly.

**17.** For just as it is the custom of the divine mysteries in the holy Scriptures that one and the same man at different times bears one or another character for the sake of signifying something, so at that time Moses bore the character of the Jewish people placed under the Law, and prefigured it in a prophetic foreshadowing. Therefore, just as Moses, striking the rock with his rod, doubted of God's power, so that people, who were held under the Law given through Moses, in fastening Christ to the wood of the cross did not believe that he was the Power of God. But just as the struck rock poured forth water for the thirsty, so the wound of the Lord's passion became life for believers. For we have on this matter the most illustrious and most faithful word of the Apostle, when he spoke of it, saying: *and the rock was Christ*[^52].

This carnal despair of Christ's divinity, then, God commands to die in the very loftiness of Christ, when he commands the death of Moses' flesh to take place on the mountain. For as the rock is Christ, so too the mountain is Christ: the rock his lowly strength, the mountain his eminent greatness. For just as the Apostle says, *the rock was Christ*, so the Lord himself says: *A city set on a mountain cannot be hidden*[^53]—asserting, namely, that he is the mountain, and that his faithful, founded in the glory of his name, are the city.

The prudence of the flesh lives when, like a struck rock, Christ's lowliness on the cross is despised: for *Christ crucified is a stumbling block to the Jews and folly to the gentiles*. And the prudence of the flesh dies when, like the eminence of a mountain, the exalted Christ is acknowledged: for *to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the Power of God and the Wisdom of God*[^54]. And so Moses went up the mountain, that with his flesh dead he might be received with a living spirit—the mountain Faustus had not climbed, so that with a dead mind he might utter carnal slanders.

Did not Peter himself, through the prudence of the flesh, shudder at the rock being struck, when he said to the Lord as he foretold his passion: *Far be it, Lord; this shall not happen; be merciful to yourself*? For the Lord did not spare this sin, when he replied to him: *Get behind me, Satan; you are a stumbling block to me, for you do not savor the things of God but the things of men*[^55]. Or where did this carnal distrust die, except in Christ's glorification, as it were in the height of the mountain? For it surely lived when Peter timidly denied him, and it surely had died when he freely proclaimed him. This distrust lived in Saul, when, detesting the stumbling block of the cross, he laid waste the Christian faith[^56]; and where had it died except on that mountain, when Paul could now say: *It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me*[^57]?

**18.** What have you then, heretical vanity, by which you think you can prove that it was not foretold of Christ: *I will raise up for them a prophet from among their brethren, like you*[^58]—since not even from this can you do so, namely from the fact that you show him dissimilar? For by other causes we too show him similar. Or is it because he was called a prophet, who also deigned to be a man and foretold so many things to come? Unless, perhaps, a prophet is something other than a man foretelling things to come beyond human conjecture? Whence he himself says of himself: *A prophet is not without honor except in his own country*[^59]. But of you I shall see later—you who a little before confessed yourself convicted when you said that your profession has bound you to believe the Gospel. Let the Jew himself come forward into the midst, who wrongly lifts a neck free from the yoke of Christ, and so still thinks it permitted him to say: "Your Christ has lied; Moses wrote nothing about him."

**19.** Let him tell me what prophet God promised when he said to Moses: *I will raise up for them a prophet from among their brethren, like you*, or, *similar to you*[^60]. For there were many prophets afterward, but he surely wished some one to be understood. Here, I believe, that successor of Moses will most readily come to his mind, the one who led the people, freed from Egypt, into the land of promise. Thinking of him, he will perhaps still laugh at me for asking of whom it was said, *I will raise up for them a prophet like you*, since he can read who succeeded Moses, when he was dead, in the same office of governing and leading that people. And when he has laughed at me as one unlearned—for such is how Faustus too describes him—I will not cease to address the man further and to recall him from his careless laughter to the care of answering,

by asking and demanding: why, to that very future successor of his—in comparison with whom he was found wanting, in that he himself was not to lead the people into the land of promise, lest, namely, the Law given through Moses not to save but to convict the sinner should be thought to lead into the kingdom of heaven, whereas *grace and truth came through Jesus Christ*[^61]—I shall therefore ask of the Jew why Moses changed the name of that very future successor of his. For he was called Hoshea, and Moses called him Joshua[^62]. Why, finally, did he so name him at the time when he sent him ahead from the valley of Paran to that same land to which the people were to come under his leadership? For the true Jesus himself said: *And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and take you to myself*[^63]. I shall also ask whether the prophet does not bear witness to this figure when he says: *God will come from the south, and the holy one from Paran*[^64]—as if to say: He will come, a holy God of that name whose name was his who came from the south, from Paran—that is, Jesus.

To this is added that the same Word of God himself is understood to speak where he promises that same successor of Moses, through whom the people would be brought into the land of promise, calling him by the name of an angel—as men too announcing something are wont to be called in the divine Scripture. And he says thus: *Behold, I send my angel before your face, to keep you on the way and to bring you into the land which I have sworn to you. Give heed to him and obey him; do not disbelieve him, for he will take nothing from you, for my name is in him*[^65].

What is this? Let now not the Manichean but even the Jew himself search through those Scriptures, and see whether God said of any angel, *my name is in him*, except of this one whom he promises as the leader into the land of promise. Then let him seek among men who, as Moses' successor, led the people in; and he will find Joshua—called this not from the beginning of his life, but with his name changed. He, therefore, who said, *my name is in him*, in that Joshua, is himself the true Jesus, the ruler and leader of the people into the inheritance of eternal life, according to the New Testament, of which the Old Testament was a figure. Thus, as far as concerns the prophetic apparatus, nothing more distinguished could be done or said, since the matter was carried even to the expression of the name.

**20.** It remains that that Jew—if he wishes to be a Jew also in secret, not by the letter but by the spirit[^66]; if he wishes to be reckoned a true Israelite, in whom there is no guile[^67]—should remember, in the figure, that dead Joshua who led into the land of the dying, and should acknowledge, in the truth, the living Jesus, under whose leadership he may enter the land of the living. For such a man, no longer bitter, will not resist so clear a prophecy, but, made gentle by the remembrance of the Joshua who led into that land of promise, will now hear the very one whose name that man bore, leading in more truly and saying: *Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land as their inheritance*[^68]. Here now even that gentile, if he did not have too stony a heart, or if he were of those stones from which God raises up sons to Abraham[^69]—would he not marvel that in the ancient books of the very nation from which Jesus was said to be born, so evident a prophecy was written about him that even his name was expressed? And at the same time he would notice there that it was not just any man who was foretold as Jesus, but God indeed; since in that man who was appointed to govern the people and to lead them into the kingdom, and was called Jesus with his name changed, God said that his own name was present, and called him an angel—by this very fact, that with his name changed he was sent announcing something great and divine. For who, even slightly tinged with that language, is unaware that "angel" is Greek for "messenger"?

For this reason any gentile, if he were unwilling to be perverse and obstinate, would not despise those books on the ground that they were Hebrew, by whose nation's law he was not bound; rather, he would set great store by the books of any nation whatever, because in them he would find written so long beforehand the things that he recognized as already fulfilled in his own times. And he would not spurn Christ Jesus himself on the ground that he saw him foretold in Hebrew writings; rather he would judge that one to be followed and venerated with vast wonder and devout religion who, before he was born among men, had deserved to be foretold and commended through so many volumes of the ages, by writings of every kind—partly by clearer testimonies, partly by figures and sacraments of deeds done and of words spoken.

Thus, from the effects of Christian things now present, the prophecy of the books would be proved true; and from the prophecy of the books, Christ would be recognized as one to be worshiped. Let me be reckoned to speak vain things, if it has not so come to pass, if it does not so come to pass, if the whole world over there is not a flocking together, through the reading of those same books, into that faith.

**21.** Hence the senselessness of these people is marvelously to be laughed at, who ask of us as though it were impossible how a gentile would wish to learn the Christian faith through the books of the Jews, when he sees all nations becoming, with such devotion and such celebrity, the disciples of those books—and all the more strongly and firmly, indeed, because such great testimonies about Christ are produced from the hands of his enemies; for the gentiles who believe can suppose nothing about him to have been fabricated for the occasion, since they find Christ in those books to which those serve who crucified Christ, and which they hold in such a high pinnacle of authority who daily blaspheme Christ.

For if the prophecies about Christ were produced by those who preach Christ, they would be thought fabricated by those very people; but as it is, he who preaches expounds what he who blasphemes recites. For every blindness of the impious is ordained by the most high God to some use of the saints, who, by the fairness of his governance, makes good use even of the wicked, so that those who by their own choice live unjustly are by his judgment justly disposed of.

Therefore, lest the testimonies of the prophecy—that Christ would be born, would work wonders, would suffer indignities, would die, would rise again, would ascend, and would scatter through all nations the Gospel of eternal life—should be believed to have been invented by those who announced him to the peoples, something great was done for our benefit out of the unbelief of the Jews: that these same people who did not have these things in their hearts for their own sake might have them in their books for our sake. Nor is the authority of those books thereby diminished, because they are not understood by the Jews; rather it is even increased, for their very blindness was foretold there. Whence, all the more, by not understanding the truth they bear witness to the truth: for when they do not understand the books in which they were foretold as not destined to understand, by this very fact too they show those books to be truthful.

**22.** Hence comes also that text, by whose ambiguity Faustus is deceived: *You will see your life hanging, and you will not believe your life*[^70]. Someone may say that these words can also be understood otherwise; but that they cannot be understood of Christ, neither did Faustus dare to say, nor will anyone at all dare, except one who denies either that Christ is the life, or that he was seen hanging by the Jews, or that they did not believe him. But since he himself says, *I am the life*[^71], and since it is established that he hung before the eyes of Jews who did not believe him, I do not see why we should doubt that he too wrote about Christ, of whom Christ says: *For he wrote about me*[^72].

Accordingly, if Faustus tried to show that the text—*I will raise up for them a prophet from among their brethren, like you*—cannot be understood of Christ, because Christ is not like Moses, and yet has been wholly convicted, what need is there to labor over this testimony? Or else, just as he said that Christ is not like Moses in order to refute that prophecy, so, in order to refute this one, let him say that Christ is not the life, or that he did not hang in the sight of the Jews who did not believe him. But since he did not say this, and since none of them would dare to say it today, there is no reason why we should delay in embracing this prophecy too, made by his servant about our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

But, you say, this too is set among other curses. Is it then not a prophecy, when the other things among which it is set are nothing but prophecies? Or is it not a prophecy about Christ, because the things woven in before or after it in that reading seem to have no bearing on Christ? As if there were anything worse among the curses that befell the Jews for the desert of their proud impiety than to see their life—that is, the Son of God—hanging, and not to believe their life! For curses, when they are spoken from prophecy, do not come from the evil wish of one imprecating, but from the foreknowing spirit of one announcing. For those that come from an evil wish are forbidden, when it is said: *Bless, and do not curse*[^73]. But these are often found in the speech of the saints, as the apostle Paul: *Alexander,* he says, *the coppersmith did me much harm; the Lord will repay him according to his works*[^74]. For the Apostle even seems, as if vexed and indignant, to have wished evil in that saying: *Would that those who unsettle you would even mutilate themselves!*[^75] Yet surely, if you consider the person of the writer, you will understand that by a most elegant ambiguity he rather wished them well. For *there are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven*[^76]. And Faustus would have tasted this too in these words, if he had brought a devout palate to the Lord's food.

For perhaps what was said—*You will see your life hanging, and you will not believe your life*—sounded to the Jews thus: that, seeing their life hang in uncertainty amid the threats or treacheries of their enemies, they would not believe they would live. But the son of the Gospel, when he hears, *For he wrote about me*, sees in this very ambiguity of the saying what the prophets cast before swine and what they signify to men; and at once there comes to his mind the life of men, Christ, hanging, and the Jews not believing him for this very reason, that they see him hanging. And someone else, indeed, might quickly say that, among the other curses in that reading which have no bearing on understanding anything of Christ, this alone there is about Christ, namely what is written: *You will see your life hanging, and you will not believe your life*. For it would not be impossible that among the various curses prophetically foretold to that impious people, this too should be set down. But I—and those who with me consider somewhat more attentively that evangelical and dominical saying, in which he did not say "For he wrote also about me," so that he might be believed to have written other things too which do not pertain to Christ, but said *For he wrote about me*[^77], so that we should, by searching, refer the whole intent of that writing to nothing but the understanding of Christ's grace—we recognize that the other curses in that reading too were foretold on account of Christ; which, if I should now wish to show, it would take far too long.

**23.** Hence it is so far from being the case that what Faustus mentioned does not pertain to Christ because it is set among other curses, that those very other things have no right understanding unless they are referred, as prophesied, to the glory of Christ, by which the human race is provided for—how much more this one! Even if Moses had been such a man as to pour out by mouth, while his heart beheld something else, I would more readily say that he prophesied without knowing it than that, when I heard it said to the Jewish people, *You will see your life hanging, and you will not believe your life*, I should deny that it was prophesied about Christ.

For Caiaphas did not behold in his mind that which was understood from his words, when, persecuting Christ as an enemy, he said it was expedient that one man die, lest the whole nation perish. To which the Evangelist added that he said this not of himself, but, being high priest, prophesied[^78]. But Moses was not Caiaphas; therefore what he said to the Hebrew people—*You will see your life hanging, and you will not believe your life*—he said not only about Christ (which, even had he said it unknowingly, ought to be understood as said about no other), but he said it knowingly. For he was the most faithful steward of the prophetic mystery—that is, of that priestly chrism from which we recognize the name of Christ;

in which mystery even Caiaphas, a most wicked man, could prophesy even unknowingly. For this is what the prophetic chrism worked in him, that he should prophesy; and this his impious life worked, that he should prophesy unknowingly. With what mouth, then, is it said that Moses prophesied nothing about Christ?—from whom that chrism took its beginning, whence the name of Christ became known, and whence even a persecutor of Christ prophesied of Christ, albeit unknowingly.

**24.** Now concerning the curse on one hanging on the wood, we have already said above, as far as seemed sufficient. But that Moses commanded the killing of a prophet or leader of the people who should wish to turn the children of Israel away from their God, or to break any of the commandments, did not command this against Christ—this is sufficiently clear both from the many things we have already treated, and to one who considers more and more the words and deeds of our Lord Jesus Christ it will become clearer and clearer: for Christ did not wish to turn any of them away from their God. For the God whom Moses had commanded them to love and worship is surely the very God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, whom the Lord Jesus Christ recalls with the same commendation, and by whose authority he refutes the error of the Sadducees who denied the resurrection, where he says: *But concerning the resurrection of the dead, have you not read what God said to Moses from the bush: I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob? He is not the God of the dead, but of the living*[^79]—for all live to him. Opportunely, then, the Manicheans are now convicted by the same voice by which the Sadducees were then convicted: for they too deny the resurrection itself, though in another manner.

Likewise, when, praising the faith of the centurion, he said, *Amen, I say to you, I have not found such great faith in Israel*, he added and said: *But I say to you that many will come from the east and the west and recline with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven; but the sons of the kingdom will go into the outer darkness*[^80]. If, then—as Faustus cannot deny—Moses commended to the people of Israel no God but the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and Christ without doubt commends that same God from these and other testimonies, he did not try to turn that people away from their God; rather, he threatened that they would go into the outer darkness because he saw them turned away from their God, in whose kingdom he says the gentiles, called from the whole world, will recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—for no other reason than that they had held the faith of the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Whence the Apostle too says: *And the Scripture, foreseeing that God justifies the gentiles by faith, foretold to Abraham, saying: In your seed shall all nations be blessed*[^81]—so that those, namely, would be blessed in the seed of Abraham who imitated the faith of Abraham. Christ, therefore, did not wish to turn the Israelites away from their God, but rather rebuked them for being turned away from him.

But whoever supposes that the Lord broke any of those commandments that were given through Moses, it is no wonder if he thinks what the Jews thought; but he errs for this reason, that in this the Jews too erred. And where Faustus mentions the very commandment that he wants believed the Lord broke, there it is needful that we show how he is deceived, just as we have already shown above, where it was needful.

Now I say this: that if the Lord had broken any of those commandments, he would not also have rebuked the Jews on this very score; for, when they slandered his disciples for eating with unwashed hands and thereby transgressing—not the commandment of God, but the traditions of the elders—he said to them: *Why do you too transgress the commandment of God to establish your traditions?* And he mentions the very commandment of God which we know was commanded through Moses. For he went on to say: *For God said: Honor your father and mother; and: He who curses father or mother shall die the death. But you say: Whoever shall say to father or mother, The gift that comes from me will profit you, need not honor his father; and you have made void the word of God on account of your tradition*[^82]. In this matter, see how many things he teaches us: both that he does not turn the Jews away from their God; and that he not only does not break his commandments, but even rebukes those by whom they were broken; and that it was none but God who commanded these things through Moses.

**25.** For this reason, since we believe that everything Moses wrote pertains to the commendation of Christ—and since we cannot demonstrate this in the present work, we have promised to show it in those passages which Faustus has selected from that writing for refuting or reproaching—it is rightly required of us as a debt that we show this too: that Moses' command, that a prophet or leader who should wish to turn them away from their God, or to break any of the commandments, be killed, pertains to guarding the faith that is learned in the Church of Christ. For he saw, by the prophetic spirit and with God speaking to him, that many heretics, teachers of various errors, would arise against the doctrine of Christ, who would not preach that Christ who is the true Christ. For he is the true one who was foretold through the prophecies issued through Moses himself and the other saints of that nation. Whoever, therefore, should wish to teach another, him Moses commanded to be killed. And what else does the catholic tongue do now but, with the spiritual sword of both Testaments, twice-sharpened in its edge, slay all who wish to turn us away from our God, or to break any of the commandments? Among whom Mani himself falls especially, when his error is destroyed by the asserted truth of the Law and the Prophets—he who wishes to turn us away from our God, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, whom Christ commends, and who wishes to break the commandments of the Law, in whose very figures we recognize that Christ was foretold.

**26.** As for that conclusion of his, whether I should call it most obtuse or most fraudulent, I do not know—for Faustus had talent; whence I rather judge that he wished to cast a fog over the less attentive reader than that he did not see what I shall say. For he says: "But if Moses did not write these things about Christ, you will either give others, or there will be none." This proposition is true; but it would have followed that he should also show both that these were not written about Christ and that others cannot be given. He did neither of these: for we have both shown how these can be taken of Christ, and above we gave many others which can have no understanding except of Christ. There is no reason, then, why you should conclude, Faustus, that nothing was written by Moses about Christ.

For attend to what you say: "But if," you say, "he did not write these things about Christ, you will either give others, or there will be none." You say truly. Accordingly, since we have taught both that these were written about Christ, or on account of Christ, and have given many others, it is rather your argument that will be none. And these things you mentioned, although you did not make your case, you at least tried to show were not written about Christ. But as to what you added—"you will either give others, or there will be none"—you ought first to have demonstrated that we cannot give others, so that you might securely infer that there are none. But as it is, as though your little book were going to have deaf hearers or blind readers, so that no one would notice what you passed over, you hurried to say: "If there are none, then neither could Christ assert what is nowhere; and so, if Christ did not assert this, this chapter will have stood proven false."

O the man who thinks himself a speaker, and another a non-thinking contradictor! Where is your sharpness? Or in a bad cause could you do no otherwise? But the bad cause forced you to speak vain things; yet no one forces you to have a bad cause. For what if we shall give others? Surely there will not be none, since there will be some. And if there will be some, Christ could assert this thing that is. So, if Christ could assert this, that evangelical chapter does not stand proven false. Return, then, to your proposition, by which you said, "You will either give others, or there will be none"; and see that you did not show that we would give no others. See also how many others we have already given above, and observe what is concluded from this: namely, that what we read Christ said in the Gospel is not false: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too; for he wrote about me*[^83].

And indeed the authority of the Gospel is so eminent and its truth so well founded that, even if, through the slowness of our understanding, we found nothing written by Moses about Christ, we ought nonetheless to believe not only that there are some things, but that everything he wrote pertains to Christ—for he did not say, "He wrote also about me," but, *He wrote about me*. But as it is, even if (which be far from us) there were to be doubt about this chapter of the Gospel, once so many testimonies about Christ in the writing of Moses had been found out, all that doubt would be removed; and since there is no doubting about the chapter of the Gospel, even if those testimonies had not been found out, they ought nonetheless to be believed to exist.

**27.** As for what you add—"that the tradition of Christ and of Moses was dissimilar; and that therefore it was not verisimilar that, if they believed Moses, they would believe Christ too; indeed, that this rather followed, that if the Jews believed the one, they would necessarily oppose the other"—you would surely not say this if you raised the eye of consideration a little, and beheld, without the blindness of contentiousness, the world believing in Moses and Christ alike, among men learned and unlearned, Greeks and barbarians, wise and foolish, to whom the Apostle called himself a debtor[^84]. If, then, it was not verisimilar that the Jews would believe Moses and Christ alike, much less verisimilar is it that the world would believe Moses and Christ alike. But since we see all nations believing both, and holding the prophecy of the one, agreeing with the Gospel of the other, by a most robust and most celebrated faith, that one nation was not being called to anything impossible when it was said to it: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too*; and rather to be marveled at and more vehemently rebuked is the hardness of the Jews, who did not do what we behold the whole world to have done.

For whatever you say about the Sabbath, and about the circumcision of the flesh, and about the distinction of foods—that the tradition of Moses was one thing, and that Christians learned something else through Christ—we have already shown above that, as the Apostle says, *all these things were figures of us*[^85]. The teaching, then, is not different, but the time is different. For one was the time at which these things had to be foretold through figured prophecies, and another is the time at which these things now have to be fulfilled through manifest and rendered truth. But what wonder is it if the Jews, understanding the Sabbath carnally, opposed Christ, who was now imparting it spiritually? Answer the Apostle, if you can, who testifies that the rest of that very day is a shadow of what was to come[^86]. But if they resisted Christ, not understanding the true Sabbath, do not you resist him, and understand true innocence. For in that very place where Jesus is especially thought a destroyer of the Sabbath—when his disciples, passing through the grain and hungry, plucked the ears and ate—he called them innocent, replying to the Jews: *If you knew what this means: I desire mercy rather than sacrifice, you would never have condemned the innocent*[^87]. For they ought rather to have had mercy on the hungry, since these did this compelled by hunger.

But among you, whoever plucks ears of grain is reckoned a murderer—not by the tradition of Christ, who calls this innocence, but by the tradition of the homicide Mani. Or did the Apostles perhaps show mercy to those very ears of grain, so as to purify the members of God by eating them, as your fable has it? Then you are the cruel ones, who do not do this. But of course Faustus knows how to destroy the Sabbath, because he knows that the power of God always and tirelessly works. Let those say this who understand that God makes all times without any temporal will. This bears hard on you, who declare that the rest of your god was shaken off by the rebellion of the nation of darkness, and disturbed by the sudden onset of enemies. Or, foreseeing from eternity that this would happen, did he never have rest, because he was never free of care—he who was thinking that he would wage so grievous a war with such great ruin and loss of his own members?

**29.** But that Sabbath which you ignorantly and impiously deride—unless it too had its understanding among the prophecies that are written about Christ, Christ would not so bear witness to it: he who, suffering by his own will, as you yourself set down in his praise, and therefore having in his power the times of his passion and resurrection, brought it about that his flesh should rest in the tomb on the Sabbath from all its works, so that, rising on the third day, which we call the Lord's day, which is counted the eighth after the Sabbath, he might declare that the circumcision of the eighth day too pertained to the foretelling of himself. For what does the circumcision of the flesh signify? What, but the stripping off of the mortality which we bear from our carnal generation? On account of this the Apostle said: *Stripping himself of the flesh, he made an example of the principalities and powers, triumphing over them confidently in himself*[^88]. For in that he says that he stripped himself of the flesh, in that place by "flesh" we understand the mortality of the flesh, according to which properly this body is named flesh.

This mortality is properly called flesh, because in that immortality of the resurrection it will not exist; therefore it is written: *Flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God*. About which words you are accustomed to slander our faith, by which we believe in the future resurrection of this body, which has already gone before in the Lord himself,

while you disregard what follows, in which the Apostle openly explains what he means. For, wishing to show what he there called flesh, he immediately added: *nor will corruption possess incorruption*[^89]. For this body, which on account of its mortality is properly named flesh, he says will be changed in the resurrection, so that it will no longer be corruptible and mortal.

Lest this be thought to be said by our own conjecture, consult his very words that follow. *Behold,* he says, *I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise, but we shall not all be changed. In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet; for the trumpet will sound, and the dead will rise incorruptible, and we shall be changed. For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality*[^90]. In order, then, that it may put on immortality, it puts off mortality.

This is the mystery of circumcision, which was commanded to be done on the eighth day[^91], and which has now been fulfilled in truth by the Lord on the eighth day—that is, on the Lord's day after the Sabbath. Hence it is said: *Stripping himself of the flesh, he made an example of the principalities and powers*[^92]. For through this mortality the envious diabolical powers held dominion over us; and he is said to have made an example of them because in himself, our head, he provided the example of what will be accomplished in his whole body—that is, the Church, to be freed from the devil's power—in the final resurrection: this is our faith. And since, as Paul recalls the prophetic testimony, *The just lives by faith*[^93]—this is our justification.

For that Christ died even the pagans believe; but that Christ rose again is the faith proper to Christians. For *if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord,* says the Apostle, *and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved*[^94]. Since, therefore, we are justified by this faith in the resurrection, this too about Christ is apostolic: *that he died for our offenses and rose again for our justification*[^95]. And since this resurrection, which when believed justifies us, was figured in that circumcision of the eighth day, therefore concerning Abraham himself, to whom it was first handed down, the Apostle says: *And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of faith*[^96]. Moses, then, wrote of this circumcision too, among the other prophetic figures, about Christ—of whom he himself says: *For he wrote about me*[^97].

But as for what the Lord says—*Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites, who go about over sea and dry land to make one proselyte; and when you have made him, you make him a son of Gehenna twice as much as yourselves*[^98]—he said this not because he is circumcised, but because he imitates the conduct of those whose imitation he forbids to his own, saying: *The scribes and Pharisees sit on the seat of Moses; what they say, do; but what they do, do not do; for they say and do not do*[^99]. In these words of the Lord you ought to notice both things: both how great an honor was paid to the teaching of Moses, in whose seat even wicked men, while sitting, were compelled to teach good things; and how the proselyte became a son of Gehenna—namely, not by hearing the words of the law from the Pharisees, but by following their deeds. This, then, could be said at that time to the circumcised proselyte, what Paul says: *Circumcision indeed profits, if you keep the law*[^100]. But because he, in not keeping the law, imitated the Pharisees, he became a son of Gehenna—and for this reason, I think, twice as much as they, because he neglected to fulfill what he had taken upon himself by his own will, being not born of the Jews but made a Jew of his own accord.

**30.** But what did you mean to say, insultingly and without consideration, that "Moses sits as an arbiter after the manner of a glutton, and commands that some things indeed be licked up as clean, but that others, as unclean, not even be touched"?—when it rather belongs to a glutton to make no distinctions; or, if he does distinguish, to choose the more pleasant. Or do you say this so that to the unlearned your continence may seem admirable, as it were from your earliest years, as though you did not know—or had already forgotten—how much more pleasantly pork tastes than mutton? But because Moses wrote these things too, in prophetic figures, about Christ, signifying by the flesh of animals the men who are either to be incorporated into the body of Christ, which is the Church, or to be rejected, he figured you too among the unclean—you who therefore do not agree with the catholic faith, because you neither chew the cud of the word of wisdom, nor, distinguishing the two Testaments, the Old and the New, in harmony, do you have, as it were, the cloven hoof. But who could endure that you too were not ashamed to follow the deceit of your Adimantus?

**31.** For you too say: "that Christ taught the indifference of foods in such a way that from his own disciples he removed all flesh entirely, but to the worldly people he granted in common everything that could be eaten; and that he asserted that nothing entering into the mouth defiled them, since the things that proceed shamelessly from the mouth are the only things that defile a man." These are your words, uttered and expressed by a falsehood the more shameless the more open it is. First, because, according to Christ's judgment, if only those things defile a man which come forth evil from the mouth, why did not those same things alone defile Christ's disciples too, so that there would be no need to forbid them, as it were, unclean flesh? Or are worldly men not defiled by the things that enter the mouth, but by the things that go out of the mouth? Then they are better fortified against uncleanness than the saints, if even the things that enter and the things that go out can pollute the saints.

But I would have these people tell me what Christ ate and drank, who, in comparison with John, who neither ate nor drank, said that he himself ate and drank. For when he was rebuking the perversity of men, who sought grounds for slander in both cases, he said: *For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say: He has a demon; the Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say: Behold a gluttonous man and a wine-drinker, a friend of tax collectors and sinners*[^101]. And John's food and drink we know: for it was not said that he did not drink at all, but that he did not drink wine or strong drink[^102]; he drank water, then. And his food was not nothing at all, but locusts and wild honey[^103]. How, then, was he called neither eating nor drinking, except because he did not use that fare which the Jews used? Unless the Lord used this, then, he would not be called, in comparison with John, eating and drinking. Or is it perhaps because the Lord fed on bread and vegetables, on which John did not feed? It is a wonder if one who eats locusts and honey is called not eating, and one who is content with bread and vegetables is called eating.

But suppose about his foods whatever you wish; certainly he would not be called a drinker and a wine-drinker unless he drank wine: why, then, do you think this too unclean? For it is not for the sake of continence and the discipline of taming the body that you forbid touching these things, but because they are unclean: for you declare that these are the filth and gall of the nation of darkness, against the Apostle who says: *To the clean all things are clean*[^104]. Behold those who dare to say that Christ was a teacher of the indifference of foods, yet that he forbade his disciples the things they themselves think unclean. Show, you deceivers, you wicked men, where he removed these from his disciples; yet, by the providence of God the avenger, so blinded that you even prompt us with that by which you are convicted.

For I am compelled by my own mind to insert here for inspection the whole of that chapter of the Gospel which this man wished to set against Moses, so that there we may see how false it is, what first Adimantus, and now Faustus, has said—that the Lord Jesus removed the eating of flesh from his disciples, and granted it in common to the worldly. For after he had answered those slandering his disciples for eating with unwashed hands, the Gospel goes on thus: *And calling the crowds together, he said to them: Hear and understand. Not what enters the mouth makes a man common; but what proceeds from the mouth makes a man common. Then the disciples, coming up, said to him: Do you know that the Pharisees, when they heard this word, were scandalized?*[^105] Here surely, addressed by the disciples, he ought, as these people would have it, to have taught them expressly to abstain from all flesh, so that what he said above—*Not what enters the mouth makes a man common, but what proceeds from the mouth*—might seem to have been said to the crowds.

Let the Evangelist, then, go on, and tell what the Lord replied now not to the crowds but to the disciples. *But he, answering, said: Every plant that my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted up. Let them alone; they are blind guides of the blind. And if a blind man leads a blind man, both fall into the pit*[^106]. This, surely, because, wishing to establish their own traditions, they did not understand the commandments of God. But the disciples had not yet asked of the Master how they themselves ought to take what he had said to the crowds. Behold, this happens too: for the Evangelist continues, and says: *But Peter, answering, said to him: Explain this parable to us*[^107]. From this we understand that Peter supposed the Lord had spoken not expressly nor openly when he said, *Not what enters the mouth makes a man common, but what proceeds from the mouth,* but that, as is his custom, he wished to signify something by the obscurity of a parable.

Let us see, then, whether now, when the disciples ask him more privately, he says what the Manicheans want—that all flesh is unclean, and that they ought not to touch any of it. What? He reproaches them for not yet understanding his open statement, and for thinking what was said expressly to be a parable. For thus it follows: *But he said: Are you too still without understanding? Do you not understand that everything that enters the mouth goes into the belly and is cast out into the privy? But the things that proceed from the mouth come out of the heart, and these make a man common. For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, fornications, thefts, false testimonies, blasphemies; these are what make a man common; but to eat with unwashed hands does not make a man common*[^108].

**32.** Surely now the deceit, made manifest, departs convicted; surely now it is clear that the Lord did not teach the crowds one thing and the disciples privately another; surely it is perceived without doubt that the Manicheans rather are the liars and deceivers, not Moses, not Christ, not the doctrine of both Testaments—there figured, here revealed; there foretold, here made present. How, then, do they suppose that the Catholics keep none of the things Moses wrote, when they observe absolutely all of them—no longer in figures, but in those very things which those figures foretold by signifying? For, supposing there were one time for writing and another for reading, we would not rightly say that the reader did not observe that writing, because he too did not make those characters; since they were figures of sounds, while he was now uttering the very sounds, occupied not with forming those figures but admonished by inspecting them.

But the Jews did not believe Christ for this reason: that they did not observe even those things which Moses had commanded not figuratively but openly. Hence he says to them: *You tithe mint and cumin, and leave the weightier matters of the Law, mercy and judgment; straining out a gnat, but swallowing a camel: these you ought to have done, without omitting the others*[^109]. Hence too is that—that by their traditions they taught how the commandment of God might be weakened, by which he had bidden that honor be paid to parents; for which pride and iniquity they deserved to be blinded, so that they did not understand the rest, because the things they did understand they impiously despised.

**33.** Do you see how I do not say to you, "If you are a Christian, believe Christ when he says that Moses wrote about him; but if you do not believe, you are not a Christian"? For you yourself see what you think of yourself, you who seek to be taught about Christ as a gentile or a Jew. I, nevertheless, have not shirked even this, and I have closed off to you, as far as I could, every approach of error. Nor have I let lie open that precipice into which you blind ones cast yourselves, saying that things in the Gospel are false wherever your heresy has found no way out—so that nothing remains to you by which you can return to believing Christ where this voice of pestilence cannot be set against you.

Indeed, you desire to be taught in such a way as the Christian Thomas, whom Christ "did not spurn as he doubted about himself, but, to heal the wounds of his mind, showed him the scars of his body." These are your words. It is well that you require to be taught in this way. For how I feared that you would contend that this too in the Gospel is false! Believe, then, the scars of Christ: for if those scars were true, those wounds too had been true; and there could be no true wounds except in true flesh—this truth overturns your whole error.

Moreover, if Christ showed false scars to his doubting disciple, then you call him a deceiver in teaching thus, and you desire to be deceived in learning thus. But since there is no one who wishes to be deceived, while many wish to deceive, I understand that you wish rather, after Christ's example, to teach deceitfully than, after Thomas's example, to learn deceitfully. Accordingly, if you believe that Christ deceived the doubter with false scars, who would wish to believe you teaching, and not rather to beware of you deceiving? But if that disciple touched the true scars of Christ, you are compelled to confess the true flesh of Christ too. Thus you will not remain a Manichean, if you believe as Thomas did; but you will remain an unbeliever, if you do not believe as Thomas did[^110].
