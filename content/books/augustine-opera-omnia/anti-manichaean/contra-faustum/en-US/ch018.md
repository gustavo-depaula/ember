# Book XVII

**1.** FAUSTUS said: Why do you not accept the Law and the Prophets, since Christ said that he came not to abolish them but to fulfill them[^1]?

Who testifies that Jesus said this?

Matthew.

Where did he say it?

On the mountain.

In whose presence?

Peter, Andrew, James, and John—these four only; for he had not yet chosen the others, nor Matthew himself.

Of these four, then, one—that is, John—wrote a Gospel?

Yes.

Does he mention this anywhere?

Nowhere.

How, then, did Matthew write what John, who was on the mountain, does not testify—Matthew, who followed Jesus at a long interval, after Jesus had come down from the mountain? And on this account there is first doubt about this very thing, whether Jesus said any such thing, since the suitable witness is silent while the less suitable one speaks. So that for the moment we have allowed that Matthew did us an injury, until we prove that even he did not write these things, but some other person, I know not who, under his name—

which the very oblique narration of that same passage of Matthew teaches. For what does it say? *And as Jesus passed by, he saw a man sitting at the tax office, named Matthew, and called him; and he, rising up at once, followed him*[^2]. And who, then, writing about himself, would say, "He saw a man, and called him, and he followed him," and not rather say, "He saw me, and called me, and I followed him"—unless it is established that Matthew did not write these things, but some other person, I know not who, under his name? Since, then, it would not even be true if Matthew himself wrote this—because he was not present when Jesus spoke these things on the mountain—how much more must it not be believed, since not even Matthew wrote these things, but another under the names of both Jesus and Matthew!

**2.** What of the fact that even from the very statement, by which he commands not to suppose that he came to abolish the Law, it may rather be understood that he did abolish it? For unless he were doing some such thing, the Jews could not suspect this. But, *Do not suppose,* he says, *that I came to abolish the Law.* Come now, then: suppose the Jews had said to him: "But what such thing are you doing, from which we might suspect this? Is it because you deride circumcision, violate the Sabbath, reject sacrifices, confound foods?" Is this, then, the "Do not suppose"? And what more, or what more manifest, could be done toward the destruction of the Law and the Prophets? Or if this is to fulfill the Law, what will it be to abolish it?

What of the fact that the Law and the Prophets do not even rejoice in being fulfilled, so full and complete do they seem to themselves, whose author and father disdains to have anything added to them no less than taken away—so that, writing in Deuteronomy, he says: *These precepts which I command you today, O Israel, you shall observe; and beware that you do not turn aside from them, neither to the left nor to the right; nor add anything to them, nor diminish; but you shall persevere in them, that the Lord your God may bless you*[^3]? Wherefore, whether for the sake of fulfilling, Jesus added something to the Law and the Prophets, he seems to have slipped to the right; or whether he took away in order to destroy, to the left; either way he certainly offended the author of the Law; and therefore either that statement means something else, or it is false.

**3.** AUGUSTINE replied: O marvelous madness, to be unwilling to believe Matthew when he relates something about Christ, and to wish to believe a Manichean! If Matthew was not present when Christ said, *I came not to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them*[^4], and therefore he is not to be believed: was a Manichean present, or even yet born, when Christ appeared among men? According, then, to this law of your faith, you ought to have believed him in nothing of what he testifies about Christ.

But we do not say that a Manichean is not to be believed because he was not present at the deeds and words of Christ, and was born long after; rather, because he speaks about Christ against Christ's disciples, and against the Gospel which was confirmed by their authority. For we have the word of the Apostle, who in the Holy Spirit saw that such men would come. Whence he said to the faithful: *If anyone preaches to you a gospel besides what you have received, let him be anathema*[^5]. For if no one speaks truth about Christ except one who saw and heard him while present, then today no one speaks truth about him. But if today true things are spoken about him to his faithful because those who saw and heard disseminated them either by preaching or by writing, why could not Matthew hear true things about Christ from the mouth of John, his fellow disciple, in a matter at which the latter was present and he himself was not—if from the book of John we can speak true things about Christ, not only we who were born so long after, but also others who will be born after us? For on this ground not only Matthew's Gospel, but also that of Luke and Mark, who followed the same disciples, has been received into an authority not unequal. To this is added that the Lord himself could have related to Matthew what, before he had called him, he had done with those whom he had called earlier.

But, you say, John himself ought to have put this in his own Gospel, if he had heard this said by the Lord—he who, when it was said, was present? As if it could not happen that, since he could not write everything he had heard from the Lord, among the other things he passed over he passed over this too, while he was intent on writing other things. Did he not himself conclude his Gospel thus, saying: *And many other things Jesus did, which, if they were written one by one, I do not think the world itself could contain the books that would be written*[^6]? Here surely he shows that he knew he had passed over many things.

But if John's authority pleases you concerning the Law and the Prophets, believe John when he bears witness to the Law and the Prophets. He wrote that Isaiah saw the glory of Christ[^7]. In his Gospel you have what we discussed a little before: *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too; for he wrote about me*[^8]. On every side your shuffling is beaten down. Say openly that you do not believe Christ's gospel: for those who in the Gospel believe what they wish and do not believe what they wish, believe themselves rather than the Gospel.

**4.** But what an elegant thing Faustus seemed to himself to say, when he wished it not to be believed that Matthew wrote these things, because, in telling of his own choosing, he did not say, "He saw me and said to me: Follow me," but, *He saw Matthew and said to him: Follow me*[^9]: which I do not know whether he said out of the error of ignorance or out of his habit of deceit. But I would not suppose him so ignorant as not to have read or heard that writers of history, when they come to their own person, are wont to compose their account as though they were narrating about another what they narrate about themselves. I rather judge, then, that he wished not, as one ignorant, but to spread a fog before the ignorant, hoping he would catch the more, who did not know these things.

And in the history of worldly affairs, indeed, examples of such narration are found; but there is no need for me to admonish our own people, or to refute this man, from another kind of literature. He himself, certainly, a little before brought forward certain testimonies from the books of Moses in such a way that he did not deny that Moses wrote them—indeed he even affirmed it—but contended that they did not pertain to Christ.

Let him read, then, in those same books, the things Moses wrote about himself, whether he wrote thus: "I said," or, "I did this or that," and not rather, *Moses said*[^10], and, *Moses did*[^11]; or, "The Lord called me," or, "The Lord said to me," and not rather, *The Lord called Moses*[^12], and, *The Lord said to Moses*[^13]; and all the rest in the same manner. So, then, Matthew too wrote about himself as about another—which John did also; for near the end of his book he too speaks thus: *Peter, turning, saw the disciple whom Jesus loved, who also leaned on his breast at the supper and had said to the Lord: Who is it that will betray you?* Did he here say, "Peter, turning, saw me"? Or do they perhaps suppose, on this account, that this man too did not write that Gospel? But a little after he says: *This is the disciple who bears witness about Jesus, and who wrote these things; and we know that his testimony is true*[^14]. Did he say, "I am the disciple who bears witness about Jesus, and who wrote these things; and we know that my testimony is true"?

Certainly it is manifest that this was the custom of writers when they narrated deeds. How many things the Lord himself says about himself in this same manner—who could suffice to count them? *When the Son of man comes,* he says, *do you think he will find faith on earth?*[^15]—he did not say, "When I come, do you think I shall find?" And: *The Son of man came eating and drinking*[^16]—he did not say, "I came." And: *The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God; and those who hear will live*[^17]—he did not say, "my voice"; and many things of this kind. Hence I now think that what has been said suffices both to admonish the studious and to convict the slanderous.

**5.** Now how feeble is that which he says—who does not see it?—that Christ could not have said, *Do not suppose that I came to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I came not to abolish but to fulfill*[^18], unless he had already done some such thing as could give rise to this suspicion? As if we deny that to the Jews, who did not understand, Christ could seem a destroyer of the Law and the Prophets! But this is the very reason why he, the truthful one and the truth, could not have said of some other Law and other Prophets that he did not abolish them, but only of those whom they suspected him of abolishing. This is sufficiently confirmed from this too, that there it follows, and he says: *Amen, amen, I say to you, until heaven and earth pass away, not one iota or one tittle will pass from the Law until all is accomplished. Whoever therefore abolishes one of these least commandments and teaches men so will be called least in the kingdom of heaven; but whoever does and so teaches, he will be called great in the kingdom of heaven*[^19].

For he was thinking of the Pharisees when he said these things—they who abolished the Law by their deeds and taught it by their words. Of whom he says in another place: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do; for they say and do not do*[^20]. Therefore here too it follows thus: *For I say to you, unless your righteousness abounds more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^21]—that is, unless you do, and so teach, what they do not do and yet so teach, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven. The very Law, then, which the Pharisees taught and did not do, Christ says he came not to abolish but to fulfill: because it pertains to the seat of Moses, on which the Pharisees, sitting and saying but not doing, are to be heard, not imitated.

**6.** Nor does Faustus understand—or perhaps he feigns not to understand—what it is to fulfill the Law, when he supposes this is to be taken of the addition of words, because it is written that nothing should be added to the Scripture of God, or taken away[^22]:

whence he says that there ought to be no fulfilling of what is commended as so perfect that nothing is to be added or diminished. These people, then, do not know how he fulfills the Law who lives as the Law commanded. For *the fullness of the Law is love*, as the Apostle says[^23]. This love the Lord deigned both to display and to bestow, by sending the Holy Spirit to his faithful. Whence the same Apostle likewise says: *The love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^24]. And the Lord himself: *By this all will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another*[^25].

The Law, then, is fulfilled either when the things commanded there are done, or when the things prophesied there are displayed. For *the Law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ*[^26]. When the Law itself was fulfilled, grace and truth came. Grace pertains to the fullness of love, truth to the fulfillment of the prophecies. And because both are through Christ, therefore he came not to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them: not that what was lacking should be added to the Law, but that what was written should be done—as his very words testify. For he did not say, "Not one iota or one tittle will pass from the Law until what is lacking is added," but, *until all is accomplished*.
