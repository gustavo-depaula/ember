# Book XXII

**1.** FAUSTUS said: Why do you blaspheme the Law and the Prophets? We are by no means enemies, or foes, of the Law and the Prophets—nor indeed of anyone at all: so much so that, if it were now permitted us through them, we would be ready to confess that all those things are false which are written about them, and for the sake of which they seem to us hateful. But you resist, and, by assenting to your own writers, perhaps bring innocent Prophets into accusation, defame the Patriarchs, dishonor the Law too; and, what is more foolish, you want both your writers not to be liars and yet those men, whose disgraces and base lives they wrote, to be religious and holy. But because both cannot stand together equally—for it must be either that these were wicked, or that those were liars and false.

**2.** Come now, if you please, with the writers condemned by common assent, let us undertake the defense of the Law and the Prophets. But by "the Law" I now mean not circumcision, nor the Sabbaths, and sacrifices, and the other such things of the Jews, but that which truly is law—that is, You shall not kill, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not swear falsely[^1], and the rest. Upon it—long ago spread abroad among the nations, that is, from the time the creation of this world subsists—the writers of the Hebrews rushing, as upon a leprosy and a scab, mingled in these abominable and most base precepts of theirs, which look to circumcision and sacrifices; come now, if you too are surely a friend of the Law, condemn with me those who dared to violate it by this commingling of precepts unsuited to it. Which precepts, unless you too knew them to be no law, nor a part of the law, you would surely either strive to keep them, professing righteousness, or confess to yourselves that you are not righteous. But as it is, both about those commandments which forbid crimes you have a solicitous care, wishing to live rightly; and about those which pertain to the Jews no mention is made: which, how will it be excused for you, unless it be established that this is not the Law? Finally, if—as you would take it an intolerable reproach, and be inflamed, if anyone called you negligent of this precept by which it was said, You shall not kill, or You shall not commit adultery—you would likewise blaze up if anyone called you both uncircumcised and negligent of the Sabbath; then there would doubtless be an understanding that both were law and a commandment of God. But as it is, both for those former ones you seek praise and glory, if you keep them; and for these you fear no loss of the same good, because you despise them. Wherefore it is established that these, as I said, are not the Law, but rather the spots and the scab of the Law: which, if they are condemned by us, are condemned as false, not as lawful. Nor does this reproach touch the Law, nor God the author of the Law, but those who inscribed him and it with their nefarious religions. But that we sometimes assail the revered name of the Law, when we attack the Jewish precepts, comes about by your fault, who wish there to be no distinction between the Hebrew institutions and the Law: otherwise, render to the Law its proper dignity, cut away the Israelite basenesses from it like warts, impute the crime of its disfigurement to the writers, and you will at once see that we have been enemies of Judaism, not of the Law. It is the name of the Law that deceives you: because you do not know to whom it ought rightly to be ascribed.

**3.** To these things, why you should suppose that we blaspheme your Prophets and Patriarchs, I do not see. For if these things which they are read to have committed had been written or dictated by us, this accusation of yours against us would be not irrational; but where the same things were written either by themselves, against the custom of honesty, snatching glory from vices, or by their associates and equals, what fault in this is ours? For we condemn, detesting them, the iniquitous deeds which the guilty confessed of themselves, of their own accord and unasked; or, if the malice of the writers feigned these things against them through envy, let the writers be punished, let their books be condemned, let the prophetic name be cleansed of unworthy report, let the authority of the Patriarchs be restored to its own gravity and censorship.

**4.** And indeed it could be that, just as those same writers shamelessly feigned so many things about God—now saying that he dwelt from eternity in darkness, and afterward marveled when he had seen the light; now ignorant of the future, so that he gave to Adam a precept which Adam would not keep; now even improvident, so that he could not see his man hiding in a corner of paradise after his nakedness was known; now even envious and fearful, lest, if his man tasted of the tree of life, he should live forever; now, on other occasions, both desiring blood and fat from every kind of sacrifice, and jealous if the same were offered to others as to himself; and now angry at strangers, now even at his own; now destroying thousands of men for light or even no offenses; now even threatening that he would come with a sword and spare no one, neither the just nor the sinner—it could be, I say, that they lied also about the men of God, who lied about God himself with such great impudence. But you, agree with us, that the writers may bear the charge, if you wish the Prophets to be freed of it.

**5.** Otherwise, neither did we write those things about Abraham, that, burning with an insane desire of having offspring, and by no means believing God, who had already promised him this from Sarah his wife, he wallowed with a concubine—the more dishonorably—with his wife's knowledge[^2]. Nor that, a most infamous huckster of his own marriage, for the sake of avarice and the belly he, lying to two kings, Abimelech and Pharaoh, at different times, that the said Sarah his wife was his sister, because she was most beautiful, sold her for intercourse[^3]. Nor that Lot, his own kinsman, freed from Sodom, lay with his two daughters on the mountain[^4]—who would more honorably have burned in Sodom by the stroke of lightning than blazed on the mountain with the flame of unlawful lust. But neither that Isaac did the same and equal things as his father, with respect to Rebecca his wife, himself too feigning that she was his sister, that through her he might live basely[^5]. Nor that Jacob his son, between Rachel and Leah, two own sisters, and each of their handmaids—the husband of four wives—wandered like a goat; so that daily there was a contest among the four harlots, which of them should first seize him, as he came from the field, for intercourse, and sometimes even hired him from one another for the night by wages[^6]. Likewise that Judah his son slept with Tamar his daughter-in-law, after the marriages of one and another son, deceived, as they say, by the garb of prostitution[^7] into which she had transformed herself—she who well knew that her father-in-law always had dealings with this kind of women. Nor that David, after so many wives in number, also committed adultery with the little wife of Uriah his soldier, and destroyed him in the war[^8]. Nor that Solomon his son had three hundred wives and seven hundred concubines, and kings' daughters without number[^9]. Nor that Hosea, first of the prophets, had children by a fornicating woman: to which baseness, that it might be worse, the counsel of God too is ascribed[^10]. But neither that Moses committed homicide[^11], that he despoiled Egypt[^12], that he waged wars, that he both commanded and did many cruel things[^13], that he himself was not even content with one marriage. These things, I say, and the like, which are found in their various books, were neither written nor dictated by us; but either these are false inventions of your writers, or the crimes of the fathers are true. Choose which of the two you wish: for we must detest either these or those alike, since we hate the wicked and base as much as the lying.

**6.** AUGUSTINE replied: You understand neither the sacraments of the law nor the deeds of the Prophets, because you know how to think of neither holiness nor righteousness. But about the precepts and sacraments of the Old Testament we have already said much and often, that it might be understood that there was one thing there which, through the grace of the New Testament, was given to be fulfilled by doing it, and another which, through the truth laid open, was shown by being removed to be fulfilled: since, by the love of God and neighbor, the precept of the Law was undertaken to be perfected, but, by the cessation of circumcision and the other sacraments of that time, the promise of the Law was shown to be paid out. For the precept made men guilty, unto the desiring of salvation; but the promise celebrated figures, unto the awaiting of the Savior: so that through the coming of the New Testament grace bestowed might free the men, and truth rendered might remove the figures. For the very Law which was given through Moses became grace and truth through Jesus Christ[^14]: grace, namely, so that, with pardon of sins given, what was commanded might be kept by God's gift; but truth, so that, with the observance of shadows removed, what was promised might be made present by faith in God.

**7.** Accordingly these people, who, reproaching the things they do not understand, say that the promissory figures of the sacraments are a leprosy or scab or warts of the Law, are like men who are displeased by things whose usefulness they do not grasp: as if a deaf man, seeing the lips of speakers move, should reproach the motions of the mouth as superfluous and ugly; or as if some blind man, when a certain house had been praised to him, should wish to test by touching what is said, and, handling the smoothness of the walls with his hand, should rush upon the windows, and find fault with them as unsuited to that evenness, and think them ruinous hollows.

**8.** Now that the deeds of the Prophets too were themselves prophetic and mystical—what shall I do that they may understand it, whose minds vanity has so occupied that they suppose it is believed by us that even God himself once dwelt in darkness, because it is written, *Darkness was over the deep*[^15]—as though we called God the deep, where the darkness was, because light was not there, before God by his word made light? But because they do not distinguish between the light that is God himself and the light that God made, they therefore think it follows that he himself was in darkness before he made light, since darkness was over the deep before he said, *Let there be light; and light was made*[^16]. For just as in the New Testament both are said of him—for we read there both, *God is light, and there is no darkness in him*[^17], and likewise there, *God, who said that light should shine forth from darkness, has shone in our hearts*[^18]—so too in those ancient books both: it was said of the Wisdom of God, which surely was not made, since through it all things were made[^19], *It is the brightness of eternal light*[^20]; and of a certain light, which can be made only through it, it is said there in this manner: *You will enlighten my lamp, O Lord; my God, you will enlighten my darkness*[^21]. Just as also in the beginning, when darkness was over the deep, God said, *Let there be light; and light was made*—which he would not have made, except the light-making Light, which is God.

**9.** For just as God suffices for himself unto eternal blessedness, and from this abounds to make others blessed, so he suffices for himself unto eternal light, and from this abounds to make others enlightened: desiring no one's good, since by him every good will enjoys; fearing no one's evil, since by him every evil will is deserted; for neither does he who is blessed by his gift increase him, nor does he who is wretched by his judgment terrify him. Such a God, Manicheans, you do not worship; far from him have you been made, while you follow your phantasms, which your vain and wandering heart, drawing in this light of the heavenly bodies through the eyes of the flesh, has, by a manifold fiction, expanded and varied for you. This light, although God too made it, is far and incomparably surpassed even by that light which God made in the minds of the devout, which he makes light from darkness, just as he justifies from impiety: how much more by that inaccessible light, which makes all these things? Nor is it inaccessible to all: for *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*[^22]. But *God is light, and there is no darkness in him*: but the impious shall not see the light, as Isaiah says[^23]. To such, then, that light-making Light is inaccessible, which made not only that spiritual light in the minds of the saints, but also this corporeal light: not that it forbids the wicked to approach it, but that it makes it to rise upon the good and the bad[^24].

**10.** When, therefore, darkness was over the deep, he who was light said, *Let there be light.* That Light made the light is manifest; for it is manifestly set down, *God said*; yet what light he made is not so manifest. For whether God then made that light which is in the minds of the angels—that is, the rational spirits themselves—or a certain corporeal light, removed even from our sight in the lofty places of this world, is disputed harmoniously among the studious of the divine Scriptures. For on the fourth day he made these conspicuous luminaries of heaven. Whether these too were made together with their own light, or were in a manner kindled from that which had already been made, is likewise asked. But whatever light was made, when, with darkness over the deep, God said, *Let there be light*—that a created light was made by the creating Light no one doubts who, by reading the holy Scriptures devoutly, becomes worthy to understand.

**11.** Nor is God therefore to be thought to have dwelt in darkness before he made light, because *the Spirit of God was borne over the waters*[^25], when it had been said before, *Darkness was over the deep.* For the deep is an immeasurable depth of waters. Whence it can occur to carnal prudence that the Spirit of God dwelt, as it were, in that darkness which was over the deep, of whom it was said, *He was borne over the waters*—to one not understanding how light shines in darkness, and the darkness does not comprehend it[^26], unless they become light through the word of God, and it be said to them: *You were once darkness, but now are light in the Lord*[^27]. But if rational minds, made dark through an impious will, cannot comprehend the light of the wisdom of God, nowhere absent—because they are far from it in disposition, not in place—what wonder if the Spirit of God, who was borne over the waters, was borne even over the darkness of the waters, by an incomparable distance indeed, but of substance, not of places?

**12.** I know indeed to how deaf men I sing these things; yet I do not despair that the truth of my song will find its own ear, which the Lord has opened, from whom are the true things we say. But these people—what judges of the divine Scriptures we endure!—are displeased even that God's works pleased God, whom they reproach as having marveled, as it were, at an unaccustomed light, because it is written, *And God saw the light, that it is good*[^28]? For he approves his works, because the things he made please him; and this is to see that they are good. For neither is he compelled to do anything unwilling, so as to do what does not please him; nor does he slip improvidently into making anything, so that, once made, it displeases him. But why should it not displease these people that our God saw his work, that it is good—since their god, when he plunged his members into darkness, set a veil against himself? For he did not see what he made, that it is good, but was unwilling to see, because it is evil.

**13.** Faustus indeed said that our God marveled, which is not written; nor is it at all consequent that, when someone has seen that a thing is good, he should also be said to have marveled. For seeing many good things, we do not marvel, as though they were so beyond our expectation; but we only approve that they ought to be so. Nevertheless, we show them that—not in the Old Testament, which they maliciously slander, but in the New, which, to deceive the ignorant, they accept—God did marvel. For they confess Christ to be God, and set this in their snare as a most sweet bait, by which to catch those devoted to Christ. God, then, marveled, when Christ marveled: for thus it is written in the Gospel, that, having heard the faith of a certain centurion, he marveled, and said to his disciples: *Amen, I say to you, I have not found such great faith in Israel*[^29]. Behold, we have expounded as we could, *God saw that it is good*; and better men perhaps expound these things better: let these people too expound why Jesus marveled at what, before it happened, he foreknew, and, before he heard it, he surely knew. For although it makes a great difference whether someone sees that a thing is good, or even marvels: yet in this there is some likeness, because Jesus too marveled at the light of faith, which he himself had made in the heart of that centurion—he who is the true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world[^30].

**14.** Whom indeed some impious pagan could so slander and reproach in the Gospel as Faustus does God in the Old Testament. For he too would call Christ improvident, not only from this, that he marveled at the centurion's faith, but also that he chose Judas among the disciples, who was not going to keep his commandments[^31]: just as this man reproaches why a precept was given in paradise to a man who would not keep it[^32]. He would also find fault with this, that he could not know who had touched him, when she who suffered the flow of blood touched the hem of his garment: just as this man found fault that God did not know where Adam was hiding. I suppose he said, *Adam, where are you?*[^33] as Christ said, *Who touched me?*[^34] He would call him envious and fearful, lest, if the other five virgins entered into his kingdom, they should live forever; against whom he so shut the door that he did not open even to those knocking pitiably[^35], as if forgetting what he himself had promised, saying, *Knock, and it shall be opened to you*[^36]: just as this man accuses God of envy and fear, because he did not admit a sinner to eternal life. He would call him desirous, not of the blood of cattle, but of men, because he said, *He who loses his soul for my sake will find it unto eternal life*[^37]: just as this man wished to slander concerning the sacrifices of animals, by which figures was promised the sacrifice of the blood by which we have been redeemed. He would reproach him as jealous, because, when he had cast out by scourging the buyers and sellers from the temple, the Evangelist mentioned that it was written of him, *The zeal of your house has consumed me*[^38]: just as this man accused God of being jealous, because he had forbidden sacrifice to be offered to others. He would call him angry at his own and at strangers: at his own, indeed, because he said, *The servant who knows his master's will and does not do things worthy of it will be beaten with many stripes*[^39]; but at strangers, because he said, *If anyone does not receive you, shake off the dust from your shoes upon them: amen, I say to you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom on the day of judgment than for that city*[^40]: just as this man charges God with being angry, now at strangers, now at his own, both of whom the Apostle mentions, saying, *For whoever sinned without the Law will perish without the Law; and whoever sinned in the Law will be judged by the Law*[^41]. He would call him a slaughterer too, and a shedder of the blood of many for light or even no offenses. For a light or no offense it would seem to a pagan either not to have a wedding garment at the wedding feast, on account of which our King in the Gospel commanded the man, with hands and feet bound, to be cast into the outer darkness[^42]; or to be unwilling that Christ should reign over them, on account of which sin he says, *But those who were unwilling that I should reign over them, bring here and kill before me*[^43]: just as this man accused God in the Old Testament, who seemed to him to slaughter thousands of men for light or no offenses. Now as for what Faustus reproaches—God threatening that he would come with a sword, by which he would spare neither the just nor the sinner—how would that pagan reproach it, hearing the apostle Paul say of our God, *that he did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all*[^44]; hearing Peter too, when he spoke of the great tribulations and slayings of the saints, exhorting to endurance and saying, *It is time for judgment to begin at the house of the Lord; and if the beginning is from us, what will be the end of those who do not believe the Gospel of the Lord? And if the just man will scarcely be saved, where will the sinner and the impious appear?*[^45] For what is more just than the Only-begotten, whom yet the Father did not spare? And what is more evident, that he spares not even the just, amending them by the variety of tribulations, since on this matter it is openly said, *And if the just man will scarcely be saved*? For not only in the Old Testament is it written, *For whom God loves, he reproves; and he scourges every son whom he receives*[^46]; and, *If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, why should we not endure evil things?*[^47]—but also in the New: *Those whom I love, I rebuke and chastise*[^48]; and that: *For if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged by the Lord; but when we are judged, we are reproved by the Lord, lest we be condemned with the world*[^49]. But still, if a pagan reproached such things in the New Testament as these reproach in the Old, would they not themselves undertake to defend them? But if they could do this, by what madness, finally, do they here reproach such things as they there defend? But if they could not, why do they grant that in one Testament only, and not rather in both, what to the impious who do not understand would seem perverse should be believed by the devout who do not understand to be right, but veiled?

**15.** Or do they perhaps dare to say that those similar things which we have brought forward from the New Testament are themselves too false and perverse, by that diabolical privilege of theirs, that whatever is in the Gospel or the canonical Epistles by which they think their heresy can be aided, they hold and persuade was said by Christ and the Apostles; but whatever from those same codices sounds against them, they do not hesitate, with shameless and sacrilegious mouth, to call inserted by falsifiers? To which madness of theirs, trying to extinguish and abolish the authority of all the books, I have already above, as far as the plan of the work undertaken seemed to permit, given no few answers.

**16.** Now I give this admonition: that, since they strive to veil their insane and sacrilegious fables with the cloak of the Christian name, they may yet see that, when they dispute these things against the Christian Scriptures, the truth of the divine codices of both Testaments is defended by us not only against the pagans, but also against the Manicheans. And these things indeed, which just now Faustus set down in his discourse as unworthy of God, from our ancient writings, against a pagan reproaching such things in the evangelical or apostolic discourse too, I would perhaps so defend as to mention equal things about their own authors, as our Paul did among the Athenians[^50]. For I would perhaps find in their writings too: a God, creator and fabricator of the world, and establisher of this light, who yet, before he founded it, did not lie in darkness; and one elated with joy from his perfected work, which is surely more than "He saw that it is good"; and a giver of law, which, if a man followed, he would do to his own good, but if he spurned, to his own evil—whom they would not on that account call ignorant of the future, because he gave a law even to those who would despise it in the future. Nor again would they call him improvident, in that he asks something, in whose books many things are asked for no other reason than that each may be convicted by his own answers; since he who asks not only knows what he wishes to be answered him by another, but also that the other will so answer. But that God envies anyone, in that he would not allow the wicked to become blessed, if he wished to say this, he would find their books full on this matter, pertaining to divine providence.

**17.** But about sacrifices a pagan would object nothing else to me than why we reproach those among them, when in our ancient books our God is read to have commanded such things to be offered to himself. Here I, discussing the true sacrifice perhaps more broadly, would demonstrate that it is owed to none but the one true God—the sacrifice which the one true Priest offered to him, the Mediator of God and men[^51]; whose promissory figures had to be celebrated in the victims of animals, on account of the commendation of the future flesh and blood, through which one victim there would be a remission of the sins contracted from flesh and blood; which will not possess the kingdom of God, because the same substance of the body will be changed into a heavenly quality—which the fire in the sacrifice signified, as it were absorbing death into victory[^52]. And in that people these things were rightly celebrated, whose both kingdom and priesthood were a prophecy of the future King and Priest, to rule and consecrate the faithful in all nations, and to lead them into the kingdom of heaven and the sanctuary of the angels and eternal life. As, therefore, the Hebrews celebrated the religious proclamations of this true sacrifice, so the pagans the sacrilegious imitations: since *what the gentiles immolate, they immolate to demons and not to God*, says the Apostle[^53]. For it is an ancient thing, the foretelling immolation of blood, testifying to the future passion of the Mediator from the beginning of the human race: for Abel is found in the sacred writings to have offered this first[^54]. It is not, then, a wonder if the prevaricating angels—whose two greatest vices are pride and deceit—flying through this air, exacted for themselves from their worshipers what they knew to be owed to the one true God, by whom they wished to be thought gods, the vanity of the human heart giving them place: especially when, from desire of the dead, images were set up, whence the use of idols arose[^55]; and, with greater flattery, divine honors were paid to them as though received into heaven, in whose place demons substituted themselves to be worshiped on earth, and demanded sacrifice to be offered to themselves by the deceived and lost. Sacrifice, then, not only when the true God justly commands it, but even when a false god proudly exacts it, sufficiently shows to whom it is owed. If these things were harder for that pagan to believe, I would persuade him even by prophecy itself, in which were written so long beforehand the things I would show now fulfilled. But if he despised this too, I would acknowledge it rather than marvel: since I would recall, in the truth of that same prophecy, that not all were going to believe.

**18.** But if he objected to me a jealous Christ or God from either Testament, and assailed the very word, he would show nothing else than that he is either ignorant or careless of all literature. For although their learned men distinguish between "will" and "cupidity," "joy" and "gladness," "caution" and "fear," "clemency" and "mercy," "prudence" and "cunning," "confidence" and "audacity"; and many in this manner, so that, in these pairs of words, those I set first they assign to virtues, those I set second to vices—yet their books are full of the abuse of those names which properly signify vices, when even virtues are so called; when either "cupidity" is set for "will," or "gladness" for "joy," or "fear" for "caution," or "mercy" for "clemency," or "cunning" for "prudence," or "audacity" for "confidence." And who could mention all the things that the custom of speech usurps for a like license? To this is added also a certain proper character of each several language. For in ecclesiastical writings I nowhere recall mercy set down in dispraise. With which the custom of daily speech too agrees. The Greeks call by one name two things indeed neighboring, but yet distinct, "toil" and "pain"; we express these by single names: just as by us "life" is called by one name, whether according to which we say "He lives," because he is not lifeless, or according to which we say "He is a man of good life"; but the Greeks signify these two by two words also. Whence it can be that, apart from the abuse of words, which lies wide open in all languages, by some proper character of the Hebrew language too "jealousy" is set in both senses: whether when a mind, troubled by the adultery of a spouse, pines away—which cannot befall God—or when a diligent guard is applied to preserving conjugal chastity, which, that God does it when he addresses his people as a spouse whom he does not wish to fornicate after many false gods, it is useful for us to confess not only without doubt but even with thanksgiving. This too I would say of the anger of God: for God is not troubled when he inflicts wrath; but "anger" is set down for "vengeance," whether by abuse, or by some proper character of the original language.

**19.** But about the thousands of men killed he would not marvel, if he did not deny the judgment of God: which not even the pagans deny, who grant that this universe is ruled and administered by the providence of God, from the highest to the lowest. But if he denied this too, he would be convicted either more easily by the authority of his own people, or somewhat longer by the disputation of sure reasonings; or, as too hard and stolid, he would be left to that very divine judgment which he would not believe to exist. Further, the light or no offenses, for which God killed men, if he mentioned them expressly, we would show to be neither none nor light: as, for example, what we set down concerning the wedding garment[^56], we would demonstrate how great a wickedness it was to approach the sacred wedding seeking glory there not of the bridegroom but his own; or if perhaps that garment were found to signify something else by a better understanding: or, that those are killed before the eyes of the king who were unwilling that he should reign over them[^57], it would appear, perhaps without a long discourse of ours, that just as it is no fault of a man if he were unwilling that any man should reign over him, so it is no or but a small fault to be unwilling that he should reign over him, in whose kingdom alone one lives rightly, blessedly, and forever.

**20.** Now that last point which Faustus set down, lying in wait against the ancient books as though they disparaged God, that he threatens a sword by which he will spare no one, neither the just nor the sinner—when it was expounded to a pagan how it is to be taken, he would perhaps resist neither the New nor the Old Testament, and the evangelical likeness would please him, which to these people, who wish to be held Christians, either does not appear, as to the blind, or displeases, as to the perverse. For that highest husbandman of the vine[^58] prepares his pruning-knife one way for the fruitful shoots, another for the unfruitful; yet he spares neither the good nor the reprobate—the former to be purged, the latter to be cut off. For there is no man endowed with such great righteousness that the temptation of tribulation is not necessary for him, whether to perfect, or to confirm, or to prove his virtue: unless perhaps these people do not number even Paul the apostle among the just, who, although he humbly and truly confesses his past sins, yet gives thanks that he was justified by faith in Jesus Christ[^59]. Or did he spare him, whom the vain do not understand saying, *I will spare neither the just nor the sinner*? Let them, then, hear him himself: *And lest I be exalted,* he says, *by the greatness of the revelations, there was given me a thorn of the flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet me; on account of which I thrice asked the Lord to take it from me, and he said to me: My grace is sufficient for you; for power is perfected in weakness*[^60]. Behold, he spared not even the just man, that he might perfect his power in weakness—he who had given him an angel of Satan to buffet him: unless you say that the devil gave it. The devil, then, was acting that Paul might not be exalted by the greatness of the revelations, and that his power might be perfected. Who would say this? By him, therefore, the just man had been handed over to be buffeted by an angel of Satan, who through him handed over the unjust too to Satan himself: of whom he says the same, *Whom I handed over to Satan, that they may learn not to blaspheme*[^61]. Do you now understand how he from above spares neither the just nor the sinner? Or, because there a sword is named, do you shudder the more? For it is one thing to be buffeted, another to be killed. As if thousands of martyrs were not laid low by diverse kinds of deaths, or as if the persecutors really had this in their power, unless it had been given them from above by him who said, *I will spare neither the just nor the sinner*; since the very Lord of the martyrs, whom *the Father did not spare*[^62], says most openly to Pilate, *You would have no power over me, unless it had been given you from above*[^63]. These pressures and persecutions of the just the same Paul says are an example of the judgment of God[^64]. Which sentence is more broadly made manifest by the apostle Peter, as I mentioned above, where he says that it is time for judgment to begin at the house of the Lord. *And if the beginning,* he says, *is from us, what will be the end of those who do not believe the Gospel of God? And if the just man will scarcely be saved, where will the sinner and the impious appear?*[^65] For from this it is understood how the impious are not spared, as shoots cut off for burning, when the just are not spared for the perfecting of their purgation. For Peter himself too testifies that these things happen by the will of him who in the ancient books says, *I will spare neither the just nor the sinner.* For he himself too says: *It is better, if the Spirit of God so wills, to suffer doing good than doing evil*[^66]. When, therefore, by the will of the Spirit of God those doing good suffer, the just are not spared; but when those doing evil, sinners are not spared: yet both happen according to the will of him who says, *I will spare neither the just nor the sinner*—scourging the one as a son, punishing the other as impious.

**21.** Behold, I have shown, as far as I could, that we do not worship a God dwelling from eternity in darkness, but him who is light, and there is no darkness in him[^67], and who in himself dwells in inaccessible light[^68], of which eternal light the brightness is the coeternal Wisdom[^69]. Nor a marveler at an unforeseen light, but the creator of the light made, that it might subsist; the approver of it, that it might remain. Nor ignorant of the future, but the commander of a precept and the condemner of an offense, so that, by a vengeance justly pronounced against disobedience, he might restrain the present and terrify those to come. Nor improvident, seeking by not knowing, but judging by asking. Nor envious and fearful, but justly forbidding the transgressor from the eternal life which is given to the justly obedient. Nor desirous of blood and fat, but, sacrifices suited to a carnal people being imposed, promising through certain figures the true sacrifice. Nor jealous with an envious disturbance, but with a tranquil goodness, lest the soul, owing chastity to the one God, be defiled, corrupted and prostituted through many false ones. Nor, with an anger as it were human, raging in turmoil, but, with another, divine anger, severely repaying what is just—which is named anger, by a certain usage of speech, not on account of a lust for avenging, but on account of the vigor of judging. Nor slaying thousands of men for light or no offenses, but, by a most equitable examination, through the temporal deaths of mortals, imposing on peoples the usefulness of fearing him. Nor, with no discernment, in blind confusion punishing the just and sinners, but distributing to the just a healthful reproof for the sake of perfection, but to sinners due severity for the sake of equity. Whence it appears, Manicheans, that you have been deceived by your suspicions, when, by understanding our Scriptures badly, or by experiencing bad interpreters, you believe false things about the Catholics; and thus, having abandoned sound doctrine, turned to sacrilegious fables, and being too perverse and estranged from the fellowship of the saints, you are unwilling to be corrected even from the New Testament, whence we bring forth such things as you accuse in the Old. Whence it comes that against you, as against the pagans, we are compelled to defend both Testaments.

**22.** But suppose someone utterly carnal, so doting as to worship God—not such as we worship, who is one and true, but such as you say we worship, who has been feigned by your slanders or suspicions: does not even this man worship a better one than you? For I ask, attend and open whatever eyes you have—for there is no need of great sharpness of wit, that what I shall say may be perceived; I call upon all, prudent and imprudent: hear, attend, judge. For how much better would it have been that your god had dwelt from eternity in darkness than that he should have plunged into darkness a light coeternal with himself and akin to him? How much better that he should marvel at and praise a new light arisen for him to put the darkness to flight, than that he should be unable to avoid old darkness rushing upon him, except by his own light being darkened? Unhappy, if disturbed; cruel, if he did this secure. For surely it were better that he should see a light made by himself, and marvel at it as good, than that he should make evil a light begotten of himself, which so repelled from him the hostile darkness that it became hostile to him. For this will be imputed as fault to those relics to be condemned in the globe—that they allowed themselves to stray from their former luminous nature, and stood forth as enemies of the holy light: which, before it befell them, from eternity, if they did not know this would be theirs, they suffered the eternal darkness of ignorance; but if they did know, the eternal darkness of fear. Behold, truly a part and substance of your god dwelt from eternity in its own darkness; nor afterward did it marvel at a new light, but ran into other, alien darkness, which it always feared. Further, that very God, whose part it was, if he feared so great an evil to come for that same part of his, the darkness of fear too had seized even him; but if he did not know this would be, he was blinded by the darkness of ignorance; but if he knew this would be his part's, and did not fear, the darkness of so great cruelty is worse than that either of ignorance or of fear: for your god did not have what the Apostle so praises in the very flesh which you most insanely believe made not by God but by Hyle: *If one member suffers, all the members suffer with it*[^70]. But we do not accuse: he foreknew, feared, grieved, but had nothing he could do. In these darknesses, then, of his own misery he dwelt from eternity; nor afterward did he marvel at a new light, which would put the darkness to flight from him, but experienced, to the great evil of his light, other darkness, which he always feared. How much better that—I will not say he should give a precept like a god, but should receive a precept like a man, which he would keep to his own good, despise to his own evil, yet in either motion of mind use free will—rather than that, pressed by an inevitable necessity, against his will, he should be forced to the darkening of his light? For it would surely have been much better that he should give a precept to a human nature which he did not know would sin, than that, pressed by necessity, he should compel his own divine nature to sin. Wake up, and tell us how he conquers darkness, whom necessity conquers. This was already with him a greater enemy, by which, conquered and commanded, he fought with the lesser. How much better that he should not know whither Adam had fled from his face, than that he himself should have nowhere to flee, first from the face of hard and dire necessity, and afterward from the face of the diverse and adverse nation? How much better that he should envy the human nature a blessed life, than that he should give his own divine nature over to misery: that he should desire the blood and fat of sacrifices, rather than that he himself should so often be even slain for idols, mingled with the fat and blood of all victims: that he should be troubled with jealousy, if those sacrifices were offered to other gods too, rather than that he himself, bound not only in all fruits but even in all flesh, should be offered through all altars to all demons? How much better that he, moved and troubled even with a human indignation, should be angry at sinners, both his own and strangers, than that he himself should be troubled not only in all the angry, but even in all the fearful, defiled in all sinners, punished in all the condemned; everywhere bound out of that part of his which, innocent, he himself condemned to such disgrace, that through it he might conquer what he feared; he himself too condemned under so deadly a necessity, that the part of him condemned could pardon him, if, since he is now wretched, he were at least humble? But as it is, who could endure that God be reproached by you for being angry at his own and strangers when they sin, when the god you feign condemns in the globe his own members, which, compelled, he compelled to go into the jaws of sin? Which indeed, when he does it, as you say, he will not have anger. But I marvel if he will have the brow to inflict, as it were, vengeance upon those from whom he ought to ask pardon, and to say: "I beg you, pardon, you are my members; when could I have done this to you, except conquered by necessity? You yourselves know that, when I sent you here, a dreadful enemy had burst out; but that I now bind you here, I fear lest he burst out again." Now surely you confess even this, that it is much better to kill thousands of men by a temporal death for no or a light fault, than both to hand over his own members—that is, the members of God, the substance of God, and plainly God—into the abyss of sin, and to condemn them by the binding of everlasting punishment. For if those members had free choice of sinning or not sinning—although, of the substance of God, which is truly the substance of God and on this account utterly immutable, how this could be said is not found. For God can in no way sin, as he cannot deny himself[^71]; but a man can sin, and deny God; yet if he does not wish, he does not do it: if, then, those members of your god, like a human and rational soul, had, as I said, free choice of will to sin or not sin, perhaps they would rightly be punished for grave crimes with that punishment of the globe; but as it is, you cannot say that those particles had freedom of will, which the whole god himself did not have: because, if he did not send them into sin, the whole, overrun by the nation of darkness, would be compelled to sin; but if they could not be compelled, he sinned when he sent them where they could be compelled; and therefore he himself is rather worthy of that as it were parricidal sack, who did this by free command, than they who, by obeying, went there where they lost free choice of living rightly. But if even he could be compelled to sin, when overrun and possessed, unless he had provided for himself first by the disgrace, then by the punishment, of his own part, then there was no free will in your god, nor in his parts: let him not feign himself a judge, but acknowledge himself guilty; not because he suffered what he did not wish, but because he pretends to repay just things, condemning those whom he knows to have suffered evil rather than done it: which he pretends only to this end, lest he be found conquered; as though it profited any wretch to be called happy or fortunate. Surely even this was better, that your god, without any examination of equity, should spare men neither just nor sinners (which Faustus, understanding nothing, set last in his reproach of our God), than that he should so rage against his own members: so that it is too little that he offered them to the enemies to be inexpiably poisoned, unless he also accuses them with the false charge of iniquity. Which he therefore rightly says hang under so monstrous and endless a punishment, because they allowed themselves to stray from their former luminous nature, and stood forth as enemies of the holy light. Whence, except because, as he himself says, they were so engrained in the first greed of the princes of darkness that they could not recall their own origin, nor distinguish themselves from the hostile nature? Therefore souls of this kind did no evil themselves, but, innocent, only suffered so great an evil. By whose doing, except that first one's, who commanded that they should proceed from him into so great an evil? They experienced, then, a worse father than enemy. For the father sent them into so great an evil; but the enemy sought them as a good, desiring to enjoy, not to harm: the one knowingly harmed, the other unknowingly. But the weak and needy god could not provide for himself otherwise, first against the wicked enemy, and afterward against the one shut up. Let him, then, at least not accuse those by whose obedience he is safe, by whose death he is secure. For if he was compelled to fight, was he compelled to slander too? For when they allowed themselves to stray from their former luminous nature and stood forth as enemies of the holy light, they were surely compelled to this by the enemy; whom if they were unable to resist, innocent they are condemned; but if they were able, and were unwilling, why do you still so fabulously introduce a nature of evil, when the origin of sin is from the will itself? For this they did surely by their own fault, not by an alien force, in that, when they were able to resist evil, they were unwilling. For since, if they did so, they would do well, but if they did not, they would sin grievously and monstrously: if they were able and did not, they surely were unwilling. If, then, they were unwilling, it is a fault of will, not of necessity. From the will, therefore, is the beginning of sin; but whence the beginning of sin, thence the beginning of evil, whether of doing against a just precept, or of suffering according to a just judgment. Accordingly there is no cause why, seeking whence evil is, you should have rushed into so great an evil of this error, as to call a nature abounding in so many goods a nature of evil, and to set in the nature of the highest good, before the commingling of a nature of evil, the horrendous evil of necessity. For of this error of yours too the cause is pride, which you will not have, if you do not wish; but you, while you wish to defend in some way that into which you have rushed, take away the origin of sin from the choice of the will, and set it in a vain and false fable of the nature of evil. And on this account it remains that you say that even those souls to be condemned in the horrible globe by an eternal binding stood forth as enemies of the holy light not by will but by necessity; and you constitute such a god as your judge, with whom you can profit nothing for those whose cause, with necessity demonstrated, you defend; and such a king, from whom you cannot obtain pardon for your brethren, his sons and members, whose enmities against you and himself you declare to have arisen not by will but by necessity. O most monstrous cruelty!—except that you turn to his defense, to excuse him too, that he does this by necessity. If, then, you could find another judge, who, free from the chain of necessity, would stand forth as a moderator of equity, he would surely not fix this one outside the globe, but shut him up within, together with the terrible enemy himself. For why should he not justly be first for the punishment of condemnation, who is first for the crime of necessity? How much better, then, would you choose, in comparison with the worse, a god—not such as we worship, but such as you either feign or suppose us to worship—who, without any examination of equity, without any distinction of condemnation and discipline, would spare neither his servants, neither the just nor the sinner, rather than that he should not spare his own members, whether innocent, if necessity is no crime, or, by obeying him, made guilty, if necessity too is a crime: so that they should be condemned forever by him, with whom they ought either to have been absolved together, if after the victory freedom revived, or to have been condemned together, if even after the victory necessity at least availed so much that equity too availed something. But as you feign a god—not that true and highest one whom we worship, but some other false one, I know not whom, whom you either judge or slander us as worshiping, who yet is himself too much better than your god (for both do not exist, and both are feigned by you, but you feign better the one you accuse as ours than the one you adore as yours).

**23.** So too you reproach the Patriarchs and Prophets not such as they are honored by us, but such as, not understanding our books, you have feigned in malevolent vanity: yet, even supposing them such as you suspect them to have been, it is too little if I say they are better than your Elect, who keep all the commandments of Mani, unless I also show that they are better than your very god—which I will not undertake to demonstrate, unless I first defend our holy fathers the Patriarchs and Prophets from your accusations, with the Lord helping me, against carnal hearts, by clear reasoning. And to you indeed, Manicheans, it would suffice so to reply that we should teach that even the vices you think ours are to be preferred to the praises of yours, adding, to the heap of your confusion, that even your god is found far worse than the men such as you boast our fathers to have been. So, then, as I said, it would suffice to reply to you: but since some, even apart from your empty talk, are stirred of their own accord, comparing the life of the Prophets in the Old Testament with the life of the Apostles in the New, and unable to discern the custom of that time in which the promise was veiled from the custom of this time in which the promise is revealed—to these I am the more compelled to reply, who either dare to prefer their own temperance to the Prophets, or seek for their wickedness patronage from the Prophets.

**24.** On this matter I say this first: that of those men not only the tongue but also the life was prophetic; and that the whole of that kingdom of the Hebrew nation was a certain great prophet, because the prophet of a certain great one. Wherefore, as far as concerns those who there were learned in heart in the wisdom of God, the prophecy of the coming Christ and of the Church is to be searched out not only in the things they said, but also in the things they did; but as far as concerns the rest, and together all the men of that nation, in the things that were done divinely in them or about them. For all those things, as the Apostle says, *were figures of us*[^72].

**25.** But these people, in certain deeds from whose loftiness they are far removed, reproach as it were the lust of the Prophets, just as certain sacrilegious pagans too reproach as it were the foolishness, or rather the madness, of Christ, because at a season of the year unsuited to it he sought fruit on a tree[^73]; or as a disposition of a certain childish silliness, that with head bowed he wrote with his finger on the ground, and, when he had answered the men questioning him, began again to do this[^74]. For they have no understanding, nor do they perceive that in great souls certain virtues are most like the vices of little ones—by some appearance, but by no comparison of equity. And those who reproach these things in great men are like ignorant boys in school, who, when they have learned, as a great thing, that a verb of the singular number must be rendered to a noun of the singular number, reproach the most learned author of the Latin tongue, because he said, *Pars in frusta secant* ("Part cut into pieces")[^75]. For he ought, they say, to have said *secat*. And because they know it is called *religio*, they blame him because with a doubled letter he said, *relligione patrum*[^76]. Whence it might perhaps not absurdly be said that, in their own kind, by as much as the figures and metaplasms of the learned are distant from the solecisms and barbarisms of the ignorant, by so much are the figured deeds of the Prophets distant from the lustful sins of the wicked: and on this account, just as a boy reproached for a barbarism, if he wished to defend himself by Virgil's metaplasm, would be beaten with ferules, so whoever, having wallowed with the handmaid of his own wife, should take up Abraham's deed—that he begot offspring from Hagar—as an example for his defense, would, would that he were corrected, not indeed with ferules, but at least with cudgels, lest with the other adulterers he be punished with eternal punishment. Those things indeed are least, but these are great; nor is the likeness drawn to this end, that a figure should be equated with a sacrament, and a solecism with adultery: yet by the proportion of each its own kind, what in those certain virtues or vices of speech skill or unskill avails, this in these virtues or vices of morals—though in a far diverse kind—wisdom or unwisdom avails.

**26.** Wherefore, lest we rush rashly into praising or blaming, accusing or defending, restraining or relaxing, condemning or absolving, seeking or avoiding any things—in all of which sins or right deeds are involved—we ought first to consider what sin is; then to inspect the deeds of the saints, written in the divine books, so that, if we find any sins even of theirs, we may see, by as careful a reasoning as we can, for what usefulness even these were set down in writing and committed to memory. But those things which we find to seem to the foolish or malevolent to be sins which are not, and yet in which no examples of virtues stand out, these too let us consider for what cause they were inserted into those writings, which we healthfully believe were composed for the usefulness of governing the present life and obtaining the future. Further, whatever evidences of righteousness shine in the deeds of the saints, no one even of the ignorant doubts they ought to have been written. There can, then, be a question about those things which can seem either written in vain, if neither right deeds appear nor are they sins; or even perniciously, if they are convicted of being sins, lest they avail for imitation—whether they are not reproached in the books themselves, and so can even be thought not to be sins; or are reproached there too, but are committed under an easy hope of pardon, because they are found even in the saints.

**27.** Sin, then, is something done or said or desired against the eternal law. But the eternal law is the divine reason or the will of God, commanding that the natural order be preserved, forbidding that it be disturbed. What, then, the natural order in man is, must be asked. For man consists of soul and body: but so does the beast. But it is doubtful to no one that the soul is, by the natural order, to be set before the body. But in the soul of man there is reason, which is not in the beast. Accordingly, just as the soul is set before the body, so the reason of the soul itself is set, by the law of nature, before its other parts, which beasts too have; and in reason itself, which is partly contemplative, partly active, doubtless contemplation excels. For in this is the image of God, by which through faith we are reformed unto the vision. The rational action, then, ought to obey the rational contemplation, whether commanding through faith, as it is while we are pilgrims away from the Lord[^77], or through vision, which will be when we shall be like him, since we shall see him as he is[^78], made, even in a spiritual body, by his grace equal to his angels[^79], having received the first robe of immortality and incorruption, with which this mortal and corruptible of ours will be clothed, that death may be absorbed into victory[^80], righteousness being perfected through grace. For the holy and sublime angels too have their contemplation and action; for they command themselves to do that which he whom they contemplate commands, by whose eternal command they freely—because sweetly—serve: but we, whose body is dead on account of sin, before God gives life even to our mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in us[^81], according to the small measure of our weakness, live justly according to the eternal law by which the natural order is preserved, if we live by faith unfeigned, which works through love[^82]: having in a good conscience the hope laid up in the heavens of immortality and incorruption, and of that righteousness to be perfected even unto a certain ineffably most sweet fullness, which in this pilgrimage must be hungered and thirsted for, as long as we walk by faith, not by vision[^83].

**28.** Wherefore the action of man, serving the faith that serves God, bridles all mortal delights and restrains them to the natural measure, setting better things before lower by an ordered love. For if nothing unlawful delighted, no one would sin. He sins, then, who relaxes rather than bridles the delight of the unlawful. But the unlawful is what that law forbids by which the natural order is preserved. But whether there is any rational creature whom nothing unlawful can delight is a great question. If there is, man was not made in that class, nor that angelic nature which did not stand in the truth: but in that class these rational beings were made, that there should be in them the possibility of bridling delight from the unlawful, which, by not bridling, they sinned. Great, then, is even the human creature, since through it that possibility is restored by which, had it willed, it would not have fallen. Great, then, is the Lord, and greatly to be praised[^84], who created it. For he created also the lower creatures, which cannot sin; he created also the better, which will not sin. For the bestial nature does not sin, because it does nothing against the eternal law, to which it is so subject that it cannot be a partaker of it. Again, the sublime angelic nature does not sin, because it is so a partaker of the eternal law that God alone delights it, to whose will it submits without any experience of temptation. But man, whose life on earth is, on account of sin, a temptation[^85], let him subject to himself what he has in common with beasts; let him subject to God what he has in common with the angels: until, with righteousness and immortality perfected and received, he be exalted above the former and made equal to the latter.

**29.** But mortal delights, unto the restoring or guarding of this mortal health, whether of each man or of the human race itself, are either to be excited or relaxed: beyond that, if they have slipped, and have carried off the man not governing himself against the reason of temperance, they will surely be unlawful and base lusts, and worthy to be amended by pains. But if they so plunge even the disturbed ruler into so great an abyss of lost habit that—either, believing they will go unpunished, he neglects the medicine of confession and penance, by which, corrected, he might emerge; or, by a worse death of the heart, applies to them a blasphemous patronage of defense against that eternal law of providence, and so ends his last day—that irreprehensible law judges him worthy now not of amendment, but of condemnation.

**30.** The eternal law, then, being consulted, which commands the natural order to be preserved and forbids it to be disturbed, let us see what father Abraham sinned—that is, what he did against this law—in those things which Faustus objected as great crimes. "Burning," he says, "with an insane desire of having offspring, and by no means believing God, who had already promised him this from Sarah his wife, he wallowed with a concubine." But Faustus, blinded by an insane desire of accusing, both betrayed the wickedness of his heresy and, unknowing and erring, praised Abraham's intercourse. For just as the eternal law—that is, the will of God, founder of all creatures, providing for the natural order to be preserved, not that lust be served unto satiety, but that the safety of the race be looked to—allows the delight of mortal flesh, under the dominion of reason, to be relaxed unto intercourse only for the propagating of offspring: so, on the contrary, the perverse law of the Manicheans, lest their god, whom they bewail as bound in all seeds, be more tightly bound in the conception of a woman, commands that offspring above all be avoided by those having intercourse, so that their god may rather be poured out by a base lapse than bound by a cruel knot. Not, then, was Abraham burning with an insane desire of having offspring, but the Manichean was raving with an insane vanity of avoiding offspring. Accordingly, he, keeping the order of nature, did nothing by human intercourse but that a man should be born: this man, observing the perversity of his fable, feared nothing in any intercourse but that his god should be taken captive.

**31.** But where Faustus, in Abraham's deed, finds fault as it were with the wife's connivance, there, with an evil mind and intention of reproaching, yet unknowing and unwilling, he praises both. For neither did she mingle her connivance with her husband's wickedness, so that he might satiate his lust with a base and unlawful pleasure: but she too, willing sons by the natural order, and knowing herself barren, claimed the fertility of the handmaid's womb for the use of her own right, by a lawful power; not yielding to a husband lusting, but commanding one obeying[^86]. Nor was this by a disordered pride: for who does not know that a wife ought to serve her husband as her lord? But as to what concerns the members of the body, by which the sex itself is distinguished, the Apostle says: *Likewise the husband has not power over his own body, but the wife*[^87]: so that, while in the other acts pertaining to human peace the woman owes service to the man, in this one thing, by which both sexes are distinguished by the carnal lot, and joined by carnal commingling, they have a like power over each other, the husband over the wife, and the wife over the husband. The offspring, then, which Sarah could not have from herself, she wished to have from the handmaid: yet from the same seed from which she ought to have had it from herself too, if she could. A woman would never do this, if she were held by carnal concupiscence in her husband's body; for she would rather be jealous of the concubine, not make her a mother: but as it is, the will to propagate in this way was devout for this reason, that the will to have intercourse was not lustful.

**32.** That indeed cannot be defended, if Abraham, as Faustus objected, by no means believing God, who had already promised him offspring from Sarah, wished to receive it from Hagar. But it is most openly false: God had not yet promised this. Let those who wish review the earlier parts of that Scripture; they will find that to Abraham's seed land had already been promised, and the abundance of an innumerable multitude[^88], yet it had not yet been made plain how the propagation of that seed would be—whether from the flesh of Abraham, if he begot from himself, or from the will, if perhaps he adopted someone: then, if from his own flesh, whether from Sarah or from another altogether, had not yet been made manifest. Let them read, I say, who wish, and they will find Faustus either imprudently deceived or impudently deceiving. And so Abraham, when he saw that sons were not born to him, and yet held the promise made to his seed, first thought of adoption. This is shown by the fact that, speaking with God, he says of his home-born servant, *This one will be my heir*—as if to say: Since you have given me no seed from myself, fulfill in this one what you promised to my seed. For if anyone's seed were called only what was born from his flesh, the Apostle would not call us the seed of Abraham[^89], who surely do not draw the origin of flesh from him, but by imitation of faith were made his seed, believing in Christ, whose flesh was propagated from his flesh. Then, therefore, Abraham heard from the Lord: *This one will not be your heir; but he who comes out of your womb, he will be your heir*[^90]. Already then, the thought of adoption removed, since Abraham hoped for seed from himself, it remained uncertain whether from Sarah or from another: which God wished to hide from him, until first, from the handmaid, the Old Testament should be figured. What wonder, then, if Abraham, seeing his wife barren, desiring for herself offspring which she could not bear, that it should come from her own maidservant and from her husband, did not yield to his own carnal desire, but obeyed the conjugal power; believing that Sarah wished this by the nod of God, who had already promised him an heir from himself, but had not foretold from what woman? In vain, then, did Faustus insanely leap to object this crime, arguing unfaithful Abraham unfaithfully. For the rest, by the blindness of not believing, he was not able to understand; but this, by the lust of slandering, he neglected even to read.

**33.** But when, calling a just and faithful man a most infamous huckster of his own marriage, he asserts that for the sake of avarice and the belly he sold for intercourse, to two kings, Abimelech and Pharaoh, at different times, Sarah his wife, lying that she was his sister, because she was most beautiful—he separates honesty from baseness not with a truthful mouth, but with a slanderous mouth turns the whole into a crime. For this deed of Abraham seems like pandering, but only to those unable, by the light of that eternal law, to discern right deeds from sins: to whom constancy too can seem obstinacy, and the virtue of confidence be thought the vice of audacity, and whatever similar things are objected against men as it were not acting rightly, by those not discerning rightly. For neither did Abraham consent to his wife's wickedness, and sell her adultery: but just as she did not permit her handmaid to her husband's lust, but offered her of her own accord for the office of begetting, with the natural order in no way disturbed, where her power lay, commanding rather than yielding to one lusting; so too he, of his chaste spouse, cleaving to him with a chaste heart, of whose mind, where the virtue of modesty dwelt, he in no way doubted, was silent that she was his wife, said she was his sister; lest, himself killed, she should be possessed as a captive by foreigners and impious men; sure of his God that he would allow her to suffer nothing base and wicked. Nor did faith and hope deceive him: for Pharaoh, terrified by portents, and afflicted by many evils on her account, when he learned divinely that she was his wife, restored her unharmed with honor; and Abimelech, admonished and instructed by a dream, did likewise[^91].

**34.** Some indeed—not slanderous and reviling, like Faustus, but paying due honor to those same books which this man either by not understanding reproaches, or by reproaching does not understand—when they considered this deed of Abraham, it seemed to them that he somewhat failed and wavered from the firmness of faith, and, through fear of death, denied his wife as Peter did the Lord[^92]. But if it were necessary to understand these things thus, I would acknowledge the man's sin; nor would I therefore judge all his merits deleted and blotted out, just as not those of that apostle: although the fault is not equal, to deny a wife and to deny the Savior. But as it is, since I have what I may understand, so as not to understand this, I am compelled by no cause to slip by rashness into reproaching one whom no one convicts of having slipped, through fear, into lying. For neither, asked whether she was his wife, did he answer that she was not: but when he was asked what that woman was to him, he indicated a sister, did not deny a wife; he was silent about something true, he did not say something false.

**35.** Or are we so foolish as here to follow Faustus, who says he lied that she was his sister—as though he had learned Sarah's lineage from elsewhere, since holy Scripture has not disclosed it? I think it just that, in a matter which Abraham knew but we do not know, we should rather believe the Patriarch speaking what he knows than the Manichean slandering what he does not know. Since, then, Abraham lived in human affairs at that time when indeed brothers, born from both or from one or the other parent, were no longer permitted to be joined in marriage, but custom, forbidden by no law, by no power, joined the sons of brothers and other blood relatives of a more distant degree of kindred—what wonder if he had his sister as wife, that is, one procreated from the blood of his father? For this very thing he himself said to the king restoring her to him, that she was his sister by his father, not by his mother: where surely he was now compelled by no fear to lie that she was his sister, since the king had learned that she was his wife, and, divinely terrified, was restoring her with honor. But that brothers or sisters are wont among the ancients to be called by a general name "blood relatives," Scripture testifies. For Tobit too says to God, when he prayed before he was joined to his wife: *And now, Lord, you know that not for the sake of lust do I take my sister*[^93]: although she was not from the intercourse of the same father, nor from the same mother's womb, but sprung from the same stock of kindred[^94]. And Lot is called the brother of Abraham[^95], though Abraham was his uncle[^96]: from which custom of the term even the brothers of the Lord are called in the Gospel—not, surely, those whom the virgin Mary had borne, but all the near kin by his blood relationship[^97].

**36.** Someone will say: Why did Abraham not rather so presume upon his God as not to fear to confess his wife? For God could both repel from him the death he feared, and keep him safe with his spouse from all ruin in that pilgrimage, so that neither would his wife, however beautiful, be desired by anyone, nor would he himself be killed on her account. God could surely have done this: who would be so mad as to deny it? But if Abraham, when asked, indicated that woman to be his wife, he would commit two things to God to be guarded: his own life, and his spouse's modesty. But it pertains to sound doctrine, when a man has what he may do, not to tempt the Lord his God[^98]. For neither could the Savior himself not guard his disciples, to whom yet he says: *If they persecute you in one city, flee to another*[^99]. Of which thing he himself gave the example first. For although he had power to lay down his soul, and would not lay it down except when he willed[^100], yet as an infant he fled into Egypt, carried by his parents[^101]; and to the feast he went up not openly but secretly, when at other times he spoke openly to the Jews who were angry and listening with a most hostile mind, yet not able to lay hands on him, because his hour had not yet come[^102]: not the hour by whose necessity he would be compelled to die, but the hour by whose opportunity he would deign to be slain. He, then, who by teaching openly, and rebuking, and yet not allowing the rage of his enemies to avail anything against him, demonstrated the power of God; yet, by fleeing and hiding, instructed the weakness of man, that he should not dare to tempt God, when he has what he may do to escape what he ought to avoid. For neither had the apostle Paul despaired of divine help and protection, and lost faith, when he was let down through the wall in a basket, to escape the hands of his enemies[^103]. Not, then, by not believing in God did he so flee, but, by tempting God, he would have been unwilling so to flee, since he could so flee. Accordingly, when, among strangers, on account of the most excellent beauty of Sarah, both her modesty and her husband's life were in doubt, and he could not guard both, but Abraham could yet guard one of these, that is, his life; lest he tempt his God, he did what he could; but what he could not, he committed to him. He, then, who was unable to hide himself a man, hid himself a husband, lest he be killed; he entrusted his wife to God, lest she be polluted.

**37.** Although it might be disputed more scrupulously whether that woman's modesty would be violated, even if someone were mingled with her flesh, since she permitted this to be done in her, for her husband's life, and not without his knowledge, but at his command, in no way deserting conjugal fidelity, and not denying the marital power; just as he was not an adulterer when, obeying his wife's power, he consented to beget offspring from a handmaid: but on account of the force of first principles—because a woman is not subjected to intercourse with two living men in the way two women are to one man—we take that much more truly and honestly: that father Abraham neither tempted God, when he provided for his life as much as a man could, and hoped in God, to whom he commended his spouse's modesty.

**38.** But now, in this deed done and set in the divine books and faithfully narrated, who would not delight to search out also the prophetic deed, and to knock at the door of the sacrament with devout faith and zeal, that the Lord may open and show who was then figured in that man, and whose wife she is who in this pilgrimage and among foreigners is not allowed to be polluted and stained, that she may be to her husband without spot or wrinkle? For unto the glory of Christ the Church lives rightly, so that her beauty is an honor to her husband, just as Abraham was honored among foreigners on account of Sarah's beauty: and to her very self, to whom it is said in the Song of Songs, *O beautiful among women!*[^104], by the merit of her very beauty kings offer gifts; just as King Abimelech offered to Sarah, marveling more in her at the comeliness of form which he could love and could not violate. For the holy Church too is, in secret, the wife of the Lord Jesus Christ. For secretly, and within, in a hidden spiritual secret, the human soul cleaves to the Word of God, so that the two are one flesh: which great sacrament of marriage the Apostle commends in Christ and in the Church[^105]. Accordingly, the earthly kingdom of this world, whose figure those kings bore who were not permitted to pollute Sarah, has not experienced nor found the Church the spouse of Christ—that is, the Church faithfully subject to him and cleaving to him as to her husband, her first principle—except when it tried to violate her, and yielded to the divine testimony through the faith of the martyrs, and honored, in its later kings, with a gift, her whom in its earlier ones it could not subject to its assault. For what was then figured in that same king, the earlier and the later, this in this kingdom is fulfilled in its earlier and later kings.

**39.** But when the Church is said to be the sister of Christ by the father, not by the mother, a kinship is commended not of the earthly generation which will be made void, but of the heavenly grace which will remain forever. According to which grace we shall not be a mortal race, having received power to be called sons of God and to be so[^106]. For this grace we received not from the Synagogue, the mother of Christ according to the flesh, but from God the Father. But this earthly kinship, which generates temporally unto death, Christ taught us, calling us into another life where no one dies, to deny, not to confess, when he says to his disciples: *Call no one your father on earth; for one is your Father who is in heaven*[^107]. Of which thing he gave an example, when he himself said: *Who is my mother, or who are my brothers?* And stretching out his hand over his disciples, he said: *These are my brothers.* And lest anyone in this term should think of earthly kinship, he added: *And whoever does the will of my Father, he is my brother, and mother, and sister*[^108]: as if to say: I name this kinship from God the Father, not from the Synagogue the mother. For I now call unto eternal life, where I was immortally born; not unto the temporal life, whence, that I might call, I was made mortal.

**40.** As for the fact, then, that whose wife the Church is, is hidden from foreigners, but whose sister she is, is not concealed—this cause for the present readily occurs: because it is hidden and difficult to understand how the human soul is coupled with the Word of God, or mingled, or whatever better and more fitting can be said, since that is God, this a creature. For according to this they are called bridegroom and bride, or husband and wife, Christ and the Church. But by what kinship Christ and all the saints are brothers, by divine grace, not by earthly blood relationship—that is, by the father, not by the mother—is both more expressibly said and more capably heard. For all the saints too are among themselves brothers by the same grace: but the bridegroom of the fellowship of the rest none of them is. Accordingly, foreigners much more easily and readily believed Christ, though of most excellent righteousness and wisdom, yet to be a man—not falsely, indeed, that he was a man; but how he was also God they did not know. Hence Jeremiah too: *And he is a man,* he says, *and who will know him?*[^109] "And he is a man"; because it is disclosed that he is a brother. "And who will know him?"; because it is hidden that he is a bridegroom. Let these things, concerning father Abraham, suffice to have been said against the most impudent and most ignorant and most slanderous voice of Faustus.

**41.** But Lot, his kinsman, just and hospitable in Sodom, and pure and unstained from all contamination of the Sodomites, deserved to escape safe from that conflagration which was a likeness of the future judgment; bearing the type of the body of Christ, which in all the saints now too groans among the iniquitous and impious, with whose deeds it does not consent, and from whose commingling it will be freed at the end of the world, with those condemned to the punishment of eternal fire. But as another kind of men was figured in his wife—namely, of those who, called by the grace of God, look back; not like Paul, who forgets the things that are behind, and stretches forward to the things that are before[^110]. Whence the Lord himself says: *No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of heaven*[^111]. Nor was that example passed over by which he might, as it were, season us with salt, that we should not, foolish, neglect, but, prudent, beware this evil. Whence she too, to admonish this, was turned into a pillar of salt. For when he commanded that each should snatch himself, by a most persevering intention toward the things before, from the things that are behind, he said, *Remember Lot's wife*[^112]. So too in Lot himself, when his daughters lay with him, not that which was figured when he was freed from Sodom, but something else, was figured. For then that same Lot seems to have borne the person of the future Law, which certain ones procreated from it and set under the Law, by understanding it badly, in a manner make drunk, and, by not using it lawfully, bring forth works of unbelief. For *the Law is good,* says the Apostle, *if anyone uses it lawfully*[^113].

**42.** Nor yet do we therefore justify this deed, whether of Lot himself or of his daughters, because it signified something that foretold the future perversity of certain people. For the daughters intended one thing in doing this, God who permitted it to be done another, that even from it he might demonstrate something, his right judgment remaining over the sin of the men then present, and his providence watching for the signification of things to come. Accordingly, that deed, when it is narrated in holy Scripture, is prophecy; but when it is considered in the life of those who committed it, it is wickedness.

**43.** Nor again is the matter worthy of so great a reproach and accusation as Faustus, an enemy and blind, vomited upon it. For that eternal law is consulted, which commands the natural order to be preserved and forbids it to be disturbed, and it judges of this deed not as if he had burned for his daughters with a nefarious lust, to enjoy their incestuous body, or to have them as wives; nor of those women, as if they had blazed for the flesh of their own father with an execrable love. For the reason of justice considers not only what was done, but also why it was done, so as to weigh deeds, hanging upon their causes, on the balance of equity. Since, then, those women sought offspring for the preservation of the race—which in them was surely a human and natural affection—and did not believe they could find other men, the world being as it were burned up by that conflagration (for they could not discern how far that fire had raged), they wished to mingle with their father. They ought rather never to have been mothers than to have so used their father: yet it makes a great difference that they used this cause, than if they had used the concupiscence of so deadly a pleasure.

**44.** But they felt their father so to shudder at that deed that they did not believe they would accomplish it, unless they procured his ignorance. For, as it is written, they made him drunk, and mingled with him as he knew not[^114]. Wherefore he is indeed to be blamed, yet not as much as that incest, but as much as that drunkenness deserves. For this too the eternal law condemns; because it admits food and drink to the natural order only by the favor of preserving health. Although, then, between a drunkard and one drunk there is a great difference—for neither is a drunkard always drunk, nor is whoever is sometimes drunk consequently a drunkard—yet in a just man the cause of this very thing, if not of drunkenness as a habit, at least of being drunk, must be sought. For what at last compelled him to consent or to trust his daughters mixing frequent wines for him, or perhaps offering them frequently unmixed? Or did he wish so to console his daughters, feigning excessive sorrow, that the grief of that bereavement and maternal mourning might be put to flight from the thought of a drunken mind, supposing that they too drank as much, while by some trick they acted so as not to drink? But how it would befit a just man to apply such a consolation to his sorrowing ones, we do not see. Or could they, by some most wicked Sodomite art, even with a few cups so make their father drunk that they committed that sin with one ignorant, or rather upon one ignorant? But it is a wonder if divine Scripture would have kept this silent, or if God would allow his servant to suffer it without some fault of his will.

**45.** We, nevertheless, defend the holy Scriptures, not the sins of men. But we are not so busy about the clearing of this deed as though our God either commanded it to be done, or approved it done; or as though just men are so called in those books that, if they wished to sin, they could not. Since, then, in the writings which these people reproach, God bore no testimony of righteousness to this deed, with what madness of rashness do they strive on this account to accuse those writings, when in other places of them these things are found most openly forbidden by divine precepts? Whence in that deed done, narrated concerning the work of Lot's daughters, these things were narrated, not praised. But some things had to be narrated with the judgment of God pronounced, some with it unspoken: so that, when it is brought forth what God judged thereof, our ignorance may be instructed; but when it is unspoken, either our skill may be exercised, that we may recall what we learned elsewhere, or our sloth may be shaken off, that we may inquire into what we do not yet know. God, then, who knows how to make good works even out of men's evil work, propagated from that seed the nations he willed, and did not condemn his Scriptures on account of men's sins. For he disclosed these things, did not do them; and he admonished them to be avoided, did not set them forth to be imitated.

**46.** With marvelous impudence indeed Faustus accused Isaac too, the son of Abraham, that he feigned Rebecca, who was his wife, to be his sister[^115]. For Rebecca's lineage is not kept silent, and it is manifest that she was his sister by a most well-known kinship[^116]. But that he was silent that she was his wife—what wonder, or what small thing, if he imitated his father; since he is defended by the same justice by which his father was found uncharged on a like objection? For the things, then, which, as far as concerns this matter, we said for Abraham against the slandering Faustus, the same avail also for Isaac his son[^117]; which it is not difficult to review—unless perhaps some studious person should ask in what sacrament of figure it is to be taken, that the foreign king then knew Rebecca to be the wife of her husband when he saw him playing with her; which he would not have known, unless he so played with his spouse as it would not befit to play with one who was not his spouse. When holy married people do this, they do it not idly, but prudently: for they descend in a manner to the weakness of the female sex, that they may say or do something with caressing cheerfulness; not enervating, but tempering, manly sternness: which yet, in him who says or does it to one who is not his wife, is base. But this I would say, which pertains to the manners of humanity, lest anyone, hard and without affection, object as a crime to a holy man that he played with his spouse. For such inhuman men, if they see some grave man chattering some playful thing even to little children, by which he may soothe their milky sense with affable and nurturing ease, reproach him as doting: forgetting whence they grew, or ungrateful that they grew. But what, in the sacrament of Christ and the Church, that so great a Patriarch played with his spouse, and that marriage was thence recognized, may mean—he surely sees, lest by erring he sin against the Church, who diligently beholds in the holy Scriptures the secret of her husband: and he finds that he hid his majesty a little, by which in the form of God he is equal to the Father, in the form of a servant[^118], that human weakness might be able to grasp him, and in that manner might fittingly suit himself to his spouse. For what is absurd—nay, what not fittingly accommodated to the foretelling of things to come—if a prophet of God played something carnal, that the affection of his wife might grasp him; when the very Word of God was made flesh, that it might dwell among us[^119]?

**47.** But now, as for his son Jacob, that four wives are objected to him as a vast crime[^120], it is cleared by a general preface. For when it was the custom, it was not a crime: and now it is therefore a crime, because it is not the custom. For some sins are against nature, some against custom, some against precepts. Since these things are so, what crime at last is it that is objected to the holy man Jacob concerning several wives held at once? If you consult nature, he used those women not for the sake of wantoning, but of begetting; if custom, at that time and in those lands this was commonly done; if precept, it was forbidden by no law. But now, why is it a crime, if anyone does this, except because both by morals and by laws it is not permitted? Whoever despises these two, even if he can use several women only for the sake of begetting, yet sins, and violates that very human society to which the propagation of children is necessary. But because men, now that morals and laws are otherwise, cannot be delighted by a multitude of wives except by a magnitude of lust, therefore they err and suppose that many wives could not at all be had, except by the burning of carnal concupiscence and of foul pleasure. For comparing, not others whose virtue of mind they cannot at all know, but, as the Apostle says, themselves with themselves[^121], they do not understand. And because they themselves, even if they have one, approach her, led manfully not by the office of begetting alone, but, conquered by the goad of intercourse, are drawn enervately; they seem to themselves to conjecture truly how much greater a disease of this kind others are captivated by, through many women, when they see that they themselves cannot keep temperance with one.

**48.** But we ought not so to commit judgment about the morals of holy men to those who do not have this virtue, just as we do not allow the feverish to judge about the sweetness and healthfulness of foods: and we rather prepare for them their nourishment from the sense of the healthy and the precept of the physicians, than from the disposition of their own sickness. Accordingly, if these people wish to receive the health, not of a false and shadowed, but of a true and solid chastity, let them believe the divine Scriptures as medicinal books, that not in vain was so great an honor of sanctity bestowed on certain men even having several wives; unless because it can happen that the mind, commander of the flesh, so prevails by so great a power of temperance that it does not permit the motion of generative delight, implanted in the nature of mortals, to exceed the laws imposed by the providence of begetting. Otherwise these people—slanderous accusers rather than truthful judges—could accuse even the holy Apostles, that they preached the Gospel to so many peoples not from love of begetting sons of eternal life, but from desire of human praise. For those evangelical fathers did not lack illustrious fame throughout all the Churches of Christ, gained by so many tongues praising them: nay rather, so great was it that greater honor and glory ought not to be paid to men by men. This glory in the Church the lost Simon coveted with a perverse will, when, blind, he wished to buy with money what they merited by divine and that gratuitous grace[^122]. Greedy of this glory is understood to have been the man whom the Lord in the Gospel calls back as he wished to follow him, saying: *The foxes have holes, and the birds of heaven nests; but the Son of man has nowhere to lay his head*[^123]. For he saw that man, darkened by a deceitful pretense and tossed by a windy elation, to have no place of faith where he might receive the teacher of humility inclining himself; because in the discipleship of Christ he sought not his grace, but his own glory. By this love of glory were corrupted those whom the apostle Paul notes, that through envy and contention they announced Christ not chastely: by whose preaching, nevertheless, the Apostle rejoices[^124], knowing it can happen that, while they follow the desire of human glory, yet, these things being heard, the faithful are born; not from their envious desire, by which they wished either to be equaled or preferred to the apostolic glory, but through the Gospel, which, even if not chastely, they yet announced; so that out of their evil God might work good: just as it can happen that a man enters intercourse not with the will of begetting, but is carried off by the lust of wantoning; and yet a man is born, a good work of God from the fertility of seeds, not from the baseness of vices. As, then, the holy Apostles took common delight with their hearers admiring their teaching, not from greed of attaining praise, but from love of sowing the truth; so the holy Patriarchs mingled with their wives receiving their seed, not from concupiscence of receiving pleasure, but from providence of propagating succession: and on this account neither did the multitude of peoples make those ambitious, nor the multitude of wives make these lustful. But why do I speak of the men, to whom a most excellent testimony is borne by the divine voice, when it sufficiently shines forth that the very women sought nothing else in intercourse than children? For when they saw themselves by no means bearing, they gave their handmaids to their husband, that they might make them mothers by flesh, themselves becoming mothers by will.

**49.** For that too which Faustus objected with a most lying accusation—that there was a contest among them as it were four harlots, which should seize him for intercourse—where he read this I do not know, unless perhaps in his own heart as in a book of nefarious deceits, where he himself truly played the harlot, but with that serpent of whom the Apostle feared for the Church, which he desired to present as a chaste virgin to one husband, Christ, lest, as the serpent deceived Eve by his cunning, so he should corrupt their minds too, by turning them away from the chastity of Christ[^125]. For these people are such friends to this serpent that they contend he benefited rather than harmed. He plainly persuaded Faustus, pouring the seeds of falsehood into an adulterated breast, that he should bring forth these ill-conceived slanders with a most unclean mouth, and even commit them to memory with a most audacious pen. For no handmaid snatched the man Jacob from another, no handmaid quarreled over his intercourse with another. There was the more order, therefore, because there was no lust; and the rights of conjugal power were preserved so much the more firmly, as the injury of carnal desire was avoided the more chastely. For in that he is even hired by a wife, there our true assertion is made manifest, there truth itself cries out for itself against the slanders of the Manicheans. For what need was there that the one should hire him, except because it was the other's turn that the husband should go in to her? For he would never have gone to another, unless she had hired him: but surely he was approaching by just turns her of whom he had begotten so many sons, and whom he had obeyed even in begetting from a handmaid, and of whom afterward, not hiring him, he begot. But then Rachel had the night in turn to stay with her husband: then that power was in her hands, about which through the Apostle the voice surely of the New Testament was not silent, saying: *Likewise the husband has not power over his own body, but the wife.* Therefore she had now agreed with her sister, to whom, made debtor, she transferred him to her own debtor. For by this name the Apostle calls it: *Let the husband,* he says, *render the debt to the wife*[^126]. To her, then, to whom the husband was debtor, she had now received from her sister what she chose by her will, that she might give to her what she had in her power.

**50.** But here this man, whom Faustus accuses as unchaste with closed—or rather extinguished—eyes, if he were a slave of concupiscence and not of righteousness, would he not the whole day long be aglow for the pleasure of that night in which he was to lie with the more beautiful—whom surely he loved more, and for whom he had paid the gratuitous labor of twice seven years? When, then, with the day now ended, he was going into her embrace, when would he be turned away from there, if he were such as the Manicheans, understanding nothing, suppose? Would he not, despising the women's agreement, rather go in to his beautiful one, who owed him that very night, not only by the manner of a spouse, but also by the right of the turn? Would he not rather use the marital power himself, since both *the wife has not power over her own body, but the husband*; and for him at that time the alternation of their service made it so: he would, then, use conjugal power much more conquerably, if he were conquered by desire of beauty. But the women would be found the better, if they contended for conceiving children, while he contended for the pleasure of his own intercourse. And so a most temperate man, and plainly a man, because he so manfully used the women that he was not subject to carnal delight, but lorded over it, attended more to what he owed than to what was owed to him; nor did he wish to abuse his power for his own pleasure, but chose to be a renderer rather than an exactor of that debt. Whence it was consequent that he should render to her whom she to whom it was owed chose to receive it for herself; so that, this agreement and pact of theirs being learned, when he was suddenly and unexpectedly called back from his most beautiful spouse, and called to the less comely one, he neither blazed with anger, nor was clouded with sadness, nor, weakly fawning, busied himself between the two that Rachel should rather render him the night: but, a just husband and a provident father, when he saw them bearing the care of offspring, and himself sought nothing else from intercourse, he judged that he must obey the will of those who desired sons for themselves individually; nothing being diminished for his own, for whom both bore. As if he said: Yield and concede among yourselves, as you wish, which of you is to become a mother: what should I contend for, when, whether born from this side or that, there will be no other father? This modesty, surely, this restraint of concupiscence, and, in the commingling of conjugal bodies, only the appetite of human posterity—Faustus, sharp as he was, would in those writings both understand and praise, were not his talent depraved by a detestable sect, both seeking what to reproach, and reckoning this one honesty of the nuptial agreement, by which males and females are coupled for the sake of procreating children, the greatest crime.

**51.** Now, the Patriarch's morals defended, and the crime refuted which a nefarious error objected, let us, as freely as we can, search out the secrets of the mysteries, and knock with the piety of faith, that it may be opened to us by the Lord what things these four wives of Jacob figured, of whom two were free, two handmaids. For we see the Apostle, in the free woman and the handmaid whom Abraham had, understand the two Testaments[^127]: but there, in one and one, what is said appears more easily; here there are two and two. Then there the handmaid's son is disinherited, but here the sons of the handmaids together with the sons of the free women receive the land of promise: whence here, without doubt, something else is signified.

**52.** For although I judge that the two free wives of Jacob pertain to the New Testament, by which we have been called into freedom, yet they are not two in vain: unless perhaps because (a thing that can be noticed and found in the Scriptures) two lives are preached to us in the body of Christ; one temporal, in which we labor, the other eternal, in which we shall contemplate the delight of God. The former the Lord declared by his passion, the latter by his resurrection. The very names of those women admonish us to this understanding. For they say that Leah is interpreted "Laboring," but Rachel "the Beginning Seen," or "the Word from which the beginning is seen." The action, then, of the human and mortal life, in which we live by faith, doing many laborious works, uncertain with what outcome they come forth unto the usefulness of those for whom we wish to provide, is itself Leah, the first wife of Jacob; and on this account she is mentioned as having weak eyes. For *the thoughts of mortals are fearful, and our foresight uncertain*[^128]. But the hope of the eternal contemplation of God, having a certain and delightful understanding of truth, is itself Rachel: whence she too is said to be of good face and beautiful form. For her every devout and zealous one loves, and for her sake serves the grace of God, by which our sins, even if they were like scarlet, are whitened like snow[^129]: for Laban is interpreted "Whitening," whom Jacob served for Rachel's sake[^130]. For no one turns, under the grace of the remission of sins, to serve righteousness, except that he may live quietly in the Word from which the beginning is seen, which is God: therefore for Rachel's sake, not for Leah's. For who at last would love, in the works of righteousness, the labor of actions and sufferings? Who would seek that life for its own sake?—just as Jacob did not seek Leah: yet, set under him by night for the use of begetting, he embraced her and experienced her fertility. For the Lord, because she could not be loved for her own sake, first made her to be tolerated, that one might arrive at Rachel, and then commended her on account of her sons. So, indeed, every useful servant of God, set under the grace of the whitening of his sins, what else has he meditated in his conversion, what else borne in his heart, what else loved, than the doctrine of wisdom? Which very many suppose they will attain and receive as soon as they have exercised themselves in the seven precepts of the law which concern the love of neighbor, that no man be harmed—that is: Honor your father and mother, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not kill, You shall not steal, You shall not bear false witness, You shall not covet your neighbor's wife, You shall not covet your neighbor's property[^131]: which, observed as much as one can, after one has, for the desired and hoped-for most beautiful delight of doctrine, cleaved through various temptations, as through the night of this world, with the endurance of labor, as it were Leah is unexpectedly joined to him for Rachel; and he sustains her in order to arrive at the other, if he loves perseveringly, having received the other seven precepts: as if it were said to him, Serve another seven years for Rachel; that he may be poor in spirit, meek, mourning, hungering and thirsting for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, a peacemaker[^132]. For a man would wish, if it could be, without any endurance of labor—which in acting and suffering must be embraced—to arrive at once at the delights of beautiful and perfect wisdom: but this cannot be in the land of the dying. For this seems to be signified by what was said to Jacob: *It is not the custom in our place that the younger marry before the elder*[^133]. For not absurdly is she called the elder who is prior in time. But prior, in the right education of man, is the labor of doing the things that are just, than the pleasure of understanding the things that are true.

**53.** To this avails what is written: *You have desired wisdom; keep the commandments, and the Lord will furnish it to you*[^134]. The commandments, surely, pertaining to righteousness: but the righteousness which is from faith, which is engaged amid the uncertainties of temptations, so that, by devoutly believing what it does not yet understand, it may attain also the merit of understanding. For as much as that which I just mentioned to be written avails—*You have desired wisdom; keep the commandments; and the Lord will furnish it to you*—so much, I judge, this too avails: *Unless you believe, you will not understand*[^135]: so that righteousness is shown to pertain to faith, but understanding to wisdom. Accordingly, in those who burn with a vast love of perspicuous truth, the zeal is not to be disapproved, but to be recalled to order, that it may begin from faith, and by good morals strive to arrive where it tends. For in that with which it is engaged there is laborious virtue; but in that which it seeks, luminous wisdom. "What need is there," he says, "to believe what is not shown me manifest? Bring forth some word by which I may see the beginning of all things." For that is what, most of all and first, a rational mind, if it is zealous for the true, blazes for. To whom let it be answered: Beautiful indeed is what you desire, and most worthy to be loved; but first Leah marries, and afterward Rachel. Let this ardor, then, avail to this, that order be not refused, but rather endured; without which one cannot arrive at that which is loved with so great ardor. But when one has arrived, there will be had together, in this world, not only beautiful understanding, but also laborious righteousness. For however keenly and sincerely the immutable good is discerned by mortals, still *the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the sense that thinks many things*[^136]. To one, then, we must tend, but on account of this many things must be borne.

**54.** And so the two wives of Jacob are free: for both are daughters of the remission of sins—that is, of the whitening, which is Laban. Nevertheless, one is loved, and the other tolerated. But she who is tolerated is herself first and more abundantly made fruitful, so that, if not for her own sake, at least for her sons' sake she may be loved. For the labor of the just has its greatest fruit in those whom they beget for the kingdom of God, by preaching the Gospel amid many temptations and tribulations; and those for whom they are *in labors more abundant, in stripes beyond measure, in deaths more often*[^137], on whose account they have *fightings without, fears within*[^138], they call their joy and their crown[^139]. But these are born to them more easily and copiously from that word of faith by which they preach Christ crucified[^140], and whatever of his humanity is more quickly perceived by human thought, and does not disturb even the weak eyes of Leah. But Rachel, clear of aspect, is beside herself in mind to God[^141], and sees in the beginning the Word, God with God[^142], and wishes to bear, and cannot; for *who will declare his generation?*[^143] Accordingly, the life that befits the zeal of contemplation—so that it may behold those things which are invisible to the flesh, not with the weak eyes of the mind, understood through the things that are made, and may ineffably discern the everlasting power and divinity of God[^144]—wishes to be free from all business, and therefore is barren. For, in striving after the leisure in which the zeal of contemplation grows hot, it is not tempered to the weakness of men, who desire to be helped in their various pressures. But because she too burns with the love of procreating (for she wishes to teach what she knows, and not to journey with envy pining away[^145]), she sees her sister abounding in sons by the labor of acting and suffering; and she grieves that men rather run to that virtue by which their weaknesses and necessities are provided for, than to that by which something divine and immutable is learned. This grief seems figured in what is written: *And Rachel was jealous of her sister*[^146]. Accordingly, because a liquid and pure understanding of that substance which is not body, and on this account does not pertain to the sense of the flesh, cannot be expressed by words brought forth in the flesh, the doctrine of wisdom chooses to insinuate divine things, to be thought of somehow through any corporeal images and likenesses whatever, rather than to cease from the office of teaching such things: just as Rachel chose to receive children from her husband and her handmaid, rather than to remain altogether without sons. For Bilhah is said to be interpreted "Grown old": this handmaid Rachel had. For from the old life given over to carnal senses corporeal images are stirred up, even when something is heard of the spiritual and immutable substance of the divinity.

**55.** Leah too receives sons from her handmaid, kindled with love of having more numerous offspring. But we find Zilpah, her handmaid, interpreted "a Gaping Mouth." Wherefore, when in the preaching of the evangelical faith we notice those whose mouth gapes and whose heart does not gape, this is understood as Leah's handmaid. For it is written of certain people: *This people honors me with the lips, but their heart is far from me*[^147]. And to such the Apostle says: *You who preach that one must not steal, steal; you who say one must not commit adultery, commit adultery*[^148]. Nevertheless, that even through this servile condition that free laboring wife of Jacob may receive sons, heirs of the kingdom, therefore the Lord says: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do*[^149]. Whence the apostolic life in the labor of bonds: *Whether by occasion,* he says, *or by truth, Christ is announced, and in this I rejoice, yes, and will rejoice*[^150]: as the handmaid too, bearing, rejoiced over more numerous offspring.

**56.** But there is a certain offspring of Leah brought forth by the favor of Rachel, when she, having received the mandrake apples from Leah's son, permits her husband, who was to lie with her by the owed night, to lie with her sister. About this kind of fruit I know some opine that, taken as food, it produces fertility in barren women; and on this account they think Rachel insisted in every way that she should receive this from her sister's son, out of desire of bearing: which I would not judge, even if she had then conceived. But as it is, since, after two other childbirths of Leah from that night, the Lord gifted her with offspring, there is no reason why we should suspect concerning the mandrake any such thing as we have experienced in no woman. I will say, then, what I think: better men, perhaps, will say better things from this. For when I myself had seen these apples, and congratulated myself that this had befallen me on account of this very passage of the sacred reading (for it is a rare thing), I searched out their nature as diligently as I could—not by some knowledge remote from common sense, which teaches the virtues of roots and the powers of herbs, but as much as sight, and smell, and taste reported to me and to any man. Accordingly I found a thing beautiful and sweet-smelling, but insipid in flavor: and therefore why the woman so greatly desired it I confess I do not know, unless perhaps on account of the rarity of the fruit and the pleasantness of the odor. But why holy Scripture would not keep silent the very deed—which surely would not take care to insinuate to us such desires of women as a great matter, unless it admonished us to seek something great in them—I can conjecture nothing more than what is suggested to me from that common sense: that by that mandrake apple I understand to be figured good fame: not that which is conferred when a few just and wise men praise a man, but that popular fame by which a greater and more illustrious notice is gained: not to be sought for its own sake, but most necessary to the intention of good men, by which they provide for the human race. Whence the Apostle says: *He must have a good testimony also from those who are outside*[^151]: who, though they have little wisdom, yet for the most part render to the labor of those by whom they are provided for both the splendor of praise and the odor of good opinion. Nor do the first of those who are in the Church arrive at this popular glory, except whoever are engaged in the perils and labor of actions. Therefore Leah's son found the mandrake apples, going out into the field—that is, walking honestly to those who are outside: but that doctrine of wisdom which, most remote from the noise of the crowd, is fixed in the contemplation of truth with sweet delight, would not attain this popular glory, however small, except through those who, in the midst of the throngs, by acting and persuading, preside over the peoples, not that they may preside, but that they may profit: because, while those active and busy men, through whom the usefulness of the multitude is administered, and whose authority is dear to peoples, bear testimony also to the life that is at leisure for the sake of the zeal of seeking and contemplating truth, in a manner the mandrake apples come, through Leah, to Rachel. But to Leah herself through her firstborn son—that is, through the honor of her fertility, in which is all the fruit of the laborious action that ventures amid the uncertainties of temptations: which very many, endowed with good talent and burning with zeal, although they could be fit to rule peoples, yet avoid on account of turbulent occupations, and are carried with their whole heart into the leisure of doctrine, as it were into the embrace of the beautiful Rachel.

**57.** But because it is good that this life too, becoming more widely known, should merit popular glory, but it is unjust that it should attain it if it detains its lover, fit and suitable for administering ecclesiastical cares, in leisure, and does not impart him to the governance of the common usefulness; therefore Leah says to her sister: *Is it too little for you that you have received my husband, do you also wish to take the mandrakes of my son?* By one husband signifying all those who, although they are able by the virtue of acting, and worthy that the rule of the Church be committed to them for dispensing to it the sacrament of faith, kindled with the zeal of doctrine and of investigating and contemplating wisdom, wish to remove themselves from all troubles of actions, and to hide in the leisure of learning and teaching. So, then, it was said: *Is it too little that you have received my husband, do you also wish to take the mandrakes of my son?*—as if it were said: Is it too little that the life of studies detains in leisure men necessary for the labor of managing affairs, do you also require popular glory?

**58.** Accordingly, that she may justly obtain it, Rachel imparts her husband to her sister that night: so that, namely, those who by laborious virtue are suited to the rule of peoples, even if they had chosen to be at leisure for knowledge, may undertake the experience of temptations and the burden of cares for the common usefulness: lest the very doctrine of wisdom, for which they resolved to be at leisure, be blasphemed, and lest it not attain from the more ignorant peoples that good estimation which those apples signify, and which is necessary for the exhortation of learners. But plainly, that they may undertake this care, they are compelled by force. This too is sufficiently signified, that when Jacob came from the field, Leah met him, and detaining him said: *You will come in to me; for I have hired you for my son's mandrakes*[^152]. As if she said: Do you wish good opinion to be conferred on the doctrine you love? Do not flee the dutiful labor. Whoever notices that these things are done in the Church, sees it. We experience in examples what we understand in books. Who does not see this done throughout the whole world—men come from the works of the world and go into the leisure of knowing and contemplating truth, as it were into the embrace of Rachel; and are taken aside by ecclesiastical necessity, and ordained unto labor, as Leah says, "You will come in to me"? To whom, chastely dispensing the mystery of God, that they may beget sons of faith in the night of this world, even that life is praised by the peoples, for love of which, converted, they abandoned the hope of the world, and from the profession of which they were taken up to the mercy of ruling the people. For they act, in all their labors, that the profession to which they had turned, because it has given such rulers to the peoples, may be more widely and more illustriously glorified, as Jacob not refusing Leah's night, that Rachel may obtain the sweet-smelling and brightly shining apples: which sometimes she too, by the mercy of God granting it, bears by herself, scarcely at last indeed; because it is very rare that *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*[^153], and whatever is devoutly and wisely said on this matter, should be grasped, healthfully and even in part, without the phantasm of carnal thought.

**59.** Let these things, concerning the three fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, whose God the God whom the catholic Church worships willed himself to be called, suffice to have been said against the slanders of Faustus. Of the merits and piety of these three men, and of the loftiness of prophecy far removed from the judgment of carnal men, there is now no place to discuss: only they had to be defended, in this present work of ours, from those crimes with which a reviling tongue, hostile to truth, assailed them; lest those who read the holy and healthful Scriptures with a perverse and adverse mind should seem to say something for themselves, while with petulant revilings they attack those who there are preached with such great honor.

**60.** But Lot, the brother—that is, the blood relative—of Abraham, is by no means to be compared to these of whom God says, *I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob*[^154]; nor to be reckoned in the number of those to whom that Scripture bears testimony of righteousness to the end: although, having lived devoutly and chastely among the Sodomites, commended also by the merits of hospitality, he was freed from the burning of that land, and to his posterity a land of possession was given by the gift of God, on account of Abraham, who had been his uncle[^155]. These merits are set forth to us in those books to be praised—not the drunkenness, not the incest[^156]: but when we find written both the right deed and the sin of the same man, one is insinuated to be imitated, the other to be guarded against. Further, if the sin of Lot—to whom, before he sinned, testimony of righteousness was borne[^157]—not only does not discolor the divinity of God or the truth of that Scripture, but even commends it as to be praised and loved, in that, like the brightness of a faithful mirror, it indicates of the persons brought before it not only what is beautiful and whole, but also what is deformed and faulty; how much more does the deed of Judah, that he lay with his daughter-in-law[^158], bring nothing at all whereby the holy authority may be blamed: which, persisting founded in those books, despises by divine right not only the slanderous quibbles of a very few Manicheans, but even the horrendous enmities of so many and so great peoples of the gentiles, whom it has now subjugated almost wholly, from the nefarious superstition of idols, to the worship of the one true God by the Christian empire, with the world tamed, not by the violence of warlike contest, but by the power of unconquered truth? For where in those writings was Judah praised? What good did that Scripture testify of him, except that, in the prophecy of Christ, who from his tribe was foretold as to come in the flesh, he stood out commended above the rest by the blessing of his father[^159]?

**61.** But to that which Faustus mentioned, that he committed fornication, we add another: that he sold his brother Joseph into Egypt[^160]. Do anyone's distorted limbs deprave the light, which shows forth all things? So neither do anyone's evil deeds make the Scripture evil, by whose disclosure they become known to readers. For when that eternal law, which commands the natural order to be preserved and forbids it to be disturbed, is consulted, it has established that the intercourse of man be done only for the sake of propagation, and this only in marriage socially ordered, which does not pervert the bond of peace: and therefore the prostitution of women set forth not for the substituting of offspring but for the satiating of lust is condemned by the divine and eternal law. For the baseness of a venal thing dishonors every buyer: and on this account Judah, although he would have sinned more grievously if, knowing she was his daughter-in-law, he had wished to lie with her (for if husband and wife, as the Lord says, are *no longer two but one flesh*[^161], a daughter-in-law is to be reckoned no otherwise than a daughter); yet there is no doubt that he, as far as in him lay, lay basely with a harlot. But she who deceived her father-in-law sinned neither from concupiscence of his flesh, nor from desire of a harlot's wage: but, seeking offspring from that very blood from which, having now been married to two brothers and denied the third, she could not have it, she fraudulently subjected her body to be impregnated by their father, her father-in-law, having received a pledge of payment, which she reserved not for ornament but for testimony. Better indeed that she should remain without sons than become a mother without the right of marriage: yet she sinned with a far other intention, in that she provided her father-in-law as a father for her sons, than if she had desired him for herself as an adulterer. Finally, when by his command she was being led forth to death, and had produced the staff, the necklace, and the ring, saying that she was made pregnant by him whose pledges those were; when he recognized the things he had given, he answered that she was more justified than he, in that he had been unwilling to join his son to her as husband; by which destitution compelled, she sought posterity in that way rather than in no way, from no other than the same stock. In which sentence, saying not that she was justified, but that she was more justified than he, he neither praised her, but preferred her in comparison with himself; namely, blaming less the desire of having offspring, by which, led, she had subjected herself to her father-in-law, than the ardor of lustful intercourse, by which he himself had entered, conquered, as to a harlot: just as it is said to certain people, *You have justified Sodom*[^162]—that is, you have sinned so much that Sodom, compared to you, seems just. Although even if this woman is understood to be, not less blamed in comparison with a worse deed, but wholly praised by her father-in-law: yet, when that eternal law of justice is consulted, which forbids the natural order to be disturbed—not only, surely, of bodies, but most of all and first of minds, because in procreating sons she did not keep the ordained society—she is rightly found blameworthy: what wonder, if a sinner is praised by a sinner?

**62.** Which Faustus, or the Manichean perversity itself, judges to be against us in this way, as though, in the veneration and worthy proclamation of that Scripture, it were necessary for us to approve the vices of men which it mentions: nay rather, it is necessary that, the more religiously we receive it, the more confidently we blame those things which, through its truth, we have learned more certainly are to be blamed. For there fornication and every unlawful intercourse is condemned by divine right[^163]: and on this account, when it mentions such deeds of certain men, about which in that place it is silent about its own sentence, it permits them to us to be judged, it does not prescribe them to be praised. For who of us, in the Gospel itself, does not detest the cruelty of Herod, when, anxious about the birth of Christ, he ordered so many infants to be killed[^164]? But this deed is there not reproached, but only narrated. But lest the insane impudence of the Manicheans contend that this is false—because they deny even the very birth of Christ, by which Herod was disturbed—let them read the savagery and blindness of those very Jews, how it is there only narrated, not reproached, and yet detested by all.

**63.** But, they say, this Judah, who lay with his daughter-in-law, is counted among the twelve Patriarchs[^165]. What of it? And that Judas who betrayed the Lord, was he not numbered among the twelve Apostles, and, with them, one of them, a devil, sent to preach the Gospel[^166]? But still they reply and say: That one, after such a crime, was destroyed by his own noose and removed from the number of the Apostles[^167]; but this one, after this baseness, was, among his brothers, by that father to whom God bears so great testimony, especially blessed and praised above all[^168]. Nay rather, from this it appears the more expressly that that prophecy pertains not to him, but to Christ, who from his tribe was foretold as to come in the flesh: and therefore the divine Scripture all the more ought not to be silent about his wickedness, as it was not silent, so that, in the words of his father, by which after that disgrace he is so praised, because he himself is not recognized, another may be sought.

**64.** Although Faustus may be understood to have wished, with his reviling tooth, to bite this very thing, that Christ is preached by us to have come from his tribe: especially because in the generations of his fathers, which the evangelist Matthew mentions, even Zerah, whom that Tamar bore from that conception, is found[^169]. For if he wished to reproach the stock of Jacob, not the generation of Christ, he had rather the firstborn Reuben, who violated his father's bed with nefarious lust[^170]: such a fornication as the Apostle says was not heard of even among the gentiles[^171]. Which deed even the father Jacob himself, when he blessed them, did not keep silent over his head, accusing and detesting it[^172]. This crime, then, Faustus would rather have objected, where there appears not an error from a harlot's garb, but an altogether voluntary contamination of the father's bed; were it not that he hated Tamar herself more, in that by that intercourse she desired nothing but to bear, than if she had burned with mere lust of carnal pleasure; and wished, by blaming Christ's ancestors, to derogate from faith in his Incarnation: not knowing, wretch, that the most true and most truthful Savior was a master not only by speaking, but also by being born. For his faithful, to come from all nations, had to learn even by the example of his very flesh that the iniquities of their parents cannot harm them. Accordingly that Bridegroom, suiting himself to his guests, who was to call good and evil to the wedding[^173], willed even to be born of good and evil: so that he might confirm that the prophecy of the Passover, in which a lamb was commanded to be eaten, taken from sheep and goats[^174], as it were just and unjust, had gone before for the prefiguring of himself. For everywhere preserving the evidences of God and man, he did not spurn parents both good and evil, on account of the fittingness of his humanity, but chose the childbearing of a virgin, on account of the miracle of his divinity.

**65.** In vain, then, Faustus, raging rather against himself with a sacrilegious tooth, accuses the holy Scripture, which the whole world now deservedly venerates: which, as I said above, like the brightness of a faithful mirror, accepts no person to be flattered, but either itself judges both the praiseworthy and blameworthy deeds of men, or sets them forth to readers to be judged; and not only intimating the men themselves as either blameworthy or praiseworthy, but also not keeping silent about certain things in the blameworthy that are to be praised, and in the praiseworthy that are to be blamed. For not because Saul was a blameworthy man is therefore his deed not praiseworthy, that, having so diligently investigated the tasting of the anathema, he tried so severely to avenge it, obeying God, who had forbidden this to be done[^175]; or that he removed the pythons and ventriloquists from his kingdom[^176]: nor because David was praiseworthy are therefore his sins, which God too reproved through the prophet[^177], to be approved or imitated. Just as in Pontius Pilate it is not to be reproached that, against the accusations of the Jews, he judged the innocent Lord[^178]; nor in Peter is it to be praised that he denied the same Lord three times[^179]; or that for which he was called Satan by him, in that, not savoring the things of God, he wished to recall him from the passion—that is, from our salvation: a little before, then, called blessed[^180], a little after he was called Satan[^181]. But what prevailed in him, his apostolate and the crown of his martyrdom testify.

**66.** So, then, we read also the sins of King David, but we read also his right deeds. But in which he prevailed, and whence he conquered what, is plain enough—not to malevolent blindness, by which Faustus rushed upon the holy books and men, but to religious prudence, by which both the divine authority and the human merits can be seen and discerned. For let these people read and see that God reproved more in David than Faustus did[^182]: but there too is the sacrifice of penance, there is that incomparable meekness even toward a most monstrous and most atrocious enemy, who, as often as he was given into his most powerful hands, so often was sent away by him unharmed from his most devout hands[^183]. There is the memorable humility under the scourge of God, and the royal neck so subjected to the dominical yoke that, armed and accompanied by armed men, he most patiently endured bitter revilings from an enemy's mouth; and most modestly restrained his companion, inflamed with wrath that his king should hear such things, and now and now proceeding with avenging right hand against the head of the reviler, adding to his royal command the weight of divine fear, and saying that this was rendered to his deserts by the supernal judgment, by which that injurious man had been sent to hurl such reproaches against him[^184]. There is so great pastoral love toward the flock committed to him that he himself wished to die for them, when, the people having been numbered, it pleased God so to punish the sin of his elation that he diminished that same number by the death of many, by whose multitude the king's heart had been tempted with pride: in which hidden judgment God, with whom there is no iniquity[^185], both withdrew from this life those whom he knew unworthy of this life; and in him who had exalted himself from the abundance of men, healed the swelling of the human mind by the diminution of that same abundance. There is so religious a fear of God that he preserved the sacrament of Christ in the holy anointing, so that his heart trembled with devout solicitude when he secretly cut off a small piece from Saul's very garment, that he might have whereby to give him assurance that he was unwilling, when he could, to kill him. There is so prudent and so great a clemency toward his sons that, when he did not mourn the innocent boy now dead, for whom, sick, casting himself down with many tears and the squalor of humility, he had entreated the Lord; the same man both wished preserved, and wept slain, the young son, headlong in parricidal fury, who had both stained his father's bed with nefarious defilements and was waging wicked war against his father: foreseeing, namely, the everlasting punishments of a soul involved in such great crimes, which, to escape, he desired him to live, humbled, that he might be corrected through penance[^186]. These and many other things to be praised and imitated are found in that holy man, if a perverse mind does not search out that Scripture which speaks of him, especially if with a mind submissive and devout and plainly faithful we follow the sentence of God, who knew the hidden things of his heart, where, in the sight of him who cannot be deceived, it so pleased that he was even set forth by him to his own sons to be imitated.

**67.** For what else but the depths of his heart did the Spirit of God discern, when, rebuked through the prophet, he said, *I have sinned*; and immediately, at this one word, deserved to hear that he had received pardon? For what thing, except for eternal salvation? For the discipline of the paternal scourge was not omitted in him, according to God's threat, so that, having confessed, he might be freed forever, and, afflicted temporally, might be proved. Nor indeed was it a mediocre strength of faith, or a small indication of a meek and obedient mind, that when he had heard from the prophet that God had pardoned him, and yet the things that had been threatened consequently came to pass, he did not say that he had been deceived by the prophet's lie, nor murmur against God, as if he had pronounced false the pardon of his sins. For the man, deeply holy, and lifting his soul not against God but to God, understood that, unless the Lord were favorable to him confessing and repenting, how great a measure of eternal punishments his sins deserved: for which, when he was being burned with temporal corrections, he saw both that pardon remained toward him, and that the medicine was not neglected. But why did Saul, rebuked through Samuel, when he too said, *I have sinned*[^187], not deserve to hear what David did, that the Lord had pardoned him? Is there acceptance of persons with God? Far be it[^188]. But in a like voice which the human sense heard, there was a dissimilar breast which the divine eye discerned. What are we taught by such examples, except that the kingdom of heaven is within us[^189], and that we ought to worship God from our inmost parts, so that out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks[^190]; but not so that the people honor him with the lips, while their heart is far from him[^191]: nor that we dare to judge otherwise about men, whose inner parts we cannot see, than God who can do this, and cannot be deceived or corrupted; whose most evident sentence about David, since the so sublime authority of the divine Scripture contains it, is human rashness, judging otherwise, much to be laughed at, or rather grieved over? For those divine books about the ancient men are to be believed, who foretold things so far in the future, which are now seen as present.

**68.** What else do we learn in the Gospel, when the same voice of Peter is heard confessing Christ the Son of God[^192], which the demons too uttered—equal words, but with a far unequal heart[^193]? Accordingly, in a like voice the faith of Peter is praised, the uncleanness of the demons restrained: by whom, except by him who knew how to inspect, not with human ear but with divine mind, the inner roots of those voices, and to discern them without any falsity? For how many other men too say that Christ is the Son of the living God, and yet are not compared to the merits of Peter: not only those who will say in that day, *Lord, Lord*, and will hear, *Depart from me*[^194]; but even those who will be set apart at the right hand[^195], among whom very many neither ever even once denied Christ, nor disapproved his passion for our salvation, nor compelled the gentiles to Judaize[^196]; and yet to Peter, who did these things, sitting on the twelve thrones, and judging not only the twelve tribes but even the angels, they will appear unequal? So too many, desiring no man's wife, persecuting to death no husband of a desired one, yet cannot have the Davidic merit before God, though this man did those things. So great is the difference of what each thing is in oneself, and how much it displeases, that it may be utterly uprooted; and what fruitful and opulent thing rises up for it with vast fertility: because to farmers too those fields are more pleasing which, with even great thorns rooted out, produce a hundredfold, than those which never had any thorns and scarcely reach thirtyfold.

**69.** So too Moses, the most faithful servant of God in all his house, minister of the holy law, and of the holy, just, and good commandment, to whom the Apostle bears witness[^197]—for these words I mentioned are his: minister too of the sacraments, not now bestowing salvation, but still promising the Savior, which the Savior himself confirms, saying, *If you believed Moses, you would believe me too; for he wrote about me*[^198], whence in its place, as much as seemed sufficient, we discoursed against the impudent slanders of the Manicheans; this Moses, then, the servant of the living God, the true God, the highest God, fabricator of heaven and earth, not from another's material but from nothing, not under the pressure of necessity but from abounding goodness, not through the punishment of a member of his but through the power of his word: this Moses, I say, humble in refusing so great a ministry[^199], submissive in undertaking it, faithful in keeping it, strenuous in carrying it out; in ruling the people watchful, in correcting vehement, in loving ardent, in enduring patient; who, for those over whom he presided, interposed himself before God when he took counsel, opposed himself before God when he was angry: this man, I say, so great and so good, far be it from us to weigh from the reviling mouth of Faustus; but from the plainly truthful mouth of God, who truly knew the man whom he had made: since he both, as a judge, recognizes even the sins of men, which he himself does not do, in those who deny them, and, as a father, pardons them in those who confess them. From his mouth altogether we both love, and admire, and as much as we can imitate Moses his servant, although we are far inferior to his merits, even with no Egyptian either killed or despoiled, no war waged—of which he did the one by the disposition of a future defender, the other by an imposed command of God.

**70.** To pass over for the moment that, when he struck the Egyptian[^200], although God had not commanded him, yet in a prophetic person it was divinely permitted that this be done, that he might pre-signify something to come: of which I am now not treating, but discussing those deeds altogether as though they signified nothing; and, that eternal law being consulted, I find that a man ought not to have been killed by one who bore no ordained power, however injurious and wicked he was. Nevertheless, souls capable of and fertile in virtue often send ahead vices, by which they indicate this very thing—to what virtue they are most suited, if they have been cultivated by precepts. For just as farmers too, what land they have seen, although useless, yet producing huge weeds, pronounce fit for grain; and where they have looked upon fern, although they know it must be uprooted, they understand it suitable for strong vines; and what mountain they have seen growing wild with wild olives, they do not doubt it useful for olives, with cultivation added: so that motion of mind, by which Moses, not observing the order of power, did not endure that a fellow countryman suffering injury from a wicked citizen should be unavenged, was not useless for the fruits of virtues, but, still uncultivated, poured forth faulty indeed, but signs of great fertility. He himself, finally, through his angel, called Moses by divine words on Mount Sinai, through whom the people of Israel would be freed from Egypt; and by the miracle of the vision in the flaming and unburning bush, and by dominical words, prepared him for the fruit of obedience[^201]: he who also called Saul, persecuting the Church, from heaven, prostrated, raised, filled him; as it were struck, cut off, grafted, made fruitful[^202]. For that savagery of Paul, when, according to the zeal of his ancestral traditions, he persecuted the Church[^203], thinking he did a service to God, was as it were a wild vice, but an indication of great fruitfulness. Hence too was that of Peter, when, with sword drawn, wishing to defend the Lord, he cut off the persecutor's ear: which deed the Lord sufficiently menacingly restrained, saying, *Put back the sword; for whoever uses the sword will fall by the sword*[^204]. But he uses the sword who, with no superior and lawful power either commanding or permitting, arms himself for the blood of another. For surely the Lord had commanded that his disciples carry the sword, but had not commanded that they strike. What incongruity, then, if Peter, after this sin, was made shepherd of the Church, as Moses, after the Egyptian was struck, was made ruler of that Synagogue? For each, not by a detestable monstrousness, but by an amendable spiritedness, exceeded the rule of justice: each sinned by hatred of another's wickedness, but the one by fraternal, the other by dominical love, though still carnal. This vice must be cut back or uprooted; but yet so great a heart, like land for crops, must be cultivated for bearing virtues.

**71.** What, then, does Faustus now object concerning the despoiled Egyptians, not knowing what he says? In doing which Moses so far from sinned that, by not doing it, he would have sinned. For God had commanded it[^205], who surely knows, not only according to deeds, but also according to the heart of man, what each ought to suffer, or through whom. And so that people was still carnal, and occupied with desire of earthly things: but the Egyptians sacrilegious and unjust; for both, using that gold—that is, a creature of God—badly, to the injury of the Creator, they served their idols, and they had afflicted foreign men with gratuitous labor unjustly and vehemently. Worthy, then, were both these, to whom such things were commanded, and those, who suffered such things: and perhaps, according to their own wills and thoughts, the Hebrews were rather permitted to do these things than commanded; but God willed his permission to be made known to them through his servant Moses, when he commanded that he should say it. But it can be that there are other most hidden causes why this was said divinely to that people: but to divine commands one must yield by obeying, not resist by disputing. The Apostle said: *For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?*[^206] Whether, then, this be the cause which I have said, or any other lie hidden in the secret and hidden disposition of God, why he said this through Moses to that people, that they should ask of the Egyptians, to carry off, things to be lent to them; this, nevertheless, I affirm: that it was said neither in vain nor unjustly, and that Moses could not do otherwise than God had said, so that with the Lord was the counsel of commanding, but with the servant the obedience of carrying out.

**72.** But, he says, the true and good God is by no means to be believed to have commanded such things. Nay rather, none but the true and good God rightly commands such things—he who alone knows what is to be commanded to each, and alone allows no one to suffer anything unsuited to him. For the rest, let that ignorant and false goodness of the human heart contradict Christ too, lest, with the good God commanding, the impious and wicked suffer some evil, when he is about to say to the angels, *Gather first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn*. He nevertheless forbade the servants wishing to do this very thing unseasonably, saying, *Lest perhaps, when you wish to gather the tares, you uproot together with them the wheat also*[^207]. Thus the true and good God alone knows what, when, to whom, through whom he either commands or permits something to be done. This human, not goodness, but plainly vanity, could also contradict the same Lord, when, with their noxious desire, he permitted the demons wishing and asking to go into the swine[^208]: especially because the Manicheans believed that not only swine, but even tiny and abject animals, have the souls of men. With which vanity disapproved and rejected, this nevertheless stands: that our Lord Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, and on this account the true and good God, granted to the desire of the demons the death of another's cattle, the destruction of living things of whatever kind, and the grave loss of men. But who, most insane, would say that he could not have excluded them from men, even if he had been unwilling to grant to their noxious will the destruction of the swine? Further, if the savage and unjust desire of spirits condemned and already destined for eternal fire was, by the Creator and Orderer of all natures, by a hidden but everywhere just governance, relaxed to that toward which it had inclined itself; what is absurd if the Egyptians deserved to be deprived by the Hebrews—men unjustly lording it, by free men, to whom they had also been debtors of wages for their so hard and unjust labors—of earthly things, which they were even using by a sacrilegious rite to the injury of the Creator? Which, nevertheless, if Moses had commanded of his own accord, or the Hebrews had done of their own accord, they would surely have sinned: although they sinned, not indeed by doing this which God either commanded or permitted, but yet perhaps by desiring such things. But that they were permitted to do this by divine dispensation, they were permitted by his just and good judgment, who knows both how, by punishments, to restrain the wicked or instruct the subject; and to give stronger precepts to the more sound, and to ordain certain medicinal steps for the weaker. But Moses is neither to be charged with cupidity in those desired things, nor with contumacy in any divine commands whatever despised.

**73.** For certain deeds that eternal law, which commands the natural order to be preserved and forbids it to be disturbed, has so placed in a certain middle position for men, that in usurping them audacity is deservedly reproached, but in carrying them out obedience is rightly praised. So great a difference is there, in the natural order, what is done by whom, and under whom each acts. If Abraham had immolated his son of his own accord, what would he be but horrible and insane? But with God commanding, what did he appear but faithful and devoted[^209]? Which truth itself so cries out that Faustus, terrified by its voice, when, against Abraham himself, seeking with nails and teeth what to say, he reached even to a slanderous lie, yet did not dare to reproach this: unless perhaps that deed did not come into his mind, so noble that, unread and unsought, it would not occur to the mind—so famous, finally, sung in so many tongues, painted in so many places, that it would strike both the ears and eyes of one feigning ignorance. Wherefore, if in killing his son a spontaneous motion is found execrable, but, with God commanding, a complying service not only inculpable but even praiseworthy; why, Faustus, do you reproach Moses, that he despoiled the Egyptians? If the wickedness, as it were, of one doing human things irritates you, let the divine authority of the one commanding terrify you. Or are you prepared to find fault with God himself too, willing such things to be done? Get behind me, then, Satan; for you do not savor the things of God, but the things of men[^210]. And would that you had been worthy to hear this as Peter did, and afterward had preached that which, in God, you reproach with a weak sense; just as he afterward, with glorious proclamation, announced to the gentiles what at first, when the Lord willed it to be done, displeased him.

**74.** Wherefore, if at last human hardness, and the depraved and perverse will in right matters, understands that it makes the greatest difference whether something is committed by human cupidity or rashness, or whether God's command is obeyed—he who knows what, when, to whom he permits or commands, what it befits each to do and to suffer—let it neither marvel at nor shudder at the wars waged through Moses, because in them too, following divine commands, he was not raging but obedient: nor did God, when he commanded these things, rage; but he repaid worthy things to the worthy, and terrified the worthy. For what is blamed in war? Is it that men die who will sometime die anyway, that those who will live may be tamed in peace? To reproach this is the part of the timid, not of the religious. The desire of harming, the cruelty of avenging, an unappeased and unappeasable mind, the savagery of rebelling, the lust of domination, and whatever similar things—these are what are rightly blamed in wars; which, for the most part, that they may even be rightly punished, against the violence of those resisting, whether God or some lawful power commanding, the wars themselves are undertaken to be waged by good men, when they find themselves in that order of human affairs where the order itself justly constrains them either to command some such thing, or to obey in such things. Otherwise John, when soldiers came to him to be baptized, saying, *And we, what shall we do?* would have answered them: Throw away your arms, desert that military service; strike, wound, lay low no one. But because he knew that they, in doing these things by military service, were not murderers but ministers of the law; and not avengers of their own injuries, but defenders of the public safety, he answered them: *Do violence to no one, slander no one, let your pay suffice for you*[^211]. But because the Manicheans are accustomed openly to blaspheme John, let them hear the Lord Jesus Christ himself commanding that pay to be rendered to Caesar which John says ought to suffice for the soldier. *Render,* he says, *to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's*[^212]. For to this end too tributes are paid, that, on account of wars, pay may necessarily be furnished to the soldier. Deservedly too he praised the faith of that centurion who said, *I too am a man set under authority, having soldiers under me; and I say to this one, Go, and he goes; and to another, Come, and he comes; and to my servant, Do this, and he does it*[^213]—he did not command the desertion of that military service. But about just and unjust wars to dispute now is long, and not necessary.

**75.** For it makes a difference for what causes and under what authorities men undertake wars to be waged: yet that natural order, accommodated to the peace of mortals, demands this, that the authority and counsel of undertaking war be with the prince; but the ministry of carrying out the war's commands soldiers owe to peace and the common safety. But a war that is undertaken to be waged by God's authority, it is not lawful to doubt is rightly undertaken, whether to terrify, or to crush, or to subjugate the pride of mortals: since not even that which is waged by human cupidity can harm anything, not only the incorruptible God, but also his saints; for whom it is rather found even to profit, for the exercising of patience, and for the humbling of the soul, and for the bearing of paternal discipline. For no one has any power over them, unless it has been given him from above. *For there is no power but from God*[^214], whether commanding or permitting. When, therefore, a just man, if perchance he serves as a soldier even under a sacrilegious human king, rightly fights at his command, keeping the order of civic peace—he for whom what is commanded is either certainly not against God's precept, or, whether it is, is not certain, so that perhaps the iniquity of commanding makes the king guilty, but the order of serving shows the soldier innocent: how much more innocently is he engaged in the administration of wars who wages war at God's command, whom no one who serves him is unaware cannot command anything evil.

**76.** But if they suppose that God could not, on this account, command a war to be waged, because the Lord Jesus Christ afterward says, *I say to you, do not resist evil; but if anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the left also*[^215]; let them understand that this preparation is not in the body, but in the heart: for there is the holy chamber of that virtue which dwelt in those ancient just fathers of ours too; but the order of the dispensation of things and the distribution of times demanded that it first appear that even those earthly goods—in which both human kingdoms and victories over enemies are reckoned, for the sake of which especially the city of the impious, spread through the world, is wont to supplicate idols and demons—pertain to nothing but the power and choice of the one true God. Whence the Old Testament too veiled the secret of the kingdom of heaven, to be opened at the opportune time, with earthly promises, and in a manner shaded it more obscurely. But when the fullness of time came, that the New Testament might be revealed which was veiled by the figures of the Old, it now had to be demonstrated by evident testimony that there is another life for which this life ought to be despised, and another kingdom for which the adversity of all earthly kingdoms ought to be most patiently endured. Accordingly, those through whose confessions, passions, and deaths it pleased God to attest this are called martyrs, which in Latin is interpreted "witnesses": of whom so great a number flourished that, if Christ, who called Saul from heaven, and, having made him from a wolf a sheep, sent him into the midst of wolves[^216], had wished to gather them armed and to aid them fighting, as he aided the Hebrew fathers, what nations would resist? What kingdoms would not yield? But that a most illustrious testimony might be borne to the truth, by which it now had to be taught that God is to be served not for the sake of temporal felicity in this life, but for eternal felicity after this life, that which is commonly called infelicity had to be undergone and borne for the sake of that felicity. And so in the fullness of times the Son of God, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law[^217], made of the seed of David according to the flesh[^218], sends his disciples as sheep into the midst of wolves, and warns them not to fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; he promises too that the very integrity of the body will be renewed even to the restoring of a hair[^219]; he recalls Peter's sword into the sheath; he restores the enemy's cut-off ear to its former form; he says he could have commanded legions of angels to destroy his enemies, unless the cup had to be drunk which the Father's will had given[^220]; he drinks it, going before, and offers it to those who follow; he reveals the virtue of patience by his precept, confirms it by his example. On account of which *God raised him from the dead and gave him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, on earth, and under the earth; and every tongue confess that the Lord Jesus is in the glory of God the Father*[^221]. The Patriarchs and Prophets, then, reigned here, that it might be shown that God gives and takes away even these kingdoms: the Apostles and martyrs did not reign here, that the kingdom of heaven might rather be laid open as the thing to be desired. Those kings waged wars, that it might appear that such victories too are granted by God's will: these, by not resisting, were killed, that they might teach that to be slain for the faith of the truth is the better victory. Although even there the Prophets knew how to die for the truth, as the Lord himself says, *From the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah*[^222]: and here, after it began to be fulfilled which, under the figure of Solomon (who in Latin is interpreted "Peaceable"), was prophesied in the Psalm concerning the Lord Christ (for he himself is our peace[^223]), *And all the kings of the earth shall adore him, all nations shall serve him*[^224]; Christian emperors too, bearing full confidence of piety in Christ, gained a most glorious victory over their sacrilegious enemies, who had placed their hope in the sacraments of idols and demons; when, by the most open and well-known proofs, of which some have already committed to memory in writing, the divinations of the demons deceived those, and the predictions of the saints confirmed these.

**77.** But if it seems a wonder to these vain people that God commanded one thing then to the dispensers of the Old Testament, where the grace of the New was veiled, and another to the preachers of the New Testament, where the obscurity of the Old is revealed, let them attend to the Lord Christ himself changing what he had said, and saying another thing: *When I sent you,* he says, *without purse and bag and shoes, did you lack anything? But they said: Nothing. He says, then, to them: But now let him who has a purse take it, likewise also a bag; and let him who has none sell his tunic and buy a sword.* These people, surely, if they found these diverse things in the two separate Testaments, the Old and the New, would even from this cry out that the two Testaments were contrary to themselves. What, then, will they now reply, when the same one himself says: Before, I sent you without purse and bag and shoes, and you lacked nothing; but now let him who has a purse take it, likewise also a bag; and let him who has a tunic sell it and buy a sword? Do they now understand how, by no inconstancy of the one commanding, but by the reasoning of the one dispensing, according to the diversity of times, precepts, or counsels, or permissions are changed? For if they say that for the sake of a certain mystery he spoke this about taking the purse and bag and buying a sword, why do they not admit that for the sake of a certain mystery the same one God then commanded the Prophets to wage wars, and now forbade the Apostles? For in that which we mentioned from the Gospel there were not only the Lord's words, but the deeds too of the obeying disciples followed. For both then they went without purse and bag, and lacked nothing, as his question and their answer declared. And now they said to him, when he had commanded about buying a sword: *Behold, here are two swords. And he answered: It is enough.* Hence Peter too was found armed, when he cut off the persecutor's ear: where his spontaneous audacity is restrained[^225]; because, just as he had been commanded to take up iron, he had not so been commanded to strike. The Lord's will surely lay hidden, why he had commanded arms to be carried which he was unwilling they should use. Nevertheless, to him it belonged to command with reason, but to those to do the commands without questioning.

**78.** With a slanderous ignorance, then, Moses is reproached for having waged wars, who ought to have been reproached less if he waged them of his own accord than if, with God commanding, he did not wage them: but to dare to reproach God himself, that he commanded such things, or to disbelieve that the just and good God could command such things, is the part of a man—to speak more mildly—unable to consider that to the divine providence, extending through all things highest and lowest, neither is that new which arises, nor does that perish which dies; but that single things, each in their own order, whether of natures or of merits, either give way, or succeed, or remain: but that the right will of men is joined to the divine law, while disordered cupidity is restrained by the order of the divine law; so that neither does the good man will anything other than what is commanded, nor can the evil man do more than is permitted; yet so, indeed, that he cannot do with impunity what he has unjustly willed. And on this account, in all the things which human weakness shudders at or fears, only iniquity is rightly condemned: the rest are either the tributes of natures or the merits of faults. But a man becomes iniquitous when he loves for their own sake things to be taken up for the sake of something else, and seeks for the sake of something else things to be loved for their own sake. For thus, as far as in him lies, he disturbs in himself the natural order which the eternal law commands to be preserved. But a man becomes just when he seeks to use things for no other end than that for which they were divinely instituted; but to enjoy God himself for his own sake, and himself and his friend in God for the sake of that same God. For he loves a friend for God's sake, who loves the love of God in his friend. But whether iniquity or justice, unless it were in the will, it would not be in our power. Further, if it were not in our power, no reward, no punishment would be just: which no one is wise enough to perceive except one who is foolish. But ignorance and weakness—so that a man either does not know what he ought to will, or cannot do all that he has willed—come from the hidden order of punishments, and from those unsearchable judgments of God, with whom there is no iniquity[^226]. For the sin of Adam was disclosed to us by the faithful word of God; and that in him all die, and that through him sin entered into this world, and through sin death, was truly written[^227]. And that from this punishment the body is corrupted and weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling presses down the sense that thinks many things[^228], is most true and most well known to us: and that from this just punishment none but merciful grace frees us, is certain. And hence the Apostle, groaning, exclaims: *Wretched man that I am! Who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord*[^229]. But what the distribution of the judging and pitying God is—why one thus, another otherwise—is done by hidden causes, yet just. Yet we do not therefore not know that all these things are done by the judgment or mercy of God, although by measures and numbers and weights set in the hidden, by which all things are disposed by God the creator of all things that naturally are[^230]; not the author, but yet the orderer, even of sins; so that the things which would not be sins unless they were against nature are so judged and ordered that they are not permitted to disturb or disfigure the nature of the universe, being assigned to the places and conditions of their merits. Since these things are so, and since through this secret of God's judgments and the motions of human wills, by the same prosperities some are corrupted, others use them temperately; and by the same adversities some fail, others profit; and since human and mortal life itself is a temptation upon the earth[^231]: who of men knows to whom it profits or harms, in peace to reign or to serve, or to be at leisure, or to die; but in war to command, or to fight, or to conquer, or to be killed? Since this, however, is established, that both to whom it profits, it profits only by divine benefit; and to whom it harms, it harms only by divine judgment.

**79.** Why, then, do we leap into rash reproaches—and would that they were of men, and not of God? Let the dispensers of the Old Testament, themselves also the foretellers of the New Testament, have served by killing sinners; let the dispensers of the New Testament, themselves also the expounders of the Old Testament, have served by dying at the hands of sinners: yet both served the one God, teaching, through diverse and fitting times, that temporal goods are both to be sought from him and to be despised for his sake, and that temporal troubles can both be commanded by him and ought to be tolerated for his sake. What, then, did Moses command or do that was cruel, when, holily zealous for the people committed to him and desiring them to be subject to the one true God, after he learned that they had slipped away to fabricating and worshiping an idol and had prostituted an unchaste mind to demons, avenging with the sword upon a few of them whom God himself, whom they had offended, had willed by a deep and secret judgment to be struck and soon struck, he both healthfully terrified the present and established discipline for the future? For that he did what he did with no cruelty, but with great love, who would not recognize in his words, praying for their sin and saying, *If you forgive their sin, forgive it; but if not, blot me out of your book*[^232]? Every devoutly prudent person, then, comparing that slaughter and this prayer, sees surely most openly, sees how great an evil it is for the soul to fornicate through the images of demons, when he so rages who so loves. So too, plainly, the Apostle, not cruelly but lovingly, handed over a man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit might be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus[^233]. He handed over others too, that they might learn not to blaspheme[^234]. The Manicheans read apocryphal writings, written under the name of the Apostles by I know not what cobblers of fables: which, in the times of their writers, would have deserved to be received into the authority of the holy Church, if holy and learned men, who then were in this life and could examine such things, had recognized that they spoke true things. There, nevertheless, they read that the apostle Thomas, when he was at a certain wedding feast, a stranger and altogether unknown, struck on the palm by a certain servant, imprecated upon the man a continual and savage vengeance. For when he had gone out to the fountain, whence he was to minister water to the feasters, a lion, rushing upon him, killed him, and his hand, with which he had struck the apostle's head with a light blow, torn from his body, a dog brought, according to the word of that same apostle desiring and imprecating it, onto the tables at which the apostle himself was reclining. What can seem crueler than this? But because there, unless I am mistaken, this too is written, that he asked pardon for him in the world to come, a compensation of a greater benefit was made; so that both how dear to God the apostle was might be commended to the unknown by this fear, and the man might, after this life that must someday end, be provided for forever. Whether that narration be true or fabricated, it is now of no concern to me. For certainly the Manicheans, by whom those writings which the ecclesiastical Canon rejects are accepted as true and genuine, are at least from this compelled to confess that that virtue of patience which the Lord teaches, saying, *If anyone strikes you on your right cheek, offer him the left also*[^235], can be in the preparation of the heart, even if it is not displayed by the gesture of the body and the expression of words: since the apostle, struck on the palm, rather asked God that the injurious man be spared in the future age, but that in the present that injury not be left unavenged, than that he offered the other side to the one striking, or admonished him to strike again. He surely held inwardly the affection of love, and outwardly sought the example of correction. Whether this be true or fabricated, why are they unwilling to believe that with such a mind Moses, the servant of God, laid low with the sword the fabricators and adorers of an idol; when even in his words it sufficiently appears that he begged pardon for a sin of this kind in such a way that, unless he obtained it, he wished himself to be blotted out of God's book? And what is similar—an unknown man struck on the palm, and God freeing from the slavery of Egypt, leading through the divided sea, covering the pursuing enemies with the waves, abandoned and despised when an idol was preferred to him? But if we compare the punishments themselves, what is similar—to be killed by iron, and to be slaughtered and torn by beasts? since judges, serving the public laws, command those guilty of a greater crime to be exposed to beasts, rather than struck by the sword.

**80.** There remains, among the reviling and sacrilegious reproaches of Faustus to which I now reply, what the Lord said in speaking to the prophet Hosea: *Take to yourself a wife of fornication, and make children of fornication*[^236]. In which Scripture their unclean heart is so blinded that they do not understand even the most open evangelical words of the Lord saying to the Jews: *Harlots and tax collectors will go before you into the kingdom of heaven*[^237]. For what is against the truth of clemency, what hostile to the Christian faith, if a harlot, having left fornication, is changed into a chaste marriage? And what so incongruous and foreign to the faith of the prophet, as if he did not believe that all the sins of an unchaste woman, changed for the better, were forgiven? And so, when the prophet made a harlot his wife, both provision was made for the woman to correct her life, and the sacrament of a figure, of which we shall presently speak, was expressed. But who does not see in this deed what rather offends the error of the Manicheans? Namely, because harlots are accustomed to take care that they do not become pregnant. Accordingly, to these people one remaining a harlot pleased more, that she might not bind their god, than one made the wife of one man, that she might bear.

**81.** But about Solomon what shall I say, except that he is more grievously refuted by the testimony of his own faithful and holy Scripture than by the petulant and trifling revilings of Faustus? For it spoke truly and faithfully about him, both what good he had first had, and what evil, by doing, he had abandoned the good in which he had first been[^238]: but this man, with eyes closed—or rather extinguished—went, not by the light that, manifesting, led him; but rushed on by the malevolence that, precipitating, carried him. For by this very thing the holy books the more insinuated to their religious readers and choosers how chastely the holy men had several wives; in that Solomon, who had them not in that manner, but rather for lust than for offspring, was disapproved and reproached with these words, so that he was called a lover of women; and thence, lapsed and sunk into the depth of idolatry, he was, by the same truth that accepts the person of no one, exposed.

**82.** Now, then, all the persons having been treated through whom Faustus thought the Scriptures of the Old Testament were to be blamed, and a fitting discourse rendered to each, by which either the men of God were defended against the slanders of heretics and carnal men, or, with the men reproached, the Scripture was nonetheless shown praiseworthy and venerable: let us see next, in the order in which Faustus mentioned those men by accusing them, what the very deeds of the men also signify, what prophecies they bear, what future things they foretell: which we have already done concerning Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, whose God God willed himself so to be called[^239], as though he were the God of them alone, who is the God of the whole creation: not in vain surely granting them so great an honor, except because he knew in them what he alone could even perfectly and supremely know, a sincere and especial love: and because in those three Fathers he in a manner consummated the great and marvelous sacrament of his future people, which they begot through free women, not only unto freedom, as through Sarah[^240] and Rebecca[^241] and Leah and Rachel[^242]; but also unto slavery, as through that same Rebecca Esau was born, to whom it was said, *You shall be your brother's servant*[^243]; and through handmaids not only unto slavery, as through Hagar[^244], but also unto freedom, as through Bilhah and Zilpah[^245]. For thus, in the people of God too, both through spiritual free people are born not only unto praiseworthy freedom, as those to whom it is said, *Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ*[^246]; but also unto damnable slavery, as Simon through Philip[^247]; and through carnal slaves are born not only unto damnable slavery, who imitate them; but also unto praiseworthy freedom, those to whom it is said, *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do*[^248]. Whoever prudently recognizes this great sacrament in the people of God guards the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace unto the end, by cleaving to some, by tolerating others. We have done this concerning Lot too; showing what of his the Scripture narrated to be praised, what to be blamed, what to be understood concerning that whole deed it signified[^249].

**83.** It follows that we consider what the deed of Judah, that he lay with his daughter-in-law[^250], signified of things to come. But first it must be premised, lest it offend anyone of small consideration, that concerning certain evil works of men, in the holy Scriptures, certain things not evil, but good, are signified as to come. For everywhere divine providence keeps the virtue of its goodness, so that, just as from the intercourse of adulterers a man is formed and born—a good work of God from men's evil work, as we have already said in the preceding discourse[^251], from the fertility of seeds, not from the baseness of vices; so in the prophetic Scriptures, which narrate not only the good deeds of men, but also the evil, since the narration itself is prophetic, something is signified, even of future good things, by the evil works of men—not by the work of the one sinning, but of the one writing. For Judah, when, conquered by concupiscence, he went in to Tamar, did not bear this intention of his lust, that something might thereby be signified which pertained to the salvation of men: just as neither did that Judas who betrayed the Lord intend this, that something might thereby be done which pertained to the same salvation of men. Further, if from so evil a work of that Judas the Lord did so good a work that by the blood of his own passion he redeemed us; what wonder if his prophet, of whom he himself says, *For he wrote about me*[^252], signified something good from the evil deed of this Judah, that by his ministry he might teach us? For those deeds of men, the Holy Spirit disposing and inspiring, the narrating prophet collected, whose interposition would not be free from the presignifying of the things he had intended to prophesy: but for signifying certain good things, it makes no difference whether those deeds by which they are signified are good or evil. For what does it matter to me, when I wish to know something by reading, whether I find written in red lead black Ethiopians, and in ink white Gauls? Nevertheless, if I saw, not a writing, but such a painting, I would without doubt reproach it. So in the deeds of men, which are set forth to be imitated or avoided, it makes a great difference whether they are good or evil: but those which are written or spoken for signifying, it matters nothing, in the morals of the doers, what praise or reproach they deserve, provided only they have some necessary fittingness for prefiguring the thing about which it is treated. For just as Caiaphas in the Gospel—as far as concerned his noxious and pernicious mind, finally as far as concerned the very words, if in them you attend to the will of the speaker, by which he was working that the just one be unjustly killed—were surely evil: yet they signified a great good, he not knowing it, when he said, *It is expedient that one man die, and the whole nation not perish*: and it was said of him, *But this he did not say of himself, but, being high priest, he prophesied that Jesus was to die for the nation*[^253]. So the deed of Judah, according to his lust, was evil, but, he not knowing it, signified a great good: for of himself indeed he did the evil, but not of himself did he signify the good. But this, which I thought must necessarily be premised, let it avail not only for this deed of Judah, but also for the rest, if any evil works of men occur, by which something good is prophesied by the one narrating.

**84.** In Tamar, then, the daughter-in-law of Judah, is understood the people of the kingdom of the Jews, to whom, from the tribe of Judah, kings as it were husbands were applied: deservedly her name is interpreted "Bitterness"; for she herself gave the Lord a cup of gall[^254]. But two kinds of princes who did not work rightly in the people—one of those who did harm, another of those who profited nothing—are signified in the two sons of Judah: of whom one was malignant or savage before the Lord, the other poured out upon the ground, lest he should give seed to make Tamar fruitful. Nor are there more than two useless kinds of men to the human race: one of those who harm, the other of those unwilling to provide and, if they have any good in this earthly life, losing it, as it were pouring it out upon the ground. And since in evil he is first who harms than he who does not profit, therefore the elder is called malignant; but the following one, who poured out upon the ground. The name too of the elder, who was called Er, is interpreted "of Skin," with such tunics as the first men were clothed in punishment of their condemnation, when sent out of paradise[^255]; the name of the following one, who was called Onan, is interpreted "Their Grief": whose, except those to whom one profits nothing, when he has whence he could profit, and loses it on the ground? For a greater evil is that of life taken away, which "of skin" signifies, than of life not helped, which "their grief" signifies: God, nevertheless, is said to have killed both, where it is figured that he took the kingdom from such men. But the third son of Judah, in that he is not joined to that woman, signifies the time from which kings began not to be made from the tribe of Judah for the people of the Jews: and therefore he was indeed a son of Judah, but Tamar did not receive him as husband; because it was the same tribe of Judah, but no one from there now reigned over the people. Whence his name too, that is, Shelah, is interpreted "His Dismissal." But to this signification holy and just men do not pertain, who, although they lived at that time, yet pertain to the New Testament, to which, by prophesying, they were knowingly useful, such as David was. At that time, surely, when Judea had already begun not to have kings from the tribe of Judah, Herod the elder is not to be reckoned among its kings, as the husband of Tamar: for he was a foreigner, nor did he cleave to her by that sacrament of the mystical anointing as by a conjugal covenant; but he lorded it as an outsider, which power he had received from the Romans and from Caesar. So too his sons the tetrarchs, of whom one was called Herod by his father's name, who agreed with Pilate in the passion of the Lord[^256]: these foreigners, then, were so far not reckoned in that mystical kingdom of the Jews, that the Jews themselves publicly cried out, gnashing against Christ: *We have no king but Caesar*[^257]. Nor was this true, except in that universal dominion of the Romans: for even Caesar was a king not properly of the Jews; but, in order to deny Christ and flatter him, they condemned themselves by such a voice.

**85.** At that time, then, when the kingdom had already failed from the tribe of Judah, the true Savior our Lord had to come, who would not harm, and would much profit. For thus it had been prophesied: *A prince shall not fail from Judah, nor a leader from his thighs, until he comes for whom it is laid up. And he is the expectation of the nations*[^258]. Now at that time too all teaching of the Jews, and the mystical anointing from which they were called christs, had itself failed, according to the prophecy of Daniel. Then came he for whom it was laid up, the expectation of the nations, and the Holy of Holies was anointed[^259] with the oil of gladness above his fellows[^260]. For he was born in the time of Herod the elder[^261], but suffered under the lesser Herod the tetrarch[^262]. The figure of this one, then, coming to the sheep that had perished of the house of Israel[^263], Judah himself bore when he went to shear his sheep in Timnah, which is interpreted "Failing." For the prince had already failed from Judah, and all the teaching and anointing of the Jews, that he might come for whom it was laid up. But he came with his shepherd the Adullamite, whose name was Hirah; and Adullamite is interpreted "Testimony in water." With this testimony plainly the Lord came, having indeed a testimony greater than John[^264]; but yet, on account of the weak sheep, he used this testimony in water. For Hirah too, which was the name of that shepherd, is interpreted "the Vision of my Brother." John altogether saw his brother—a brother according to the seed of Abraham, according to the kinship of Mary his mother and Elizabeth his own mother; and the same as his Lord and his God, because, as he himself says, of his fullness he received[^265]. He altogether saw him, and therefore among those born of women none greater arose than he[^266]: because, of all those foretelling Christ, he himself saw what many just men and Prophets desired to see and did not see[^267]. He saluted him from the womb[^268]; he recognized him more perfectly from the dove, and therefore, as an Adullamite, he truly bore testimony in water[^269]. But the Lord came to shear the sheep—that is, to unburden them of their laborious loads, from which the teeth of the Church, praised in the Song of Songs, would be like a flock of shorn sheep[^270].

**86.** Now let Tamar change her dress; for Tamar is also interpreted "Changing": but in her surely let the name of bitterness remain; not of that bitterness in which she ministered gall to the Lord, but of that in which Peter wept bitterly[^271]. For Judah too in Latin is "Confession." Let bitterness, then, be mixed with confession, that true penance may be presignified. By this penance the Church, established in all nations, is made fruitful. For it behooved Christ to suffer, and to rise on the third day, and that penance and remission of sins be preached in his name through all nations, beginning from Jerusalem[^272]. For the harlot's dress itself too is the confession of sins. For Tamar already bears the type of the Church, called from the Gentiles, sitting in this dress at the gate of Enaim, which is interpreted "Fountains." For she ran like a deer to the fountains of waters, to come to the seed of Abraham: there she is made fruitful by one not recognizing her, because it was foretold of her: *A people whom I did not know served me*[^273]. She received in secret the ring, the necklace, and the staff: by vocation she is sealed, by justification adorned, by glorification exalted. For *whom he predestined, them he also called; and whom he called, them he also justified; and whom he justified, them he also glorified*[^274]. But these things, as I said, are still in secret, where too the conception of holy fruitfulness occurs. But the promised kid is sent as to a harlot, the kid being the reproach of sin; through the same Adullamite, as it were rebuking and saying, *Brood of vipers*[^275]. But the reproach of sin did not find her whom the bitterness of confession had changed. But afterward, the signs now made public—of the ring, necklace, and staff—she overcame the rashly judging Jews, whose person Judah himself now bore, who even today say that this is not the people of Christ, nor do we have the seed of Abraham: but, with the most certain proofs of our vocation, justification, glorification brought forth, they will without doubt be confounded, and will confess us to be more justified than themselves. More minutely, and member by member as it were, and point by point, I would search out and discuss these things, as much as the Lord aided my intention, were I not prohibited from this more laborious diligence by the care of finishing this work, which is already more prolix than I would wish.

**87.** Now I will touch, with as much brevity as I can, on what the sin of David[^276] signified in prophecy. For the very names, interpreted, sufficiently show what this deed too prefigured. David is interpreted "Strong of Hand," or "Desirable": and what is stronger than that Lion of the tribe of Judah, who conquered the world[^277]? and what more desirable than he of whom the prophet says, *he who is desired by all nations shall come*[^278]? Bathsheba is interpreted "Well of Satiety," or "Seventh Well": and whichever of these interpretations of this name we take up for that which we intend to say, it fits well enough. For in the Song of Songs that bride is the Church, who is called *a well of living water*[^279]: and to this well the name of the sevenfold number is joined, in signification of the Holy Spirit, on account of the reckoning of Pentecost, on which day the Holy Spirit, sent from heaven, came[^280]. For that this same feast day consists of weeks, the Scripture of Tobit too testifies[^281]. But to forty-nine, which is seven times seven, one is added, by which unity is commended. In this reckoning lives that apostolic sentence: *Bearing with one another in love, being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*[^282]. By the spiritual gift, then—that is, the sevenfold—the Church was made a well of satiety: because there was made in her a fountain of water springing up unto eternal life[^283], which whoever has shall not thirst forever[^284]. But Uriah, who had been her husband, what else does he signify by the interpretation of his name than the devil? To whose most wicked marriage were bound all whom the grace of God frees, that the Church, without spot or wrinkle, may be coupled to her own Savior[^285]. For Uriah is interpreted "My Light is God's"; but Hittite, "Cut off," or that he did not stand in the truth[^286], but was cut off from his supernal light, which he had from God, by the merit of his pride; or that, falling, his true powers lost, he yet transforms himself into an angel of light[^287], daring still to say, "My light is God's." Therefore this David indeed sinned grievously and wickedly; which crime of his God too reproved by rebuking through the prophet, and he himself washed away by repenting: yet that one desirable to all nations loved the Church washing herself upon the roof—that is, cleansing herself from the filth of the world, and, by spiritual contemplation, transcending and treading down the house of clay; and, the acquaintance of the first meeting with her begun, afterward killed the devil, utterly separated from her, and coupled her to himself in perpetual marriage. Let us hate sin, then, but not extinguish the prophecy: let us love that David as much as he is to be loved, who freed us from the devil by his mercy; let us love also this David, who healed in himself so grievous a wound of iniquity by the humility of penance.

**88.** Now about Solomon what shall I say, whom the holy Scripture vehemently reproves and condemns[^288], and mentions nothing at all of his penance or of God's indulgence toward him? Nor does it at all occur to me what good, at least in allegory, this lamentable overthrow of his signifies; unless perhaps someone should say that the foreign women, with love of whom he had burned, signify the Churches chosen from the Gentiles. This could perhaps be understood not absurdly, if those women, for Solomon's sake, abandoned their gods and worshiped his God: but since he himself, for their sake, offended his God and worshiped their gods, there is nothing good we can conjecture from this. Yet I do not judge that it signifies nothing; but something evil, as we said about the wife and daughters of Lot. For there appears, in the person of this Solomon, both a marvelous excellence and a marvelous overthrow. What, then, existed in him at diverse times—first good, and afterward evil—this in the Church, in this present world, is shown together at one time. For by his good, I think the good of the Church; but by his evil, the evil of the Church are signified: as, in the unity of one threshing-floor, as in that one man, the good in the grains, the evil in the chaff; as, in the unity of one crop, the good in the wheat, the evil in the tares[^289]. If indeed, with the things written about him more diligently treated, something more probable on this can shine forth, either to me, or to more learned and better men, yet now we have not so dismissed the matter as that the interrupted series of the connected discourse, hastening to other things, should hinder our intention.

**89.** But about the prophet Hosea, there is no need for it to be said by me what that command or deed signifies, when the Lord said to Hosea, *Go, and take to yourself a wife of fornication, and make children of fornication*; since the Scripture itself sufficiently demonstrates whence and why it was said. For it follows: *For the land, fornicating, will fornicate away from the Lord. And he went, and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim; and she conceived, and bore him a son. And the Lord said to him: Call his name Jezreel; for yet a little while, and I will visit the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Judah, and I will make the kingdom of the house of Israel to cease and turn away. And it shall be, on that day I will break the bow of Israel in the valley of Jezreel. And she conceived again, and bore a daughter. And the Lord said to him: Call her name Without-Mercy, for I will no longer add to have mercy on the house of Israel, but with forgetting I will forget them; and on the house of Judah I will have mercy, and I will save them in the Lord their God, and I will not save them by bow, and by sword, and by war, and by horses, and by horsemen. And she weaned her who was Without-Mercy, and conceived, and bore a son. And the Lord said to him: Call his name Not-My-People, for you are not my people, and I will not be your God. And the number of the children of Israel will be like the sand of the sea, which is without measure, and shall not be numbered; and it shall be, in the place where it was said to them, You are not my people, it shall be said to them, Sons of the living God. And the children of Judah and the children of Israel shall be gathered together, and shall set themselves one head, and shall come up from the land, for great is the day of Jezreel. Say to your brothers: My people; and to your sister: She who has obtained mercy*[^290]. Since, then, the Lord himself, through the same Scripture, evidently lays open the figure of this command and deed, and since the apostolic writings testify that this prophecy was fulfilled in the preaching of the New Testament: who is there who would dare to say that the command and deed were not for the sake of that for which he himself, in the holy writings, who commanded it, expounds that he commanded it and made the prophet do it? For the apostle Paul says: *That he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared for glory; whom also he called, namely us, not only from the Jews, but also from the Gentiles: as he says too in Hosea: I will call Not-My-People my people; and Not-Beloved, beloved: and it shall be, in the place where it was said to them, You are not my people, there they shall be called sons of the living God*[^291]. This, then, prophesied about the Gentiles, Paul shows. Whence Peter too, writing to the Gentiles, with the prophet indeed not named, yet inserts his prophecy in his own words, saying: *But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people of acquisition, that you may announce the virtues of him who called you out of darkness into his admirable light: who once were not a people, but now are the people of God; who once had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy*[^292]. From this it evidently appears that what was said through the prophet—*And the number of the children of Israel will be like the sand of the sea, which is without measure, and shall not be numbered*; and what was consequently added—*And it shall be, in the place where it was said to them, You are not my people, it shall be said to them, Sons of the living God*—was said not at all about that Israel which is according to the flesh; but about that of which the Apostle says to the Gentiles, *You, then, are the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise*[^293]. But since many of that Israel of the Jews too believed and will believe (for thence were the Apostles, thence the so many thousands who were associated with the Apostles in Jerusalem[^294], thence the Churches of which he says to the Galatians, *But I was unknown by face to the Churches of Judea which are in Christ*[^295]: whence too he so interprets the Lord, called a cornerstone in the Psalms[^296], that he joined two walls in himself, namely of the circumcision and of the uncircumcision: *That he might make the two in himself into one new man, making peace, and might reconcile both in one body to God, through the cross slaying the enmities in himself, and, coming, might announce the gospel of peace to those who are far off and to those who are near*—that is, to the Gentiles far off and the Jews near; *for he himself is our peace, who made both one*[^297]); deservedly this prophet too, setting the children of Judah for the Jews, and the children of Israel for the Gentiles, says: *And the children of Judah and the children of Israel shall be gathered together alike, and shall set themselves one head, and shall come up from the land.* Whoever, then, contradicts this prophecy, so manifestly declared by the very outcome of things, contradicts most impudently not only the prophetic, but also the apostolic writings; and not only any writings whatever, but even things fulfilled and suffused with the clearest light. Something, then, the deed of Judah perhaps required of more diligent intention, that this woman of fornication—who signifies the Church gathered from the prostitution of the superstitions of the Gentiles—might be recognized in the dress of that woman who was called Tamar: but here, since the Scripture itself lays itself open, and since it is illustrated by the consonant writings of the Apostles, why do we delay here any further, and not now see what remains concerning the servant of God Moses—what those things too signify which Faustus objected?

**90.** For that, defending his brother, he killed the Egyptian, occurs most easily to anyone, that the devil, injurious to us in this pilgrimage, is killed for us who are defended by the Lord Christ. But that he buried the slain one in the sand[^298], it is manifest that his now dead presence lies hidden in those who have no stable foundation. Whence the Lord builds the Church upon a rock; and those who hear his word and do it he compares to a prudent man who builds his house upon a rock, lest it yield to temptations and fall: but those who hear and do not do, he compares to a fool building upon sand, whose house, when tempted, has a great fall[^299].

**91.** But that he despoiled the Egyptians at the command of the Lord his God[^300], commanding nothing but most justly, what it prefigured I recall having already set down, in certain books which I entitled On Christian Doctrine, as much as then occurred to me: that by the gold and silver and garment of the Egyptians were signified certain teachings which, in the very custom of the Gentiles, are learned with not unprofitable zeal. But whether it signifies this, or that—that from the Gentiles themselves precious souls, like vessels of gold and silver, with their bodies surely, which the garments signify, join themselves to the people of God, that they may be freed together from this world as from Egypt; whether, then, this, or that, or something else was figured from this, it is yet certain to those who read these writings devoutly that not in vain, nor without the foretelling of things to come, were those things commanded, done, and written.

**92.** Now further, about the wars waged through Moses, it is too long to treat them all. Let it suffice, then, that about that war waged with Amalek[^301], I have already above, in this very work in which I reply to Faustus, expounded, as much as seemed sufficient for the business undertaken, what prophecy it sent ahead, what mystery it contained. Now let us see that in which they are accustomed to object the vice of cruelty to Moses, whether the enemies of these writings, or those devoid of all literature: which indeed Faustus did not set down expressly, when he said that he both commanded and did many cruel things. But because I know what they are especially and invidiously accustomed to boast, therefore I myself mentioned this, and defended it above, lest anyone—whether the Manicheans themselves who might wish to be corrected, or any other of the ignorant or impious—should suppose there was any crime in that deed. But now it must be asked what prophetic signification it also had, that he commanded many of those who had fabricated an idol for themselves while he was absent to be killed without any distinction of any kinship[^302]. It is easy to understand that the killing of those men signifies the persecution of such vices as those by which they slipped down to that idolatry. For the Psalm commands us to rage against such vices, when it says, *Be angry, and do not sin*[^303]. The Apostle commands us to rage against such vices, when he says, *Mortify your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, wantonness, evil concupiscence, and avarice, which is the service of idols*[^304].

**93.** But what that which he first did means—that he burned the very calf with fire, and broke it into tiny pieces, and scattered it into water, and gave it to the people to drink—requires a greater intention of searching out the signification. For if he broke the tablets, which he had received written by the finger of God—that is, by the working of the Holy Spirit—because he judged those to whom he should read them unworthy: if, finally, that that calf might be utterly abolished among them, he burned it, ground it, scattered it in water, and submerged it, why did he also give this to the people to drink? Whom would this deed not stir to search out and understand the prophetic signification? Let it then occur now to intent minds, as it were the body of the devil in the calf—that is, men in all nations, to whom for these sacrileges the head, that is, the author, is the devil. Golden, for this reason, because the rites of idolatry seem instituted as it were by wise men: of whom the Apostle says, *For, knowing God, they did not glorify him as God or give thanks; but they became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened: saying they were wise, they became fools, and changed the glory of the incorruptible God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, and of four-footed beasts, and of serpents*[^305]. From this as it were wisdom that golden calf, such figments as the very chief men of the Egyptians too, and as it were learned men, were accustomed to adore. By this calf, then, was signified the whole body—that is, the whole society of the Gentiles given over to idolatry. This sacrilegious society the Lord Christ burns with that fire of which he says in the Gospel, *I came to send fire upon the earth*[^306]: so that, since there is no one who hides himself from its heat[^307], while the Gentiles believe in him, the diabolical form in them may be dissolved by the fire of his virtue. Then that whole body is broken into pieces—that is, torn from that fusion of evil conspiracy, it is humbled by the word of truth: and, broken into pieces, it is put into water, that the Israelites—that is, the preachers of the Gospel—may transfer them by Baptism into their own members, that is, into the Lord's body. To which Israelites, concerning these same Gentiles, it was said to Peter, *Kill and eat*[^308]. If "Kill and eat," why not also "Break and drink"? Thus that calf, through the fire of zeal and the edge of the word and the water of Baptism, was absorbed rather by those whom it had tried to absorb.

**94.** If, then, these passages of the Scriptures too—about which the heretics slander those same Scriptures—when searched out, and in a manner interrogated, answer that, the more obscure they seem, the more marvelous treasures of mysteries lie hidden in them; how much more ought the blasphemous mouths of the impious to be wholly struck dumb, when they are stopped up by the most open truth, against which, with their breath choked, they do not find what to mutter; and the wretches prefer to have their throats stopped by its manifestation than their breast filled by its sweetness? All these things, then, sound forth Christ; that head which has now ascended into heaven, and this body of his which labors on earth unto the end, the whole intention of those writing the truly sacred writings brought to birth: nor is anything to be believed narrated in the connected text of the prophetic Books which does not signify something of things to come; except the things which are set down for this, that from them, in a manner, those things may be linked together which foretell that king and his people, whether by proper or by figured speeches or deeds. For just as in lyres and musical instruments of this kind, not all the things that are touched indeed resound something tuneful, but only the strings; yet the rest in the whole body of the lyre were fabricated for this, that there might be where they should be bound, whence and to where those strings should be stretched which the artist, about to tune and strike for the sweetness of the melody, plays: so in these prophetic narrations, which from the deeds of men are chosen by the prophetic spirit, either they already sound something by the signification of things to come; or, if they signify nothing of the kind, they are interposed for this, that there may be whence those signifying things, as it were sounding ones, may be connected.

**95.** But if the heretics are unwilling to accept these allegorical narrations of deeds, as they are expounded by us, or even contend that they signify nothing but what they properly sound; one must not wrestle with men who say, "It does not taste to my palate what you say tastes to yours": provided only that the things which are divinely commanded are either believed or understood either to form morals and piety, or to signify something figuratively, or both rather than neither: provided only that the very things which are understood as figuratively said or done are referred to those same good morals and piety. And on this account, even if to the Manicheans, or to any others, our understanding, or reasoning, or opinion about these figures of deeds is displeasing; let this suffice, that our fathers, to whom God bears testimony of good life and of obedience in his precepts, are defended by that rule of truth which cannot be displeasing except to depraved and distorted hearts; and that that Scripture, to which the perversity of that error is hostile, in whatever deeds of men—which it either praised or reproved or only narrated and set forth to us to be judged—itself persists inculpable and venerable.

**96.** But what more useful or healthful could be provided and looked to for those who devoutly read or hear those sacred writings, than that not only praiseworthy men should be set forth in them to be imitated, and reprehensible ones to be avoided; but also certain declinings and falls of the good into evil, whether likewise corrected again they return to the way, or remain irrevocable; and again certain changes of the evil and advances into good, whether to persevere, or to relapse again into their former state; so that neither may the just be lifted into pride by security, nor the wicked be hardened against the medicine by despair? But those deeds of men which pertain neither to imitation nor to avoidance, and yet are found in holy Scripture, are either set down for that connection by which, on certain occasions, one arrives at necessary things; or by the very fact that they seem superfluous, they sufficiently admonish that in them must be sought the oracle of some mystical signification. For we are not speaking of those books in which no, or few and not many, things most openly foretold by the prophetic spirit—now, too, with the very things fulfilled—attest the divine authority with the most faithful and most illustrious light of truth: so that whoever supposes that they spoke anything superfluously or as it were foolishly is altogether senseless, who sees not only all kinds of men and talents subjected to them, but also reads this foretold by them, and recognizes it accomplished.

**97.** If, then, someone reading the deed of David, of which he repented with the Lord rebuking and threatening, should make for himself from it a fuel for sinning, is that Scripture to be blamed; and not rather is he to be condemned the more severely, the more he wished to abuse, for wounding or killing himself, that thing which was written for healing and freeing? For because men, fallen into sin, either through pride neglect the medicine of penance, or through distrust of recovering salvation and obtaining pardon perish utterly, an example was set forth of so great a man, by which the sick may be healed, not by which those who are healthy may be wounded. For it is no fault of medicine, if either the insane kill themselves, or the malignant kill others, with medical instruments.

**98.** But yet these our fathers, the Patriarchs and Prophets, to whom that Scripture bears so illustrious a testimony of sanctity and piety—which Scripture none denies to have been divinely dispensed for the salvation of the human race, except whoever either does not know it, or has lost all sense of rational consideration—even if they had been lustful and cruel, as the error, or rather the fury, of the Manicheans accuses them: would they not even so be shown to be better, I do not say than their Elect, but than even that god of theirs? Is it not better for a man to wallow with a concubine, a woman, than for the most sincere light to be defiled by mingling itself with darkness? Behold, a man, for the sake of avarice and the belly, lying that his wife was his sister, sold her for the intercourse of others: how much worse and more execrable is he who, feigning his nature accommodated to the lust of those desiring it, subjected it to them, for nothing, to be polluted and corrupted! Now he who even knowingly lay with his daughters, does he not perpetrate less evil than he who mingles his members with the lusts of all such and worse basenesses? For what such thing is committed by the unclean and wicked, where your god, Manicheans, is not polluted with all basenesses? Finally, if Jacob truly, as Faustus says, wandered like a goat among four wives with foul concupiscence, bearing no care of offspring, but of wanton pleasure alone; how much less wretched would he be than your god, who would not only suffer all that disgrace of lust in him and in his four wives, congealed with all their bodies and motions; but in the very goat, to which the filthy man compared that man, endures all that generative motion and heat, and, everywhere mixed in a base condition, is inflamed in the he-goat, sown in the she-goat, generated in the kid? And on this account, even if Judah existed not only as a fornicator, but even as a knowing and nefarious incestor of his daughter-in-law, in the lust of that incest too your god would cleave, would be defiled, would burn. But David repented of his iniquity, in that he loved another's wife and ordered her husband to be killed: but when will your god repent, that, loved by the tartarean male and female race of the princes of darkness, he granted his members to their lust; and killed, not the husband whose spouse he had loved, but his own sons, in the members of the demons, by which demons he himself had been loved? But even if David had not repented, nor by such medicine recovered the health of righteousness, even so he would have stood better than this god of the Manicheans. For let us suppose this one, by that one deed, and by however many others of the kind, how many one man could commit; but that one, in all such deeds of all men, is convicted of being defiled and polluted by that mingling of his members. And the prophet Hosea is accused by Faustus: who, if, captivated by foul concupiscence, he had loved and married a harlot, you surely declare the souls of both, both the wanton lover and the obscene harlot, to be parts and members and the nature of your god: that harlot, then—for why should I beat about with words, and not say it plainly?—that harlot would be your god. For you cannot say that, with the sanctity of his nature preserved and uncorrupted, he fell upon that harlot's body not bound but only present: but you confess both that these members of your god are most defiled, and that on this account they need a great purgation. That harlot, then, of whom you dare to accuse the man of God, would be your god, even if she were not changed for the better into a chaste marriage: or, if you are unwilling, you do not at least deny that that soul of the harlot is a particle of your god, though the least. By this, then, he is already better than your god, since she would be one single harlot, but he, by the condition of that mingling of his with the whole race of darkness, is prostituted in all harlots, finally is wallowed, dissolved, bound, in all males and females widely and variously fornicating and corrupting themselves, again to be wallowed, dissolved, bound in their offspring, until at the last globe the most unclean part of your god is led forth, like an inexpiable harlot. These evils, surely, these basenesses, these disgraces your god could not remove from his members, and, compelled by the necessity of a monstrous enemy, came to these things: for he could not destroy the injurious and violent one with his own citizens or parts unharmed. How much better, then, is he who, the Egyptian killed, defended his unharmed brother, whom Faustus accuses with marvelous vanity, and does not look at his own god with a more marvelous blindness? How much better that he himself should have taken away vessels of gold and silver from the nation of the Egyptians, than that his members should be plundered by the nation of darkness? And yet, when he himself had waged so wretched a war, his worshipers object to the servant of our God that he waged wars, in which he always, with all his own, triumphed as victor over his enemies—who, what captive man or woman, while Moses warred, could be led from the people of Israel? Which your god too, if he could have done, would surely have done. This, then, is not to accuse the wicked, but to envy the more fortunate. But what cruelty of Moses was it, that he avenged with the sword upon a people that had grievously sinned against God? Of which sin, however, offering himself for them even to the divine vengeance, he begged pardon. Nevertheless, even if he had done this too not mercifully but cruelly, even so he would be better than your god. For he would in no way afterward, if he had conquered, himself condemn any of his innocent and obedient ones, if he had been sent to break a wedge of the enemy and had been captured: which this god is going to do with his own part, which he will fix in the globe, because it obeyed him commanding, because it went forth into the hostile wedges, with its own death set before it, for the salvation of his kingdom. But, he says, in this series of ages, now mingled and congealed with evils, it did not obey the precepts. Let us ask why. If by its own will, true fault and just punishment: but if now the will is guilty, there is no nature contrary unto sinning, and therefore all the deceit of the Manicheans is convicted and overturned. But if, oppressed by the enemy whither it was sent, if overcome by another's evil which it had not been able to resist, the punishment is unjust and a great cruelty. But the necessity of god is brought forward for excuse. Let those worship such a god who are unwilling to worship God. Surely, what must be confessed, even his very worshipers, although by worshiping such a god they are most wicked, are nevertheless better than he, because at least they exist: but he is nothing, except a fiction of falsehood and a thought of vanity. But now let us see the rest of Faustus's clever ravings.
