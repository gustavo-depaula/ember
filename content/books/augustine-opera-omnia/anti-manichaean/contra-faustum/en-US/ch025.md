# Book XXIV

**1.** FAUSTUS said: Why do you deny that man is made by God? We do not by any means deny that man is made by God; but we ask who, and when, and how he is made. Since indeed, according to the Apostle, there are two men, one of whom he sometimes calls the outer, but for the most part the earthly, sometimes also the old; but the other he calls the inner and heavenly and new[^1]. Which of these, then, is made by God, we ask. Since indeed there are two times of our birth too: one, that by which nature brought us, entangled in carnal chains, into this light; the other, when the truth, having converted us from error, regenerated us to itself, initiated unto faith. Which time of the second birth Jesus, signifying in the Gospel, says: *Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.* Which, when Nicodemus, little understanding, hesitated at, and inquired how it could happen—for a man could not enter into his mother's womb and be born again—Jesus, answering him, said: *Unless one is born of water and the Holy Spirit, he cannot see the kingdom of God.* And it follows: *What is born of the flesh is flesh; and what is born of the spirit is spirit*[^2]. Wherefore, if even birth is not that alone by which we are begotten in the body, but also that other by which we are reborn in the spirit, it is a no less concern to ask this very thing too: in which of them God makes us. The manner of being born too is twofold: one proper to fury and intemperance, by which we are basely and through lust sown by our begetters; the other of honesty and sanctimony, by which in Christ Jesus, through the Holy Spirit, under the teachings of good men, we are disciplined unto faith: whence too every religion, and especially the Christian, calls the untaught to the sacrament as infants. Which the Apostle too, signifying, says: *My little children, of whom I am again in travail, until Christ be formed in you*[^3]. Wherefore it is no longer asked whether God makes man; but when, and which one, and how he makes him: for if, when we are formed in the womb, as it pleases nearly the Gentiles and the Jews and you yourselves, God then forms us to his image, then he both makes us old and creates us through fury and lust, which I do not know whether it befits his divinity. But if, when we believe and are converted to a better state of life, then we are formed by God, as it pleases nearly Christ and his Apostles and us; surely he both makes us new, and makes us honestly and purely: than which what is more consonant or more befitting his holy and venerable majesty? But if you too do not spurn the authority of Paul, from him we will show you which one, and when, and how God makes man. He says to the Ephesians: *That you put off, according to the former conversation, the old man, who is corrupted by the desires of error: but be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man, who according to God was created in the righteousness and holiness of truth*[^4]. You see, then, when man is created to the image of God; you see here both another man shown, and another birth, and another manner of being born. For when he says, "Put off and put on," he signifies surely the time of believing: but when he testifies that the new man is created by God, then he indicates that the old is neither from him nor formed according to him. And when he goes on, saying that the former is made in holiness and righteousness and truth, then he designates and demonstrates that other manner of birth which I said is far dissimilar to this one, which sowed our bodies by the furious embraces of our begetters; and which he also shows is not from God, when he showed that one alone to be from God. And likewise to the Colossians he says the same thing again: *Strip off the old man with his deeds, and put on the new, who is renewed in the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him in you.* And from this too, then, he not only shows that the new man is the one whom God makes, but taught when also and by what manner he is formed, that is: "In the knowledge of God," by which is signified the time of believing. And further: "According to the image," he says, "of him who created him": so that from this it is clear that the old man is neither the image of God, nor formed by him. For that too which follows, saying, *Where there is no male and female, Jew and Greek, barbarian and Scythian*[^5], more and more shows that this birth, which made us males and females, Greeks and Jews, Scythians and barbarians, is not that in which God works when he forms man; but that which, stripping us of all variety of nations and sex and condition, renders us one, after the likeness of him who is one, that is, Christ: as the same Apostle again says: *As many as were baptized in Christ have put on Christ: there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither male nor female, there is neither slave nor free; but all are one in Christ*[^6]. Therefore man is then made by God, when he is made one out of many, not when out of one he is divided into many. But the first birth, that is, the corporeal, divided us; the second unites, the intelligible and divine: and therefore most rightly we have thought this one to be ascribed to the nature of the body, but that one to the supernal majesty. Wherefore the same Apostle again says to the Corinthians: *In Christ Jesus, through the gospel, I begot you*[^7]: and to the Galatians, of himself: *When it pleased him who set me apart from my mother's womb, that he might reveal his Son in me, that I might announce him among the Gentiles, immediately I did not consent to flesh and blood*[^8]. You see, then, that everywhere he asserts that we are formed by God in this second birth of ours, the spiritual one only; not in that former obscene and shameful one, which conceived and formed and bore us in the maternal womb no more excellently or cleanly than the other animals. About which matter, if you are willing to consider, you will find that in this part we differ from you not so much in profession as in understanding. For if it pleases you to attribute this to the old and outer and earthly man, that he is formed by God; but we, on the contrary, would give this to the heavenly, and attribute it to the inner and new man: nor that rashly or presumptuously, but learning it from Christ and his Apostles, who are shown to have first taught these things in the world.

**2.** AUGUSTINE replied: The apostle Paul indeed wishes the inner man to be understood in the spirit of the mind, but the outer in the body and this mortal life: yet that he ever called both of these together two men is nowhere read in his writings; but one, whom God made wholly—that is, both that which is inner and that which is outer: but he made him to his image only according to that which is inner, not only incorporeal but also rational, which is not in beasts. Not, then, did he make one man to his image, and another not to his image: but because both this inner and outer together are one man, this one man he made to his image, not according to that which has a body and corporeal life, but according to that which has a rational mind, by which he may know God, and by the same excellence of reason be set before all irrational things. But that inner one Faustus grants is made by God: "When," he says, "he is renewed in the knowledge of God, according to the image of him who created him." This apostolic sentence I plainly acknowledge[^9]; why does he himself not acknowledge the other: *God set the members, each one of them, in the body as he willed*[^10]? Behold, God is preached even as the maker of the outer man by the same Apostle: why does he choose from there what he thinks is for him, and is silent about, or rejects, what cuts off the fables of the Manicheans? Likewise, when the same Paul discoursed about the earthly and heavenly man, distinguishing between the mortal and the immortal, between what we are in Adam and what we shall be in Christ, from the very Law, from the very book and from the very passage he applied the testimony of the earthly—that is, the animal body—where it is written that God made the earthly man too. For when he was treating how the dead will rise, and with what body they will come, when he had given the likeness from the seeds of grain, that bare grains are sown, and God gives them a body as he willed, to each seed its own proper body: where likewise he overturns the error of Mani, who says that the nation of darkness, not God, creates both grains and herbs and all roots and shrubs; and believes that God is rather bound in these forms and kinds of things, than that he works any of these: when, then, against the sacrilegious vanity of Mani he had said these things too, he came to the differences of fleshes: *Not all flesh,* he says, *is the same flesh*: thence to those of heavenly and earthly bodies, thence to the change of our body by which it can become spiritual and heavenly: *It is sown,* he says, *in dishonor, it will rise in glory: it is sown in weakness, it will rise in power: it is sown an animal body, it will rise a spiritual body.* And thence, wishing to show the origin of the animal body: *If there is,* he says, *an animal body, there is also a spiritual; as it is written: The first man Adam was made into a living soul*[^11]. But this is written in Genesis[^12], where it is narrated how God made man, and animated the body which he had formed from the earth. But by the old man the Apostle means nothing else than the old life, which is in sin, in which one lives according to Adam: of whom he says, *Through one man sin entered into the world, and through sin death; and so it passed into all men, in whom all sinned*[^13]. Therefore that whole man, that is, in both his inner and outer part, grew old on account of sin, and was assigned to the punishment of mortality: but he is renewed now according to the inner man, where he is reformed according to the image of his Creator, putting off unrighteousness—that is, the old man; and putting on righteousness—that is, the new man. But then, when the spiritual body, which is sown an animal one, will rise, the outer too will receive the dignity of the heavenly condition: so that the whole that was created may be recreated, and the whole that was made may be remade, by him recreating who created, and remaking who made. Which he briefly explains where he says: *The body indeed is dead on account of sin, but the spirit is life on account of righteousness. But if the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you; he who raised Christ from the dead will give life also to your mortal bodies through his Spirit dwelling in you*[^14]. For who, instructed in catholic truth, is unaware that according to the body some men are male, others female, not according to the spirit of the mind, in which we are renewed according to the image of God? Yet that God made both, again the same Apostle is witness, where he says: *Neither is the woman without the man, nor the man without the woman, in the Lord: for as the woman is from the man, so too the man is through the woman; but all things are from God*[^15]. What does the inept deceit of men estranged from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them on account of the blindness of their heart[^16] say to these things, except: "In the apostolic writings what we wish is true, what we do not wish is false"? So rave the Manicheans: but let them come to their senses, and not be Manicheans. Of whom if it be asked (since they confess the inner man is renewed to the image of God, and bring forth this testimony even of their own accord: but then, says Faustus, God makes man, when the inner is renewed in the knowledge of God), whether he himself made him who remakes him, he himself founded him who renews him; they will answer: He himself. To which answer of theirs, if we add, asking when he formed him whom he now reforms: they will seek where to hide themselves, lest they be compelled to lay open the disgrace of their fable. For they do not say that he was formed or founded or instituted by God, but that a part of his substance—plainly his own—was sent against his enemies: nor that he grew old by sin, but that he was captivated and deformed by his enemies through necessity, and the rest which it is now tedious to say. For there too they mention a first man—not the one whom the Apostle calls earthly, from the earth[^17], but I know not what one of their own, leaping forth from the ark of lies: about whom Faustus is altogether silent, when he had set himself a question about man; fearing lest in some way it become known to those against whom he disputes.
