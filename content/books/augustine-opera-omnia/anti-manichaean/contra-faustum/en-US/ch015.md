# Book 14

**1.** Faustus said: Why do you not accept Moses?

For the sake of the love and piety by which we worship Christ. For who is so irreligious as to look gladly upon one who has cursed his father? Wherefore we too execrate Moses—although he never spared blaspheming any human or divine thing—yet all the more for this, that he assailed Christ the Son of God, who for the sake of our salvation hung on the wood, with a dire reviling of imprecation: whether willingly or by chance, you must see. For by neither of these will he be excused or commended, so as to deserve to be accepted. For he says that everyone who hangs on wood is cursed[^1]. This man, then, you wish me to accept, in this man to believe: when, if he was divine, it is established that he knowingly and willingly cursed Christ; but if he cursed him unwillingly and unknowingly, it is established that he was not divine. Choose, then, either one: either that Moses was not a prophet, and sinned through imprudence, so that, while he cursed others after his manner, he unknowingly blasphemed God too; or that he was indeed divine, and was not ignorant of these things to come, but, envying our salvation, which was to come from the wood, poured forth the poisons of his cursing mouth against its author.

And who, then, would believe that this man saw or knew the Father, who so tore the Son: that this man could foretell the coming of the Son, who was ignorant of the outcome of his ascension? Add to this that I also consider how widely this reviling is scattered, and how many things it embraces and violates: so that it touches even all the just and the martyrs, as many as departed this life by the outcome of a similar passion, like Peter, and Andrew, and the rest of the same lot. Whom, unless Moses either did not know, as no prophet, or, if he was a prophet, hated as malignant, he would not have torn with the insult of so cruel an imprecation. For he does not even say that they are cursed at least commonly, that is, only among men, but cursed before God. And if that is so, whence will any hope of blessing be left, either to Christ, or to the Apostles, or to ourselves, if it should happen that we be crucified for the sake of his name?

How imprudent, finally, and devoid of divine inspiration he was, that he could not consider that men are hung on wood for various causes; some indeed for a wicked crime, but some for the sake of righteousness, and on account of God: and therefore confusedly cast all, without any distinction, under the same curse: when, if any prudence were in him—I do not say divination—and if the cross so offended him that it alone was excepted and rejected out of every kind of punishment, he should have said that every wicked and impious man who had hung on wood was cursed; so that there might be some distinction between the just and the unjust: although not even so would he have said true things, since Christ brought the robber from the wood with him into the paradise of his Father[^2]. Where, then, is: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood?* Or was Barabbas, that notorious robber, who was not only by no means hung on wood, but even sent out of prison at the request of the Jews[^3], more blessed than he who with Christ ascended from the cross into heaven?

What, finally, of the fact that he even calls cursed him who adores the sun or the moon[^4]? If, then, placed under a Gentile king, I be compelled to adore the sun, and when I resist, fearing this curse, I be ordered to be crucified, I shall run into another curse of his, which he uttered against him who hangs on wood. Or is it that to him it is customary to curse all good things? But we ought to value his imprecations as much as those of irritated old women. For so too he pursues all the boys and virgins of God with an equal cursing, saying that everyone is cursed who has not raised up seed in Israel[^5].

Which reviling likewise touches principally Jesus, who, himself sprung also, as you say, from the Jews, yet raised up no offspring among them for the sake of preserving posterity. Then also his disciples, some of whom indeed he separated from their wives, whom he had found joined: but some he even forbade to be joined, whom he found untouched. On which account you may rightly recognize that we execrate this unpunished tongue of Moses, assailing with the darts of his curses Christ, who seeks light, holiness, all divine things. And lest perhaps you think there is much difference between one hung and one crucified—for this too you are wont to take up as a defense for yourselves—you may hear Paul prescribing against these your contrivances: *Christ redeemed us from the curse of the Law, having been made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*[^6].

**2.** Augustine replied: The pious man Faustus grieves that Christ was cursed by Moses, and on this account hates Moses, because he loves Christ. Meanwhile, before I open in how great a mystery, and how piously, it was said: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*[^7], I ask these pious men why they are angry at Moses, since his curse did not reach the Christ of these men. For if Christ hung on wood, he was surely fastened with nails. Hence he even showed those scars after the resurrection to the less believing disciple[^8]. And if that is so, he surely had a woundable and mortal body, which these men refuse to confess. If, then, both those wounds and those scars were false, false too is that he hung on wood. So that curse could not reach him; there is no reason they should be angry at him from whose mouth it proceeded. And so, if they themselves pretend to be angry at him who cursed the false death of Christ—which I would say according to them—how greatly are they to be avoided, who do not curse Christ, but, what is more execrable, accuse him? For if he who brings a curse upon mortality is not to be accepted; how is he to be detested who casts deceit at the truth? But now let us see, on the occasion of the slandering heretics, how that mystery is expounded to the faithful.

**3.** The death of man is from the punishment of sin, whence it too is called sin: not because a man sins while he dies, but because it came about from sin that he dies. Just as in one way "tongue" is properly called the flesh which moves within the teeth under the palate; and in another way "tongue" is called what is done through the tongue, according to which manner one is called the Greek tongue, another the Latin: and "hand" in one way is properly called that very member of the body which we move to work, and in another way "hand" is called the writing which is done by the hand. For we say: His hand was produced; his hand was read against him; I have your hand; take back your hand. Hand is surely properly a member of man: but I do not think that that writing is a member of man, and yet it is called a hand, because it was made by the hand.

So too "sin" is called not only the evil deed itself, which is worthy of punishment, but even death itself, which was made by sin, has been called sin. That sin, then, by which one would be guilty of death, Christ did not commit: but that other, that is, death, which was inflicted on human nature by sin, he took up for us. This he hung on the wood, this was cursed through Moses: there death was condemned that it should not reign, and cursed that it should perish. Wherefore through such a sin of Christ our sin too was condemned, that we might be freed, lest, with sin reigning, we remain condemned.

**4.** Why, then, does Faustus wonder that sin was cursed, that death was cursed, that the mortality of the flesh was cursed—without the sin of Christ, yet made from the sin of man even in Christ? For from Adam he assumed a body, because from Adam was the Virgin Mary, who bore Christ. But God had said in paradise: *On the day you touch it, you will die the death*[^9]: this is the curse which hung on the wood. Let him deny Christ cursed who denies him also dead. But he who confesses him both dead, and cannot deny that death is from sin, and on this account that it too is called sin, let him hear the Apostle saying: *That our old man was crucified together with him*[^10]; and let him understand whom Moses called cursed. And therefore the Apostle says securely of Christ: *Made a curse for us*[^11]: just as he did not fear to say: *He died for all*[^12]. For this is, he died, which, he was cursed; since death itself is from a curse, and every sin is cursed, whether that which is done, so that punishment follows, or the punishment itself, which in another way is called sin, because it comes from sin. But Christ took up, without guilt, our punishment, that thence he might loose our guilt, and end also our punishment.

**5.** I would say these things from my own wit, did not the Apostle so often inculcate this, so as both to rouse the sleeping and to choke the slanderers. *God sent*, he says, *his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, that he might condemn sin from sin in the flesh*[^13]. That flesh, then, was not sinful flesh, because it had not come into Mary through a male from the transmission of mortality: but yet, because death is from sin, and that flesh, although from a virgin, was nonetheless mortal; by that very fact that it was mortal, it had the likeness of sinful flesh. This he also calls sin, consequently saying: *That he might condemn sin from sin in the flesh.* Likewise in another place: *Him*, he says, *who knew no sin, he made sin for us, that we might be the righteousness of God in him*[^14]. Why, then, should Moses fear to say "cursed," which Paul did not fear to say "sin"? Plainly this the Prophet both ought to have foreseen and to have foretold, prepared to be reproached by the heretics along with the Apostle. For whoever reproaches the Prophet for having said "cursed" is compelled to reproach the Apostle for having said "sin": for surely a curse is the companion of sin.

**6.** Nor is there for this reason greater odium, that he added "before God," so as to say: *Cursed before God is everyone who hangs on wood*[^15]. For unless God hated sin, and our death, he would not send his Son to take it up and destroy it. What wonder, then, if that is cursed before God which God hates? For God grants us immortality, which is to come at Christ's coming, all the more gladly, the more mercifully he hates our death, which hung on the wood at Christ's dying. But as for the addition of "everyone," so that it was said: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*, Moses did not, indeed, foresee less that the just too would be on the cross, but he well foresaw that heretics would deny the true death of the Lord, and therefore would wish to separate Christ from this curse, in order to separate him also from the truth of death. For if that death was not true, no curse hung on the crucified Christ on the wood, because he was not truly crucified either.

But against the heretics to come from afar, from how far does Moses cry "you shuffle without cause," to whom the truth of Christ's death is displeasing: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*: not this one or that one, but absolutely everyone! Even the Son of God too? Even him, utterly. For this is what you do not want: from this you busy yourselves, from this you seduce. For "cursed for us" displeases you, because "dead for us" displeases you. For then he would be outside the curse of that Adam, if he were outside his death. But since from man and for man he took up death, from him and for him he did not disdain to take up also the curse which accompanies death—even he, utterly even that Son of God, ever living in his righteousness, but dead on account of our sins[^16], in flesh assumed from our punishment. So too ever blessed in his righteousness, but cursed on account of our sins, in death assumed from our punishment: and by this the word "everyone" was added, lest Christ be said not to pertain to true death, if, by a foolish honoring, he were separated from the curse which is joined to death.

**7.** But he who is faithful from evangelical truth understands that it is as little an insult to Christ from the mouth of Moses, when he called him cursed—not from the divinity of his majesty, but from the condition of our punishment, by which he was hung on wood—as it is no praise of Christ from the mouth of the Manicheans, when they deny that he had mortal flesh, in which he might suffer true death: because from that prophetic curse the praise of his humility is understood, while from this heretical honor, as it were, the crime of falsehood is cast at him. If, then, you deny the curse, deny his death: if you deny his death, you fight no longer against Moses, but against the Apostles. But if you confess his death, confess that he took up the punishment of our sin without our sin. And now, when you hear of the punishment of sin, either believe that it comes from a blessing, or from a curse: if the punishment of sin comes from a blessing, choose to be always in the punishment of sin; but if you choose to be freed from it, believe that, through the justice of the divine sentence, it came from a curse. Confess, then, that he took up the curse for us, whom you confess to have died for us; and that Moses wished to signify nothing else, when he said: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*[^17]; except: Mortal is everyone, and dying is everyone, who hangs on wood.

For he could have said: Cursed is everyone mortal; or: Cursed is everyone dying: but this is what the Prophet asserts, because he knew that Christ's death would hang on the cross, and that there would be heretics who would say: He hung indeed on the wood, but in a certain appearance, not so as to die truly. By crying out, then, *Cursed*, he cried out nothing else than that he truly died, knowing that the death of sinful man, which without sin he himself took up, comes from that curse by which it was said: *If you touch it, you will die the death*[^18]. To this pertains also that serpent hung on wood, by which it was signified that Christ did not feign a false death, but hung up that true death on the wood of his passion, into which that serpent cast man by his evil persuasion. This true death these men refuse to look upon: and therefore they are not healed from the poison of the serpent, just as in the desert all who looked upon him were healed[^19].

**8.** And so we confess that it is said by the unskilled that it is one thing to be fastened to wood, another to hang on wood. For thus some think this question is to be resolved, by saying that Judas was cursed by Moses, who hanged himself with a noose; as though they first knew whether he hanged himself from wood or from stone. But it is true, what Faustus too recalled, that the Apostle does not allow it to be understood otherwise than as foretold of Christ. But such lack of skill in some Catholics is the hunting ground of the Manicheans. For they are wont to pursue such men, to entangle such men in their deceits: into such men we had fallen, with such we had stuck; from such, not by our own strength, but by the mercy of God, we were torn away.

**9.** But what divine things did Moses assail, as Faustus accuses, saying that "he never spared any human or divine thing"? For he said it, and goes off: he was at no pains to prove anything, he took no care to show anything. But we know that Moses piously praised all things truly divine, and, with a fitness suited to his time and by the grace of his dispensation, justly governed human things. Let these men demand that I teach this, when they too have tried to teach what Faustus objects—cautiously indeed, sharp as he was, but on this account incautious, because by his own sharpness he destroyed himself. For a heart sharp toward truth is happy, but unhappy against truth.

He did not say, he spared none of men or of gods, but, he says, of human and divine things. For if he said that Moses did not spare God, he would easily be convicted of a false accusation; since Moses would be found everywhere honoring and preaching the true God, who made heaven and earth. But if he said that he spared none of the gods, he would betray to the Christians that he worships those gods whom Moses forbids to adore: and so he would not, with the chicks fleeing under the wings of mother Catholic Church, gather what he did not bear. Therefore, in order to lay snares for the little ones, he said that Moses spared none of the divine things, so that neither could the Christians, by an open worship of gods, flee the impiety of these men too abhorrent from the Christian religion, nor could the pagans favor them against us, who knew that Moses said many true and worthy things against the idols and against the gods of the gentiles, which are demons.

**10.** But if this displeases these men, let them openly confess themselves to be worshipers of idols or demons: which indeed they would be unknowingly, by this alone, that they are heretics. For of such the Apostle said: *That in the last times some will withdraw from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, to the doctrines of demons in the hypocrisy of liars*[^20]. For who, except demons, to whom deceit is a friend, would persuade these men that Christ suffered deceitfully, died deceitfully, showed scars deceitfully; that is, that he neither truly suffered, nor truly died, nor were those scars true from true wounds? What are more evident doctrines of lying demons than these, by which it is urged that the Son of God, that is, the very truth, is a liar? But these men have in their teaching also an open worship—not, indeed, of demons, but yet of a creature—which the Apostle condemns, saying: *And they worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator*[^21].

**11.** Accordingly these men, in the phantasms of their fables, worship idols and demons without knowing it: but in the sun and the moon they know that they serve a creature; and as for their thinking that they serve also the Creator, they are much deceived: for they serve their own phantasm, but in no way serve the Creator, when they deny that God created those things which the Apostle plainly shows to pertain to the creation of God, saying, when he was dealing with foods and flesh: *For every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be cast away which is received with thanksgiving*[^22].

See what sound doctrine is, which, not enduring, you turned to fables. As the Apostle both praises the creation of God, and yet forbids the worship of religion to be shown to it: so too Moses (who seems to you to have spared none of the divine things, for no other reason, I suspect, than that he forbade the sun and the moon to be adored[^23], toward whose circuit you turn yourselves through every corner, to adore them) praised the sun and the moon with true praise, when he narrated that they were made by God, and set in the celestial order to accomplish their works, just as it is: *The sun for the power of the day, the moon for the power of the night*[^24]. But the sun and the moon do not rejoice in your false praises.

The devil knows how to rejoice in false praise, a creature that has transgressed. But the powers of the heavens, which have not fallen by sin, wish their maker to be praised in them: whose true praise is that by which no injury is done to their Creator. But injury is done when it is said that they are his parts, or his members, or something of his very substance. For he, perfect, and needing nothing, and nowhere flowing away, neither cut apart, nor stretched out through places, wholly unchangeable in himself, and self-sufficient, blessed in himself, out of the abundance of his goodness, through his Word, said, and they were made; commanded, and they were created[^25]. Accordingly, if earthly bodies, of which the Apostle was speaking when he said that no food is unclean, are good, because every creature of God is good; how much more the heavenly ones, among which the sun and the moon excel, since the Apostle himself says: *Heavenly bodies and earthly bodies; but one is the glory of the heavenly, another of the earthly*[^26].

12.1. Moses, then, does not revile the sun and the moon, when he forbids them to be adored; but he praises them as a heavenly creature: but he praises God as the creator of heavenly and earthly things; nor does he want God to be offended, when those are adored in place of him who, on account of him and from him, are praised.

12.2. But how cleverly Faustus seemed to himself to reproach Moses for even calling cursed the one who adores the sun and the moon. "If, then," he says, "placed under a gentile king, I am compelled to adore the sun, and when I resist, fearing this curse, I am ordered to be crucified, I shall run into another curse of his, which he uttered against him who hangs on wood." No gentile king indeed compels you to adore the sun, which not even the sun itself would compel, if it reigned on earth, since not even now does it want this done by you: but just as the Creator himself bears with his impious blasphemers until the judgment, so the heavenly bodies themselves tolerate their vain worshipers until the judgment of their Creator. Yet remember that a Christian king cannot compel the sun to be adored. For Faustus set forth the example of a gentile king, knowing well that what you do, when you adore the sun, pertains to the gentiles. This, then, is not Christian: but everywhere the partridge now sets down the name of Christ, that it may gather what it did not bear[^27].

See, however, how easily truth answers, and how easily sound doctrine breaks this, as it were inescapable and two-headed, snare of your question. Behold, let us suppose someone armed with royal power threatening a Christian man that, if he refuses to adore the sun, he be hung on wood. If I avoid, you say, the curse which the Law uttered against the adorer of the sun, I shall fall into that which the same Law uttered against him who hangs on wood. So indeed you will be troubled: but you—rather, not even you, who adore the sun with no one compelling. But the Christian, built upon the foundation of the Apostles and Prophets[^28], attends to the individual causes, and the individual curses: he sees that one pertains to the mortal body, which is hung on wood; the other to the mind, by which the sun is adored. For although the body is bowed in adoring, the mind nonetheless either worships what it adores, or feigns; but each is destructive. Wherefore, since death deserved the curse in both, just as it is the death of the body to hang on wood, so it is the death of the mind to adore the sun. The curse, then, is to be chosen in the death of the body, by which curse even the body itself will be freed in the resurrection: but the curse in the death of the soul is to be avoided, lest, with its body, it be condemned in the eternal fire.

For the Lord resolved this question for us, saying: *Do not fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul: but fear him who has the power to kill both soul and body in the Gehenna of fire*[^29]. As though he said: Do not fear the curse of bodily death, which is loosed temporally: but fear the curse of spiritual death, by which the soul is tormented forever with its body. Behold, it is not an old wives' curse, but a prophetic foretelling: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*[^30]. For thus Christ takes away curse from curse, just as death from death, sin from sin. Thus, then, Moses did not blaspheme by saying: *Cursed is everyone who hangs on wood*: just as the Apostles did not blaspheme by saying: *He died*[^31]; and: *Our old man was fastened to the cross with him*[^32]; and: *From sin he condemned sin*[^33]; and: *Him who knew no sin he made sin for us*[^34]; and many things of this kind.

But you, when you shudder at the cursed Christ, confess that you shudder at the death of Christ. Here appears your imprecation—not an old wives' curse, but a devilish dissembling—you who do not believe in the death of Christ's body, by the death of your soul. Yet you urge that death of Christ to be not true, but feigned: as though you did not dare to deceive men through the Christian name, unless you made Christ himself the master of deceit.

**13.** But as for the fact that Moses seemed to Faustus envious of continence or virginity, because he said: *Cursed is everyone who has not raised up seed in Israel*[^35]; let them read Isaiah crying out: *Thus says the Lord to all the eunuchs: Those who keep my precepts, and choose the things I will, and keep my covenant; I will give them in my house and within my walls a named place, better than that of sons and daughters: an eternal name I will give them, and it will not fail them*[^36]. Or if they think Isaiah contrary to Moses, let this one please them, if that one displeases; it is no small thing against these men. For it is enough for us to know that one God spoke both through Moses and through Isaiah: and that cursed is everyone who has not raised up seed in Israel,

whether then, when, for the propagation of the people according to the flesh, even the working of carnal offspring in conjugal chastity pertained to civic duty; or now, lest anyone born spiritually should think it ought to suffice him, and should not press upon the Lord's gains, by which each, according to his measure, by preaching Christ, ought to beget Christians. So that divine sentence embraces the times of both Testaments with a wondrous brevity: *Cursed is everyone who has not raised up seed in Israel.*
