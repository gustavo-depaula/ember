# Book 3

**1.** Faustus said: Do you then accept the generation?

For my part I long tried to persuade myself of this very thing, such as it is—that God was born; but, offended above all by the disagreement of the two evangelists who write his genealogy, Luke and Matthew[^1], I hesitated, uncertain which of them I should rather follow. For I thought it might be that, since I am not prescient, the one I supposed to be lying might himself be speaking the truth, and the one I supposed to be speaking the truth might himself perhaps be lying. So, leaving aside their endless quarrel, interminable for me, I betook myself to John and Mark—not unequally, from two to two, and from Evangelists to professors of the same name. Their beginnings pleased me for the time, and not without reason, because they introduce neither David nor Mary nor Joseph; but John indeed says that in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God[^2], meaning Christ; while Mark says: *The Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God*[^3]—as though reproaching Matthew, who put "the son of David." Unless perhaps the one announces one Jesus, and the other another. This, then, is the reason why I do not accept that Christ was born. But you, if you are great enough to take away this offense from me, bring it about that they agree among themselves; and somehow I will yield—although not even so will it be worthy to believe that God was born from a womb, and the God of Christians at that.

**2.** Augustine replied: Oh, if only you had read the Gospel with pious zeal, and had preferred to seek out diligently the things in the Evangelists that struck you as contradictory, rather than rashly to condemn them—so that at least, on account of that most evident contradiction which meets one as if at first glance, you might consider that, unless something great lay hidden there, it could hardly have come about that such great authority was granted them throughout the world, by which they subjected to themselves the minds of so many most learned men.

For what is so great about seeing what you have seen—that some of Christ's forebears according to the flesh are recorded by Luke, others by Matthew—when both set down Joseph, Matthew as the one at whom he ends, Luke as the one from whom he begins; who, on account of a certain holy and virginal marriage with his mother, himself also deserved to be called the parent of Christ, so that, on account of the dignity of the husband, the generations might be woven from him, or up to him? What, then, is so great about what you have seen—that Joseph has one father according to Matthew, another according to Luke; and one grandfather according to the one, another according to the other, and thereafter on upward all the way to David, through so many generations, other forebears of his counted by the one, others by the other?

Would so many keen and learned men, most diligent treaters of the divine Scriptures, not have seen so open and manifest a difference? Of these there are very few in the Latin tongue; but who could count those in the Greek? They saw it plainly. For what is easier, or who, looking even a little, does not see these things? But, thinking piously of the eminence of so great an authority, they believed that something lay hidden there which would be given to those who asked, denied to those who railed; found by those who sought, withdrawn from those who reproached; opened to those who knocked[^4], closed to those who assailed; they asked, they sought, they knocked, they received, they found, they entered.

**3.** The whole question lies in this: how Joseph could have had two fathers. For if it is shown that this could have happened, there is no cause at all why any of these Evangelists should be believed to have spoken falsely in counting the generations differently. For from two fathers it will no longer be strange or contradictory that there could also be two grandfathers, two great-great-grandfathers, two great-grandfathers, and whatever is above, up to David, whose sons both were—both Solomon, who belongs to the order Matthew followed, and Nathan, who is in the series Luke set out. For some attend to these things and see that a man cannot be begotten by two men through the mingling of flesh; and therefore they think this question cannot be resolved. But they do not consider what is most usual and most easy—that a person's father is called not only the one by whom he was begotten, but also the one by whom he was adopted.

For the bond of adoption was not alien to the customs of the ancients, since we find even women adopting sons for themselves who were not born from their own womb: as Sarah adopted from Hagar[^5], and Leah from her maidservant[^6], and Pharaoh's daughter adopted Moses[^7]. And Jacob himself adopted his grandsons, the sons of Joseph[^8]. And that the very word "adoption" has the greatest force in the mystery of our faith, apostolic teaching testifies. Hence the apostle Paul, when he was speaking of the merits of the Jews, says: *Theirs is the adoption, and the glory, and the covenants, and the giving of the Law; theirs are the fathers, and from them is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all things, God blessed forever*[^9]. Likewise he says: *And we ourselves groan within ourselves, awaiting the adoption of the sons of God, the redemption of our body*[^10]. Likewise in another place: *But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law, that we might receive the adoption of sons*[^11]. How great a mystery this adoption is, then, appears sufficiently from these and similar testimonies.

For God has one only Son, whom he begot of his own substance, of whom it is said: *Though he was in the form of God, he did not count it robbery to be equal to God*[^12]. But us he did not beget of his own substance; for we are a creation, which he did not beget but made; and therefore, that he might make us brothers of Christ after his own manner, he adopted us. This manner, then, by which God—when we already existed, not born of him but founded and established—begot us by his Word and his grace, that we might be his sons, is called adoption. Hence John says: *He gave them power to become the sons of God*[^13].

Since, then, the right of adopting sons was in use among our fathers and in the holy Scriptures, what madness of impiety hurls one headlong to charge the Evangelists with falsehood, because they recorded different generations—as though both could not be true—rather than to consider and attend to and see what is most easy: by how frequent a custom of the human race one man could have had two fathers, one by whose flesh the man was begotten, the other by whose will he was adopted as a son when he was already a man? And if such a one is not rightly called father, then neither do we rightly say, *Our Father, who art in heaven*[^14], to him of whose substance we were not born, but by whose grace and most merciful will we were adopted, according to apostolic teaching and the most faithful truth. For we have him as both God, and Lord, and Father: God, because by him we were founded, even from human parents; Lord, because we are subject to him; Father, because by his adoption we have been reborn.

**3.** It was easy, then, for religious searchers of the divine writings to consider a little, and to see, in the different generations of Christ recorded by the two evangelists, how Joseph could have had two fathers, whose forebears are counted differently. You too could easily see this, if the zeal of contention did not blind you. But other things as well were sought out and found by those men, as they handled all the parts of these narratives; whereas these things are very far removed from your understanding. And so, even though you are established in the error of Mani, you could see by reflection—if you did not read with a hostile mind—what is wont to happen in human affairs: that one man begets a son by flesh, another adopts him by will, and thus one man has two fathers.

**4.** But why Matthew began from Abraham, descending all the way to Joseph, while Luke began from Joseph, ascending, not to Abraham, but to God who made man and, having given a commandment, gave him the power, by believing, to become a son of God; and why the former counted the generations at the head of his book, while the latter did so after the Lord Savior had been baptized by John; and what the number of the generations according to Matthew means, that they are arranged in three sets of fourteen, which, when reckoned in a sum, seem to come to one less; while the number of the generations according to Luke, recorded at the Lord's baptism, reaches seventy-seven—a number which the Lord himself commends in the forgiveness of sins, saying: *Not only seven times, but until seventy times seven*[^15]: all this you could in no way trace out, unless either you heard it from some Catholic—not just anyone, but one zealous for the divine Scriptures and as skilled as he could be—or, corrected yourselves from error, you asked with Catholic piety, that you might receive; sought, that you might find; knocked, that you might enter.

**5.** Therefore, since the thing that so disturbed Faustus about the difference of the generations—because the knot here lay in understanding the two fathers—is undone once nature and adoption are understood, he wished in vain to betake himself to the two Evangelists and to withdraw from the other two: more likely to stumble against those to whom he betook himself than to take anything from those from whom he withdrew. For the saints do not love those who choose them, if they find them deserters of their associates.

For they rejoice in unity, and in Christ they are one; and though one says one thing and another another, or one says it in one way and another in another, yet all say true things, in no way contrary to one another, if a pious reader approaches, if he reads gently, if he searches not with a heretical mind, for something to quarrel over, but with a faithful heart, for something to be built up by.

Now then, since we have believed that the individual Evangelists undertook to narrate the generations of the individual fathers—it being not alien to the human race for one man to have two—our faith is not alien to the truth. Therefore, since the Evangelists now agree among themselves, as Faustus promised, yield somehow.

**6.** Or does that which he added still disturb you? "Although not even so," he says, "will it be worthy to believe that God was born from a womb, and the God of Christians at that." As though we believed that the divine nature itself came forth from a woman's womb. Did I not just now recall the testimony of the Apostle, where he says of the Jews: *Theirs are the fathers, and from them is Christ according to the flesh, who is over all things, God blessed forever*[^16]? Christ, then, our Lord and Savior, the true Son of God according to divinity, and the true son of man according to the flesh, was not born of a woman in respect of that by which he is God over all things, blessed forever; but in respect of that infirmity which he assumed from us, that in it he might die for us and heal it in us. Not from the form of God, in which, when he was, he did not count it robbery to be equal to God, was he born of a woman; but from the form of a servant, by taking which he emptied himself[^17]. For he is said to have emptied himself for no other reason than by taking the form of a servant, not by losing the form of God. For while that nature, by which in the form of God he is equal to the Father, remained unchangeably, he assumed our changeable nature, through which he might be born of a virgin. But you, while you shudder to commit Christ's flesh to a virginal womb, have committed the very divinity of God to the wombs not only of men, but even of dogs and pigs! You refuse to believe that Christ's flesh was conceived once in the womb of a virgin, in which God was not only not bound, but not even changed; and yet you preach that God's very part and divine nature is bound, oppressed, and defiled in the male seeds and female wombs of all men and beasts, in all conceptions, throughout all lands, and through all waters, and through all hours, and cannot even afterward be wholly freed!
