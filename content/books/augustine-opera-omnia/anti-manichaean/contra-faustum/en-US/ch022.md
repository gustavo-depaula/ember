# Book XXI

**1.** FAUSTUS said: Is there one God, or two? Plainly one. How, then, do you assert two? Never, indeed, in our assertions has the name of two gods been heard. But whence you suspect this, I desire to know. Because you hand down two principles, of good things and of evil. There is indeed the fact that we confess two principles, but one of these we call God, the other Hyle; or, to put it commonly and in the usual way, a demon. But if you think this signifies two gods, you could also, when a physician discourses about sickness and health, think these two are healths; and when someone names good and evil, you could think these two are goods; and hearing of plenty and want, you would think these two are plenties. And if, when I discourse about white and black, and cold and hot, and sweet and bitter, you say that I have shown two whites, and two hots, and two sweets, will you not seem out of your mind, and of a brain not at all sound? So too, when I teach two principles, God and Hyle, I ought not on that account to seem to you to show two gods. Or is it because we assign all maleficent force to Hyle, and the beneficent to God, as is fitting, that you therefore think it makes no difference whether we call each of them God? But if it is so, you could also, hearing of poison and antidote, think it makes no difference whether each be called antidote, because each of them has its own force, each does and works something; and also, hearing of a physician and a poisoner, you would call both physicians; and hearing of a just man and an unjust, you could call both just, because each of them does something. But if to do this is absurd, how much more absurd to think God and Hyle two gods, because each of them works something? Wherefore this argument is inept and quite drained of force, that, because you cannot answer me on the matter, you stir up odium from names alone. For I do not deny that we too sometimes name the adverse nature "god"—but not according to our faith, but according to the name already presumed for it by its worshipers, who imprudently judge it to be a god: just as the Apostle too says, *The god of this world has blinded the minds of unbelievers*[^1]—naming it "god," because it was so already called by its own; but adding that it blinds minds, so that from this it may be understood not to be the true God.

**2.** AUGUSTINE replied: We are indeed accustomed to hear of two gods in your disputations—which, although you first denied it, yet a little after you yourself even confessed, as it were giving a reason why you say this: because the Apostle too says, *The god of this world has blinded the minds of unbelievers.* This sentence indeed very many of us so punctuate as to say that the true God blinded the minds of unbelievers. For when they have read, *In whom God,* they suspend the utterance, and then bring in: *has blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this world.* For even if you do not punctuate it thus, but, for the sake of exposition, so change the order of the words: *In whom God has blinded the minds of the unbelievers of this world,* the same sense shines forth as in that punctuation. For even such an operation, by which the minds of unbelievers are blinded, can in a certain manner pertain to the true God—which not malice does, but justice: as the same Paul says elsewhere, *Is God unjust, who inflicts wrath?*[^2] Likewise elsewhere: *What then shall we say?* he says, *Is there injustice with God? Far be it.* For he says to Moses, *I will have mercy on whom I have had mercy, and I will show mercy to whom I have been merciful*[^3]. When, therefore, he had premised what must be held unshaken, that there is no injustice with God, notice a little after what he says: *But if God, willing to show wrath and to demonstrate his power, bore with much patience the vessels of wrath, which are fitted for destruction, and that he might make known the riches of his glory upon the vessels of mercy, which he prepared for glory*, and so on[^4]. Surely here it can in no way be said that one God shows wrath and demonstrates his power upon the vessels fitted for destruction, and another shows the riches upon the vessels of mercy. For that one and the same God does both, the apostolic doctrine bears witness. Hence too that saying: *For this reason God handed them over to the desires of their heart, to uncleanness, so that they dishonor their bodies among themselves*; and a little after: *Therefore God handed them over to passions of ignominy*; likewise a little after: *And since they did not approve of having God in their knowledge, God handed them over to a reprobate sense*[^5]. Behold how the true and just God blinds the minds of unbelievers. For never in these words of the Apostle which I have mentioned was any other God understood than he who sent his Son saying: *For judgment I have come into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind*[^6]. For here too it sufficiently appears to the minds of the faithful how God blinds the minds of unbelievers. For something hidden goes before in hidden things, where God works the most just examination of his judgment, so that the minds of some are blinded, of some enlightened: to whom it was most truly said, *Your judgments are a great deep*[^7]. The impenetrable height of which depth the Apostle, marveling, exclaims: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments!*, and so on[^8]

**3.** But you, unable to discern what God does by benefit, what by judgment—because both from your heart and from your mouth our Psalter is far, where it is said, *Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord*[^9]—whatever offends you on account of the weakness of human mortality, you alienate entirely from the choice and judgment of the true God: having, namely, another evil god ready, whom not truth shows you but vanity feigns, to whom you may attribute not only whatever you do unjustly, but even whatever you suffer justly; thus attributing to God the benefits of gifts, and taking from him the judgments of punishments, as if Christ said of another, that he prepared eternal fire for the wicked[^10], than of him who makes his sun rise upon the good and the bad, and rains upon the just and the unjust[^11]. Whence do you not understand that so great a goodness here, and so great a severity there, pertain to one God, except because you do not know how to sing mercy and judgment? Is not the same one who makes his sun rise upon the good and the bad, and rains upon the just and the unjust, yet the one who breaks the natural branches and against nature grafts in the wild olive? Does not the Apostle say of one and the same there: *You see, then, the goodness and the severity of God: toward those who fell, severity; but toward you, goodness, if you remain in goodness*[^12]? Surely you hear, surely you notice, how he neither takes from God his judiciary severity, nor from man his free will. It is hidden, it is deep, shut off by an inaccessible secret from human thought, how God both condemns the impious and justifies the impious: for the truth of the holy Scriptures speaks both of him. Does it then please you to prattle against the divine judgments, because they are unsearchable? How much more fitting, how much more suited to our small measure, to be afraid there where Paul was afraid, and to exclaim: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and how untraceable his ways!*[^13] How much better to so marvel at what you cannot trace out, than on that account to wish to feign another evil god, because you could not comprehend the one good one? For the question is not about a name, but about a work.

**4.** For Faustus quickly seems to have defended himself when he says: "We do not say two gods, but God and Hyle." Yet when you ask what he calls Hyle, you will plainly hear another god described. For if a formless matter, capable of corporeal forms, were called by them Hyle, as it was called by the ancients, none of us would object that it is called god. But as it is, how great an error, how great a madness, either to call the matter of bodies the maker of bodies, or to deny that the maker of bodies is God! Since, then, what the true God does—that is, the qualities and forms of bodies, of elements, of animals, so that they are bodies, elements, animals—you say that some other one or other does, by whatever name you call him, you are rightly said to introduce, by your error, another god. For in this one matter you err twice with a sacrilegious error: once, indeed, in that you say the things God does are done by him whom you blush to confess as God—but in no way will you bring it about that he is not God, unless you deny that he does such things as none but God does; and again, in that the things which the good God makes good, you suppose both to be made by an evil one and to be evil, shuddering with a childish sense at the things that are unsuited to the weakness of penal mortality, and loving those that are suited. Accordingly you call evil him who made the snake; but this sun you think so great a good that you believe it not even made by God, but brought forth or sent. But the true God—in whom that you do not yet believe I grieve too much—both made the snake among other lower things, and the sun among other higher things; and still, in higher things—not corporeal heavenly things, but already spiritual ones—many things far better than this light, which no carnal man perceives: how much less you, who, when you detest the flesh, detest nothing but your own rule, by which you measure good and evil? For there can be in you no thought either of evils, except such as offend the carnal sense; or of goods, except such as delight the carnal eye.

**5.** So that I may consider those works of God at the bottom of things—earthly, weak, mortal, but yet works of God, such as we can see—I am ineffably moved by praise of their Creator, who is so great in great works that he is not less in the least. For the divine art, by which heavenly and earthly works are made, although these are unlike one another, is itself like itself in all things, because in perfecting each in its own kind it is everywhere perfect. For it does not found the universe in single things, but, founding single things toward the embrace of the universe, presents itself whole to the founding of single things too, making and disposing all things fittingly in their places and orders, and granting to all, particularly and universally, what is fitting. Behold here, in this as it were lowest base of the whole creation, look at the animals that fly, and swim, and walk, and creep. Surely they are mortal; surely their life, as it is written, *is a vapor appearing for a little while*[^14]. For this small measure, received from the best Founder, they confer as it were in common to the universe, for the completing of the portion of their own kind, so that with these lowest things all good things may be, among which the higher are better than they. Nevertheless, attend, and give me any one, however most abject, animal whose soul hates its own flesh, and does not rather nourish and cherish it, and by vital motion quicken and rule it, and in a manner administer, for the smallness of its kind, a certain universe of its own, conciliated to itself for the guarding of its safety. For in that the rational soul chastises its body and subjects it to servitude, lest by an immoderate appetite of earthly things it be hindered from the perception of wisdom, even so it surely loves its own flesh, which it lawfully subjects and orders to obey it. Finally, you yourselves, although by a carnal error you detest the flesh, cannot but love your own flesh, and provide for its safety and soundness, avoiding all blows, and falls, and the inclemency by which it is harmed, but seeking the defenses and healthfulness by which it is preserved: thus you show that the law of nature prevails against the opinion of your error.

**6.** What of the vital organs in the flesh itself, the harmony of the whole form, the members for working, the vessels for perceiving, all distinguished in their places and offices, and woven together in concordant unity, by the moderation of measures, the equality of numbers, the order of weights—do they not point to their craftsman, the true God, to whom it was truly said: *You have arranged all things in measure, and number, and weight*[^15]? If, then, you had a heart not perverse and corrupted by vain fables, you would clearly see his invisible things, understood even through these things which are made in this weak and carnal creature[^16]. For whence do these creatures have the things I have mentioned, except from him by whose unity all measure is set, by whose wisdom all beauty is formed, by whose law all order is disposed? But if you have no eye for beholding these things, let the apostolic authority lead you.

**7.** For the Apostle, when he was commanding concerning holy love, of what kind the love of husbands toward their wives ought to be, took an example from the soul of the lover. *He who loves his wife,* he says, *loves himself: for no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, as Christ does the Church*[^17]. Behold, in your sight is the whole carnal substance; see how into every animal conciliated to itself for safety this communion of nature extends, so that it loves its own flesh. For this is not only in men, who, when they live rightly, not only provide for the safety of their flesh, but even tame and bridle the carnal motions to the use of reason; but even the beasts flee pain, dread destruction; and whatever can scatter and sever from its concordant joining that fitting-together and bond of members, of flesh and spirit, they avoid with as great an agility as they can, themselves too nourishing and cherishing their own flesh. For *no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes,* he says, *and cherishes it, as Christ also the Church.* See whence, to what height he has ascended: behold, if you can, what force the creature draws from the Creator, from the very heavenly arrays down to flesh and blood, bounded by the fullness of the universe, adorned by the variety of forms, ordered by the gradations of things.

**8.** Again the same Apostle, when he was teaching us concerning the diverse spiritual gifts, yet consonant unto unity—a thing plainly great and divine and hidden—gave a likeness from this very flesh of ours, whose craftsman, God, when he spoke these things, he by no means passed over in silence. And although it is long, yet, because it is very necessary, I will not be loath to insert that whole passage from his Epistle to the Corinthians into this work. *But concerning spiritual gifts, brethren, I do not want you to be ignorant. You know that when you were gentiles, you were led astray to mute idols, however you were led. Therefore I make known to you that no one speaking in the Spirit of God says "Anathema to Jesus"; and no one can say "The Lord Jesus" except in the Holy Spirit. Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of ministries, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of workings, but the same God, who works all things in all. To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for profit: to one indeed is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom; to another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit; to another faith in the same Spirit; to another the gift of healings in the one Spirit; to another the workings of powers; to another prophecy; to another the discerning of spirits; to another kinds of tongues; to another the interpretation of speeches; but all these things one and the same Spirit works, distributing to each his own as he wills. For just as the body is one and has many members, but all the members of the body, though they are many, are one body, so also is Christ. For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, whether slaves or free; and we all drank one Spirit. For the body is not one member, but many. If the foot should say, Because I am not a hand, I am not of the body, is it therefore not of the body? Or if the ear should say, Because I am not an eye, I am not of the body, is it therefore not of the body? If the whole body were an eye, where would be the hearing? If the whole were hearing, where would be the smelling? But now God has set the members, each one of them, in the body as he willed. But if they were all one member, where would the body be? But now there are many members, yet one body. And the eye cannot say to the hand, I have no need of you; nor again the head to the feet, I have no need of you; but much rather the members of the body which seem to be weaker are more necessary; and those which we think to be the more dishonorable parts of the body, on these we bestow more abundant honor; and our unseemly parts have more abundant comeliness; but our comely parts have no need: but God has tempered the body, giving more abundant honor to that which lacked it, so that there should be no division in the body, but the same, that the members should be solicitous for one another; and if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it; if one member is glorified, all the members rejoice together*[^18]. If there is any—I do not say Christian faith, that you should believe the Apostle, but—any human sense in you, that you may perceive manifest things, let each one see and consider these things in himself, how true, how certain they are, how great in a small thing, and in the lowest how good: since the Apostle sets these things down in praise, so that through these lowest corporeal things which are seen, those sublime spiritual things which are not seen may more easily be understood.

**9.** Whoever, then, denies that God is the craftsman of these members and of our body, which the Apostle so commends, so praises, you see whom he contradicts, announcing to you besides what we have received[^19]. What need, then, is there that he be refuted by me, rather than anathematized by all Christians? The Apostle says, *God has tempered the body*[^20]; and this man says, Hyle, not God. What is more open than these enmities, to be anathematized before they are refuted? Did the Apostle here too, when he said "God," add "of this world"[^21]? Where, even if anyone understands the devil to blind the minds of unbelievers, we do not deny it—by evil persuasions; and those who consent to them lose the light of justice, God repaying what is just. All these things we read in the holy Scriptures: for that too was said of seduction coming from without: *I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so your minds be corrupted from the simplicity and chastity that is in Christ*[^22]. To which is like: *Evil conversations corrupt good morals*[^23]; and that whereby each is a seducer to himself: *But he who thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, seduces himself*[^24]; and that concerning God's vengeance, which I mentioned above: *God handed them over to a reprobate sense, to do the things that are not fitting*[^25]. So too in the ancient books, when it had foretold, *God did not make death, nor does he rejoice in the destruction of the living*[^26], a little after it says: *By the envy of the devil death entered into the world*[^27]. And again concerning death itself, lest men set themselves outside of fault: *But the impious by their hands and words,* it says, *summoned it; and counting it a friend, they pined away*[^28]. But elsewhere: *Good things and evil, life and death, riches and poverty, are from the Lord God*[^29]. Here troubled men do not understand that in one and the same evil work—not afterward, with another that will be manifest following, but with a certain vengeance immediately accompanying—one thing comes from the cunning of the one persuading, another from the wickedness of the one willing, another from the justice of the one punishing: when the devil suggests, man consents, God deserts. Wherefore in an evil work—that is, the blinding of unbelievers—if the devil too be understood, on account of the malignity of persuading, so that "the god of this world" be so distinguished, it does not seem to me absurd. For it is not said without an addition, "god," when there is joined "of this world"—that is, of impious men, willing to flourish only in this world: according to which the world too is called evil, as it is written, *That he might rescue us from the present evil world*[^30]. For such is that too: *Whose god is the belly*[^31]: were there not there "whose," it would in no way say "god, the belly." Nor in the Psalm could demons be called gods, unless there were added "of the gentiles." For thus it is written: *For the gods of the gentiles are demons*[^32]. But here it is neither "the god of this world," nor "whose god is the belly," nor "the gods of the gentiles are demons," but it is set down simply: *God has tempered the body*; which cannot be understood except as the true God, creator of all. For those things are said with reproach, but this with praise. Unless perhaps Faustus understands that God tempered the body not by the disposition of the members—that is, by fabricating and constructing it—but by an admixture of his own light: so that, namely, another set these members thus distinguished and located in their seats, who fabricated this; but God, by mixing in his goodness, tempered the malice of this fabric. For with such fables they dull childish souls. But God, succoring the little ones through the mouths of his saints, did not permit them to say even this. For you have also a little above: *God has set the members, each one of them, in the body as he willed*[^33]. Who now does not understand that, according to this, God was called the temperer of the body, in that he fabricated the body from many members, which preserve the offices of diverse works in the framework of unity?

**10.** Let the Manicheans, then, say whether the animals which, according to their ravings, Hyle had fabricated in the nation of darkness before God had mixed his light into them, did not have this concord of members which the Apostle so praises: whether there the head would say to the feet, or the eye to the hand, *I have no need of you*[^34]. Never did they say this, nor could they say it: for they attribute to them such acts and works—they crept, walked, swam, flew, each according to its kind; they also saw, and heard, and perceived by the other senses, and nourished and cherished their own bodies with suitable foods and temperings. Hence too fertility of offspring was at hand: for they assign marriages to them too. All these things, certainly, which Mani reproaches as the works of Hyle, cannot happen without the concord of members which the Apostle praises and assigns to God. Do you still doubt who is to be followed, and who is to be anathematized? What of the fact that there were there certain ones who even spoke, so that, when they harangued, all the creeping, four-footed, flying, swimming things heard, understood, consented? Marvelous this, and altogether divine eloquence; and they had heard no grammarian or rhetorician, nor had they learned these things amid the tears of ferules and rods. Surely this Faustus, in order to prattle these vanities eloquently, came even late to the discipline of making a speech; and although he was sharp in talent, yet by reading he burst his stomach, so that so few assented to him as he spoke. O wretched man, who was born in this light, and not in those darknesses! For there, every two-footed, every many-footed creature, every serpent too, from the dragon to the snail, would gladly hear him haranguing against the light, and would briskly obey; but here, as he disputed against the darkness, the more named him eloquent than learned, and many a most perverse seducer; and among the few Manicheans applauding him as a great master, no beast nodded to him, nor did even his nag know anything from that doctrine—as if for all animals the divine part was congealed for this purpose, that they should become stupid. What is this, I ask? Wake up at last, wretches, and compare, in your fable, the former time and the present of all living things—then in their own land, now in this world: then their bodies were strong, now they are weak; then the keen edge of the eyes for seeing, with delight in invading the region of God, now so dulled that it is turned away from the sun's rays; then the minds of animals enlarged for understanding the speech of the one haranguing, now dull and utterly estranged from any such capacity; then a natural eloquence so great and so powerful, now scarcely a small and scanty one with such great zeal and labor. O how great goods the nation of darkness lost by the commingling of the good!

**11.** This very Faustus, in this very discourse to which I am now replying, seemed elegantly to set against each other many contraries: health and sickness, plenty and want, white and black, hot and cold, sweet and bitter. Among these I forbear to say anything about white and black; or, if there is any weight of good and evil in colors, so that they say white pertains to God but black to Hyle—since they declare that Hyle created all kinds of birds—if God sprinkled the white color on their feathers, where were the ravens hiding when the swans were suffused with whiteness? Likewise there is no need to discuss hot and cold: for each, applied with moderation, is healthful, but immoderately, ruinous. Let us see the rest. Good and evil, which perhaps he ought to have set first among these same contraries, he seems to have so set that he wished them understood as general categories—namely, so that to good pertain health, plenty, white, hot, sweet; but to evil, sickness, want, black, cold, bitter: how ignorantly and inconsiderately, let him see who can. But I, lest I be thought to slander the man, object nothing about white and black, hot and cold, sweet and bitter, health and sickness. For if white and sweet are two goods, but black and bitter two evils, how is it that very many a grape, and every olive, by blackening grows sweet—that is, by having more of evil becomes better? Likewise, if heat and health are two goods, but cold and sickness two evils, why do bodies grow sick by growing hot? Or do the healthy perhaps have a fever? These things, then, I do not object, which he perhaps incautiously, or as instances of any contraries rather than of goods and evils, mentioned: especially since they have never said that the fire of the nation of darkness was cold, but that its heat was surely evil.

**12.** But, to omit these, let us see those things which he so mentioned as goods among these contraries that he would not have it doubted: health, plenty, sweetness. Was there, then, no health of bodies in that nation, in which those animals could be born, and grow, and beget, and so endure, that when some of them, pregnant, were—as they dote—captured and bound up in heaven, the fetuses, not even full-term but premature, falling from such a height to the earth, could both live and grow and propagate these innumerable kinds of flesh that now exist? Or was there no plenty there, where trees could be born not only in waters and winds, but even in fire and smoke, and be enriched with such great fecundity that from their fruits the animals of each their own kind were begotten, and, nourished and fed by the fruitfulness of those trees, were preserved, whose fattening the fecundity of offspring too attested with joy: especially where there was no labor of agriculture, nor the inclemency of summer and winter; for the sun did not go about there, so that the seasons of the year should run their course in alternation? Accordingly there was a perpetual fertility of trees, which had at hand perpetually the element and the nourishment of their kind—as it had been present for begetting, so for bearing—and made the fruits never fail: just as we see citron trees bear flowers and fruits the whole year, if they are continually watered. Great plenty, then, was there, and great security in having it: for not even hail was feared, where there were no exactors of light, whom you fable to stir up the thunders.

**13.** But if they did not have their own sweet and pleasant foods, they would never seek them, never would they invigorate their bodies by them. For the matter stands thus, that, according to the suitability of each body, the food either delights or offends. If it delights, it is called sweet or pleasant; but if it offends, bitter, or harsh, or to be rejected for some unpleasantness. Are not we men ourselves so constituted that very often one seeks the food which another shudders at, whether according to the temperament of nature, or according to the use of habit, or according to the disposition of health? How much more can bodies of beasts of far diverse kinds find pleasant what is bitter to us! Or would goats otherwise be strung up to gnaw the wild olive? For just as to some, by a disease of men, honey is bitter, so to the nature of cattle the wild olive is pleasant. Thus there is suggested to prudent examiners of things what force order has—namely, when to each are applied and rendered the things proper to it; and how great a good this is, from lowest to highest, from corporeal to spiritual things. And so in the nation of darkness, when an animal of some element fed on the food that was born in its own element, the suitability itself doubtless produced sweetness; but if it had fallen upon the food of another element, the unsuitability itself would produce an offense to the sense of the one tasting: which offense—whether bitterness, or harshness, or unpleasantness, or whatever else, or, if it is so excessive that by an alien force it breaks up the framework and concord of the body, and so destroys it or takes away its strength—is even called poison, only through unsuitability, what to another kind is, through suitability, food: just as bread, which is our daily food, if a hawk takes it, kills it; and us, if hellebore, on which very many cattle feed; of which herb, nonetheless, a certain measure, when applied, is even medicine. And if Faustus knew or considered this, he would surely not set poison and antidote as an example of the two natures, of evil and good, as though God were the antidote and Hyle the poison: when one and the same thing and one and the same nature, taken or applied now suitably, now unsuitably, either profits or harms. And so, according to their fable, it can be said that their god was poison to the nation of darkness, whose so firm bodies he so corrupted that he rendered them most feeble; but because the light itself too was captured, oppressed, corrupted, they were poison to each other in turn.

**14.** Why, then, do you not say these are either two goods or two evils, or rather both two goods and two evils—two goods in themselves, two evils toward each other? Afterward, if there be need, we will ask which of these is better or worse: for the present, because they were two goods in themselves, it is thus considered: God reigned in his own land, Hyle reigned too in hers. Health of the reigning both there and here; plenty of fruits both there and here; fecundity of offspring on both sides; sweetness of their own pleasures with both. But that nation, they say, except that it was evil toward the neighboring light, was evil also toward itself. For the present I have already said many of its goods; if you too can show its evils, there will be two good kingdoms, but that better one where there was no evil: what, then, do you say were the evils of this one? They laid waste, he says, harmed, killed, consumed one another. If only this were carried on there, how would so great hosts be begotten there, nourished, brought to completion? There was, then, both rest and peace there. Nevertheless, let us confess that that was the better kingdom, where there was no discord: yet I would say much more aptly that these were two goods than that one was good and the other evil; so that that is the better where neither did individuals harm themselves, nor one another; but this a lower good, where, although they were adverse to one another, yet each animal guarded its own safety, soundness, and nature. Nevertheless, at least that prince of darkness can be compared, not by so long an interval, to your god—he whom no one resisted, whom, reigning, all things served, whom, haranguing, all things followed; which could not happen without great peace and concord. For there are happy kingdoms where, with the full consent of all, the kings are obeyed. To this is added that to that prince not only those of his own kind—that is, the two-footed, whom you call the parents of men—but even all kinds of the other animals were subject, and were turned at his nod, doing what he had commanded, believing what he had persuaded. Saying these things, do you suppose the hearts of men so deaf as to expect another god to be named by you, whom they see plainly and openly described? For if this prince's forces could do this, great power; if honor, great splendor; if love, great concord; if fear, great discipline. If in all these goods there were some evils, is its nature on that account to be called evil—except by those who do not know what they say? Further, if you therefore think it a nature of evil, because it was evil not only toward the other nature, but also had evil in itself, do you judge no evil that hard necessity which your god suffered before the commingling of the contrary nature—that he was compelled to war with it, and to send his members into its jaws, to be so oppressed that it could not be wholly purified? Behold, there was a great evil even in itself, before there was mingled with it what alone you call evil. For either it could not be harmed and corrupted by the nation of darkness, and by its own folly suffered that necessity; or, if its substance could be corrupted, you do not worship an incorruptible God, such as the Apostle preaches[^35]. What then? Does not even that very corruptibility, by which indeed that nature was not yet being corrupted, but by which nonetheless it could be corrupted, seem to you an evil in your god?

**15.** Who too does not see this, that either there was no foreknowledge there—where it is now your task to consider whether it is no fault of God to lack foreknowledge, and to be utterly ignorant of what threatens—or, if there was foreknowledge there, there could be no security there, but eternal fear; and how great an evil this is, you surely acknowledge. Or did he not fear lest at any moment the time should come when his members would be so laid waste and defiled in that battle that scarcely, with such great labor—and yet not wholly—would they be freed and cleansed? But if this did not pertain to himself—which indeed you see how harshly it is said—certainly those very members of his, which were going to suffer such great evils here, surely feared. Or did they not know this would be? Therefore, to whatever part of the substance of your god foreknowledge was surely lacking. Count up the evils in your highest good. Or did they not fear, because they foresaw alike their own coming liberation and triumph? Surely at least they feared for their associates, whom they knew would be alienated from their kingdom by eternal chains in that globe.

**16.** Or was there no love there, so that there was no fraternal compassion, surely for those upon whom, with no preceding sin, eternal punishments hung? What of those very souls to be bound in the globe—were not they too members of your god? Is it not one kind and one substance? They at least, foreknowing their own everlasting chain to come, surely feared, surely mourned. Or if they did not know this would be, one part of your god was provident, another improvident: how, then, is it one and the same substance? Since, then, so great evils were there too, before there was a commingling of an alien evil, why do you glory in it as a pure and simple and highest good? Therefore even in themselves you are compelled to confess these two natures to be either two goods or two evils. We grant you, if you say two evils, that you may say which of them you wish the worse; but if two goods, say which of them is the better—there will be a more careful consideration afterward; provided only that that error of yours be removed, by which you say there are two principles of two natures, the good and the evil; and plainly two gods, one good and the other evil. Now if something is evil for this reason, that it harms another, these harmed each other in turn: one part was the more reprobate, because it first sought another's. One, then, wished to inflict evil, the other repaid evil for evil—and not by the law of retaliation, as it were an eye for an eye[^36], which you are accustomed imprudently and impudently to reproach, but much more grievously. Choose, then, which you call the worse: the one that first wished to harm, or the one that both wished and was able to harm more. For the former coveted to enjoy the light according to its small measure; the latter uprooted it utterly. The former, if it had fulfilled what it sought, would surely have done itself no harm; but the latter, in order utterly to overturn the hostile adversity, grievously harmed even its own part. As is that most famous and frenzied sentence, committed to the memory of certain writings: *Let friends perish, provided enemies fall along with them*[^37]. For a part of god was sent to inexpiable contamination, that there might be wherewith to cover the globe in which the living enemy is to be buried forever: for he will be so feared even when conquered, will so terrify when shut up, that the everlasting misery of a part of god will grant to the rest of god some kind of security. O great innocence of goodness! Behold, your god too will do that for which you horribly accuse the nation of darkness, namely, that he harms both his own and others. That last globe convicts your god of this very thing, in which both the enemy is shut up and the citizen is fixed: nay rather, that part which you call god surpasses it in harming more, both others and his own. For Hyle did not wish to uproot the alien kingdom, but to hold it; but its own, even when by destroying it killed some of them by means of certain others of its own, it nonetheless changed anew into other forms, so that, by dying and being reborn, they might at least, through the intervals of times, enjoy the gladness of their life: but God—such as you describe him, almighty and best—both uproots others forever, and condemns his own; and, what is believed with a more marvelous madness, Hyle harms her own animals in her own battle, God punishes his own members in his own victory. What is this, vain men? Surely you remember Faustus's words about God as an antidote, and Hyle as poison: behold, your antidote harms more than the poison. Would Hyle shut up either God or her own entrails forever in so horrendous a globe? And, what is more wicked, he slanders those very relics, lest he seem to have failed, in that he could not purify them. For Mani says, in the Letter of the Foundation, that those souls become worthy of such punishment because they allowed themselves to stray from their former luminous nature, and stood forth as enemies of the holy light—when he himself sent them into that error, by which they would be so darkened as to become a light hostile to the light; if unwilling, he is unjust, for compelling them; if willing, he is ungrateful, for condemning them. If they were able to foreknow that they would be enemies of their own origin, then both before the war they were tortured with fear, and in the war inexpiably stained, and after the war condemned forever, never blessed. But if they were not able to foreknow it, then both before the war they were improvident, and in the war weak, and after the war wretched, never divine. And surely what they are, that God is, according to the unity of substance. Do we think you consider how monstrously you blaspheme? And yet sometimes, wishing as it were to defend the goodness of God, you say that even to Hyle herself some good is granted, so that, shut up in herself, she does not rage. She will have, then, some good, when no good has been mingled with her? Or perhaps, just as God before the war, without commingling of evil, had the evil of necessity, so Hyle after the war, without commingling of good, will have the good of cessation? Say, then, two evils, but one worse than the other; or two not highest goods, but one better than the other—yet so, indeed, that what is better, this you call the more wretched: for if of so great a war the outcome here will be that, with Hyle conquered, by her own devastation and by God's members fixed in the globe, some good is granted to the enemies, and so great an evil inflicted on the citizens, consider who has conquered. But of course Hyle is poison, who was able to form, strengthen, nourish, invigorate her own animals; and God is the antidote, who was able to condemn, but unable to heal, his own members. Madmen, that is not Hyle, nor is he God. Thus rave those who, not enduring sound doctrine, turn to fables[^38].
