# Book XXIX

**1.** FAUSTUS: Then it was magic, that he was seen and suffered, if he was not born. The same argument is turned back upon you in turn, so that this too was magic—that he was carried in the womb, or that he was brought forth, if he was not sown. It is established to be outside the law of nature for a virgin to have given birth, and much more for her to have been found a virgin even after the birth. Why, then, will you not have it that this too happened beyond nature—that he was able to suffer when he willed, without the lot of birth? Believe me; so far as the matter goes, both of us in this profess things contrary to nature; but the difference is this, that we do so honorably, you basely: we render some account of his passion, and a probable one; you render of his birth either none, or a false one. Finally, we confess that he suffered only in appearance, and did not truly die; you believe for certain that there was a childbearing, and that he was carried in a woman's womb. Or if it is not so, confess, you too, that this also was done imaginarily, so that he seemed to be born, and all our contention will be overthrown. For that, indeed, which you are accustomed often to affirm—that he was necessarily born, because otherwise he could not be seen or speak to men—is ridiculous; since many times, as has already been proved by our people, angels are shown both to have been seen by men and to have spoken.

**2.** AUGUSTINE: It is not said to you that it is magic for one who was not born to die; for we have already noted above that this was done in Adam. But even if it had never been done, and the Lord Christ had willed to come in such a way that, not assuming flesh from a virgin, but nevertheless appearing in true flesh, he should redeem us by a true death, who would dare to say that he could not? But that was better which he did, that he should also be born of a virgin, and should deign also to commend, by being born, both sexes, for the freeing of which he was going to die, his masculine body procreated from a woman—speaking against you yourselves most of all by the very deed, and subverting you, who proclaim that male and female are the work, not of God, but of the devil. But this is what you are said to assert, which is like magic: that you say his passion and death were done only in appearance, and were deceitfully shadowed forth, so that he might seem to die who was not dying. From which it comes about that you say his resurrection too was shadowy, imaginary, and deceitful; for there cannot be a true resurrection of one who has not truly died. So it comes about that he showed false scars to his doubting disciples; and that Thomas, not confirmed by truth, but deceived by deception, cried out: *My Lord and my God*[^1]; and yet you try to persuade us that you speak the truth with your tongue, when you say that Christ lied with his whole body. These are the things charged against you, who have fashioned for yourselves such a Christ, whose true disciples you cannot be unless you too have been liars. But the flesh of man born of a virgin cannot for this reason be thought magic, because the flesh of Christ alone was so born—just as neither is it magic that the flesh of Christ alone rose on the third day, never thereafter to die. Otherwise all the miracles of God will be magical, because they were done singularly; but they were truly done, and showed what was true; they did not delude the eyes of men with deceitful tricks. These indeed are often called against nature, not because they are opposed to nature, but because they exceed the measure of nature which is customary to us. May the Lord, then, repel from the minds of his little ones what Faustus, as if admonishing, has tried to persuade—that we too should profess an imaginary, not a true, birth of Christ, and that so all contention between us might be ended; nay rather, let there remain for us against them a struggle for truth, rather than with them a concord in falsehood.

**3.** Nevertheless, I ask of them: if our contention is ended when we have said this, why do they not say it themselves? Why do they affirm that Christ's death itself was not true, but imaginary, while they have chosen to say that his birth was not even such, but altogether nothing? If they blushed at the weight of evangelical authority, and therefore did not dare to say that Christ did not suffer even imaginarily, the same evangelical authority testifies also to his birth. For even if two evangelists narrated the very childbearing of Mary[^2], yet none of the evangelists was silent that Jesus had a mother[^3]. Or did it irk them to preach this too as simulated, because Matthew traces out one set of generations, Luke another, so that they seem not to agree with each other? But take a man who does not understand, and he will think that even in many things which pertain to Christ's passion the evangelists do not agree with each other; but take one who does understand, and everywhere they agree. Or is it that to simulate death is honorable, but to simulate birth too is base? Why, then, does he exhort us to confess this, by which our contention might be ended? Hence, why it seems to me that he wished to preach Christ's birth, not even simulated as his death, but altogether nothing, will appear in the following discourse, where we shall answer another question.

**4.** But far be it that there should be any baseness in the holy members of the saints, even the genital ones. They are indeed called unseemly, because they do not have that form of beauty which the members placed in open view have. But see what the Apostle says, when from the very unity and frame of the members of our body he urges love upon the Church. *Much rather*, he says, *those members of the body which seem to be weaker are necessary; and those parts of the body which we think to be less honorable, upon these we bestow more abundant honor; and our unseemly parts have more abundant comeliness, whereas our comely parts have no need; but God has tempered the body, giving more abundant honor to that part which lacked it, that there might be no divisions in the body*[^4]. Base, therefore, is the unlawful use of those members, not subject to the laws of temperance—not the members themselves, which not only do celibates and virgins keep in excellent integrity, but the holy fathers and mothers themselves who were married so used them, taking thought only for procreation, that that natural movement was in no way base which served, not lust, but reason. How much more, then, in the holy Virgin Mary, who conceived the flesh of Christ by faith, did the members have nothing of baseness, which served not a human and lawful conception, but only a divine birth? Rightly indeed so honored, that she might pour over into us bodily, with her integrity preserved, that Christ whom we might conceive by believing with whole hearts, and in a manner bring forth by confessing. For in no way did Christ by being born make his mother worse, so as to take away from her the glory of virginity from one to whom he had brought the gift of fruitfulness. These things were done truly, not deceitfully; but they are new, but they are unusual, but they are against the most well-known course of nature, because they are great, because they are wondrous, because they are divine—and therefore all the more true, certain, and well-established.

"And angels," he says, "were seen and spoke, although they were not born." As if we say that Christ, unless he were born of a woman, could neither be seen nor speak. He could, but he did not will it; and that is better which he willed. And that he willed this is certain for this reason, because he did it—he who would do nothing by necessity, as your god does, but everything by will. And that he did this we do not doubt, for this reason, because we believe, not some heretic, but his Gospel.

[^1]: See Jn 20:28.
[^2]: See Mt 1:25; Lk 2:7.
[^3]: See Mt 2:11; Mk 3:32; Lk 2:33; Jn 2:1.
[^4]: 1 Cor 12:22–25.
