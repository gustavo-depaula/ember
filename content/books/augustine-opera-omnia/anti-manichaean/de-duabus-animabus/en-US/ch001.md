# Revisions I

## Chapter 15 (14): The Two Souls, Against the Manichees, in One Book

**1.** After this book, while still a priest, I wrote against the Manichees *The Two Souls*. They say that one of these souls is a part of God, and the other comes from the race of darkness, which God did not create and which is coeternal with God. And they rave that these two souls, one good and the other evil, are in one and the same human being. They say that the evil one is proper to the flesh, which flesh they also say belongs to the race of darkness, but that the good one comes from that part of God which arrived from elsewhere, which clashed with the race of darkness and mingled the two together. And they attribute all the good in a human being to that good soul, but all the evil to that evil soul.

In this book what I said—*that there is no life of any sort whatever which, by the very fact that it is life and insofar as it is life at all, does not pertain to the highest source and origin of life*[^1]—I said in such a way that it should be understood to pertain to the Creator as a creature does, and not be thought to be of him as a part of him.

**2.** Likewise, what I said—namely *that sin is nowhere except in the will*[^2]—the Pelagians can suppose was said in their favor on account of little children, who, they say, have no sin to be forgiven in baptism, since they do not yet make use of the choice of the will. As if the sin which we say they contract from Adam by origin—that is, being implicated in his guilt and on that account held subject to punishment—could ever have been anywhere except in the will, the will by which it was committed when the transgression of the divine precept took place.

That statement in which we said *that sin is nowhere except in the will* can also be thought false, because the Apostle said, *But if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but the sin that dwells in me*[^3]. For this sin is so far from being in the will that he says, *I do what I do not want.* How, then, is sin nowhere except in the will? But this sin, of which the Apostle speaks in this way, is called sin because it was brought about by sin and is the punishment of sin—since this is said of the concupiscence of the flesh, as he makes clear in what follows when he says, *I know that nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh; for the will is present to me, but to accomplish the good is not*[^4].

For the perfection of the good is that not even the concupiscence of sin should be in a person, the concupiscence to which the will does not consent when one lives well. Yet one does not accomplish the good, because the concupiscence against which the will struggles is still present. The guilt of this concupiscence is dissolved in baptism, but the weakness remains, against which every faithful person who makes good progress fights most zealously until it is healed. But the sin which is *nowhere except in the will* is especially to be understood as that which a just condemnation has followed; for this is the sin that *entered into the world through one man*[^5]. And yet this sin too, by which one consents to the concupiscence of sin, is committed only by the will. On this account I also said in another place, *Therefore one sins only by the will*[^6].

**3.** Likewise, in another place I defined the will itself, saying, *The will is a movement of the mind, with nothing compelling it, either toward not losing or toward acquiring something*[^7]. This was said in order that by this definition the willing person might be distinguished from the unwilling, and that the point might thus bear upon those who were the first in paradise, the origin of evil for the human race, sinning with nothing compelling them—that is, sinning by free will—since they both acted knowingly against the precept, and that tempter persuaded them to do this but did not compel it. For even one who sinned unknowingly can not unfittingly be said to have sinned unwillingly; yet what he did unknowingly he nevertheless did willingly, so that not even his sin could be without the will.

This *will*, as it was defined, was indeed *a movement of the mind, with nothing compelling it, either toward not losing or toward acquiring something.* For what he would not have done had he not willed it, he was not compelled to do. Because he willed it, then, he did it, even if it was not because he willed it that he sinned, not knowing that what he did was a sin. Thus not even such a sin could be without the will, but it was by the will of the deed, not by the will of the sin—and yet what was done was sin, for what was done was something that ought not to have been done. But whoever sins knowingly, if he can resist without sin the one who compels him to sin and yet does not do so, certainly sins willingly, since one who can resist is not compelled to yield. But one who cannot resist a compelling desire with a good will, and so acts against the precepts of justice—this is now sin in such a way that it is also the punishment of sin. Therefore it is most true that sin cannot be without the will.

**4.** Likewise, the definition of sin in which we said, *Sin is the will to retain or to obtain what justice forbids and from which one is free to abstain*[^8], is true because what was defined is that which is only sin, not that which is also the punishment of sin. For when it is such that it is at the same time the punishment of sin, how much is the will capable of under the dominion of desire, unless perhaps, if it is devout, that it pray for help? For the will is free to the extent that it has been set free, and to that extent it is called will. Otherwise the whole of it is desire, which is what should properly be called the will—which is not, as the Manichees foolishly suppose, an addition of an alien nature, but a defect of our own, from which we are not healed except by the grace of the Savior. But if anyone says that desire itself is nothing other than the will, though a defective will that serves sin, we need not resist; nor should a controversy be made over words when the matter is settled. For even so it is shown that no sin is without the will, whether in act or in origin.

**5.** Again, as to what I said: *I could already ask whether that evil kind of souls, before it was mingled with the good, had any will. For if it did not, it was without sin and innocent, and therefore in no way evil*[^9]. Why then, they say, do you speak of the sin of little children, whose will you do not hold guilty? The answer is that they are held guilty not by the property of their own will, but by origin. For what is every earthly human being by origin except Adam? And Adam certainly had a will, and when he had sinned by that will, *sin* entered *into the world* through him[^10].

**6.** Likewise, as to what I said: *They can in no way be of an evil nature*[^11]—if it is asked how we are to understand what the Apostle says, *We too were by nature children of wrath, like the rest*[^12]—we answer that by "nature" in these words of mine I meant to be understood that nature which is properly called nature, in which we were created without defect. For that nature which is so called on account of origin does have a defect, which is contrary to nature.

And again, as to what was said: *That anyone should be held guilty of sin because he did not do what he could not do is the height of injustice and madness*[^13]—why then, they say, are little children held guilty? The answer is that they are held guilty from the origin of him who did not do what he could have done, namely keep the divine commandment.

But what I said—*If those souls do whatever they do by nature and not by will, that is, if they lack a movement of the mind free both to act and not to act, and if, finally, no power is granted them of abstaining from their work, we cannot hold them guilty of sin*[^14]—is not disturbed by the question about little children, because they are held guilty from the origin of him who sinned by will, when he did not lack a movement of the mind free both to act and not to act, and had full power to abstain from the evil work. This the Manichees do not say of the race of darkness, which they introduce in the most fabulous way, contending that it was a nature always evil and never good.

**7.** It can be asked, however, how I said: *Even if there are souls—which for the present is uncertain—given over to bodily functions not by sin but by nature, and even if they touch upon us by some inner kinship, although they are inferior, they ought not on that account to be considered evil because we, when we follow them and love bodily things, are evil*[^15]—since I said this about those souls of which I had begun to speak earlier, saying: *Although, even if it be granted them that we are enticed to base things by some other inferior kind of souls, they do not thereby establish either that those souls are evil by nature or that these of ours are the highest good*[^16]. For about these I carried the discussion as far as this place, where I said: *Even if there are souls—which for the present is uncertain—given over to bodily functions not by sin but by nature*, and so on.

It can be asked, then, why I said *which for the present is uncertain*, when I ought not to have doubted at all that there are no such souls. But I said this because I had met people who said that the devil and his angels are good in their own kind and in that nature in which God created them, in their proper order, just as they are, but that it is evil for us if we are enticed and seduced by them, while it is honorable and glorious if we beware of them and overcome them. And those who say this seem to themselves, in order to prove it, to bring forward suitable testimonies from the Scriptures: either what is written in the book of Job, when the devil is being described, *This is the beginning of the Lord's handiwork, which he made to be mocked by his angels*[^17], or that text in the one hundred and third Psalm, *This dragon which you formed to mock him*[^18].

This question—which would have to be taken up and resolved not against the Manichees, who do not hold this view, but against others who do hold it—I did not then wish to treat and unravel, lest I should make the book far longer than I wished, since I saw that even if this were granted, the Manichees nevertheless ought to be, and even then could be, refuted, since they introduce by a most insane error a nature of evil coeternal with the eternal good. Therefore I said *which for the present is uncertain*, not because I myself was in doubt about it, but because between me and those whom I had found to think thus this question was not yet resolved. Yet in others of my much later books, the *Literal Meaning of Genesis*, I resolved it according to the holy Scriptures with as much clarity as I could[^19].

**8.** In another place I say: *For this reason we sin by loving bodily things, because we are both commanded by justice to love spiritual things and able by nature to do so; and then we are best and most blessed in our own kind*[^20]. Here it can be asked why I said we are *able* by *nature* and not by grace. But the question at issue against the Manichees concerned nature.

And certainly grace brings it about that nature, once healed, can do what, when corrupted, it cannot, through him who *came to seek and to save what was lost*[^21]. Yet, mindful even then of this grace, I prayed for my closest friends, who were still held by that deadly error, and I said: *Great God, almighty God, God of the highest goodness, whom it is right to believe and to understand to be inviolable and unchangeable, Triune Unity whom the Catholic Church worships, as a suppliant I pray, having experienced your mercy in myself, that you not allow the men with whom from boyhood I have had the closest fellowship in all my dealings to differ from me in worshiping you*[^22].

In praying thus, I already held by faith that not only are those who have been converted to God helped by his grace to make progress and be brought to perfection—where one can still say that this grace is given on account of the merit of their conversion—but that the very turning to God also pertains to the grace of God itself, since I prayed for those who were exceedingly turned away from him, and prayed that they might be turned toward him.

This book begins thus: *With the help of God's mercy.*
