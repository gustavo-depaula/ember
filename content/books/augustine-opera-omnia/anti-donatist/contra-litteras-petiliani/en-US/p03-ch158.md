# The Donatists made supplication to Julian.

**92.** 205. Or is it that, because those whom I mentioned were of ancient times, you therefore judge that they do not oppose you at all, because you did not say: Whom righteousness has never found anything but hostile; but said, *whom Christianity has never found anything but hostile*; wishing them perhaps to be understood as envying the righteous from that time at which they began to be called Christians? What, then, do the examples of ancient times mean to you, by which you wished to teach, more imprudently, what you imprudently said?

For did not the Maccabees, or the three boys, or Daniel, either do those things or endure them before Christ was born on earth? Then, the one whom I mentioned a little before, why did you make supplication to Julian, a true enemy of Christianity? Why did you ask the basilicas from him? Why did you say that with him justice alone had a place? If an enemy of Christianity hears these things, what are they from whom he hears them?

But Constantine, by no means an enemy of the Christian name, but plainly glorious in the Christian name, mindful of the hope he bore in Christ, judging most justly for its unity, deserved to be accepted by you—though you did not even appeal to him. Both were now in Christian times, but both were not Christians; and if both were enemies of Christianity, why did you appeal to one of them thus, and make supplication to the other thus? For when your elders petitioned, Constantine had granted an episcopal judgment, both at Rome and at Arles; the first of which you accused before him, from the second you appealed to him. But if, as is true, one of them had believed in Christ, the other had apostatized from Christ; why is the Christian who consults for unity despised, and the apostate who favors division praised?

Constantine commanded the basilicas to be taken from you, Julian to be restored. Which of these befits Christian peace, do you wish to know? The one was done by him who had believed in Christ, the other by him who had abandoned Christ.

Oh, how you would wish to say: It was wrongly done that supplication was made to Julian thus, but what does this have to do with us? And if you said this, even in these your very words the Catholic Church would win, to whose saints spread throughout the whole world whatever you say has much less to do, against whom you wish, as you wish.

But you cannot say that it was wrongly done that supplication was made to Julian thus; the authority of your own house stops your throat, presses your tongue; Pontius did it, Pontius made supplication, Pontius called the apostate most just; Pontius proclaimed that with the apostate justice alone had a place. That Pontius made supplication to him in these words Julian himself expressed by name, without ambiguity, in that same rescript of his. Your pleadings are extant: not uncertain rumor, but public records testify to these things. Or is it perhaps that, because the apostate granted something to your petition against the unity of Christ, you think it true that it was said that with him justice alone had a place? But because Christian emperors, against your will, establish what they judge to avail for the unity of Christ, are they called enemies of Christianity? So may all heretics lose their senses, and come to their senses, that they may not be heretics.
