# Scripture often says that kings helped the faithful.

**92.** 204. *What,* you say, *have you to do with the kings of this world, whom Christianity has never found anything but hostile?* When you had said this, you tried to enumerate which kings the righteous found to be enemies; and you did not consider that more could be enumerated whom they had as friends. Father Abraham, by a king divinely admonished not to touch his wife, was both treated most amicably and received a gift[^1]. Isaac his son likewise experienced a most friendly king[^2]. Jacob, received honorably in Egypt by a king, even blesses him[^3].

What shall I say of his son Joseph, who, after the affliction of prison, by which, as by fire, his chastity was tested like gold, was raised to great honors by him, and even swore by the health of Pharaoh[^4], not as one lifted up by pride, but as one not ungrateful for the king's good will. A king's daughter adopted Moses[^5]. David, driven by the iniquity of the king of Israel, fled to a foreigner[^6]. Elijah ran before the chariot of a most wicked king, not by his command, but in his own homage[^7]. Elisha thought that, if the hostess woman wished anything to be granted her by the king through his intercession, it should even be offered to her unasked[^8].

But I will come now to those very times when the people of God was held captive, where, to put it more mildly, a strange forgetfulness crept upon you. For, wishing to prove that Christianity has never found kings anything but hostile, you mentioned the three boys and Daniel, what they suffered from persecuting kings, and you could be admonished not by neighboring matters, but plainly by these very ones, what sort the king himself proved to be after the miracle of the harmless flames, whether in praising and proclaiming God, or in honoring the boys themselves;

what sort of Daniel the king had, and with what gifts he adorned him, Daniel not refusing them, since he, rendering the honor due to the royal power, as sufficiently appears in his words, did not withhold the gift of God with which he was endowed, in indicating it, in interpreting the king's dream.

Hence, when the king was compelled, unwilling, to throw him into the lions' den by the holy man's enviers, slandering him with sacrilegious madness, although he did it in sorrow, he nevertheless presumed that, through the help of his Lord, he would be safe. And so, when Daniel had lived unharmed, the rage of the lions divinely restrained, with the king's own anxious and friendly voice sent ahead to him, he too answered with a blessing from the den: *O king, live forever*[^9].

Why these friendships of kings with the saints, when your discourse was dwelling on that very subject, when you yourself were mentioning the examples of those servants of God in whom these things were done, did you either not see, or refuse to see, or—what I know not how you would excuse—keep silent about though you saw and knew them?

But if the zeal for building up falsehood did not hinder you, like the defender of a most evil cause, and turn you, unwilling or unaware, away from the light of truth; you would surely recall, without any difficulty, that some kings were good, some evil, and some friends of the saints, others enemies.

And we wonder that your Circumcellions hurl themselves down so. Who was running after you, I beg you? What Macarius, what soldier was pursuing? Surely none of us pushed you into the precipice of this falsehood.

Why, then, with closed eyes were you so headlong that, when you had said: *But what have you to do with the kings of this world?* you did not add, whom Christianity has often found hostile; but did not hesitate to say, *whom Christianity has never found anything but hostile*?

Did you yourself really not think, nor suppose that those who read your writings would think, that the examples of so many kings cry out against you: He does not know what he is saying?
