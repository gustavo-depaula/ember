# Authority ought to prevent evil.

**83.** 184. AUGUSTINE REPLIED: No one indeed is to be compelled to faith against his will, but through the severity, indeed even through the mercy of God, faithlessness is wont to be chastised by the scourges of tribulations. Is it that, because the best conduct is chosen by freedom of the will, therefore the worst conduct is not punished by the integrity of the law? And yet the avenging discipline of evil living is out of order, except when the preceding teaching of good living is despised.

If, then, any laws have been established against you, you are not compelled by them to do good, but you are prevented from doing evil. For no one can do good unless he has chosen, unless he has loved what is in the free will; but the fear of punishments, even if it does not yet have the delight of a good conscience, at least restrains evil desire within the enclosure of thought. Yet those who established laws against you, by which your boldness may be checked, are they not those of whom the Apostle says that they do not bear the sword without cause; for they are ministers of God, avengers to execute wrath on him who does evil[^1]?

The whole question, then, is whether you are not doing evil, you against whom the whole earth charges the sacrilege of so great a schism: and, neglecting the discussion of this question, you speak superfluously; and though you live as robbers, you boast that you die as martyrs. And because you dread either the laws themselves, or the resentment, or you are unequal to resisting—I do not say so many men, but so many Catholic nations—you even boast of your meekness, that you compel no one to your party. In this way even the kite, when frightened it could not seize the chicks, might call itself a dove. For where have you had the power, and not done it? From which you show how much more you would do, if you could.

When Julian, envying the peace of Christ, gave back to you the basilicas of unity, what slaughters were made by you, when along with you the very demons exulted at their temples thrown open, who could suffice to recount? In the Firmian war, what Rogatus the Moor suffered from you, let Caesarean Mauretania itself be questioned. In the time of Gildo, since one of your colleagues was his most intimate friend, let the Maximianists consider what they felt. For Felician himself, who is now with you, if it were permitted to call him to take an oath whether Optatus compelled him, not unwilling, to return to your communion, would not dare to move his lips, especially if the people of Musti were looking on his face, with whom as witnesses those things were then done. But these men, as I said, let them consider what they suffered from those with whom they had done such things to Rogatus.

The Catholic Church itself, made firm with Catholic princes ruling by land and sea, was savagely and hostilely assailed by Optatus with armed mobs. This matter then for the first time forced that law about ten pounds of gold to be invoked against you before the vicar Seranus—which none of you has yet paid, and you charge us with cruelty! But what could be milder than that your so great crimes should be punished by the coercion of fines? And who could relate all the things that each of you, where he could, by no friendship of judges or of any powers, commits by his own domination in your places? Which of us, among our own people, has not either learned of some such thing from his predecessors, or experienced it himself? Is it not the case that at Hippo, where I am, there are those who remember that your Faustinus, in the time of his rule, since there was a small number of Catholics there, commanded that no one should bake them bread, so that the baker tenant of a certain deacon of ours threw away his landlord's bread unbaked, and, though the man was condemned by no law of exile, denied him fellowship not only in the Roman city, but even in his own homeland, and not only in his own homeland, but even in his own house?

What of recently, which I still grieve over: did not your Crispinus of Calama, when he had bought an estate, and that on lease, not hesitate, on the estate of Catholic emperors, by whose laws you were ordered not even to be in the cities, in one onslaught of terror to submerge nearly eighty souls, muttering with pitiable groaning, by rebaptizing them? But by what, except such deeds, did you force even those very laws of which you complain to be enacted—far indeed below the desert of your crime, but nevertheless such as they are? Or would we not, by the violent incursions of your Circumcellions, who under your rule serve in frenzied bands, be driven from the fields on every side, unless we held you as hostages in the cities—you who in any case are unwilling to bear, if not out of fear, then out of shame, the very public face and the reproach of honorable men? Do not, then, say: *Far be it, far be it from our conscience, that we should drive anyone to our faith.* For you do it where you can; and where you do not do it, you cannot, whether out of fear of the laws, or of resentment, or because of the multitude of those resisting.

[^1]: See Rom 13:4.
