# Book III

## Petilian answered by reviling, not by refuting.

**1.** 1. I have read your letter, Petilian, when I was able to read it, by which you have sufficiently indicated that, against the catholic Church, on behalf of the party of Donatus, you have neither been able to say anything fit, nor been permitted to be silent. What tossings you suffered, with how great a tempest of heart you wavered, when you had read the things by which I replied, with as much brevity and clearness as I could, to a part of your letter, which had then come into my hands!

For you saw the truth which we hold and defend so strengthened with such firmness, so illumined with such light, that you could not find what was to be said against it, by which, refuted, it might be confuted. You also noticed that the expectation of the many who had read these things was turned toward you, desiring to know what you would say, what you would do, by what way you would escape, where you would break out from the great straits in which the word of God had hemmed you.

Here you, who ought, with the opinion of the empty-minded despised, to have proceeded into a true and sound view, did what Scripture foretold about such men: *You have loved malice above kindness, iniquity rather than to speak equity*[^1]. Accordingly, if I too wished to repay you curses for curses, what else would we be but two revilers, so that those who read us would, some, in sound seriousness, cast us off in disgust, others sweetly drink us in with malevolent pleasure?

When I reply to anyone either by speaking or by writing, even when provoked by abusive accusations, as far as the Lord grants me, with the stings of vain indignation reined in and crushed, taking thought for the hearer or reader, I do not act so as to become superior to the man by reviling, but more wholesome by refuting his error.

## Augustine turns his discourse away from Petilian and directs it to the readers.

**1.** 2. For if those who consider what you have written have any heart at all; what has it profited you, for the cause which is at issue between us about catholic communion or about the party of Donatus, that, leaving aside the public business, so to speak, by a certain private grudge you have pursued the life of one man with reviling reproaches, as though that man were the cause being inquired into?

Have you thought so ill, I will not say of Christians, but of the human race itself, that you did not believe your writings could come into the hands of any prudent persons, who would lift themselves above our persons, and rather inquire into the question that was at issue between us, and would attend not to who or what sort we were, but to what we said for the truth or against error?

The judgment of these men ought to have been revered by you, the reproach of these men guarded against, lest they should judge that you had found nothing to say, except by setting before yourself someone to revile in any way at all. But, no doubt, by the levity and vanity of certain people, who gladly hear the quarrels of disputing speakers, so that, while they attend to how eloquently you revile, they do not perceive how truly you are confuted.

At the same time, I suppose, you did this too, that, occupied with my defense, I too might desert the cause I had undertaken; and so, with men turned not to the words of those debating, but of those quarreling, the truth might be obscured, which you dread to have grow clear and known.

I, therefore, against such a plan, what shall I do, except, with my own defense rather neglected, hold to the matter, from which no accuser of mine may shake my purpose? The house of my God[^2], whose beauty I have loved, I will extol by the proclamation of a serving voice, but myself I will humble and cast down. For I have chosen to be cast down in the house of my God, rather than to dwell in the tents of heretics[^3]. From you, then, Petilian, I will turn my discourse for a little while; and I will turn it to those whom you tried to turn away from me by your curses, as though I were contriving this, that men should be turned to me, and not rather with me to God.

...and addresses them with apostolic words.

**2.** 3. Hear, then, whoever you are who have read the curses which Petilian, more angry than considerate, vomited against me. First I will address you with apostolic words; which certainly, whatever sort I myself may be, are true. *So let a man regard us as ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God. Here now it is required among stewards that one be found faithful. But to me it is a very small thing that I be judged by you, or by any human day; nor do I even judge myself*[^4]*.*

Now what follows, although I do not dare to apply to myself, so as to say: *For I am conscious of nothing in myself*[^5], yet I confidently say in the sight of God: Of none of the things with which Petilian accused the time of my life, after I was baptized in Christ, am I conscious in myself; *Yet I am not thereby justified; but he who judges me is the Lord. Therefore do not judge anything before the time, until the Lord comes, and illumines the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the thoughts of the heart, and then each will have praise from God. But these things, brothers, I have transferred in a figure to myself, that you may not be puffed up, one for the one against the other, beyond what is written*[^6]. *And so let no one glory in man. All things are yours, but you are Christ's, and Christ is God's*[^7].

Again I say: *Let no one glory in man*[^8]*;* more often I repeat: *Let no one glory in man.* If you notice anything praiseworthy in us, refer it to the praise of him from whom comes *every best gift, and every perfect gift: for it comes down from above, from the Father of lights, with whom there is no change, nor shadow of a moment*[^9]. For what do we have that we have not received? But if we have received it, let us not glory as though we had not received it[^10].

And in all these things, whatever good you know in us, be imitators of us, if indeed we are of Christ[^11]; but if you suspect or believe or see any evils in us[^12], hold to that word of the Lord, by which, secure, you may not desert his Church on account of the evils of men: What we say, do; but what evils you think or know us to do, do not do[^13]. For it is not the time to clear myself to you, since, with my own cause neglected, I have undertaken to commend to you a wholesome matter, that *no one may glory in man*[^14]. For *cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^15].

With this command of the Lord and the Apostle held and kept, even if I, in my own cause, as the enemy desires me to be regarded, fail and am overwhelmed, the cause which I serve will be victorious.

For if you most firmly hold what I exhort and strive for—that cursed is everyone who places his hope in man, that no one may glory in man; you will in no way desert the Lord's threshing floor on account of the chaff which either now, struck by the wind of pride, flies away, or at the final winnowing will be separated[^16], nor will you flee the great house on account of the vessels made for dishonor[^17], nor will you go out through torn nets on account of the bad fish to be separated on the shore[^18],

nor will you leave the good pastures of unity on account of the goats to be placed on the left by the shepherd who will divide[^19], nor, on account of the mingled weeds, will you, by a wicked tearing-apart, separate yourselves from the fellowship of the wheat, whose head is that grain put to death and multiplied, and which grows together throughout the world until the harvest[^20]. For *the field is the world,* not Africa; *the harvest, the end of the age*[^21], not the time of Donatus.

## The apostolic words admonish that the chaff and the grain not be divided.

**3.** 4. These evangelical likenesses surely you recognize, given for no other purpose than that no one may glory in man, and that no one, puffed up for the one against the other, may be divided, saying: *I am of Paul*; when surely Paul was not crucified for you, nor were you baptized in the name of Paul, how much less in the name of Caecilian or of any of us[^22]: so that you may learn, as long as the chaff is threshed with the grain, as long as the bad fish swim with the good within the Lord's nets, before the time of winnowing rather to tolerate, for the sake of the good, the mixture of the bad, than to violate, on account of the bad, the love of the good?

For this mixture is not eternal, but temporal; not spiritual, but bodily. In it the angels will not err, when they *gather the bad from the midst of the just, and cast them into the furnace of burning fire*[^23]. For the Lord knows who are his. And if for a time he cannot bodily withdraw from the wicked, let everyone who names the name of the Lord nevertheless withdraw from iniquity itself[^24].

For it is permitted meanwhile to be separated from and to depart from the wicked in life, conduct, heart, and will: which separation ought always to be kept. But bodily separation should be awaited, confidently, patiently, bravely, until the end of the age: on account of which awaiting it was said: *Wait for the Lord, act manfully; let your heart be strengthened, and wait for the Lord*[^25].

For the greatest palm of toleration is, among false brothers brought in secretly, seeking their own, not the things of Jesus Christ[^26], with the love of those who seek not their own but the things of Jesus Christ, to disturb nothing by any turbulent and rash dissension, nor to tear apart, by a proud and wicked contention, the unity of the Lord's net, which gathers from every kind of fish, while it is drawn to the shore, that is, to the end of the age,

when each one thinks himself to be something, while he is nothing, and so deceives himself[^27]; and wishes his own judgment, or that of his own people, to suffice for the separation of Christian peoples—those who say that they most plainly know certain wicked men to be unworthy of the communion of the sacraments of the Christian religion: about whom, nevertheless, whatever they say they know, they cannot persuade the universal Church, which is spread, as it was foretold, throughout all the nations.

And when they flee the communion of these men, as of those whom they have known, they desert the unity of that Church: when they ought rather, if there were in them a love that bears all things, lest they divide themselves from the good, whom in all the nations they could not teach the evils of others, themselves to tolerate, in one nation, what they had known[^28].

Hence too, even when the cause is not discussed, in which by the gravest proofs they are convicted of having slandered the innocent, it is more probably believed that they fabricated false crimes of handing-over, who did not hesitate to commit, far more wickedly, the crime of a wicked division. For even if whatever they boasted about the handing-over were true; yet they ought by no means to have abandoned the fellowship of the Christians whom divine Scripture commends to the very ends of the earth, on account of what they themselves knew, but those others did not know.

## To tolerate is not to neglect ecclesiastical discipline.

**4.** 5. Nor would I say this so that ecclesiastical discipline be neglected, so that each one be permitted to do what he wishes, without any rebuke, and a certain medicinal punishment, and a terrible gentleness, and the severity of love. For where will that word of the Apostle be: *Rebuke the unruly, console the fainthearted, support the weak, be patient toward all. See that no one renders evil for evil to anyone*[^29]? When he added this: *See that no one renders evil for evil to anyone;* he sufficiently showed that it is not a rendering of evil for evil to rebuke the unruly, although for the fault of unruliness the penalty of rebuke be rendered.

The penalty of rebuke, then, is not an evil, since the fault is an evil. For it is not the iron of an enemy wounding, but of a physician cutting. These things are done in the Church, and that spirit of inner[^30] gentleness burns with zeal for God, lest the chaste virgin, betrothed to one husband, Christ, be corrupted in any of her members from the chastity that is in Christ, as Eve was seduced by the cunning of the serpent[^31].

Nevertheless, far be it from the servants of the head of the household that they be unmindful of the command of their Lord, and so blaze with the burning of holy indignation against the multitude of weeds that, while they wish to gather them before the time[^32], the wheat too be uprooted along with them.

Of which sin these men would be held guilty, even if they had shown that they charged true crimes against the traitors whom they accused: since they were separated, by an impious presumption, not only from the wicked, whose fellowship they were, as it were, avoiding, but also from the good faithful established in all the nations, to whom they could not prove what they said they knew; and they drew along with them into the same ruin many among whom they prevailed by some authority, and who were less able to understand that the unity of the Church spread throughout the whole world was by no means to be deserted for the sins of others;

so that, even if they themselves knew that they were charging true crimes against certain persons, in that way the weak one would perish in their knowledge, for whom Christ died[^33], while, offended by the evils of others, he destroyed in himself the good of peace which he had with the good brothers, who had, some of them, not heard such things, some had feared to believe rashly things undiscussed and unproved, some had left them, whatever they were, with peaceful humility to the ecclesiastical judges, to whom the whole cause was brought across the sea.

## We must bear with one another in love for the sake of unity.

**5.** 6. You, then, holy offspring of the one mother, the catholic Church, subject to the Lord, with as great a vigilance as you can, guard against an example of this kind of crime and error. With whatever light of learning and reputation he may shine, and boast himself to be a precious stone, whoever wishes to draw you after himself, remember that that strong woman, lovable to one husband alone, whom holy Scripture describes at the end of Proverbs[^34], is more precious than precious stones. Let no one say: I will follow him, because he made me a Christian; or: I will follow him, because he baptized me. *Neither is he who plants anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^35]. And *God is love, and he who remains in love remains in God, and God in him*[^36].

No one, even though he preaches the name of Christ, and bears and administers the sacrament of Christ, is to be followed against the unity of Christ. *Let each one prove his own work; and then he will have glory in himself alone, and not in another. For each one will bear his own burden*[^37], the burden, that is, of rendering an account: for *each one of us will render an account for himself. Let us therefore no longer judge one another*[^38]. For as far as concerns the burdens of mutual love, *bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ. For he who thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, deceives himself*[^39]. Let us, then, bear with one another in love, taking pains to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace[^40]: outside which whoever gathers, does not gather with Christ: but whoever does not gather with Christ, scatters[^41].

## To bear false crimes is a most abundant consolation.

**6.** 7. Accordingly, whether about Christ, or about his Church, or about any other matter that pertains to your faith and life, I will not say we, by no means to be compared to him who said: *Even if we;* but altogether what he added, following: *If an angel from heaven should announce to you anything beyond what*, in the lawful and evangelical Scriptures, *you have received, let him be anathema*[^42].

Transacting these things with you, and with all whom we desire to win for Christ, and, among the rest, preaching the holy Church, which we read promised in the Letters of God, and which we behold rendered, as it was promised, in all the nations, we have earned the flames of hatreds in return for the giving of thanks, from those whom we desire to be drawn to her peaceful bosom: as though we had bound them in that part, on behalf of which they find nothing to say; or as though we had commanded the prophets and apostles so long before to put in their books no testimonies by which the party of Donatus might be taught to be the Church of Christ. And we indeed, dearly beloved, when we hear false crimes from those whom we offend by preaching the utterances of truth and by refuting the empty talk of error, have, as you know, a most abundant consolation. For if, in those things for which they accuse me, the testimony of my conscience does not stand against me in the sight of God, on which no mortal eye is fixed; not only ought I not to be saddened, but I ought even to rejoice and exult, because my reward is great in heaven[^43].

For one must consider not how bitter, but how false is what I hear, and how truthful is he for whose name I hear this, and to whom it is said: *Your name is ointment poured out*[^44]. And rightly it is fragrant among all the nations, whose fragrance these men, who curse us, try to shut up in one little part of Africa. Why, then, should we take it ill that those defame us who so detract from the glory of Christ, to whose party and contention it is hostile, that about his ascension to the heavens, and about the pouring out of his name as ointment, it was foretold so long before: *Be exalted above the heavens, O God; and over all the earth your glory*[^45]?

## Those who endure false crimes are like Christ.

**7.** 8. Bringing forward these and such divine testimonies against human empty talk, we endure bitter reproaches from the enemies of the glory of Christ. Let them say what they will, while he exhorts us, saying: *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed shall you be when they persecute you, and revile you, and say every evil against you, lying, for my sake*[^46]*.* What he said above, *for the sake of righteousness;* this he repeated, what he said afterward, *for my sake:* because *he was made for us wisdom and righteousness and sanctification and redemption; that, as it is written: He who glories, let him glory in the Lord*[^47]. And since he says: *Rejoice and exult, for your reward is great in heaven*[^48]; if what was said, *for the sake of righteousness* and *for my sake,* I hold in a good conscience, whoever willingly detracts from my reputation, unwillingly adds to my reward.

For he did not teach me only by his word, and not also confirm it by his example. Follow the faith of the holy Scriptures, you will find that Christ rose from the dead, ascended into heaven, sits at the right hand of the Father. Follow the accusations of his enemies, and you will now suppose that he was stolen from the tomb by the disciples. What else, then, ought we, as far as he grants, defending his house, to expect from his enemies? *If they called the head of the household Beelzebul, how much more those of his household*[^49]? *If, then, we endure, we shall also reign with him*[^50]. But if not only does the wrath of the accuser strike the ear, but the truth of the crime also bites the conscience, what does it profit me if the whole world extols me with continual praises? So neither does the proclamation of the one praising heal a bad conscience, nor does the reproach of the one reviling wound a good one. Nor, even so, is your hope, which is in the Lord, deceived[^51], even if perhaps in secret we are such as the enemy desires us to be thought: because you did not place it in us, nor ever heard this from us. You are secure, then, whatever sort we may be, who have learned to say: *Hoping in the Lord, I shall not be moved*[^52]? and: *In God I will hope, I will not fear what man may do to me*[^53]. And to those who strive to seduce you to the earthly heights of proud men, you know how to answer: *In the Lord I trust: how do you say to my soul, Migrate to the mountain like a sparrow?*[^54]

## Christians are secure in Christ and not in man.

**8.** 9. And not only you, to whom the very truth of Christ in us is pleasing, however and wherever it is preached through us, and who, because you gladly hear it through the small ministry of our tongue, think well and kindly even of us, are secure, whatever sort we may be; because your hope is in him whom we preach to you out of his mercy upon you; but also whoever of you have received, by our dispensation, even the sacrament of holy baptism, rejoice with the same security; since you were baptized not in us, but in Christ. And so you put on not us, but Christ[^55];

nor did I ask you whether you were being converted to me, but to the living God; nor whether you believed in me, but in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. And if you answered with a truthful mind, what saved you was not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the inquiry of a good conscience[^56]; not a fellow servant, but the Lord; not a herald, but the judge. For it is not truly, as Petilian inconsiderately said: *The conscience of the one giving,* or what he added: *The conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving.*

For when that which is God's is given, even a conscience that is not holy gives something holy. Which conscience, certainly, whether it be holy or not holy, cannot be inspected by the one receiving; but plainly that which is given can be, which, known to him who is always holy, by whatever sort of minister it is received, is most securely received.

For unless the words from the chair of Moses were holy, the Truth would not say: *What they say, do.* But if those who gave the holy words were themselves holy, he would not say: *What they do, do not do; for they say and do not do*[^57]. For in no way is a grape read of from thorns; because it never springs from the roots of thorns; but when the shoot of the vine has entangled itself with thorny[^58] hedges, the fruit that hangs there is not therefore shuddered at; but the thorn is avoided, the grape is plucked.

## Let no one glory, not even in a good man.

**9.** 10. And so, as I have often said, and vehemently impress, whatever sort we may be, you are secure, who have God as Father and his Church as mother. For although the goats are pastured along with the sheep, they will not stand at the right hand. Although the chaff is threshed along with the wheat, it will not enter the barn. Although the bad fish swim along with the good within the Lord's nets, they will not be put into the vessels. Let no one glory, not even in a good man; let no one flee the good things of God, not even in an evil man.

## Petilian did not say true things about Augustine.

**10.** 11. Let these things suffice you, dearly beloved catholic Christian brothers, for the present business; which if you hold with catholic love, since you are one flock secure under one shepherd, I do not much mind that some enemy reviles me—whether your fellow member of the flock, or certainly your dog; provided that he compels me to bark more for your defense than for my own;

which defense, nevertheless, if it were needed for the cause, I would use most briefly and most easily—namely, condemning and detesting, along with everyone, the whole time of my life before I received the baptism of Christ, as far as concerns my own desires and errors, lest in the defense of that time I should seem to seek my own glory, not that of him who freed me, through his grace, even from myself. Hence, when I hear that life of mine reproached, with whatever disposition he who does this may do it, I am not so ungrateful as to grieve; for as much as he accuses my vice, so much do I praise my physician. Why, then, should I now labor to defend those past and abolished evils of mine, about which Petilian said indeed many false things, but did not say more true ones?

But about the time of my life which is after baptism, to you who know me I speak superfluously of things which can be known to men; but those who do not know me ought not to be so unjust toward me as to believe Petilian about me rather than you. For if a praising friend is not to be believed, neither is a detracting enemy.

There remain the hidden things of a man, where the conscience alone is witness, which cannot be a witness among men[^59]. In these Petilian says that I am a Manichee, speaking of another's conscience: this I say that I am not, speaking of my own conscience. Choose whom to believe. Nevertheless, since there is no need even of this brief and easy defense of mine, where the question turns not on the merit of any one man, but on the truth of the holy Church; to you also I must say more, whoever in the party of Donatus have read the curses which Petilian wrote against me, which I would not hear from him if I despised your perdition: if I did not have the bowels of Christian love.

## The readers are admonished to judge without partisanship.

**11.** 12. What wonder, then, if, when I draw the grains shaken out from the Lord's threshing floor inward along with the earth and chaff, I suffer the injury of the dust that springs back: or, when I diligently seek the lost sheep of my Lord, I am torn by the brambles of thorny tongues? I beseech you, lay aside for a little while the zeal of partisanship, and judge between me and Petilian with some fairness. I wish you to know the cause of the Church, he the cause of me: for what other reason, except that he does not dare to tell you not to believe my witnesses, whom I steadfastly bring forward in the cause of the Church; for they are the prophets, and the apostles, and Christ himself, the Lord of the prophets and apostles: but whatever he wishes to say about me, do you easily believe a man against a man, and yours against another?

And if I should bring forward any witnesses of my life, what great thing is it for him to say that they are not to be believed, and to persuade you of this quickly: especially since whoever speaks any word for me will at once be reckoned an enemy of the party of Donatus, and through this yours? And so Petilian reigns: when he hurls any insults at me, you all applaud and clap for him.

He has found for himself a winnable cause, but with you as judges: he requires neither witness nor argument, for whom this alone is to prove what he says, that he reviles most copiously the one whom you most hate.

For since the testimonies of divine Scripture, when they are recited so densely and so clearly for the catholic Church, remain mute to your grief;

he has chosen a subject in which he may speak with you favoring him, though conquered as to the truth even if he says such things, or even more wicked ones, about me a thousand times over.

For the matter I am now treating, it is enough for me that, whatever sort I may be in it, nevertheless the Church for which I speak is unconquered.

## What the weapons of righteousness on the right and on the left are.

**12.** 13. For I am a man from the threshing floor of Christ; chaff if bad, grain if good. The tongue of Petilian is not the winnowing fork of this threshing floor: and through this, whatever evil he may even truly have said against its chaff, in no way prejudices its wheat. But whatever of curses and slanders he may have hurled against the wheat itself, their faith is exercised on earth, their reward is increased in heaven. For to the holy servants of God, soldiering in holiness for God, not against Petilian and flesh and blood of this kind, but against the principalities and powers and rulers of such darkness[^60], as are all the adversaries of the truth, to whom would that we might say: *You were once darkness, but now light in the Lord*[^61]; to the servants of God soldiering in this warfare, whatever criminal insults are spoken by the enemies, which among the malevolent and rashly credulous make a bad reputation, are weapons on the left; even by these the devil is overcome. For when, through a good reputation, we are tested whether we lift ourselves up into pride, and, through a bad reputation, we are tested whether we love the very enemies by whom things are fabricated against us, through the weapons of righteousness on the right and on the left we conquer the devil. Which, when the Apostle had mentioned, saying: *Through the weapons of righteousness on the right and on the left;* at once, as though expounding what he had said: *Through glory,* he says, *and dishonor, through ill repute and good repute*[^62], and other such things: counting glory, that is, and good repute among the weapons on the right, but on the left dishonor and ill repute.

## Against hatred the example of Christ is to be imitated.

**13.** 14. If, then, I am a servant of God and a soldier not rejected, however eloquent a reviler Petilian may prove against me, ought I to take it ill that a most skillful smith of left-hand weapons has been procured for me? It is necessary that I fight most skillfully with these in the help of my Lord, and that I strike with them him against whom I fight invisibly, who, with a most perverse and most ancient cunning, craftily aims and acts so that, on account of these things, I may hate Petilian, and thus may not be able to fulfill the command of Christ which says: *Love your enemies*[^63].

May the mercy of him avert this from me, who loved me, and handed himself over for me, so that, hanging on the cross, he might say: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do*[^64]: and might teach me to say about Petilian and such enemies of mine: Lord, forgive them, for they do not know what they say.

## Petilian uses curses because he cannot answer.

**14.** 15. Accordingly, if I obtain from you, as I have aimed, that, with all zeal of partisanship removed from your minds, you be fair judges between me and Petilian; I will show you that he did not answer my writings: so that you may understand that, compelled by the poverty of truth, he abandoned the cause, and hurled what curses he could against the man who so conducted it that he could not answer.

Although the things I am going to say shine forth with such great clearness that, even if, through zeal of partisanship and hatred of me, your minds are alienated from me, if only you read both, you would certainly confess, among yourselves in your hearts, that I have spoken the truth.

## To which question of the greatest weight Petilian did not answer.

**14.** 16. For I, in replying to the first part of his writings, which had then come into my hands, passing over the abusive and sacrilegious verbosity where he says: *They charge us with a second baptism, those who, under the name of baptism, have polluted their souls with a guilty washing, than whom indeed, in their obscenity, all filth is cleaner; whom a perverse cleanness has caused to be defiled by their own water*;

set myself to discuss and refute what follows, where he says: *For the conscience of the one giving is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving.* And I asked whence he is to be washed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one about to receive does not know this.

## Let his writings be examined.

**15.** 17. Read now his most copious curses, which, puffed up and angry, he poured out against me; and see whether he answered me when I asked whence he is to be washed who has received baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one receiving does not know this. I beseech you, search attentively, examine all the pages, count all the lines, turn over all the words, pick apart all the syllables; and tell me, if you find it, where he answered, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, whence that of the one who does not know this, about to receive it, is to be washed.

## The word "in holiness" has no bearing on the matter.

**15.** 18. For what did it bear on the matter, that he added a word which he said had been omitted by me, and so contends that he wrote thus: *For the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving?*

For—that you may know it was not omitted by me—the addition in no way hinders my inquiry, in no way relieves his failure. For I ask again of the very words, and inquire whether he answered: If *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving;* whence is the conscience of the one receiving washed, when the conscience of the one giving is stained, and the one about to receive does not know this?

Demand that this be answered, and do not permit the man, abandoning the cause, to be abusively alienated: If *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded:* see that I did not say, *the conscience of the one giving;* but I added, *of the one giving in holiness:* If *the conscience,* I say, *of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving;* whence, then, is he to be washed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one about to receive does not know this?

## Certain accusations of Petilian.

**16.** 19. Let him go now, and with panting lungs and swollen throat accuse me as a dialectician: rather, not me, but dialectic itself, as the artificer of lying, let him summon as a defendant to a popular tribunal, and against it let him distend the mouth of a forensic advocate with whatever most thunderous din.

Let him say whatever he wishes among the ignorant, at which the learned grow indignant, and the unlearned are deluded. Let him brand me, on account of rhetoric, with the name of Tertullus the orator, by whom Paul was accused[^65]: let him impose on himself, on account of the advocacy in which he once boasts of his power, the name of the Paraclete, and on this account let him rave that he is—not that he is, but that he was—the namesake of the Holy Spirit.

Let him most gladly amplify the uncleannesses of the Manichees, and try, by barking, to twist them onto me. Let him recite the deeds of condemned men, unknown and known to me, and let him turn the fact that there a former friend of mine named me, absent, rather for his own defense, into the slander of a prejudged crime, by some new law of his own. Let him read the titles of my letters, inscribed by himself, or by his own people, as it pleased them, and let him exult as though he had caught me confessing in them.

Let him defame, with a ridiculous name of poisonous shamefulness and frenzy, the eulogiae of bread given simply and cheerfully, and let him think so ill of your heart as to presume that it can be believed of him that love-charms were given to a woman, with her husband not only aware but even favoring it. What the ordainer of my future episcopate, angry, wrote about me while I was still a presbyter, let him wish to avail against me: but that he asked and obtained pardon from the holy council for this, in which he so sinned against us, let him be unwilling should avail for me; so ignorant or forgetful of Christian gentleness and the evangelical command that he charges as a crime even that which was clemently forgiven to a brother humbly asking that it be forgiven him as for himself.

## Other accusations.

**17.** 20. Let him proceed too, with verbose, but plainly empty, speech, into things which he utterly does not know, or rather in which he abuses the ignorance of very many: and from the confession of a certain woman, that she said she was a catechumen of the Manichees, who had been a consecrated woman in the catholic Church, let him say and write what pleases him about their baptism, not knowing, or pretending not to know, that there catechumens are not so called as though baptism were owed to them at some time; but that those are called this who are also called Hearers, because, forsooth, they cannot observe the better and greater precepts which are observed by those whom they think to be distinguished and honored by the name of the Elect.

Let him contend, with wonderful rashness, whether deceived or deceiving, that I too was a presbyter of the Manichees. Let him set forth, under whatever understanding seems good to him, and find fault with, the words of the fourth book of my Confessions, which are by themselves, and from much said before and after, most plain to readers. Finally, let him, as a thief of his words, because I omitted two, exult as victor, as though they had been restored.

## But no answer.

**18.** 21. In all these things, certainly, as you will be able to learn or relearn by reading, he exercised the onslaught of his tongue in whatever circuit of boasting he pleased: nowhere, however, did he say whence the conscience of the one receiving is washed, when he does not know the stained conscience of the one giving.

But I, amid his great din, or after his great din, and—as he himself thinks—exceedingly terrible, slowly, as the saying goes, and well, repeat this very thing, demanding that he at least answer it: If the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving; whence is he to be washed who does not know the stained conscience of the one giving? And throughout his whole letter I find nothing said to this.

**19.** 22. For perhaps one of you will say to me: All these things which he said against you he wished to avail for this, to deform you, and through you those with whom you communicate, lest henceforth they think you to be of any importance, whether they themselves, or those whom you strive to bring over to your communion. But from the point at which he set forth the words of your letter, from that point it is to be considered whether he answered nothing to them. So, then, let us do: from that very place let us consider his writings.

Setting aside, then, the preface by which I wished to prepare the reader's mind, and passing over a few of his first words, abusive rather than pertinent to the matter: *He says,* I say: *For the conscience of the one giving is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving. What if the conscience of the one giving is hidden, and perhaps stained? How will it be able to wash the conscience of the one receiving, if, as he says, the conscience of the one giving is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving?*

*For if he says that whatever evil has been hidden in the conscience of the one giving does not pertain to the one receiving, that ignorance will avail perhaps for this, that the receiver, not knowing, cannot be stained by the conscience of his baptizer. Let it suffice, then, that another's stained conscience, when it is unknown, does not stain: but can it also wash? Whence, then, is he to be washed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one about to receive does not know this; especially since he adds and says: For he who has taken faith from a faithless man does not receive faith, but guilt*[^66]*?*

## Petilian does not confute Augustine's words.

**20.** 23. All these words Petilian set forth from my letter to be refuted: let us see, then, whether he refuted them, whether he answered at all. For I add the words which he slanderously claims I omitted, and repeat those very ones, even more briefly.

For by adding these two words, he has also helped me greatly toward the brevity of this proposition. If *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving,* and *he who knowingly takes faith from a faithless man does not receive faith, but guilt;* whence is the conscience of the one receiving washed, when he does not know the stained conscience of the one giving; and when, not knowing, he takes faith from a faithless man? I ask, whence is he washed?

Let him tell us, let him not go off into something else, let him not spread clouds before the ignorant. Finally, with however many and winding circuits interposed and gone through, let him at last at some time tell us whence the conscience of the one receiving is washed, when the stained conscience of the faithless baptizer is hidden, if *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving, and he who knowingly takes faith from a faithless man does not receive faith, but guilt.*

For, not knowing, he takes from a faithless man who does not have the conscience of one giving in holiness, but a stained and hidden one: whence, then, is he washed? Whence does he receive faith?

For if he is neither washed then, nor receives faith then, when the faithless and stained baptizer is hidden; why, when that man has afterward been betrayed and condemned, is he not baptized anew, that he may be washed and receive faith? But if, with that faithless and stained man hidden, he is washed and receives faith; whence is he washed, whence does he receive, where there is not the conscience of one giving in holiness, which washes that of the one receiving?

Let him say this, let him answer this: Whence is he washed, whence does he receive, if *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving;* and this is not there, when the stained and faithless baptizer was hidden? Nothing at all has been answered to this.

## Petilian shuffles, so as not to answer.

**21.** 24. But behold, hemmed in in the cause, he again makes a cloudy and windy onslaught against me, so that the serenity of the truth may be obscured; and he becomes copious out of the utmost poverty, not by saying true things, but by vomiting empty curses.

Hold most intently and most firmly, indeed, what he ought to answer, that is, whence the conscience of the one receiving is washed, when the stained conscience of the one giving is hidden: lest perhaps his blast shake this out of your hands, and you yourselves be swept away by the dark tempest of turbid speech, so that you do not at all know either whence the departure was made, or whither return is to be made.

And see the man wandering wherever he can, while he cannot stand firm for the business he undertook. See how many things he says, having nothing to say. He says *that I slip and am slippery, but am held: that I neither establish nor confirm what I object, that I devise uncertain things for certain, that I do not permit readers to believe what is true, but make them suspect the doubtful things more deeply.* He says *that I have the damnable talent of the Academic Carneades.*

He even tries to insinuate what the Academics hold about the falsity or deceptiveness of human sense, being utterly ignorant, even in these things, of what he is saying. He asserts *that it is said by them that snows are black when they are white, and that silver is black, that a tower seems round or smooth when it is square, that an oar in water is broken when it is whole.*

And all this for the reason that, when he had said: *The conscience of the one giving* or *of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which washes that of the one receiving;* I, on the contrary, said: *What if the conscience of the one giving is hidden, and perhaps stained?*

These are the black snows, and the black silver, and the round tower for the square, and the oar in water broken instead of whole! For I said a thing which can be supposed, and cannot be: that the conscience of the one giving is hidden, and perhaps stained.

## A certain citation of Terence.

**21.** 25. And he pursues himself, and cries out: *What is 'What if'; what is 'Perhaps'? except the uncertain and inconstant hesitation of one doubting, about which that man of yours says: What if I now return to those who say: What if the sky should now fall?*[^67].

Is this which I said: *What if the conscience of the one giving is hidden, and perhaps stained*; such as if I had said: *What if the sky should now fall?*

It was said, surely: *What if*; because it can be that it is hidden, it can be that it is not hidden: for when it is not known what he thinks, or what he has committed, the conscience of the one giving is certainly hidden from the one receiving; but when his sin is made manifest, it is not hidden.

It was said: *And perhaps stained*; because it can be that it is hidden and clean, and again it can be that it is hidden and stained. Therefore: *What if*; therefore: *Perhaps.* Is this like: *What if the sky should now fall?*

O how often have men been both convicted and confessed that they had stained and adulterous consciences, when the unwitting were baptized by them, after, the crime being made manifest, they were degraded, and yet the sky did not fall! What does Philus do here, and Furius, who defended injustice against justice?

What does the atheist Protagoras do here, who denied that God exists, so that of him the prophet seems to have foretold: *The fool said in his heart: There is no God*[^68]? What do these men do here? Why were they named, except to intervene for a man who has nothing to say; so that, while at least something is said about them without cause, the cause may seem to be conducted, and what has not been answered may be believed to have been answered?

## Augustine did not omit words from Petilian's text.

**22.** 26. Finally, if those two or three words: *What if,* and *Perhaps,* are so intolerable that on their account the long-sleeping Academics are roused, and Carneades, and Philus, and Furius, and Diagoras, and the black snows, and the falling of the sky, and the rest, likewise insane and absurd; let them be removed from the midst. For in truth, without them, what we wish to say can be said. See, it suffices, what was put down a little after, and interposed by him himself from my letter: *Whence, then, is he to be washed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one about to receive does not know this?*[^69] Surely here there is no: *What if,* and *Perhaps.* Let it be answered, then. Attend, lest perhaps in what follows he answer this: *But I,* he says, *bind you, cavilling, to the faith of believing, that you may wander no further. Why do you turn life away with arguments of folly, with errors? Why do you disturb the reason of faith about irrational matters? By this one word I hold and confute you.*

These things Petilian said, not I. These are from the letter of Petilian; but to it I added those two words which he charges me with having taken away, and nonetheless showed that the statement of my inquiry, to which he does not answer, stands fixed much more briefly and clearly. Surely these are those two, *in holiness,* and *knowingly*: so that it is not, forsooth, *the conscience of the one giving,* but *the conscience of the one giving in holiness*; and it is not, *he who takes faith from a faithless man, but he who knowingly takes faith from a faithless man.* Which words indeed I had not omitted, but had not found in the codex which had been given to me. But it can be that it was faulty; for neither is this incredible, that even from this an Academic resentment be roused against me, and that what I said—that the codex was faulty—be asserted to be such as if I said the snows are black. For why do I repay him a rash suspicion, so as to say that he himself afterward added what he pretended I had omitted; when the codex, which does not grow angry, can bear this mark of faultiness without my abusive rashness?

## More on these words.

**23.** 27. The first of these, namely *of one giving in holiness,* does not in the least hinder my inquiry, on which he is hard pressed: whether I should put it this way, If *the conscience of the one giving is regarded*, or rather this way, If *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving*; for then, how is the one who receives baptism to be cleansed, if the conscience of the one giving is defiled, and the one who is going to receive it does not know this?

As for the other word that is added, *knowingly,* so that it does not read, *For whoever receives faith from one who is faithless*, but rather, *Whoever knowingly receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt*;

I admit that I said certain things as though it were missing, the loss of which I bear easily, since they brought more delay to my ease than help to my argument.

For how much more readily, how much more plainly and briefly, do I put the question thus: If *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving*, and, *whoever knowingly receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt*, then by what is the one cleansed from whom the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness is hidden, and from what does the one receive true faith who is unknowingly baptized by one who is faithless?

Let it be said from what, and there the whole case of baptism will lie open, there everything that is in question will come to light—but only if it is said, not if the time is used up in cursing.

## Petilian speaks beside the point.

**24.** 28. Whatever, then, he slanderously alleges about these two words—either when they are taken away or when they are added, in which he glories—you see that it in no way hinders my question. Finding nothing to answer to it, yet so as not to be silent, he repeatedly rushes upon my person; I would say that he wanders away from the case, if he had ever come to the case at all. As though it were a matter of me, and not of the truth of the Church or of baptism, he says *that I argued by removing those two words, so that it would not tell against my conscience, as he puts it, that I was ignorant of the sacrilegious conscience of the one who, as he says, defiled me.*

But if that were so, the word that was set down, *knowingly,* would rather have helped me when added and harmed me when taken away. For if I had wanted to defend myself in such a way as to be thought ignorant of the conscience of the one who baptized me,

I would have taken Petilian to have spoken on my behalf, since he does not say merely, *Whoever receives faith from one who is faithless*, but, *Whoever knowingly receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt*—so that from this I might boast that I had received not guilt but faith, because I would say: I did not knowingly receive it from one faithless, but, being human, I was ignorant of the conscience of the man who gave it.

See, then, and count, if you can, how many superfluous things he says against the one word *I was ignorant,* which he sets forth as my having said, though I said no such thing at all—for it was not a matter of me, that I should say it, nor did any charge appear against the one who baptized me, such that I should be forced, in my own defense, to say that I was ignorant of his conscience.

## How Petilian evades.

**25.** 29. And yet, so as not to answer the things I have said, he sets before himself what I did not say; and he leads people away from attending to what he owes, so that what he must answer is not demanded. He frequently interposes, *You say, I was ignorant*; and he replies, *But if you were ignorant*; and he, as it were, refutes me, so that I may not be allowed to say, *I was ignorant.*

He recalls *Mensurius, Caecilian, Macarius, Taurinus, and Romanus*, and affirms *that they acted against the Church of God* in ways *that I could not be ignorant of, since I am an African, and now nearly an old man in age*—although, as I hear, Mensurius died in the unity of communion before the party of Donatus tore itself away, and I have read the case of Caecilian, that they brought it before Constantine, and that he was acquitted, both once and again, by the bishops whom that emperor had appointed as judges, and a third time by the emperor himself, when they appealed to him against them.

As for Macarius, Taurinus, and Romanus, whatever they did by judicial or executive power against the obstinate fury of those men on behalf of unity, it is established that they did it according to the laws—the very laws which those same men compelled to be enacted and applied against themselves by bringing the case of Caecilian to the emperor's judgment.

## Augustine was not condemned as a Manichee.

**25.** 30. Among many things that have nothing at all to do with the matter, he also says *that I was struck by a sentence of the proconsul Messianus, so that I fled from Africa;* and on account of this falsehood (which, if he did not himself invent it, he at least believed with ill will when malicious people invented it), he did not fear, with astonishing rashness, not only to say but even to write many other falsehoods consequent upon it;

whereas I came to Milan before Bauto was consul, and on the first of January, before so great an assembly and in the sight of so many people, I recited a panegyric in his honor, as consul, in keeping with my profession of rhetoric at the time; and from that sojourn I returned to Africa only after the death of the tyrant Maximus, while the proconsul Messianus heard the case of the Manichees after the consulship of Bauto, as the date of the proceedings inserted by Petilian himself shows. And if it were necessary to prove these things to those who doubt or who believe the contrary, I could bring forward as most reliable witnesses many men illustrious in the world for that whole period of my life.

## The real question is not answered.

**26.** 31. But why do we pursue these matters? Why do we both endure and cause superfluous delays? Are we going to find from this how the conscience of the one receiving is to be cleansed, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of the one giving; and from what he receives faith, who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless? When Petilian had set this before himself from my letter, as though to answer it, he said whatever else he pleased rather than what the case required.

How often did he say, *If you were ignorant*, as though I had said—what I never said—that I was ignorant of the conscience of the one who baptized me? And with his slanderous mouth he seemed to be doing nothing else than to prove that I was not ignorant of the evils of those among whom I was baptized and in whose communion I was joined—understanding well enough that ignorance would not make me guilty. See, then: if I were ignorant, as he so often repeated, I would without doubt be innocent of all those evils. From what, then, would I be cleansed, who, being ignorant of the conscience of one not giving in holiness, could in no way be hindered by his crimes?

From what would I receive faith, who am unknowingly baptized by one faithless? For it was not in vain that he so often repeated, *If you were ignorant,* except that he might not allow me to be thought innocent—indicating without doubt that no one's innocence is violated if he unknowingly receives faith from one faithless and is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness. Let him say, then, from what such people are cleansed, from what they receive faith and not guilt.

But let it not deceive you: let him say it; let him not, by saying much, say nothing, or rather, by saying nothing, say much. Then there is something that comes to mind and must not be passed over. If I am guilty for this reason, that I was not ignorant—to speak as he does—and the reason I was not ignorant is that I am both an African and now nearly an old man in age, then at least the children in the rest of the world should not be guilty, who by neither nation nor age could know these charges of yours, whether true or false; and yet, if they fall in with you, they are rebaptized without any regard.

## Augustine presses with his own questions.

**27.** 32. But now we are not asking about that; let him rather answer this (and to avoid being forced to answer it, he digresses most copiously):

from what is the conscience of the one receiving cleansed, who is ignorant of the stained conscience of the one giving, if *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving;*

and from what does he receive faith, who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless, if *whoever knowingly receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt.*

Setting aside, then, his curses, which he flung at me without any sound consideration, let us still attend, in case he should perhaps say in what follows the thing we are asking for. It is worth observing, too, how garrulously he set out my words, as though he were going to demolish and overturn them most easily. *But let us return,* he says, *to that argument of your fantasy, in which you fancied you could paint for me with words any baptizer you pleased. For it was fitting that you should imagine verisimilar things, you who do not see the truth.* These words of his own Petilian spoke first, intending to set forth my words. Then he added: *See, you say, the faithless one stands ready to baptize, but the one who is to be baptized is ignorant of his faithlessness*[^70].

He did not state my whole proposition and question, and soon he himself began to question me, saying: *Who is this, or from where did he spring up, whom you set forth? Why do you seem to yourself to see one whom you feign, so as not to see the one whom you ought most diligently to see and examine and approve? But because I understand that you are ignorant of the order of the sacrament, I tell you this briefly: you ought both to have examined the baptizer and to have been examined by him.*

What is it we were waiting for? That he should say from what the conscience of the one receiving is cleansed, who is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness, and from what he receives faith and not guilt, who unknowingly receives baptism from one faithless. See, we have heard that the baptizer ought to be most diligently examined by the one who wishes to receive faith and not guilt, so that he may find the conscience of one giving in holiness, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving.

For the one who has not examined him, and has unknowingly received from one faithless, by the very fact that he did not examine him and was ignorant of the stained conscience of the one giving, had nothing from which to receive faith, but guilt.

What help, then, was it that he added, as something great, the word *knowingly,* which he slanderously claimed I had removed? For when he refused to have it said this way—*Whoever receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt*—he seemed to have left some hope for the one who is ignorant.

But now, when he is asked from what he receives faith, who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless, he answered that the man ought to have examined the baptizer; without doubt he does not even permit the wretched man to be ignorant, since he finds nothing from which the man could receive faith unless he has placed his hope in the man baptizing him.

## A man's hope is not to be placed in men.

**28.** 33. This is what we shudder at in you, this is what the divine sentence condemns, crying out most truly and most plainly: *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^71]. This is what holy humility and apostolic love most openly forbids, when Paul cries out: *Let no one boast in men*[^72]. This is why the onslaught of empty slanders and bitter curses grows ever fiercer against us, so that, with the man as it were overthrown, no hope may remain for those to whom we minister the word of God and the sacrament, according to the stewardship entrusted to us. We answer them: *How long will you set yourselves upon a man?*[^73]

The venerable catholic society answers them: *Shall not my soul be subject to God? For from him is my salvation. For he himself is my God and my protector; I shall not be moved*[^74]. For what other cause was there for these men to depart from the house of God, except that they pretended they could not bear the vessels made for dishonor[^75], without which that house will not be until the day of judgment—although it appears, from the deeds done and recorded at the time, that this was rather their own doing, which they slanderously charged against others?

Concerning these vessels made for dishonor, lest on account of them he should be disturbed and depart from the great house, which belongs to one great householder, the servant of God—the good and faithful man, or the one who is to receive faith in baptism—says what I recalled a little before: *Shall not my soul be subject to God?* To God, indeed, not to man. *For from him is my salvation,* not from man.

But this man, so that he might not even then give the man to God to be cleansed and made clean, when the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness is hidden, and someone unknowingly receives faith from one faithless—*This,* he says, *I tell you briefly: you ought both to have examined the baptizer and to have been examined by him.*

## The example of John the Baptist contradicts Petilian.

**29.** 34. I beg you, attend to this: I ask from what the conscience of the one receiving is cleansed, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness, if the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving;

and from what he receives faith, who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless, if whoever knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt; and he answers me that both the baptizer and the one to be baptized must be examined.

And to prove this, about which there is no question, he brings forward the example of John, that those who asked him who he said he was examined him[^76]; and that he too examined those to whom he said: *Brood of vipers, who showed you how to flee from the wrath to come?*[^77] What has this to do with the matter? What has this to do with the case?

God had borne witness to John's most excellent holiness, with a most splendid prophecy preceding both when he was conceived and when he was born. But those men, already believing him to be holy, were asking which of the holy ones he said he was, or whether he himself was the holy one of holy ones, who is Christ Jesus. For so great was the grace attributed to him that whatever he said about himself was immediately believed. But if from this example it is asserted that any baptizer now must be examined, then whatever each one says about himself will have to be believed. And who is the feigned man, whom indeed the Holy Spirit flees, as it is written[^78], who does not want the best to be believed about himself, and who does not try to bring this about with whatever words he can? So then, when he has been asked who he is, and has answered that he is a faithful steward of God and has a conscience defiled by the stain of no crime, will this be the whole examination, or must his character and life also be looked into more carefully? Yes, indeed—but it is not written that those who questioned John in the desert of the Jordan as to who he was did this.

## This example does not pertain to the matter in question.

**30.** 35. Hence this example does not pertain at all to the matter in question. That apostolic sentence rather instills sufficient care, where it says: *Let them first be tested, and so let them serve, having no charge against them*[^79]. And since this is done carefully and customarily by almost everyone on both sides, how is it that so many reprobate men have been found after the time of their stewardship was carried out, except that human diligence is very often deceived, and that some who were at first good change for the worse?

Since these things happen so often that they let no one disregard or forget them, what is the point of Petilian's teaching us briefly and insultingly that the baptizer must be examined by the one to be baptized, when we are asking from what the conscience of the one receiving is to be cleansed, when the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness is hidden, if the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving? *Because I understand,* he says, *that you are ignorant of the order of the sacrament, I tell you this briefly: you ought both to have examined the baptizer and to have been examined by him.* What an answer!

In so many places so great a multitude of people surrounds him, people who were baptized by men who, though they seemed at first to be just and chaste, were afterward, when their crimes were laid bare, convicted and cast down;

and he thinks he can escape the force of this question, by which we ask from what the conscience of the one receiving is cleansed, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness, if the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving—because he briefly says that the baptizer must be examined.

Nothing is more wretched than not to consent to the truth, by which a person is so hemmed in that he can find no way out. We ask from whom he receives faith, who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless. The answer is: *He ought to have examined his baptizer.* So then, because he does not examine him and receives faith, even unknowingly, from one faithless, does he receive not faith but guilt?

Why, then, are those not baptized anew who are established to have been baptized, while they were still in hiding, by men afterward exposed and convicted?

## Some questions of Augustine.

**31.** 36. *And where is the word I added,* he says, *namely 'knowingly,' so that I would not say, 'Whoever receives faith from one faithless,' but, 'Whoever knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt'?*

Therefore the one who unknowingly received from one faithless received faith, not guilt; and so I ask from what he received it. Here, hemmed in, he answers: *He ought to have examined him.*

Granted, he ought to have; he did not, or was not able to. What is your judgment about him? Was he cleansed or not? If he was cleansed, I ask, from what? For the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness, of which he was ignorant, could not cleanse him. But if he was not cleansed, then command that he be cleansed. You do not so command; therefore he was cleansed. Say from what!

Say, at least, what he did not say. For I set forth the very words to which he could not reply. *See, the faithless one stands ready to baptize; but the one who is to be baptized is ignorant of his faithlessness. What do you think he will receive—faith or guilt?*[^80] What has been set forth thus far is enough: answer it, or look carefully at what he answered.

You will find his reproaches refuted. For he blames me, as though mocking, *because I set forth verisimilar things, I who do not see the truth.* And repeating my words, with the sentence cut in half, he says: *You say, see, the faithless one stands ready to baptize, but the one who is to be baptized is ignorant of his faithlessness.* Then he adds: *Who is this, or from where did he spring up?* As though there were one or two, and not everything full on both sides of such men.

Why does he ask me who this man is, or from where he springs up, and not rather look around and see that the churches are rare, whether in the cities or in the country, that do not have men detected in crimes and cast down from the clergy? While they were in hiding, and wished to seem good when they were evil, and to be thought chaste when they were adulterers, they were surely feigned; surely the Holy Spirit fled from them, as it is written[^81].

From the crowd of these men still lying hidden, then, sprang up that faithless one whom I set forth. Why does he ask me from where he sprang up, closing his eyes against so great a crowd, from which—if one considers only those who could be convicted and cast down—their noise is enough even for the blind?

## The case of Quodvultdeus.

**32.** 37. What of the fact that he himself put in his letter *that Quodvultdeus, convicted of two adulteries among you and cast out, was received by our people*? What then (and I would say this without prejudice to that man, who either proved or persuaded that his cause was good): when such men among you, not yet detected, baptize, what is received from them—faith or guilt?

Surely not faith, because they do not have the conscience of one giving in holiness, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving. But neither guilt, on account of that added word: *For whoever knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt.* And when people were baptized by these men, they certainly did not know what sort of men they were.

Accordingly, since they did not receive faith from them, because they did not have the conscience of one giving in holiness, and did not receive guilt either, because they were baptized not knowing but unknowing of their crimes, they remained without faith and without guilt.

They are not, then, in the number of such criminals. But neither can they be in the number of the faithful, since they could receive faith from them no more than guilt. Yet we see them reckoned by you in the number of the faithful, and none of you decides that they should be baptized, but you ratify the fact that they were baptized.

So they received faith; yet they did not receive it from those men, who did not have the conscience of one giving in holiness, which would cleanse the conscience of the one receiving. From what, then, did they receive it? This is what I am at pains over, this is what I urgently ask, this is what I most vehemently demand to be answered.

## Other passages of Petilian's letter are examined.

**33.** 38. Look now at Petilian, lest he answer this, or lest he be found unable to answer it: see how he wanders about emptily through his abuse of us, accusing and proving nothing; and where he perhaps tries to resist for his cause, as though pugnaciously, he is everywhere most easily overcome; and yet to this one thing that we ask, he answers nothing at all: *If the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving, from what is the one to be cleansed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one who is going to receive it is ignorant of this.*

For with these words recalled from my letter, he set forth my question and showed himself answering nothing. For when he had said the things I have already reviewed, in which I showed that he did not answer what I asked,

and when, hemmed in by great difficulties, he was forced to say that the baptizer must be examined by the one to be baptized, and the one to be baptized by the baptizer; and when he had tried to establish this by the example of John, in case he should perhaps find the most negligent or most ignorant hearers, he brought forward other testimonies from the Scriptures that do not pertain to the matter:

namely, that the eunuch said to Philip, *See, here is water; what prevents me from being baptized?*[^82] *because he knew,* he says, *that the lost are prevented*; and that Philip did not forbid him to be baptized because he had approved, from his reading, how he believed in Christ—as though Philip had forbidden Simon Magus.

And that the prophets feared to be deceived by a false baptism, and that Isaiah therefore said, *deceitful water, having no faithfulness*[^83], as though showing that among faithless men the water is deceitful—although it was not Isaiah but Jeremiah who said this of deceitful men, calling the people figuratively "water," which is most plainly shown in Revelation[^84]. And that David said, *Let not the oil of the sinner anoint my head*—although he said this of the flattery of a fawner deceiving with false praise, by which the head of the one praised swells into pride.

The words set down earlier in the same psalm open up this meaning. For he says thus: *The just man will correct me in mercy and reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head*[^85]. What is clearer than this sentence? What more evident? For he prefers to be reproved by the harsh correction of a just man, mercifully, so that he may be healed, rather than to be anointed by the soft flatteries of a fawner, lest he be puffed up.

## The testimony of the apostle Paul is brought forward by Petilian.

**34.** 39. Petilian also recalls that the apostle Paul warned us not to believe every spirit, but to test the spirits, whether they are from God[^86]—as though this diligence were applied so that now, before the time, the wheat might be separated from the chaff, and not rather so that the wheat might not be deceived by the chaff; or as though, indeed, if even a lying spirit said something true, it must be denied because a detestable spirit said it. Whoever thinks this is so mad that he might perhaps contend that Peter ought not to have said, *You are the Christ, the Son of the living God*[^87], because the demons had already said something of the kind[^88]. Since, then, the baptism of Christ, whether administered through an unjust or a just man, is nothing other than the baptism of Christ, the cautious and good believer must avoid the man's wickedness, not condemn the sacraments of God.

## The case of a certain bishop.

**34.** 40. In all these things, certainly, Petilian does not answer: if the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving, from what is the one to be cleansed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one who is going to receive it is ignorant of this.

A certain colleague of his from Thubursicu, named Cyprian, was caught with a most disgraceful woman in a brothel, was brought before Primianus of Carthage, and was condemned. This man, when he baptized, before he was detected and cast out, certainly did not have the conscience of one giving in holiness, which would cleanse the conscience of the one receiving.

From what, then, were those cleansed who today, now that he is condemned, are certainly not cleansed again? It would not have been necessary for me to name the man, except so that Petilian might not again say, *Who is this, or from where did he spring up?*[^89] Why did your people not examine this baptizer, as John, on Petilian's view, was examined? Or did they examine him too, as far as men could examine a man, but failed for a long time to find one who lay hidden by cunning pretense?

Was this man's water not deceitful, or is the oil of a fornicator not the oil of a sinner? Or is it as the Catholic Church says, and as is true—that the water and the oil belonged not to the one who administered them, but to the one whose name was invoked there? Why did those who were baptized by this feigned and hidden man not test the spirit, since it was not from God? *For the Holy Spirit of discipline flees the feigned man*[^90]. Or did the Spirit both flee him and yet not desert his own sacraments, although administered through him?

Finally, since you do not deny that those were cleansed whom, now that he is condemned, you do not trouble to have cleansed again,

see whether, amid so many clouds spread about in manifold ways, Petilian answers at any point from what these were cleansed, if the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving—a conscience which the hidden, unclean man could not have.

**35.** 41. Answering nothing, then, to what is so insistently asked of him, and next procuring for himself a broad scope of speech, he says: *Since both the prophets and the apostle feared these things cautiously, with what face do you say that for those who believe well the baptism of a sinner is holy?* As though I, or any Catholic, would say that the baptism is a sinner's baptism, which is given or received with a sinner administering it, and not the baptism of him in whose name the one believing is baptized.

From there he now runs off to Judas the traitor, and says against him whatever he can, bringing in the prophetic testimonies that were sent forth about him so long before—as though he would drench the Church of Christ, spread throughout the whole world, whose cause is at issue in this question, with the impiety of Judas the traitor; not considering that from this very thing he ought to have been admonished that it is no more to be doubted that the Church of Christ is the one spread among all nations, since this was truly prophesied so long beforehand, than it is to be doubted that Christ had to be betrayed by one of his disciples, since this too was likewise prophesied.

## On the baptism of the Maximianists.

**36.** 42. But clearly, after these things, when Petilian comes to that objection of ours—that they received the baptism of the Maximianists, whom they had condemned[^91]—although in setting forth this question he preferred to use his own words rather than mine (for we do not say that the baptism of sinners ought to benefit us, since we say it is the baptism not only of no sinners, but of no men at all, because we recognize that it is Christ's)—he set it forth thus: *But you persistently assert,* he says, *that the baptism of sinners ought to benefit you, on the ground that we too, as you said, preserve the baptism of the guilty whom we justly condemned.*

When he came to this question, as I said, all his strength, even his feigned combat, failed him. Where to go, by what way to get out, by what passage—searched out or contrived—to escape secretly, or by what force to break out, he did not find. *Although I shall show in the second book,* he says, *how great a difference there is between us and our people and you and yours, whom you call innocent—still, in the meantime, first rescue yourselves from the crimes of your colleagues, which you know, and then inquire into the case of those whom we cast out.* Is this how anyone answers? What man would answer thus, except one who opposes the truth, against which he finds nothing to answer? If, then, we too say the same words—In the meantime, first rescue yourselves from the crimes of your colleagues which you know, and then lay any charge against us concerning those whom you think evil among us—have we both won, or have we both been defeated?

Rather, he won for his Church and in his Church who taught us in his Scripture, *that no one should boast in man, and that he who boasts should boast in the Lord*[^92]. For see: we who say, with the word of truth, that the believer is justified not by the man by whom he is baptized, but by him of whom it is written, *To the one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness*[^93]—since we do not boast in man, and strive, with him giving the grace, when we boast, to boast in the Lord—how secure we are, whatever error or crime he could prove about certain men of our communion!

For among us whoever are evil either lie wholly hidden, or, being known to some, are tolerated for the sake of the good to whom they are unknown and before whom they cannot be convicted, for the sake of the bond of unity and peace, lest the wheat be rooted up along with the weeds; so they bear the burden of their own wickedness in such a way that no one shares in it with them except those whom their iniquity pleases. For we do not fear that those whom they baptize cannot be justified, since those believe in him who justifies the ungodly, so that their faith is reckoned as righteousness[^94].

## There are countless sinners throughout the whole world, who nevertheless do not contaminate the Church.

**37.** 43. Accordingly, among us neither the man he said was cast out by us for the crime of the Sodomites, with another appointed in his place, and then restored again to our college—not knowing what he is talking about—nor the man whom he recalls to have been a penitent among you—however their cases may or may not be defended—prejudge anything against the Church of God, which is spread among all nations and grows in the world until the harvest;

in which, if those you accuse are truly evil, they are no longer in the Church itself but in its chaff; but if they are good, then when you defame them with unjust accusations, they themselves are tested like gold, while you burn like chaff. Yet the Church is not stained by others' sins—the Church which, spread throughout the whole world according to the most faithful prophecies, awaits the end of the age as a shore, where, once it has been brought, it will be free of the bad fish, with which, within the same nets of the Lord, so long as it ought not impatiently to be separated from them, the inconvenience of nature could be borne innocently.

Nor, for all that, is ecclesiastical discipline neglected by the steadfast, diligent, and prudent stewards of Christ, where crimes are made manifest in such a way that they cannot be defended on any probable ground. Countless instances exist among those who were either bishops or clergy of some such rank, and who, now degraded, have either gone off to other lands out of shame, or crossed over to you yourselves or to other heresies, or are known in their own regions; the multitude of these, scattered across the lands, is so great that, if Petilian had thought about it with the rashness of his cursing reined in a little, he would by no means have fallen into so plainly false and empty a sentence, by which he thought he must say to us: *None of you is innocent, where no guilty person is condemned.*

## The Church condemns wicked overseers and ministers.

**38.** 44. For, to pass over others dwelling in various lands—for scarcely anywhere is this kind of man lacking, from whom it is clear that wicked overseers and ministers are wont to be condemned in the Catholic Church as well—he could have looked close at hand at Honorius of Mileu.

And as for Splendonius, whom he made a presbyter after he had been condemned as a deacon in the Catholic Church and rebaptized by him—whose condemnation in Gaul, sent to us by the brethren, our colleague Fortunatus there at Constantina set out to be read publicly, and whom afterward this same Petilian cast out, having experienced his dreadful treacheries—about this Splendonius, then, how could he fail to be reminded in what way evil men are degraded even in the Catholic Church?

I wonder on what precipice of rashness his heart stood when he dictated these things, where he dared to say: *None of you is innocent, where no guilty person is condemned.* Therefore the evil, mingled bodily but separated spiritually in the Catholic Church—both when they are unknown by the human condition and when they are condemned by the consideration of discipline—bear their own burdens.

And on this account those are secure, provided they do not share in their sins by imitation and consent, whoever are baptized through them with the baptism of Christ; for even if they were baptized by the best of men, they would be justified only by him who justifies the ungodly. For to those who believe in him who justifies the ungodly, faith is reckoned as righteousness[^95].

## The Donatists are defeated by the sentence by which the Maximianists were condemned.

**39.** 45. But you, when the Maximianists are brought up against you—condemned by the sentence of a council of three hundred and ten, attacked by that same council cited in so many proconsular and municipal records,

driven out of the basilicas they held by the orders of judges and the help of the cities, and then received back and honored by you, together with those whom they had baptized outside your communion, without any question of baptism—you find nothing to answer.

For you are defeated by a sentence—not the true one, but your own—by which you contend that within the same communion of the sacraments some perish by the crimes of others, and that each one is such as is the man by whom he was baptized: if guilty, guilty; if innocent, innocent.

And if these things are true, then—to pass over countless others—the crimes of the Maximianists at least, whose wickedness your people exaggerated in so numerous a council, even to a likeness of those whom the earth swallowed alive[^96], have ruined you. But if the crimes of the Maximianists have not ruined you, then the things you so believe are false, and much less could some unproven crimes or other of Africans have ruined the whole world. And on this account, as the Apostle writes, *each one bears his own burden*[^97]; and the baptism of Christ belongs to no one but Christ; and Petilian promises in vain that, regarding what we urge about the Maximianists, he will speak in the second book—thinking far too ill of human hearts, as though they would not understand that he has nothing to say.

## Without the love of unity the sacraments of Christ are not salutary.

**40.** 46. For if the baptism that Praetextatus and Felicianus administered in the communion of Maximian was theirs, why was it received by you, in those whom they baptized, as the baptism of Christ? But if it is truly Christ's, as it is, and yet could not benefit those who possessed it together with the wickedness of schism, what can you say that you conferred on those whom you received together with that same baptism, except that—through the bond of peace, the crime of wicked division being blotted out—they were not forced to receive the sacrament of the holy washing as though they did not have it, but that what they had, which was previously ruinous, began now to be useful to them?

Or if this was not conferred on them in your communion, because no such thing could be conferred on schismatics among schismatics, yet this is conferred on you in the Catholic communion: not that you may receive baptism as though it is lacking to you, but that the very baptism you received may benefit you. For all the sacraments of Christ are held, without the love of the unity of Christ, not for salvation but for judgment. But since it is your sentence, not the true one, that *through the baptism of some traitors or other the baptism of Christ has perished from the whole world,* it is fitting that you find nothing to answer concerning the baptism of the Maximianists that you took back.

## Petilian did not answer the question put by Augustine.

**40.** 47. See, then, and remember most vigilantly, how Petilian did not answer even those very things which he sets before himself in such a way as to seem to say something. But that other thing he long ago abandoned altogether, nor was he willing to tell us—since indeed he could not, nor will he ever, down to the end of his volume, say it—which he recalled from the first parts of my letter as though it were to be refuted.

For even with the two words added—which he boasted I had removed, as though they were his strongest defenses—he nonetheless lies undefended, finding nothing to answer when it is asked: *If the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving, from what is the conscience of that one to be cleansed who is ignorant of the polluted conscience of one not giving in holiness; and if anyone who knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt, from what is the one to receive faith and not guilt who is unknowingly baptized by one faithless*—to this it is manifest that he has answered nothing for a long while now, ever since he began to speak.

**40.** 48. Next he proceeded with his abusive mouth to the disparagement of monasteries and monks, even accusing me of having instituted this kind of life. What that kind of life is like he does not know at all; or rather, he pretends not to know what is most widely known throughout the whole world. Then, asserting that I had said Christ is the baptizer, he subjoined certain words from my letter, as though I had said from my own opinion what I said from yours and his own; and he inveighed with most copious savagery, as though against me for having said it—though that which he reproves is not my opinion but his own and yours, as I shall presently show clearly, as far as I am able.

Then he tried to teach us, with many superfluous words, that Christ does not baptize but that baptism is given in his name, together with the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit; and concerning that very Trinity he said either what he wished or what he could: *that Christ is the middle of the Trinity.* Then, from the name of the magicians Simon and Bar-Jesus, he heaped on us such insults as it pleased him.

Then, holding the case of Optatus of Thamugadi in suspense step by step, lest he be drenched with the odium of that man, he said that neither he nor his people had been able to pass judgment on him; and in that very point he charged us with the fact that Optatus had been pressed by my suggestions.

**41.** 49. Finally, he concluded his letter by exhorting and warning his own people not to be deceived by us, and lamenting concerning our people that we had made them worse than they had been.

These things, then, having been carefully considered and examined, it appears clearly enough from the very letter that he wrote that Petilian did not answer at all what I first put forward in my letter: if, as he says, *the conscience of the one giving*, or rather—to add what he thinks a great support—if *the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving*, from what is the one to be cleansed who receives baptism, when, if the conscience of the one giving is polluted, the one who is going to receive it is ignorant.

Hence it is not to be wondered at that a man resisting on behalf of falsehood, pressed against the straits of the truth coming upon him, gasped out insane reproaches rather than being willing to walk with that truth which cannot be overcome.

## On what ground Petilian did not answer.

**41.** 50. Now, then, I beg you, attend for a few moments, so that I may show you plainly what he feared, in order not to answer this, and may bring to light what he tried to obscure.

He could certainly have answered us most easily, when we ask from what the one is to be cleansed who receives baptism when the conscience of the one giving is polluted, and the one who is going to receive it is ignorant of this: From the Lord God; and he could have said with complete confidence: God indeed cleanses the conscience of the one receiving, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness.

But the man, who had already been driven by zeal for your sect to place the cleansing of the one receiving in the conscience of the one giving—since he had said, *For the conscience of the one giving*, or *of the one giving in holiness, is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving*—feared that a man might seem better baptized by a hidden evil man than by a manifest good man; for then he would be cleansed not by the conscience of a man giving in holiness, but by the most excellent holiness of God himself.

Fearing this, then—lest so great an absurdity, or rather madness, should follow him, that he would not know which way to flee—he was unwilling to say from what the conscience of the one receiving is to be cleansed, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness; and, throwing everything into confusion with quarrelsome clamor, he preferred to hide what was being asked of him rather than to answer this to the one asking, by which he would at once be choked—never, however, supposing that our letters could be read by people of sound heart, or that his own could be read by those who had read mine, to which he pretended to be replying.

## Christ is always the root, head, and origin of the baptized.

**42.** 51. For what I just said is set down most plainly in that very letter of mine, against which he said nothing in his writing; and what he did there, I ask you, attend for a little; and although you favor him and hate us, bear it, if you can, with an even mind.

For since in his earlier letter—to the first parts of which alone, the only parts that then came into our hands, I had first replied—he had so placed the hope of the man being baptized in the man baptizing that he said, *For everything consists of its origin and root, and if a thing does not have a head, it is nothing*[^98]; because, then, Petilian had said this, wishing the origin and root and head of the man being baptized to be understood as nothing but the man by whom he would be baptized, I added and said: *We therefore ask: when that faithless baptizer is hidden, if then the one whom he baptizes receives faith and not guilt, if then his baptizer is not his origin and root and head, who is the one from whom he receives faith? Where is the origin from which he arises? Where is the root from which he sprouts? Where is the head from which he begins? Or perhaps, when the one being baptized is ignorant that the baptizer is faithless, then Christ gives faith, then Christ is the origin and root and head*[^99]*?*

This, then, I both say now and cry out, as I cried out there too: *O human rashness and pride! Why do you not rather allow that Christ always gives faith, intending to make a Christian by giving it? Why do you not allow that Christ is always the origin of the Christian, that the Christian fixes his root in Christ, that Christ is the head of the Christian? For not even when spiritual grace is imparted to believers through a holy and faithful steward does the steward himself justify, but that one alone of whom it is said that he justifies the ungodly*[^100]. *Or was the apostle Paul the head and origin of those he had planted, or Apollos the root of those he had watered, and not rather he who had given them the increase, since the same Paul says: 'I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase; so neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase'*[^101]*? Nor was Paul himself their root, but rather he who says: 'I am the vine, you are the branches'*[^102]. *And how could he be their head, when he most plainly proclaims in many places that we, though many, are one body in Christ, and that Christ himself is the head of the whole body? Therefore, whether anyone receives the sacrament of baptism from a faithful or a faithless steward, let all his hope be in Christ, lest he be the cursed man who places his hope in man*[^103].

## If it were otherwise, a great absurdity would follow.

**43.** 52. These things I set down in the earlier letter, when I was replying to Petilian, both plainly and truly, I think. These things I have recalled now too, conveying and commending this: that our hope should in no way be in man, and that we should believe Christ, God, to be the cleanser and justifier of men, of those who believe in him who justifies the ungodly, so that their faith is reckoned as righteousness[^104]—whether the man who administers baptism be holy, or ungodly and feigned, whom the Holy Spirit flees[^105].

Then, next, I added how great an absurdity would follow if it were otherwise, and I said what I say now too: *Otherwise, if each one is reborn in spiritual grace such as is the one by whom he is baptized, and when the one who baptizes is a manifest good man, he himself gives faith, he himself is the origin and root and head of the one being born; but when the faithless baptizer is hidden, then each one receives faith from Christ, then he draws his origin from Christ, then he is rooted in Christ, then he glories in Christ as head—then it must be wished by all who are baptized that they have faithless baptizers and are ignorant of them.*

*For however good the baptizers they have, Christ is surely incomparably better, who will then be the head of the baptized, if the faithless baptizer is hidden. But if it is most insane to believe this—for Christ always justifies the ungodly, by making a Christian out of the ungodly; faith is always received from Christ; Christ is always the origin of the regenerated and the head of the Church—what weight do those words have, which empty readers do not consider for what they contain within, but only for how they sound?*[^106] These things I said then; these things are written in that letter of mine.

## How a good tree and a bad tree are to be understood.

**44.** 53. Then a little later, since he had said: *Since these things are so, brothers, what perversity can there be, that one who is guilty of his own crimes should make another innocent, when the Lord Jesus Christ says: 'A good tree makes good fruit, a bad tree makes bad fruit; do they gather grapes from thorns?'*[^107] *and again: 'Every good man brings forth good things from the good treasure of his heart, and every evil man brings forth evil things from the treasure of his heart'*[^108]*?*—by which words Petilian shows plainly enough that the man who baptizes is to be taken as the tree, and the one who is baptized as the fruit—to this I had answered: *If the good tree is the good baptizer, so that his fruit, the one whom he has baptized, is good, then whoever is baptized by an evil man, even one not manifest, will not be able to be good, for he has arisen from a bad tree. For a good tree is one thing, a hidden but nonetheless bad tree another*[^109]*.*

What else did I wish to be understood by these words, except what I had set down a little above: that the tree and its fruit are not the one who baptizes and the one who is baptized, but that the man is to be taken as the tree, and his work and life as the fruit, which the good man always has good, and the evil man evil—lest that absurdity follow, that a man baptized even by a hidden evil man should be evil, as the fruit of a tree that is hidden but nonetheless bad? Against which he answered nothing at all.

## Petilian deceives his hearers or readers and accuses Augustine.

**45.** 54. But lest he, or any of you, should say that when the one who baptizes is a hidden evil man, then the one whom he baptizes is not his fruit but the fruit of Christ, I immediately added what a mad error this opinion too entails, and I repeated, though in other words, what I had said a little before: *If, when the bad tree is hidden, whoever is baptized by it is born not from it but from Christ, then those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men*[^110].

Petilian, then, hemmed in by these so forceful straits, was silent about the earlier matters on which these depended, and recalled in his reply these absurd things that follow from his error, as though I had said them from my own opinion—things that were said in order that he might attend to how much evil followed his opinion, and might be forced to change it.

Practicing this deceit, then, upon his hearers or readers, and despairing altogether that what we wrote could be read, he began to inveigh against me gravely and impudently, as though I had held that it must be wished by all who are baptized that they have faithless baptizers and be ignorant of them, since, however good the ones they have, Christ is incomparably better, who will then be the head of the baptized if the faithless baptizer is hidden. Likewise, as though I had held that those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men;

whereas this remarkable madness was recalled by me for the very reason that it necessarily follows those who think with Petilian that the baptized man stands to his baptizer as the fruit stands to the tree from which it is born—good from a good tree, bad from a bad—since, when we tell them to answer whose fruit they think the baptized man is when he is baptized by a hidden evil man (since they do not dare to rebaptize him), they are forced to answer that he is then not the fruit of that hidden evil man, but the fruit of Christ; and on this account there follows for them what they do not want and what it is mad to hold: for if a man is then the fruit of the man who baptized him when he is baptized by a manifest good man, but when he is baptized by a hidden evil man he is then not that man's fruit but Christ's, then those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men.

## But there he shows by how great a force of truth he was overcome.

**46.** 55. When Petilian attributes this to me, as though I had held it, and inveighs against me gravely and vehemently, he himself surely demonstrates, by that very grave invective of his, how great a wickedness it is to hold such things; and on this account, whatever he wished to seem to have said against me on account of this opinion is found to have been said against himself, who is convicted of holding such things. For there he shows by how great a force of truth he was overcome, where he found no other way out than to feign that I had held what he himself holds. It is just as if those whom the Apostle reproves, because they said there is no resurrection of the dead, wished to accuse the same Apostle, since he said, *Neither has Christ risen*[^111], and that the preaching of the apostles is empty, and the faith of believers also empty, and that they who had said that he raised up Christ are found to be false witnesses against God. This is what Petilian wished to do to me, despairing that what I wrote could be read—what he could not answer—and greatly desiring to be thought to have answered.

But just as, if anyone had done that to the Apostle, then when the whole passage itself was recited from his letter, and the preceding words restored from which whoever reads understands that those things depend, all that slanderous reproach would be hurled back into the face of the reprovers; so, when the preceding words are restored from my letter, while Petilian accuses me, they are thrown back with greater force into his own face, from which he tried to remove them.

## How Paul corrected the error about the resurrection of the dead.

**46.** 56. For the Apostle, refuting those who denied the resurrection of the dead, corrects them by that absurdity which follows, against their will, those who hold such things, so that, while they shudder at what it is wicked to say, they may correct what they dared to believe. Accordingly he says thus: *If there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ has not risen; but if Christ has not risen, our preaching is empty, and your faith also is vain; and we are even found to be false witnesses of God, because we have testified against God that he raised up Christ, whom he did not raise up*[^112]—so that, while they fear to say that Christ has not risen, and the other evil and wicked things that follow, they may correct what they said foolishly and faithlessly, that there is no resurrection of the dead.

If, then, you take away what is set at the head of this reasoning, *If there is no resurrection of the dead*, the rest is wrongly said and must be attributed to the Apostle. But if you restore what they depend on and set it at the beginning, *There is no resurrection of the dead*, then there rightly follow: *Neither has Christ risen*, and: *our preaching is empty, your faith also is empty*, and the other things that are connected; and all these were said well and prudently by the Apostle, since whatever evil they contain is to be imputed to those who denied the resurrection of the dead.

So too in my letter: take away what is set down, *If each one is reborn in spiritual grace such as is the one by whom he is baptized, and when the one who baptizes is a manifest good man, he himself gives faith, he himself is the origin and root and head of the one being born; but when the faithless baptizer is hidden, then each one receives faith from Christ, then he draws his origin from Christ, then he is rooted in Christ, then he glories in Christ as head*; take away these things, on which what follows depends, and then it is very badly said and must be attributed to me: *It must be wished by all who are baptized that they have faithless baptizers and be ignorant of them. For however good the ones they have, Christ is surely incomparably better, who will then be the head of the baptized, if the faithless baptizer is hidden.*

But let those things which you say be restored, and then this which depends on them and follows, bound to them, is found not to be held by me, and whatever evil it contains is turned back upon your opinion. Likewise, take away what is set down: *If the good tree is the good baptizer, so that his fruit, the one whom he has baptized, is good, and if, when the bad tree is hidden, whoever is baptized by it is born not from it but from Christ*; take away these things, which you are forced to confess both from your sect and from Petilian's letter, and that insane thing which follows will be mine and must be imputed to me: *Those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men*. But restore those things on which this depends, and you will at once see both that I rightly set this down to correct you, and that the whole of what deservedly displeases you in this opinion has fallen back upon your own face.

## The Donatists are like those whom Paul refutes.

**47.** 57. Accordingly, just as those who denied the resurrection of the dead could in no way defend themselves from the great evils that the Apostle connected in order to refute them, saying, *Neither has Christ risen*[^113], and the other wicked things likewise, unless they changed their opinion and confessed that there is a resurrection of the dead; so too you, if you do not want to have imputed to you what we say in order to convict and correct you—that those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men—change your opinion, and do not place in man the hope of those who are baptized.

For if you do place it there, see what I say, lest someone again suppress this and say that I held what I recall in order to refute and correct you. See what I say, on what depends what I am about to say: If you place the hope of those who are to be baptized in the man by whom they are baptized; if you make the man baptizing, as Petilian wrote, the origin and root and head of the one who is baptized; if you take the good man baptizing as the good tree, and his good fruit as the one who has been baptized by him—then you prompt us to ask of you: from what origin does the one whom a hidden evil man baptizes arise, from what root does he sprout, to what head is he joined, from what tree is he born? For to this inquiry belongs also that question to which I have so very often noted that Petilian did not answer: From what is the one cleansed who receives baptism, when he is ignorant of the stained conscience of one not giving in holiness? For he wants this conscience of the one giving, or of the one giving in holiness, to be the origin, root, head, seed, tree, from which the sanctification of the baptized arises, is propagated, begins, sprouts, is born.

## The Donatists are urged to abandon their opinion.

**48.** 58. When, therefore, we ask from what the one was cleansed whom you do not rebaptize in your communion, even when it is established that he was baptized by someone who, on account of hidden wickedness, no longer had the conscience of one giving in holiness, what are you going to answer except: By Christ, or by God—although Christ too is God over all, blessed forever[^114]—or by the Holy Spirit, since he too is God, because this Trinity, a unity, is God? Hence Peter, when he had said to the man, *You have dared to lie to the Holy Spirit*, at once went on to add what the Holy Spirit is, and said, *You have not lied to men, but to God*[^115].

Finally, even if you say that he is cleansed and made clean by an angel, when he is ignorant of the polluted conscience of one not giving in holiness, see that of holy men, when they have risen to eternal life, it is then said that they will be equal to the angels of God[^116]. Whoever, then, is cleansed even by an angel, is cleansed better than if he were cleansed by the conscience of any man whatever. Why, then, do you not want it to be said to you: If a man cleanses when he is a manifest good man, but when he is a hidden evil man—since he does not have the conscience of one giving in holiness—it is no longer he himself, but God or an angel that cleanses, then those who are baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men? And if this opinion displeases you, since indeed it ought to displease everyone, take away the things from which it is born, correct the things to which it is bound; for then those do not go before, and this does not follow.

## In baptism Christ sanctifies not by a bodily ministry, but by the invisible work of his majesty.

**49.** 59. Do not say, then: *The conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving*; lest it be said to you: When the polluted conscience of the one giving is hidden, who cleanses the conscience of the one receiving?

And when you have answered, Either God or an angel (since you have nothing else to answer), there follows you something by which you are confounded: Those who are baptized by hidden evil men, so as to be cleansed either by God or by an angel, are more holily justified than those who are baptized by manifest good men, who are not compared to God or to the angels.

But say what the Truth and the Catholic Church say: that not only when the minister of baptism is evil, but even when he is holy and good, hope is not to be placed in man, but in him who justifies the ungodly, in whom faith is reckoned as righteousness to those who believe[^117]. For when we say, Christ baptizes, we do not say it of a visible ministry, as Petilian supposes, or wishes us to be supposed to say; but of a hidden grace, a hidden power in the Holy Spirit, as was said of him by John the Baptist: *This is he who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^118].

Nor, as Petilian says, has he now ceased to baptize; but he still does it, not by a bodily ministry, but by the invisible work of his majesty. For when we say, He himself baptizes, we do not say, He himself holds and dips the body of believers in the water; but, He himself invisibly cleanses, and this the whole Church entirely. For the apostle Paul is not to be disbelieved, who said of him: *Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word*[^119]. See, then, that Christ sanctifies; see that Christ, even by that very washing of water in the word, where the ministers are seen to work bodily, he himself cleanses, he himself makes clean.

Let no one, then, arrogate to himself what is God's; the hope of men is sure in this way, when it is fixed on him who cannot deceive; for *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^120]; and: *Blessed is the man whose hope is the Lord God*[^121]. For the faithful steward will receive as his reward eternal life; but as for the unfaithful steward, when he distributes the Lord's provisions to his fellow servants, far be it that through his own unfaithfulness he should make the provisions useless. For, *What they say,* he says, *do; but what they do, do not do*[^122]. This was commanded against evil stewards for this reason: that the good things of God may be received through them, while their own evil life is avoided by being unlike it.

## Petilian does not touch all of Augustine's words.

**50.** 60. But if it is manifest that Petilian did not answer these first words of my letter, and that where he tried to answer, he rather showed that he could not answer, what shall I say about those parts of my writings which he did not even attempt to answer, which he did not touch at all? Yet if anyone who has both my writings and his own wishes to review what sort they are, I think he will understand by how great a firmness they are strengthened. To show you this briefly, recall, or review by reading, the very testimonies brought forth from the holy Scriptures, which he set down as though against us, and those which I too set down in my reply against you; and see how I showed that the things he set down are contrary not to us but rather to you, while he did not touch at all the things I set down as most necessary; and in that one passage of the Apostle which he tried to treat as though on his own behalf, you will see how he found no way out[^123].

## Petilian's propositions are reviewed.

**50.** 61. For the part of the letter that he wrote to his own people, from the beginning up to the place where he says, *This the Lord commands us: 'When men have persecuted you in this city, flee to another; and if they persecute you in that one too, flee to another'*[^124], first came into our hands; to this we replied; and when our reply had also come into his hands, he wrote this one against it, which we are now refuting and showing that he did not answer ours.

In that first part, then, of his writings, to which we first replied, these are the testimonies of the Scriptures which he thought were against us: *A good tree makes good fruit, and a bad tree makes bad fruit; do they gather grapes from thorns?*[^125] and again: *Every good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and every evil man brings forth evil things from the treasure of his heart*[^126]; and again: *If one is baptized by a dead man, his washing profits him nothing*[^127].

By these testimonies he wished to show that the one who is baptized becomes such as was the one by whom he is baptized. I, on the contrary, showed how these testimonies were to be understood, and that they in no way helped his purpose. And as for the rest, which he set down as said against evil and wicked men, I taught well enough that they are not against the Lord's wheat—which, as was foretold and promised, is spread throughout the whole world—and that they can rather be said by us against you. Review them, and you will find it so.

## Augustine's propositions are taken up again.

**50.** 62. But the things I set down in support of the Catholic Church's assertion are these: regarding baptism, that the fact that we are regenerated, cleansed, and justified by the grace of God is not to be attributed to the man giving it: *It is better to trust in the Lord than to trust in man*[^128]; and: *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^129]; and that *salvation belongs to the Lord*[^130]; and: *Vain is the salvation of man*[^131]; and that *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^132]; and that he in whom one believes justifies the ungodly, so that his faith is reckoned as righteousness[^133].

And for the sake of the unity of the Church itself, which is spread among all nations, and with which you have no communion, I set down these testimonies as having been foretold of Christ: that *he will rule from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^134]; and: *I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession*[^135]. And that the covenant of God made with Abraham is recited on behalf of our communion, that is, the catholic one, where it is written: *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^136]; which seed the Apostle interprets, saying: *And to your seed, which is Christ*[^137]. From which it appears that in Christ not only the Africans or Africa, but all the nations, through whom the Catholic Church is spread, were to have the blessing promised so long beforehand. And that the chaff is with the wheat until the final winnowing, lest anyone, by the slander of others' crimes, should excuse the sacrilege of his own separation, in that he has left and deserted the communion of all the nations.

And lest, on account of evil stewards—that is, overseers—the Christian society be divided, I also set down that testimony: *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do; for they say and do not do*[^138].

These testimonies from the holy Scriptures, set down by me, he neither showed how they ought to be understood otherwise—so as to show that they are not for us nor against you—nor was he willing to touch them at all; rather, he busied himself with the tumult of his reproaches, so that, if it could be done, it might come into no one's mind at all that these things were said by me, of anyone who, after reading my letter, also wished to read his own.

## The argument drawn from Paul's letter is turned back against Petilian.

**51.** 63. But as for what he tried to treat on his own behalf, drawn by me from the writings of the apostle Paul, attend for a moment to what sort it is. *For you said,* he says, *The apostle Paul rebukes those who said they belonged to the apostle Paul, and says: 'Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?'*[^139].

*Therefore, if those who wished to belong to Paul were in error, and would perish unless they were corrected, what hope at last is there for those who wished to belong to Donatus? For these men act in such a way that the origin and root and head of the baptized is none but the one by whom he is baptized.*

These words and this testimony of the Apostle he recalled from my letter, and set before himself to be refuted. Accordingly, see whether he answered to the point. For he says: *This is an empty saying, inflated and childish and foolish, and far removed altogether from the account of our faith.*

*For you would rightly say this if we said: We were baptized in the name of Donatus; or: Donatus was crucified for us; or: We were baptized in our own name. But since these things are neither said nor are said by us, because we follow the form of the divine Trinity, it is certain that you, who lay such things to our charge, are mad. Or if you think that we were baptized in the name of Donatus, or in our own name, you are ruinously deceived, and at the same time you sacrilegiously confess concerning yourselves that you defile your wretched people in the name of Caecilian*[^140]*.*

These things Petilian answered against those words of mine, not attending—or rather clamoring lest it be attended to by anyone—that he had answered nothing at all that pertained to the matter in question.

For who does not see that this testimony of the Apostle was the more rightly set down by us, precisely because you do not say that you were baptized in the name of Donatus, nor that Donatus was crucified for you, and yet on account of the party of Donatus you separate yourselves from the communion of the Catholic Church—just as those whom Paul reproved certainly did not say that they had been baptized in the name of Paul, or that Paul had been crucified for them, and yet were making a schism over the name of Paul?

Just as, then, to those for whom Christ, not Paul, was crucified, and who had been baptized in the name of Christ, not in the name of Paul, and yet said, *I belong to Paul*, it is for this reason the more rightly said, *Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?*—that they may cleave to him who was crucified for them, and in whose name they were baptized, and not be divided over the name of Paul; so to you also, much more, because you do not say, We were baptized in the name of Donatus, and yet wish to belong to the party of Donatus, it is fittingly said: Was Donatus crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Donatus? For you know that Christ was crucified for you, and that you were baptized in the name of Christ, and yet, on account of the name and party of Donatus, you resist with such obstinacy the unity of Christ, who was crucified for you, and in whose name you were baptized.

## Augustine takes up Petilian's propositions again.

**52.** 64. But that Petilian did this by his writings, *that the origin and root and head of the baptized should be none but the one by whom he is baptized*, and that this was said by me neither emptily, nor childishly, nor foolishly, review the opening of his letter, to which I then replied, and you will see. Rather, attend diligently while I recall it. *The conscience,* he says, *of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving; for whoever knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt.*

And, as though it were said to him, How do you prove this? *For everything,* he says, *consists of its origin and root; and if a thing does not have a head, it is nothing; nor does anything regenerate well unless it is regenerated by good seed*. *Since these things are so, brothers, what perversity can there be, that one who is guilty of his own crimes should make another innocent, when the Lord Jesus Christ says: 'A good tree makes good fruit, a bad tree makes bad fruit. Do they gather grapes from thorns?'*[^141] *and again: 'Every good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and every evil man brings forth evil things from the treasure of his heart'*[^142]*; and again: 'If one is baptized by a dead man, his washing profits him nothing'*[^143].

You see what all these aim at: namely, that the conscience of the one giving in holiness (lest anyone receiving from one faithless should receive not faith but guilt) should itself be the origin, and root, and head, and seed of the baptized. For wishing to prove that the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving, and that the one who knowingly receives faith from one faithless receives not faith but guilt, he at once added: *For everything consists of its origin and root; and if a thing does not have a head, it is nothing; nor does anything regenerate well unless it is regenerated by good seed.*

And lest anyone be so slow that he still did not understand that he was speaking of the one by whom each is baptized, he explains this in what follows, and says: *Since these things are so, brothers, what perversity can there be, that one who is guilty of his own crimes should make another innocent, when the Lord Jesus Christ says: 'A good tree makes good fruit; do they gather grapes from thorns?'* And lest the hearer or reader, blind with an incredible dullness of heart, should still not see that he is speaking of the man baptizing, he adds another text where he even names the man: *And again,* he says, *'Every good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and every evil man brings forth evil things from the treasure of his heart'; and again: 'If one is baptized by a dead man, his washing profits him nothing.'*

Surely it is now manifest, surely it now needs no interpreter or disputant or demonstrator, that these men aim at this: that the origin and root and head of the baptized should be none but the one by whom he is baptized; and yet, overpowered by the force of truth, and as though forgetful of what he had said, Petilian afterward concedes to me that Christ is the origin and root of the regenerated and the head of the Church, and not any man who is the steward and minister of baptism.

For when he had said that the apostles baptized in the name of Christ, and laid Christ as the foundation, that they might make Christians, and proved this by the testimonies and examples of the holy Scriptures, as though we denied it, he says: *Where now is that voice with which, rattling on with tiny and frequent little questions, you said many involved things about Christ and for Christ and in Christ, against human rashness and pride, enviously and haughtily? See, Christ is the origin of the Christian, Christ is the head, Christ is the root.* Hearing these things, what should I do but give thanks to Christ himself, who forced the man to confess it?

False, then, are the things he said at the opening of his letter, when he wished to persuade us that the conscience of the one giving in holiness is regarded, which cleanses the conscience of the one receiving; and that when anyone knowingly receives faith from one faithless, he receives not faith but guilt.

For wishing as it were to show how much lies in the man baptizing, he had subjoined, as though great proofs, saying: *For everything consists of its origin and root; and if a thing does not have a head, it is nothing.* But afterward, when he says what we too say—*See, Christ is the origin of the Christian, Christ is the head, Christ is the root*—he cancels what he had said before, *that the conscience of the one giving in holiness is the origin and root and head of the one receiving.* The truth, then, has prevailed, so that the man who desires the baptism of Christ should not place his hope in the man who is the minister, but should come securely to Christ himself as to an origin that is not changed, a root that is not torn up, a head that is not cast down.

## What the minister does in baptism, according to Petilian and according to Augustine.

**53.** 65. And now, who would not notice from how inflated a vein it comes, that when he was as it were expounding the Apostle's sentence, he said: *He who said, 'I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase'*[^144]*, what else did he say but: I made a man a catechumen in Christ, Apollos baptized, God confirmed what we did?* Why, then, did Petilian not add what the Apostle added, and what I especially commended, so that he might expound this too for us: *Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^145]? And if he wished to expound it according to what he set down above, it follows without doubt that neither he who makes a catechumen, nor he who baptizes, is anything, but God who gives the increase.

For what does it now matter to the question how it was said, *I planted, Apollos watered*—whether it is really to be taken as though he had said, *I made a catechumen, Apollos baptized*, or whether there is another truer and more fitting meaning? See, meanwhile, according to his own exposition, neither he who makes a catechumen, nor he who baptizes, is anything, but God who gives the increase. But there is a great difference between confirming what another does and doing it. For he who gives the increase does not confirm the tree or the vine, but creates it.

For by that increase it comes about that even the planted wood produces and fixes a root; by that increase it comes about that the seed that is cast forth sends up a shoot. But why should we discourse on this at greater length? It is enough that, according to him, neither he who makes a catechumen, nor he who baptizes, is anything, but God who gives the increase. But when would Petilian say this, so that we might understand him to be saying: Neither is Donatus of Carthage anything, nor Januarianus, nor Petilian? When would that swelling allow it, by which a man thinks himself to be something when he is nothing, and deceives himself[^146]?

**54.** 66. Finally, a little later too, when he set himself and intended to handle again, as it were, those words of the Apostle that we had brought up, he was unwilling to set down these words which I had said, but others in which human inflation could somehow draw breath. *For, that we may unfold again,* he says, *the words of that Apostle which you had brought up against us, he said: 'What then is Apollos, or what is Paul? Ministers of him in whom you believed'*[^147]. *What else, for example, does he say to all our people but: What is Donatus of Carthage, what is Januarianus, what is Petilian, except ministers of him in whom you believed?*

I did not set down this testimony of the Apostle, but that one which he was unwilling to recall, I set down: *Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^148]*.* But he chose to insert those words of the Apostle where he asks what Paul is, or what Apollos is, and answers, *Ministers of him in whom you believed.* This the swollen neck of heresy could somehow bear; but that other text, where he did not ask and answer what he was, but said that he is not anything, it could not bear at all. But now I wish to ask whether the minister of Christ is not anything.

Who would say this? How, then, is it true: *Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*—except that with respect to one thing he is something, with respect to another he is not anything? With respect to ministering and dispensing the word and the sacrament, he is something; but with respect to cleansing and justifying, he is not anything; for this is not worked in the inner man except by him through whom the whole man was created, and who, remaining God, was made man—namely, he of whom it was said: *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^149]; and: *To the one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly*[^150]. This testimony Petilian chose to set down among my words, but in his own he neither treated nor touched it.

## Even evil ministers, such as Judas, can baptize.

**55.** 67. The minister, then—that is, the steward of the word and the gospel sacrament—if he is good, becomes a partner of the gospel; but if he is evil, he is not for that reason not a steward of the gospel. For if he is good, he does this willingly; but if he is evil—that is, seeking his own interests, not those of Jesus Christ—he does it unwillingly, for the sake of other things that he seeks. Yet see what the same Apostle said: *If, then, I do this willingly,* he says, *I have a reward; but if unwillingly, a stewardship has been entrusted to me*[^151]. As though he said: If I am good and announce the good, I myself also arrive; but if I am evil, I announce the good. For did he say: If I do it unwillingly, I shall not be a steward?

Peter and the other good men announced it willingly, Judas unwillingly, yet, sent with them, he announced it. They have a reward; to him a stewardship was entrusted. But those who received the gospel from all of them announcing it could be cleansed and justified not by the planter or the waterer, but by him who gives the increase. For we are not going to say that Judas did not baptize, when he was still among the disciples, when that was being done which is written: *He himself did not baptize, but his disciples*[^152].

Or because he had not yet betrayed Christ—he who held the money box and carried off what was put into it[^153], and who could not be innocent as a keeper of money—was he yet a steward of grace without loss to those receiving? Or, if he did not baptize, at least you confess that he evangelized.

And if you reckon this small and slight, see what you think of the apostle Paul himself, who said: *For Christ did not send me to baptize, but to evangelize*[^154].

To this is added that Apollos, who watered by baptizing, begins to be superior to Paul, who planted by evangelizing—although Paul claims for himself the office of father toward the Corinthians on this account, and does not concede this name to those who came to them after him. For he says: *Though you have ten thousand guides in Christ, yet not many fathers; for in Christ Jesus, through the gospel, I begot you*[^155]. He says, *I begot you*, to those to whom in another place he says: *I thank God that I baptized none of you, except Crispus and Gaius and the household of Stephanas*[^156]. He had begotten them, then, not through himself, but through the gospel.

And even if he were seeking his own interests, not those of Jesus Christ, and did this unwillingly, without a reward of his own, he would nonetheless be dispensing the Lord's money; which, though he himself were evil, he would not make evil or useless to those who received it well.

## Much more the good ministers.

**56.** 68. And if this is rightly said of the gospel, how much more is it to be said of baptism, which so pertains to the gospel that without it indeed no one arrives at the kingdom of heaven, but only if righteousness is added to the sacrament? For he who said, *Unless one is reborn of water and the Spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^157], the same one said: *Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^158].

The form of the sacrament is given through baptism, the form of righteousness through the gospel. The one without the other does not lead to the kingdom of heaven. Nevertheless, even the less learned can baptize perfectly, but to evangelize perfectly is a much more difficult and rarer work. Therefore the teacher of the gentiles, more excellent than very many, was sent to evangelize, not to baptize; since the latter could be done by many, the former by few, among whom he was eminent. And yet we read that in several places he said, *my gospel*[^159]; but he never said, my baptism, nor that of anyone through whom it was administered. For only that baptism which John gave was called the baptism of John[^160].

That man received this as the chief gift of his stewardship, that the forerunning sacrament of the washing should be called even his, through whom it was dispensed; but the baptism that the disciples of Christ administered was called the baptism of none of them, so that it might be understood to belong to him of whom it was said: *Christ loved the Church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word*[^161]. If, then, the gospel, which so belongs to Christ that even the minister can call it his own on account of the gift of stewardship, a man can receive even through an evil steward without danger—doing what he says, but not doing what he does—how much more can anyone receive the baptism of Christ, which none of the apostles administered in such a way as to dare to call it his own, without the contagion of the evil minister, if he comes to Christ in good faith?

## On certain true traitors.

**57.** 69. Accordingly, if Petilian, when I did not omit to demonstrate that the testimonies he set down from the Scriptures were not against us, himself partly did not touch at all the things I set down, and partly, in what he chose to treat, showed nothing other than that he could not get out of it, you need not be long exhorted or admonished to see what you ought to hold and what to avoid. But perhaps he appeared able in the testimonies of the holy Scriptures, and prevailed somewhat in those records of the proceedings carried out among men concerning the schism itself?

Rather, in these too—although after the divine testimonies they are sought superfluously—what did he recall or what did he prove? When he had inveighed gravely against the traitors, and had proclaimed many testimonies against them even from the holy Books, he yet said nothing by which he might show us to be traitors. But I recalled that Silvanus of Cirta—whom, with certain men intervening, he himself also succeeded—was set down as a traitor in the municipal records[^162], while he was still a subdeacon. Against this he dared to mutter nothing.

And surely you see how great a constraint compelled him to answer—namely, that he should show his predecessor, and not only his associate but, so to speak, his very fellow occupant of the chair, to be innocent of the crime of betrayal—especially since you place your whole cause in this, that you call traitors those whom you either feign or suppose to have succeeded traitors along the line of communion.

He, then, who by the very necessity of your cause—if I were to say that someone of Rusiccada or of Calama or of any other city of your party was shown to be a traitor in the municipal records—would in every way be forced to defend him, kept silent about his own predecessor. Why, except that here he found no fog to spread, by which he might at least deceive men of the slowest and most somnolent mind? For what would he say, except that these things are falsely alleged about Silvanus? But we recite the records, both when it was done and when it was also brought before the consular Zenophilus.

How could he resist these things, hedged about on every side—the cause of the Catholic Church being the best, yours the worst? Hence I recall these words from that letter of mine, to which he wished to seem to have replied through this one that I am now refuting—precisely so that you may see how invincibly it was set down, against which he could find nothing safer than silence.

## Petilian answered nothing at all to Augustine's question.

**58.** 70. For when he had set down from the gospel a testimony as though against us, where the Lord says, *They will come to you in the clothing of sheep, but within they are ravening wolves; by their fruits you will know them*[^163], I replied and said: *Let us consider the fruits, then*; and at once I subjoined and added: *You charge us with betrayal; this very thing we charge against you with much more probability. And, not to run through many cases: in that same city of Constantina your elders ordained Silvanus as bishop at the very beginning of their schism. This man, while he was still a subdeacon, was set down as a most manifest traitor in the municipal records.*

*If you too bring forward any documents against our elders, a fair condition is demanded: that we believe either both true or both false. If both are true, you are without doubt guilty of schism, who pretended to flee, in the communion of the whole world, the crimes you had in that very fragment of your splitting-off. But if both are false, you are without doubt guilty of schism, who, on account of false crimes of traitors, are stained with the monstrous crime of separation. But if some are brought forward by us and none by you, or true ones by us and false by you, it need not be discussed how utterly your mouths are closed.*

*What if the holy and true Church of Christ should convict and overcome you, even if we held no documents of betrayal, or false ones, while you held some and true—what would then remain for you, but that, if you are willing, you should love peace; but if you are not willing, you should at least fall silent?*

*For whatever you might now bring forward, I would say most easily and most truly that you ought then to have proved it to the plenary and catholic unity of the Church, now spread and established among so many nations, so that you might be within, while those whom you convicted were driven out.*

*But if you tried to do this, without doubt you could not prove it; and, conquered, or in anger, you separated yourselves by a monstrous sacrilege from the innocent, who could not condemn what was uncertain.*

*But if you did not even try to do this, then with a most execrable and impious blindness you cut yourselves off from the wheat of Christ—which grows throughout the whole field, that is, the whole world, until the end—offended by a few weeds in Africa.*

To these things, which I have recalled from that earlier letter of mine, Petilian answered nothing at all. And surely you see that in these few words the whole cause that is argued between us is contained. For what would he try to say, where, whatever he chose, he would be defeated?

## Another refutation of Petilian.

**58.** 71. For when documents concerning traitors are brought forward both by us against your people and by you against ours (if indeed any are brought forward by you—which to this day we know nothing of at all; for Petilian, who so diligently took care to recall and insert against me those parts of the proceedings that pertain to the cause, would not have failed to insert them in his writings); nevertheless, as I had already begun to say, if such things are brought forward both by us and by you, then surely either both are true, or both false, or ours true and yours false, or ours false and yours true: nothing more can be said.

## Conclusions.

**59.** 72. But in all these four cases the truth is on the side of the Catholic communion. For if both are true, then on account of men such as you too had, you ought by no means to have deserted the communion of the whole world. If both are false, then on account of no crimes of betrayal, the most atrocious crime of division was to be avoided.

If ours are true and yours false, you have long had nothing to say. If yours are true and ours false, we could be deceived, along with the whole world, about the wickedness of men, not about the truth of the faith. For the seed of Abraham, spread among every nation, ought not to have regarded what you said you knew, but what you proved to the judges.

Hence, how do we know what those did whom your elders accused, even if true charges were laid against them, so that to the examiners of the cause, or certainly to the Church spread everywhere—which ought to regard nothing but the sentences of the examiners—those charges were thought not true but false? God does not therefore absolve whatever crime of men they cannot, as men, know; yet I think no one is rightly judged guilty who has believed a man innocent who was not convicted.

Whence, then, is the whole world guilty, if it could not know even a crime of Africans that was perhaps true; and this either it could not know because no one brought it to it, or because, in what was brought, it believed the judging examiners rather than the murmuring defeated? On this account, then, Petilian is to be praised, in that, since he saw this to be altogether most invincible, he passed it over in silence. He is plainly not to be praised, in that he tried to cover with clouds of words the other things, likewise invincible, which he nonetheless thought could be obscured, and in that he made me the case when he had failed in the case—saying nothing even about me myself except what was either altogether false, or not to be blamed, or no longer pertained to me.

But meanwhile you, whom I have set as judges between me and him, do you have the wit to discern at all between true and false? Between the inflated and the solid, between the turbid and the calm, between the swollen and the sound, between things divinely foretold and things humanly presumed, between proofs and accusations, between documents and fabrications, between the conduct of a case and the turning away from a case? If you have it, well and rightly; but if you do not have it, we shall not regret having taken care of you; for even if your heart is not turned to peace, our peace nonetheless returns to us[^164].
