# The matter concerns Macarius and Ursacius.

**92.** 208. For now you go on, and name bishops, whom you are accustomed to accuse of the handing over of books. About whom we too are accustomed to reply: Either you do not prove it, and it pertains to no one; or you prove it, and it does not pertain to us. For each one bore his own burden, whether good or bad[^1]: and good indeed we believe it; but whatever kind, still his own; just as your wicked ones bear neither yours, nor you theirs; but the common, most evil burden of you all is the schism. This we have already often said.

Show forth, then, not the names of bishops, but of our emperors and judges, who perished by persecuting you. For this you had set out, this you had promised, for this you had made us most intent. *Hear,* you say, *Macarius perished, Ursacius perished, and all your Counts likewise perished by the vengeance of God.* You mentioned only two, of whom neither was an emperor. Whom does this please, I ask you? Do you not displease even yourself?

You promise that you will mention very many of our emperors and judges, who perished by persecuting you, and, with the emperors passed over in silence, you name two, either judges or Counts. For what you add: *And all your Counts likewise perished by the vengeance of God,* does not bear on the matter. For in this way you could long ago have finished this passage, so as to name no one at all. Why, then, did you not mention our emperors, that is, those of our communion? Or did you fear to be accused as guilty of treason? Where is the Circumcellion courage?

Then what do you do about those whom you named, so many above, who could more rightly say to you: Why did you seek us out? For they helped your cause not at all, and yet they were named. Then what sort of man are you, who fear to name those whom you mention as having perished? At least you might name more of those very judges or Counts, whom you seem not to have feared. But you have come down to Macarius and Ursacius. Are these two, then, the very many? Or do you know only what we learned as boys? For if you should ask me what number two is, singular or plural; what should I answer it to be, but plural? But even for this I do not lack what to say. I take Macarius out from there: for you did not say how he perished. Or is it perhaps that whoever persecutes you, unless he is immortal in this land, when he has died, will be reckoned to have died on your account?

What if Constantine, who first established many things against your error, had not lived in so long a reign and with such long good fortune; and what if Julian, who gave you the basilicas, had not been so quickly snatched from life? When would you cease to chatter these things, since even now you are unwilling to be silent? Nor, however, do we say that Julian died quickly for the reason that he gave you the basilicas. For we could be equally copious in these matters, but we do not wish to be equally empty.

Accordingly, as I had begun to say, I separate Macarius from those two. For when you had set forth two, him and Ursacius, you repeated the name of Ursacius, to show us what death he deserved, and said: *For Ursacius, laid low in a barbarian battle, the birds with savage talons and the greedy teeth of dogs tore apart with their bites.* From which it sufficiently appears that, since you are accustomed to stir up greater resentment over Macarius, so that you call us Macarians, not Ursacians; you would by far most of all have spoken about him, if you had been able to say something of the kind about his death. From these two, therefore, where you put a plural number, with Macarius set apart, there remains Ursacius, a proper name of singular number. Where, then, is that so threatening and dreadful promise of so very many?

[^1]: See Gal 6:5.
