# Book I

To his most beloved brothers belonging to the care of our stewardship, Augustine: greetings in the Lord.

## Augustine answers the letter of a certain Donatist.

**1.** 1. You know that we have often wished to bring the sacrilegious error of the Donatist heretics into clear view, and to refute it, not so much from our own mouth as from their own; and so it came about that we sent letters even to some of their leading men—not letters of communion, of which they have by their dissent already in the past made themselves unworthy from the Catholic Church;

yet not insulting letters either, but peaceable ones: that, the question being thrashed out with us which tore them away from the holy communion of the whole world, they might, upon considering the truth, be willing to be corrected; and that they might not with a still more foolish obstinacy defend the spirited perversity of their forebears, but might be restored to the Catholic root for the fruit of love. But since it is written, *with those who hated peace I was peaceable*[^1], so they spurned my letters, just as they hated the very peace that was being sought for them through those letters.

But just now, when I was in the church of Constantina, in the presence of Absentius and of my colleague Fortunatus its bishop, the brothers brought me a letter which they said the bishop of that same schism had sent to his presbyters, as the heading of the letter itself also indicated. When I had read it, I was so astonished that with its very first words it cut off at the root the whole communion of his own party, that I was unwilling to believe the letter belonged to that man whom rumor is wont to proclaim as excelling among them above all in learning and eloquence.

But because, as I read, there were present some who recognized the polish and ornament of his style, they began to persuade me that the discourse was indeed entirely his. Yet, whosever it was, I thought it should be refuted, lest whoever wrote it should seem to himself to have written something against the Catholic Church before the unskilled.

## Certain assertions of the Donatists about the validity of baptism.

**1.** 2. This, then, is what he first put in his letter, saying *that we charge those people with administering baptism twice who under the name of baptism pollute our souls with a guilty washing.* But what good is it to recount all his insulting words?

For since it is one thing to establish proofs and another to handle abuse by refuting it, let us rather attend to that by which he wished to show that we do not have baptism, and that therefore he is not repeating what already was, but giving what was not. For he says: *For the conscience of the giver is what is regarded, the conscience that cleanses that of the receiver.*

What if the conscience of the giver is hidden, and is perhaps stained? How will it be able to cleanse the conscience of the receiver, if, as he says, *the conscience of the giver is regarded, the conscience that cleanses that of the receiver*? For if he says that whatever evil lay hidden in the conscience of the giver does not pertain to the receiver, then that ignorance will perhaps be of avail to this extent, that one who does not know cannot be stained by the conscience of the one who baptizes him. Let it suffice, then, that another's stained conscience does not stain when it is not known: but can it indeed even cleanse?

## Augustine questions Petilian about these assertions.

**2.** 3. By what, then, is one to be cleansed who receives baptism, when the conscience of the giver is polluted, and the one who is to receive it does not know this? Especially since he adds and says: *For he who receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt.* See, the faithless man stands ready to baptize, but the one who is to be baptized does not know his faithlessness: what do you think he is going to receive—faith or guilt?

If you say faith, you will grant that it can come about that someone receives faith, not guilt, from one who is faithless; and that statement will be false which was made: *He who receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt.* For we find that it can come about that someone receives faith even from one who is faithless, if he is unaware of the giver's faithlessness.

For he does not say: He who receives faith from one who is openly or knowingly faithless, but: *He who*, he says, *receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt;* which is certainly false, when someone is baptized by a faithless man who is hidden.

But if he says: Even when the one baptizing is faithless and hidden, the receiver does not receive faith from him but guilt: then let them rebaptize those whom it is established were baptized by men who long lay hidden among them as criminals, and who afterward were exposed, convicted, and condemned.

**3.** 3. For during that time when they lay hidden, whomever they baptized, they could not hand on faith to them but guilt; if anyone who receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt. Therefore let them be baptized by good men, that they may be able to receive faith, not guilt.

## Can a person be certain about another's conscience?

**3.** 4. But how will they be secure even about these men, if the conscience of the giver is what is regarded, the conscience that is hidden from the eyes of the one who is to receive? Thus, according to their view, that spiritual salvation becomes uncertain, while, against the holy Scriptures, which say, *It is good to trust in the Lord rather than to trust in man*[^2], and, *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^3], they take away the hope of those to be baptized from the Lord God and persuade them that it must be placed in man.

And so it comes about that salvation is not merely uncertain but altogether nonexistent, because: *Salvation belongs to the Lord*; and: *Vain is the salvation of man*. And so whoever has placed his hope in man, even in one he knows to be just and innocent, is cursed. Hence the apostle Paul also rebukes those who said they belonged to Paul, and says: *Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?*[^4]

## What is the origin, root, and head of one baptized?

**4.** 5. Therefore, if those who wished to belong to Paul were in error, and unless corrected would perish, what hope at last is there for those who seek to belong to Donatus? For these men are at pains to make the origin, root, and head of the baptized person be none other than the one by whom he is baptized.

So it comes about that, since for the most part it is uncertain what sort of man the baptizer is, with an uncertain origin, an uncertain root, an uncertain head, all hope too is uncertain. And since it can happen that the conscience of the giver is criminal and stained, and the receiver does not know it, it follows accordingly that with a criminal origin, a criminal root, a criminal head, the hope of the baptized person too proves empty and vain.

Indeed, since this man puts it in his letter and says: *For everything consists of an origin and a root, and if it has not something for a head, it is nothing.* And since he means by the origin and root and head of the baptized person the man by whom he is baptized, what good does it do the wretched baptized person that he does not know how evil his baptizer is? For he does not know that he has an evil head, or that he is altogether without a head. Yet what hope is there for him whose head, whether he knows it or not, is utterly wicked or nonexistent? Does that very ignorance become a head for the one whose baptizer is either an evil head or no head? But whoever thinks this is truly without a head.

## Christ is the head, origin, and root.

**5.** 6. We therefore ask—since this man said: *He who receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt;* and at once joined to it, saying: *For everything consists of an origin and a root, and if it has not something for a head, it is nothing:* we ask, then, when that faithless baptizer is hidden, if then the one whom he baptizes receives faith, not guilt; if then his baptizer is not his origin and root and head, who is the one from whom he receives faith? Where is the origin from which he springs? Where is the root from which he sprouts? Where is the head from which he begins? Or perhaps, when the one who is baptized does not know that the one baptizing is faithless, then Christ gives faith, then Christ is the origin and root and head?

O human rashness and pride! Why do you not rather allow that Christ always gives faith, since it is he who will make a person a Christian by giving it? Why do you not allow that Christ is always the origin of the Christian, that the Christian fixes his root in Christ, that Christ is the head of the Christian? For not even when spiritual grace is imparted to believers through a holy and faithful steward does the steward himself justify, but rather that one of whom it is said that he *justifies the ungodly*[^5]. Or is the apostle Paul really the head and origin of those whom he had planted, or Apollos the root of those whom he had watered, and not he who had given them faith in believing? For the same man says: *I planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the growth: so neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the growth*[^6]. Nor was he their root, but rather he who says: *I am the vine; you are the branches*[^7]. And how could he be their head, when he most plainly proclaims in many places that we, though many, are one body in Christ, and that Christ himself is the head of the whole body?

## What follows from the author's assertions.

**6.** 7. Therefore, whether anyone receives the sacrament of baptism from a faithful steward or from a faithless one, let all his hope be in Christ, lest he be *cursed who places his hope in man*[^8]. Otherwise, if each person is reborn in spiritual grace such as the one is by whom he is baptized, and when the one who baptizes is manifestly a good man, that man gives faith, that man is the origin and root and head of the one being born; but when a faithless baptizer is hidden, then each one receives faith from Christ, then he draws his origin from Christ, then he is rooted in Christ, then he glories in Christ as his head: then all who are baptized must take pains to have faithless baptizers and not to know them. For however good the baptizers they have, Christ is in every way incomparably better, who will then be the head of the baptized if a faithless baptizer is hidden.

## Catholic replies to the Donatists.

**7.** 8. But if it is utterly mad to believe this (for Christ always justifies the ungodly by making a Christian out of the ungodly[^9], faith is always received from Christ[^10], Christ is always the origin of the regenerated[^11] and the head of the Church[^12]), what weight do those words have which empty readers do not attend to as to what they hold within, but only as to how they sound?

But one who takes in the words not with the ear alone, but also gazes with the mind upon their meaning, when he has heard, *The conscience of the giver is regarded, the conscience that cleanses that of the receiver,* will reply: Often human conscience is unknown to me, but I am certain of Christ's mercy.

When he has heard, *He who receives faith from one who is faithless receives not faith but guilt,* he will reply: Christ, from whom I receive faith, not guilt, is not faithless. When he has heard, *For everything consists of an origin and a root, and if it has not something for a head, it is nothing,* he will reply: My origin is Christ, my root is Christ, my head is Christ. When he has heard, *Nor does anything rightly regenerate unless it is regenerated by good seed,* he will reply: The seed by which I am regenerated is the word of God, which I have been warned to hear obediently, even if the one through whom I hear it does not himself do what he says to me, since the Lord says, and makes me secure: *What they say, do; what they do, do not do: for they say, and do not do*[^13].

When he has heard, *What perversity can there be, that one who is guilty by his own crimes should make another innocent?* he will reply: No one makes me innocent except him who died for our offenses and rose again for our justification[^14]. For I do not believe in the minister through whom I am baptized, but in him who justifies the ungodly, that my faith may be reckoned to me as righteousness[^15].

## Other replies.

**8.** 9. When he has heard, *A good tree bears good fruit; a bad tree bears bad fruit: do they gather grapes from thorns?*[^16] and, *Every good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart; and the evil man brings forth evil things from the treasure of his heart*[^17], he will reply: This, then, is the good fruit—that I should be a good tree, that is, a good man, so that I may yield good fruit, that is, good works. But this will be given me not by the one who plants and the one who waters, but by God who gives the growth[^18].

For if the good tree is a good baptizer, so that the fruit he baptizes is good, then whoever has been baptized by an evil man—even one not openly evil—will not be able to be good: for he has sprung from a bad tree. For a good tree is one thing, a hidden but nonetheless bad tree another. Or if, when the bad tree is hidden, whoever has been baptized by it is born not of it but of Christ, then those baptized by hidden evil men are more holily justified than those baptized by manifestly good men.

## Whether a dead man can baptize.

**9.** 10. Again, when he has heard, *He who is baptized by a dead man—what good does his washing do him?*[^19], he will reply: *Christ lives, he dies now no more, and death shall no longer have dominion over him*[^20], of whom it is said, *It is he who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^21]. But those are baptized by the dead who are baptized in the temples of idols. For not even they suppose that they receive from their own priests the sanctification they imagine, but from their gods:

who, since they were men, and so died that they are neither upon the earth nor in the rest of heaven, are truly themselves baptized by the dead—or in whatever other way these words of holy Scripture can be truthfully examined and wholesomely discussed and understood. For if in this place I should understand the dead man to be a sinful baptizer, that same absurdity will follow, that whoever has been baptized even by a hidden ungodly man, as if baptized by a dead man, has been washed to no purpose. For he does not say: He who is baptized by a manifestly dead man; but says without qualification, *by a dead man.*

And if they think a man dead when they know him to be a sinner, but alive even if a criminal lies most cunningly hidden in their communion: then, first, by an execrable pride they claim more for themselves than they grant to God, so that when a sinner is known to them, he is called dead, but when he is known to God, he is reckoned alive.

Next, if that sinner is to be called dead who is known to men, what will they reply about Optatus, whom, though a criminal known to them for a long time, they were afraid to condemn? Why are those who were baptized by him not said to be baptized by a dead man? Or was he alive because God was his companion? This was wittily and elegantly said, they say, by some leading colleague of theirs, and they are even wont to boast of it and praise it, not understanding that, by the end that befell the most proud Goliath, the head is taken off by his own sword[^22].

## Whether the Maximianists, having been condemned, are dead.

**10.** 11. Finally, if they are willing to call dead neither the hidden criminal nor the open one who has not yet been condemned by them, but only the one who is both manifest and condemned—so that whoever is baptized by him is himself baptized by a dead man, and his washing does him no good—what will they say about those whom, as they said, they condemned by the *truthful mouth* of their *plenary council* together with Maximian and the rest who ordained him? I mean Felicianus of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuras, of whom I speak for the moment, who are named among the twelve who ordained Maximian and set up an altar against their altar, at which Primian presides. These men are certainly reckoned by them among the dead. Witness that, of course, splendid sentence of their council, which once, when it was read out among them to be decreed, they acclaimed with full-throated voice; but now, if perhaps it should be read out to them by us, they fall silent—whereas they ought rather, in the first place, not to have rejoiced in it as eloquent, lest afterward they should bewail it as published abroad.

For there they speak thus about the Maximianists shut out from the fellowship of their communion: *That the shipwrecked limbs of some have been cast by the truthful wave upon the rough rocks, and, very much after the example of the Egyptians, the shores are full of the corpses of the perishing; whose punishment in death itself is the greater, in that, after their soul has been wrenched out by the avenging waters, they do not even find burial.*

Thus indeed do these men insult their own schismatics, that they call them both dead and unburied: but surely they ought to have wished them buried, lest, from the multitude of corpses lying unburied on the shore, Optatus the Gildonian, advancing with his column of soldiers, like a raging wave leaping forth still further, should afterward suck Felicianus and Praetextatus back inside.

## Whether the Maximianists who returned to the Donatists are dead.

**11.** 12. Of them I ask: did they come back to life by going over to their sea, or do they still remain dead there? For if they are nonetheless corpses, then the washing does no good either to those who are baptized by these dead men. But if they came back to life, how does the washing do good to those whom, while they lay lifeless, they baptized outside beforehand—if it must be understood in the way they think: *He who is baptized by a dead man, what good does his washing do him?*[^23]

For those whom Praetextatus and Felicianus baptized while they were still in communion with Maximian, they now have among themselves, not rebaptized, mingled in their communion together with these same baptizers of theirs, that is, with Felicianus and Praetextatus. If, on the occasion of this deed, they did not foster the primacy of their own obstinacy but considered the so-certain ruin of their spiritual salvation, they certainly ought to wake up, and, recovering soundness of mind, breathe again in Catholic peace—if, laying aside the swelling of pride and overcoming the fury of stubbornness, they were willing to attend to with how monstrous a sacrilege the baptism of the churches across the sea, which we have learned from the holy books to be founded in the earliest times, is execrated, while the baptism of the Maximianists, whom they condemned by their own mouth, is accepted.

## The ignorant cannot be contaminated by crimes.

**12.** 13. But our brothers themselves, the children of the churches I have mentioned, neither knew then nor know now what was done so many years ago in Africa; so that the crimes which were charged by their party against the Africans, even if they were true, could not contaminate men who were ignorant of them. These men, however, openly separated and divided, although they are said even to have taken part in Primian's ordination, condemned Primian, ordained another bishop against Primian, baptized apart from Primian, rebaptized after Primian, and returned to Primian together with their own people baptized outside by them and rebaptized inside by no one.

If so great a union with the Maximianists does not stain the Donatists, how could the reputation of the Africans stain people across the sea? If, in the kiss of peace, lips that had condemned one another come together without crime, why is anyone condemned by them in churches far removed by their own judgment, across the sea, blown upon as an ungodly pagan, not kissed as a faithful Catholic?

But if they made peace, for the sake of their own unity, with the Maximianists received back: behold, we find no fault, except that they cut their own throats by their own judgment, when, for the sake of the unity of their own faction, they gather back together what was cut off from them, yet scorn to mend that very cutting-off of theirs for the sake of true unity.

## For the peace of Christ, even true crimes are to be borne.

**13.** 14. If, for the sake of the unity of the party of Donatus, no one rebaptizes those baptized in a wicked schism, and the men guilty of so great a crime—so great that in their own council they compared them to those ancient authors of schism whom the earth swallowed alive[^24]—are either not punished when separated, or, when condemned, are fully restored;

why is not, for the sake of the unity of Christ, which is spread throughout the whole world—of which it was foretold that *he shall have dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^25]; and what was foretold is seen and proved to be fulfilled—why then, for the sake of this true and full unity, is the law of that inheritance not acknowledged, which sounds forth from the books common to us all: *I will give you the nations for your inheritance, and the ends of the earth for your possession*[^26]?

For the unity of Donatus they are not compelled to recall what they scattered, but are admonished to heed what the Scriptures cry out: why do they not understand that this was done with them by the mercy of God, so that, because they accused the Catholic Church of false crimes, by whose contagion, as it were, they were unwilling to have their excessive holiness contaminated, they should be compelled, through the reign of Optatus the Gildonian, to take back again, and to ally to themselves, the true and very great crimes condemned by the *truthful mouth,* as they say, of their *plenary council*?

Now let them at last perceive how they are being filled with the true crimes of their own people, who fabricate false ones against their brothers,

which, even if they had spoken them truly, they ought now at last to perceive how great are the things to be borne for the sake of peace, and, for the peace of Christ, to return to the Church which has not condemned things unknown—if, for the peace of Donatus, it pleased them to recall things condemned.

## What proofs are to be brought forward.

**14.** 15. And so, brothers, let this one thing alone, which was done among them in the case of the Maximianists, suffice us for admonishing and correcting them: we do not sift old archives, we do not air out ancient cabinets, we do not send our proof off into distant lands; but we set aside all the documents of our forebears, we put off the testimonies that cry out throughout the whole world.

## The schism of the Maximianists.

**15.** 16. Behold the cities of Musti and Assuras; there still survive in this life and in this province those who separated themselves and those from whom they were separated, those who set up an altar and those against whom they set it up, those who condemned and those who were condemned, those who received and those who were received, those who were baptized outside and were not rebaptized inside:

if these things stain a man, despite the unity, let the stained ones hold their tongue; if these things do not stain a man, despite the unity, let the corrected ones drop the quarrel.

## Still on the Maximianists.

**16.** 17. Let the very man who wrote it now laugh at the words of his own letter, who, by an unlearned and lying use of the testimony he cites as written, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what good does his washing do him?*[^27], tries to show us *that a betrayer is to be regarded as dead in his life.*

And he adds, saying: *Dead is the one who has not deserved to be born by true baptism; dead likewise is the one who, begotten by just baptism, has been mingled with a betrayer.* If, then, the Maximianists are not dead, why do they say in their plenary council that *the shores are full of the corpses of those of theirs who perish?* But if they are dead, whence comes the living baptism that they gave? Again, if Maximian is not dead, why is there rebaptizing after him? But if he is dead, why is Felicianus of Musti, his ordainer, not dead with him, and how could a man from across the sea die together with some African betrayer as his colleague? Or if he too is dead, how do there live with him among you, inside, not rebaptized, those who were baptized by that dead man outside?

## The Donatists act contradictorily.

**17.** 18. Then he added: *Both lack the life of baptism, both he who never had it at all and he who had it and lost it.* Therefore the one whom the Maximianist Felicianus or Praetextatus baptized outside never had it, while they themselves lost what they had. When, then, these men were received back together with their own people, who gave to those whom they had baptized what they did not have; and who restored to the baptizers themselves what they had lost? But if they carried off with them the form of baptism, while losing the very power of baptism through their evil schism, why do you blow upon that very form, which is always and everywhere holy, in the case of Catholics whom you have not heard, and embrace it in the case of the Maximianists whom you have punished?

## The Donatists contradict themselves concerning the betrayal.

**17.** 19. But whatever he seemed to say accusingly about Judas the betrayer, what does it pertain to us, who are neither proved betrayers by them, nor, if the betrayal of some who died before us in our communion were proved, would that very thing, disapproved and displeasing to us, stain us in any part? For if they themselves are not stained by crimes which they have condemned and then received back, how much less can we be stained by things heard and disapproved?

Therefore, however much he inveighs against betrayers, let him reckon that in just as many words I too accuse them. But I make this clear distinction: that he accuses before me a man whom, already long dead, my knowledge has not judged; while I point to one clinging to his own side, whom, condemned by himself, or at any rate separated by a sacrilegious schism, he took back without any diminishment of his honor.

## The objection about the persecution of the Donatists is rejected.

**18.** 20. *You,* he says, *a most wicked betrayer, have shown yourself a persecutor and executioner of us who keep the law.* If the Maximianists kept the law when they were separated from you, then may you too be a keeper of the law, since you are separated from the Church which is spread throughout the whole world. But if you are dealing with persecutions, I answer quickly: if you have suffered anything unjustly, it does not pertain to those who, though men do such things wickedly, nevertheless bear them praiseworthily for the sake of the peace of unity.

Therefore you have nothing to charge against the Lord's grain, which bears its own chaff until the final winnowing—from which you would never have departed, had you not, as the lighter chaff, flown off by the wind of temptation and before the coming of the winnower.

But that I may not depart from this one example, which the Lord has thrown back into their faces to close the mouths of these men, and to correct them if they are wise, but to confound them if they remain in their perversity: if those who suffer persecution are more righteous than those who inflict it, then those same Maximianists are more righteous—whose basilica too was razed to the ground, who were grievously harried by the military escort of Optatus, and against whom the proconsul's orders, that they all be shut out of the basilicas, obtained by the Primianists, are manifest. Therefore, if, under emperors who detest their communion, they dared so much against the Maximianists in persecuting them, what would they do if they were allowed to accomplish anything through the communion of kings?

But if they did these things in order to correct the wicked, why do they wonder if Catholic emperors decree that those be pressed and corrected with greater power who try to rebaptize the whole Christian world, when they have no cause for dissenting—seeing that even they themselves testify that the wicked, even if they could charge them with true crimes, must nevertheless be borne for the sake of peace, since they received back those whom they had condemned, with their honors and with the baptism given outside?

Let them consider at some time what they deserve to suffer from the Christian powers of the whole world, being enemies of Christian unity spread throughout the whole world. If, then, correction is slow, let there at least be shame; lest these same men, when they begin to read what they write, be overcome by laughter, while they do not recognize in themselves what they wish to seem to see in others, nor recognize in themselves what they charge against others.

## Why the Donatists conceal their writings.

**19.** 21. What, then, does it mean that this man put in his letter that the Lord says to the Jews: *Therefore I send to you prophets and wise men and scribes; and some of them you will kill and crucify and scourge*[^28]? For if they wish themselves to be understood as the wise men and scribes and prophets, and us as the persecutors of the wise men and prophets, why are they unwilling to speak with us, when they have been sent to us?

Indeed, the very man who wrote that letter to which we are now replying, if he were pressed by us to acknowledge it as his own by signing it with his own hand, would perhaps not do so: to such a degree do they fear lest we hold any of their words. For when we wished somehow to obtain the latter part of that same letter—since those by whom it was given to us were unable to copy it whole—no one of whom it was asked was willing to give it, after they learned that we were replying to the part which had reached us. And so, although they read how the Lord says to the prophet, *Cry aloud, spare not, and write down their sins with my pen*[^29]: these true prophets who have been sent to us fear and guard against nothing so much as that their cry should be heard by us—which surely, if they were saying true things about us, they would not fear.

Not without reason, as it is written in the Psalm: *The mouth of those who speak iniquity has been stopped*[^30]. For if they do not accept our baptism on the ground that we are a *brood of vipers*[^31], as this man put it in his letter, why did they accept that of the Maximianists, about whom their council speaks thus: *That the hollow of a poisoned womb has long concealed the harmful offspring of a viperous seed, and the moist curds of conceived crime have, with slow heat, swollen into the limbs of asps*? Was it not consequently said about these same men, in the same council, that *the venom of asps is under their lips, whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness, their feet are swift to shed blood, ruin and misery are in their ways, and the way of peace they have not known*[^32]? And yet they now have these same men in full honor, inside, together with those baptized outside by them.

## The Donatists are not prophets, but false prophets.

**20.** 22. Therefore all these things about the brood of vipers, and about the venom of asps under their lips, and the other things that were said against those who have not known the way of peace—if these men truly wished to speak them, they themselves are rather the ones to whom they apply; since they received the baptism of those against whom they said these things by the sentence of their council, for the sake of the peace of Donatus, and they repudiate the baptism of the Church of Christ spread throughout the whole world, whence that very peace came into Africa, to the sacrilegious injury of the peace of Christ.

Who, then, are rather the false prophets, who come in the clothing of sheep, but inwardly are ravening wolves[^33]:

whether those who either do not know the wicked in the Catholic Church and innocently communicate with them, or cannot separate from the Lord's threshing floor those whom they cannot remove before the winnower comes, and bear them for the sake of the peace of unity—or those who, in a schism, do the very things they blame in the Catholic Church, and who pretend to flee in unity the things, uncertain at that, which are to be borne, yet take back, in their own open and self-condemned cutting-off, those very things?

## Some of the evil fruits of the Donatists are enumerated.

**21.** 23. In fine, it was thus said, and he too cited this: *By their fruits you shall know them*[^34]. Let us, then, consider the fruits. You charge us with betrayal: this very thing we charge against you far more credibly. And, not to run through many points, in this same city of Constantina your forebears, at the very outset of their schism, ordained Silvanus as bishop. When he was still a subdeacon, he was shown by the municipal records to be a most manifest betrayer. If you too bring forward some documents against our forebears, an equal condition is demanded: that we believe either both to be true or both false. If both are true, you are without doubt guilty of schism, who pretended to flee in the communion of the whole world crimes which you had in that very fragment of your cutting-off. But if both are false, you are without doubt guilty of schism, who are stained by the monstrous crime of separation on account of false crimes of betrayal. And if some are brought forward by us and none by you, or true ones by us and false ones by you, there is no need to discuss how utterly your mouths are stopped.

## What the Donatists ought to do.

**22.** 24. What if the holy and true Church of Christ should convict and overcome you, even though we held no documents of betrayal, or false ones, while you held some that were true—what would then remain for you except to love peace, if you are willing; or, if you are not willing, at least to fall silent? For whatever you might now bring forward, I would say most easily and most truly that you ought then to have proved it to the plenary and Catholic unity of the Church, already spread through so many nations and established, so that you might be inside, while those whom you convicted were driven outside.

But if you tried to do this, beyond doubt you could not prove it, and, defeated or enraged, by a monstrous sacrilege you separated yourselves from innocent people who could not condemn things uncertain. But if you did not even try to do it, then by a too execrable and impious blindness, offended by a few weeds in Africa, you cut yourselves off from the grain of Christ, which grows throughout the whole field, that is, the whole world, until the end.

## Another contradiction in the Donatists.

**23.** 25. Finally, a testament is said to have been handed over to the flames by certain men in the time of persecution: now let it, from whatever source it has been produced, be read out. Certainly, at the outset of the testator's promises, this is found said to Abraham: *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^35]: and what this means the truthful Apostle interprets: *In your seed,* he says, *which is Christ*[^36]. No one's betrayal has made void the faithfulness of God. Be in communion with all the nations: and then boast that you have preserved the testament from the destruction of the flames. But if you are unwilling, which party is rather to be believed to have insisted on burning the testament, except the one that is unwilling to consent to it when it is produced? For how much more certainly, without any sacrilegious rashness, is the man judged to have succeeded the betrayers who now persecutes with his tongue the testament which those men are said to have persecuted with the flame?

You charge persecution: the Lord's grain answers you: Either it was done justly, or our chaff did it. What do you say to this?

You charge that we do not have baptism: the same grain of the Lord answers you, that the form of the sacrament does no good even to certain people inside, as it did no good to Simon Magus who was baptized[^37]; and much more does it do no good to those who are outside: yet that it is present in them when they depart hence is proved from the fact that it is not restored when they return. Will you, then—unless most shamelessly—be able to cry out against this grain and call them false prophets clothed in the skins of sheep, but inwardly ravening wolves, seeing that they either do not know the wicked in Catholic unity, or, for the sake of unity, bear those whom they do know?

## If the Donatists loved peace, they would not tear unity apart.

**24.** 26. But if we consider your fruits: I pass over the tyrannical dominations in the cities and especially on others' estates, I pass over the fury of the Circumcellions, and the sacrilegious and profane cults of corpses of those who hurled themselves to death of their own accord, the drunken revels, and, under the one Optatus the Gildonian, the ten-year groaning of all Africa:

I pass over these things, because there are some among you who proclaim that these things displease them and have always displeased them; but they say that, because they cannot suppress them, they bear them for the sake of peace: in which they condemn themselves by their own judgment; for if they loved peace, they would not tear unity apart.

For what madness is so great as to wish to abandon peace within peace itself, and to wish to retain it in dissension? And so, on account of those who pretend not to see the evils of that same party of Donatus, which all see and blame—so much so that even about Optatus himself they say: What did he do? Who accused him? Who convicted him?

I do not know, I have not seen, I have not heard. On account of these, then, who pretend to be ignorant of things manifest, the Maximianists arose, in whom their eyes may be opened and their mouths stopped: openly they separate themselves, openly they set up an altar against an altar, openly in council they are called sacrilegious men and vipers, and swift to shed blood, and to be compared to Dathan and Abiram and Korah, and, detested in harsh words, are condemned, and openly they are received back again in their own honors together with those baptized by them.

These are their fruits, of men who do such things for the peace of Donatus, so as to cover themselves with the sheep's skin, and who refuse the peace of Christ throughout the whole world, so as to be ravening wolves inside.

## Schism is the greatest Donatist crime.

**25.** 27. I think I have passed over none of the things which this man put in his letter, at least such as I could find in the part which reached us: let them produce the remaining part too, lest perhaps there be in it something that cannot be refuted. But these things which, with the Lord's help, we have answered, I urge upon your Charity, that you not only give them to those who ask, but also press them upon those who do not ask.

Let them answer if they wish anything: and if they are unwilling to answer us, let them at least send letters to their own people, which, however, they must not order to be hidden from us; and if they do this, they most plainly betray their own fruits, by which the ravening wolves clothed in the skins of sheep[^38] may be shown, who secretly lie in wait for our sheep, and openly dread to answer the shepherds.

We charge against them only the crime of schism, in which all of them are utterly entangled: not the crimes of certain of them, which some of them may reply displease them. But they themselves, if they do not charge against us crimes belonging to others, have nothing to charge: and therefore they cannot in any way defend themselves from the crime of schism; because, whether on account of crimes false and fabricated by themselves, or even on account of true ones—yet crimes of the chaff—they have separated themselves by a wicked sundering from the Lord's threshing floor and from the innocence of the grain growing throughout the whole world.

## On the error of the Manichees.

**26.** 28. Unless perhaps you expect me also to refute what he interposed about the Manichee. In which matter nothing displeases us, except that he blamed a most pestilent and most pernicious error, that is, the heresy of the Manichees, with an altogether very slight and almost no reproof; an error which the Catholic Church overthrows with the strongest proofs of truth. For the inheritance of Christ, established among all the nations, is safe against all the disinherited heresies: but, as the Lord says, *How can Satan cast out Satan?*[^39], so how can the error of the Donatists overthrow the error of the Manichees?

## The case of the Maximianists is above all to be held fast.

**27.** 29. Therefore, most beloved, although this error, convicted in many ways, is overcome, and dares to resist the truth by no assertions of any sort of reasoning, but by stubborn shamelessness alone: nevertheless, lest a multitude of documents burden your memory, hold fast this one deed of the Maximianists, fix it on their forehead, press it into their mouths to suppress their deceitful tongues, with it, as with a three-pronged weapon, slaughter their calumny like a three-headed beast. They charge betrayal, they charge persecution, they charge false baptism: from the Maximianists alone, answer all of it. For that their forebears handed over the holy books to the flames, they think to be hidden: but since the sacrilege of schism surpasses the crime of betrayal, they received back into their honors the Maximianists defiled by the sacrilege of schism—this, certainly, they cannot hide. Likewise the most violent persecutions, which they inflict on whomever they can wherever they can, they think to be hidden: but since spiritual persecution surpasses bodily persecution, they received back into their honors the Maximianists whom they themselves persecuted bodily, and about whom they themselves said: *Their feet are swift to shed blood*[^40]—this, certainly, they cannot hide.

**28.** 29. Finally, the question of baptism, by which they deceive the wretched, they think to be hidden: but since they say that all who are baptized apart from the communion of the one Church do not have baptism, they received back into their honors the Maximianists together with those whom they had baptized outside the communion of these men in schism—this, certainly, they cannot hide.

**28.** 30. *But these things,* they say, *do not stain a man, for the sake of peace, and it is good to bend the rigor of severity toward mercy, so that branches once broken may be grafted in again.* So, then, the whole case is concluded, defeated in them, undefeated for us: because if the name of peace is taken up as a kind of shadow of defense for bearing the wicked in schism, then beyond doubt, with a horrible crime and without any defense, true peace itself is violated by the unity of the whole world.

## Conclusions.

**29.** 31. These things, brothers, hold fast, to be done and preached with untiring gentleness: love the people, kill the errors; presume upon the truth without pride, contend for the truth without savagery. Pray for those whom you refute and convict. For the prophet beseeches God for such people, saying: *Fill their faces with shame, and they shall seek your name, O Lord*[^41]. And this indeed the Lord has already done, in filling their faces most plainly with the shame of the Maximianists: it remains that they learn to blush wholesomely. For thus they will be able to seek the name of the Lord, from which they have most perniciously turned away, while for its sake they exalt their own name.

May you live and persevere in Christ, and may you be multiplied, and abound in the love of God, toward one another and toward all, most beloved brothers.
