# Proceedings of the Conference of Carthage (2)

## First Hearing

**1.** In the year after the consulship of the most distinguished Varanes, on the first day of June, at Carthage, in the council chamber of the Baths of Gargilius, in the presence of Sebastianus, Maximianus, and Peter, most devoted members of the household guard; with Ursus, Petronius, and Libosus, ducenarii, in attendance, along with Boniface and Evasius, officers of the illustrious and eminent authorities; Philetus, officer of the illustrious comital seat; Exitiosus, assistant to the chief clerk; Possidius, Quodvultdeus, and Colonicus, assistants to the record keepers of the staff of our lord the most distinguished and eminent proconsul; Navigius, assistant to the accounts department of the staff of our lord the most distinguished and eminent vicar; Nampius, clerk on the staff of the most distinguished legate of nurturing Carthage; Rufinianus, clerk to the most distinguished curator of lofty Carthage; with Hilarus and Praetextatus, recording clerks to our lord the most distinguished and eminent proconsul, also taking down the proceedings, together with Fabius, recording clerk to our lord the most distinguished and eminent vicar, and Romulus, recording clerk to the most distinguished legate of nurturing Carthage; with Januarius and Vitalis, notaries of the Catholic Church, and Victor and Cresconius, notaries of the Donatist church, also taking down the proceedings, Ursus, ducenarius of the illustrious and eminent authorities, said:

*Some time ago, in accordance with the imperial instruction, Your Sublimity sent us and the others assigned to your proceedings, both from the most sacred imperial court and from the African staffs, to the various provinces. You also sent edicts throughout Africa requiring all bishops, both Catholic and Donatist, to attend the conference within four months. That period appears to expire today. Since all are now ready, from every African province—namely, the Proconsular Province, Byzacena, Numidia, the Mauretanias of Sitifis and Caesarea, and also Tripolitania—if Your Nobility commands, let these venerable men attend, so that everything may be carried out in its proper order.*

**2.** Flavius Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*Let the venerable men whom you report to be present attend.*

And all the bishops of the party of Donatus entered, as did Aurelius, Alypius, Augustine, Vincent, Fortunatus, Fortunatianus, and Possidius, bishops of the Catholic Church, with their fellow bishops Novatus, Florentius, Maurentius, Boniface, Priscus, Serenianus, and Scyllacius, and Deuterius, Leo, Asterius, and Restitutus, likewise bishops and custodians of the proceedings.

**3.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*I would indeed wish that the holiness of this great gathering, enrolled under the religion of the one Christ our God, were divided by no disagreement. But since God certainly wishes the error long ago introduced by the ancient enemy to be corrected now, so that the true religion may no longer make a spectacle of itself before the nations through its own quarrels, I acknowledge that this judicial task exceeds my merit: I appear to judge between men by whom it would have been more fitting for me to be judged. Yet it is certain that this case must be conducted with God watching and the angels as witnesses, under the examination of heavenly faith, which, when upheld, brings a reward, and, when violated, judgment. Therefore, so that the truth may finally shine more clearly through the discussions of the bishops present, let the imperial decree first be read, so that everyone may understand the manner and form in which the hearing is to be undertaken, although it is sufficiently clear that everyone has already been informed of it.* Martialis, the recording clerk, read:

**4.** *The emperors Caesars Flavius Honorius and Theodosius, pious, fortunate, victorious, and triumphant, forever Augusti, to their Flavius Marcellinus, greetings.*

*Among the greatest concerns of our empire, reverence for the Catholic law is always either the first or the only one. For in the labors of war and the counsels of peace we pursue and arrange nothing else but that the devoted people of our world should preserve the true worship of God.*

*The Donatists too should long ago have been brought to fulfill this, whether through fear or admonition. With their empty error and needless division, they disgrace Africa, a very great part of our realm that faithfully performs its civil duties. Yet we do not tire of repeating more frequently what the authority of all previous emperors, devoted to God, has determined, lest posterity rightly hold us responsible if anything injurious to the Catholic law should arise in our time. Nor are we unaware of the wording of the imperial pronouncement that the Donatists, by their harsh interpretation, claim can advance their error. Although it invited their distorted minds more gently to correction, we already ordered it abolished, so that no opening might be afforded to superstitions. Now too we decree with like authority that the fraudulent claim be excluded, and we rightly declare that we gladly remove the measures previously established, lest anyone think that he can offend divine worship under our authorization. And although there is one clear judgment shared by all, that the full truth of the Catholic law has been confirmed by the right worship of human beings and by the judgment of heaven, in our desire for peace and reconciliation we have gladly received the delegation of the venerable bishops. It requests that the Donatist bishops assemble in the most celebrated city, so that, with bishops selected by each party, discussions may be held and clear reasoning may refute superstition.*

*We order this matter to be concluded within four months, so that our clemency too may learn sooner, as it desires, that the people’s minds have been converted* (Augustine, Summary of the Conference, first day, ch. 1*). But if the Donatist bishops deliberately avoid assembling within the prescribed period, let the time allowed the contumacious be brought to an end by summons through three edicts. When those periods have elapsed and passed, if they scorn to attend after being summoned, let the people who recognize that their teachers have been defeated by silence yield together with their churches. Let them at last rejoice in being conquered, and know that, if not our instructions, at least the true commands of the Catholic law must be obeyed.*

*We wish you to preside as judge at this debate—you remember fully whatever you have previously received in your instructions—and we wish you to undertake every responsibility both for assembling the bishops and for summoning them if they scorn to attend, so that with proven diligence you may fulfill both the earlier instructions and the measures you now learn have been established. Above all, observe this: with the recent fraudulent claim removed, preserve intact and inviolate everything concerning the Catholic law that antiquity ordained long ago, the religious authority of our predecessors established, or our serenity confirmed. To ensure that appropriate assistance is not lacking for your proceedings, our serenity has admonished the eminent proconsul and vicar that, if they wish to retain the standing of their respective offices and have their staffs avoid the severest penalties, they must arrange for ample necessary personnel to be assigned both from their own staffs and from the staffs of all the governors. It will also be your responsibility, if you learn that anything is being delayed by any contrivance, to notify us through reports, so that suitable punishment may correct the negligent. You must report everything established by the completed debate when the bishops have assembled together, or determined by a judgment against the contumacious if they happen to withdraw, so that we may learn more quickly what our instruction has accomplished toward strengthening the Catholic faith*.

And in the imperial hand:

*Farewell, Marcellinus, most dear to us. Given at Ravenna on the day before the Ides of October, in the consulship of the most distinguished Varanes*.

Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*Let the venerable law that has been read be inserted into these proceedings. Its determination has become clear; therefore let the text of my edicts now be read in sequence* (Augustine, Summary of the Conference, first day, ch. 2).

**5.** Martialis, the recording clerk, read:

*The tenor of the foregoing imperial letter shows what our most clement lord Honorius has decreed for the strengthening of the Catholic faith. Gladly receiving the delegation of the venerable bishops, he would not allow Africa, always devoted to his rule, to be disturbed and disgraced any longer by conflicting forms of religion. In his concern for peace and faith, he wished the divisions of so many peoples, arising from longstanding conviction, to be calmed by a manifestation of the truth. Thus, through a debate between both parties, he would recall those whom he saw could not be calmed and brought to one understanding of the faith by the imperial decrees he had issued. He wished all the bishops of both the Catholic party and the party of Donatus to assemble together, so that, with the more knowledgeable men selected from each party and the truth of the assured faith examined, clear reasoning might expose superstition. In this way, whatever each person considers relevant to his own side may be brought forward, and the light of the true faith may shine forth, even if late. The matching requests of both parties led the emperor to this decision. For just as the Catholics recently requested a conference, so there is no doubt that a very short time earlier the Donatist bishops requested a conference before the court of the illustrious authorities. Since imperial clemency gladly gave its assent and decreed that a general council of both parties should be held in Africa* *at the equal request of their bishops, the emperor wished me to preside as judge at this debate.*

*Accordingly, by this edict I admonish all bishops throughout Africa, of both the Catholic party and the party of Donatus, not to delay assembling in the splendid city of Carthage for the purpose of holding a council within the time prescribed by law, that is, within four months, a period that will undoubtedly conclude by the first day of June. Otherwise, the party found absent may appear to have passed judgment on the merits of its own faith (perhaps: to have doubted them). I likewise admonish all curators, magistrates, and members of the municipal councils in every province, as well as the agents, procurators, and elders of individual places, on pain of liability affecting their own reputation and rank and at risk to their safety, to make haste to notify the bishops of both parties in the cities and places where they reside, either with a formal record drawn up or with some written attestation preserved. If the bishops cannot be found in their cities at that time, they must also be sought in the countryside and informed both of the imperial instruction and of this edict, so that within these four months I may learn each party’s intention from the reports of the municipal councils.*

*Nor could I pass over this point: they must know that, whether judgment is given for the Donatists or the Catholics, the Donatist bishops will suffer no trouble whatsoever, but will return to their own places free and untouched by any kind of injury. I promise to ensure this, both by the dreadful day of judgment and by the oaths mentioned above. All those who were sent to the various provinces must know that henceforth they are not to proceed in any way against any Donatist or cause them any trouble through enforcement of the earlier order, unless they prefer to incur punishment for disregarding the more recent instruction. Everyone belonging to the party of Donatus will be free to complain of extortion and plundering by those who were sent, if they know of any such offenses committed, so that, once the nature of the crimes has been proved, fitting punishment may be inflicted on them.*

And in another hand: *Let it be published*.

**6.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*The edict that has been read will be attached to these proceedings. It establishes that I clearly offered, if you wished, to have another presiding judge chosen by your side sit with me. If he is here, let him kindly enter.*

**7.** Bishop Petilian said:

*It is not fitting for us to choose a second presiding judge when we did not request the first.* And in another hand: *I have checked this.*

**8.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*The wording of the august instruction has made it perfectly clear that a conference, not a presiding judge, was requested, and that the emperor chose of his own accord to entrust the judgment to my modest person. I must comply with him in every way, since it is also clear that Your Holiness has assembled to satisfy the imperial enactments.*

**9.** Bishop Petilian said:

*With all our rights preserved, both concerning standing and concerning the case, let those who succeeded in obtaining these measures state their case.* And in another hand: *I, Bishop Petilian, have checked this*.

Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*With everything granted by the laws preserved intact, let the texts of the edicts be read in sequence, as I previously directed*.

**10.** Martialis, the recording clerk, read:

*The importance of this case, undertaken to determine truth and religion, should have been weighed in the dignity and authority of the disputants themselves, even if its weight were insufficiently assessed by examination of its own significance. For no one doubts the gravity of a case that must be discussed between bishops. But since the emperor’s graciousness entrusted the hearing of this debate to me and appointed me judge over men whose inferior I acknowledge myself to be, out of reverence for their great title, I have taken thought for the integrity of my responsibility in this way: I wish my own judgment to be judged*.

*Therefore, by the authority of this published notice, I have determined to inform all the bishops of both parties beforehand that no more than seven bishops from each side will engage in the discussion with one another, selected by their own parties for the task of debate. Imperial authority has indeed instructed that a fixed number be chosen from the parties. Seven other bishops from each side will also attend, selected by their respective parties. Maintaining disciplined silence throughout the entire discussion, they must understand that they are present solely for this purpose: if any of the seven speakers on either side wishes to consult one of his colleagues, he may withdraw with him to a place apart there, and, after calmly sharing their reasoning about the discussion, return to his own place. Each must remain faithful to his duty: the one whose role requires it commends what is to be heard by keeping silent; the other, whose task it is, supports the case he has undertaken by speaking*.

*The most suitable place for the conference will be the Baths of Gargilius, where only the bishops designated are to assemble on the first day of June. From this it is surely clear that there must be no gathering of the people at all in a place where not even all the bishops themselves are allowed to assemble. For the patience needed for discussion, which is friendly only to silence, recoils from all the noise of a crowd assembled in masses; it makes no difference whether a throng of people or a crowd of bishops obstructs it. Therefore only the bishops mentioned are to assemble at the stated place and time, on this condition: before the day appointed for the examination, all the other bishops of both parties must indicate, in letters sent to my office from each side, that they will regard as valid whatever their seven fellow bishops on either side do. All must also add the attestation of their own signatures to those letters before me. For it is entirely fair that everyone should promise to uphold the entire conference conducted by those whom everyone has selected. Let no one, therefore, whether layman or bishop beyond the fixed number, attempt to enter that most peaceful place of council contrary to this prohibition. Instead, let the bishops address their own congregations with a religious admonition to quiet and restraint, speaking of this beforehand in their own churches, so that the whole multitude stays away from the day and place of the debate. Thus, soothed by religious instruction in patience, the people may be prepared for Christian peace. For not only the bishops, but also reasonable people agree that, while the designated men debate and their minds are intently occupied with the inquiry into truth, absolutely nothing disorderly, tumultuous, or untimely should disturb them.*

*For whatever will contribute to everyone’s salvation is to be made public afterward; first, privacy must be afforded to those examining the truth. My judgment, however, must be promulgated in every respect in such a way that, once released to public knowledge, it is weighed with the entire people of splendid Carthage as its judge. The whole course of the completed debate will be laid before the eyes not only of this city but also of the entire province, so that the statements of the disputing bishops and the sequence of my own responses may be set out in publicly displayed volumes. This is a judge’s confident integrity: not to fear being judged himself. Therefore, to prevent malicious suspicion from creeping into the examination undertaken to uncover truth, and to prevent the objections of either party from clouding in any way the clear light that the assured credibility of manifest proofs will reveal to the public mind, this further provision has necessarily been made against the disguises of suspicion and the devices of deception. Everyone must know that the following must be observed, and that it is beneficial to do so: after I have first signed all my interventions, every bishop taking part in the debate must sign his own statements on the draft, without any excuse whatsoever. The requirements of good faith demand that, against anyone who might attempt treacherously to withdraw his assent to his own assertions and deny what he said, the authority of his own attestation should stand, in a contest where victory benefits truth alone.*

*Those performing the recording duties, in addition to the personnel from the public staffs assigned to my office, must include four church notaries from each party, taking turns in attendance. To prevent any uncertainty about their trustworthiness, four bishops from each party, selected by their colleagues, are to be placed over them, keeping the recording clerks and notaries under vigilant and careful observation. Going out in turns with these recording clerks and notaries, they are to have what has been said immediately arranged in a clear transcription. Thus, while the bishops mentioned above are still engaged in debate, some completed portion of the discussion may be put into legible writing, so that, once the seven bishops on each side have added their signatures, it may promptly satisfy the public’s expectation of information. After the first day of the conference, the hearing will be adjourned to leave the following day free for transcribing and signing the proceedings. If anything remains from the preceding conference, the examination will resume on the third day. Accordingly, an interval of one day will separate each period of discussion, allowing the proceedings to be transcribed in the meantime for signing, under the supervision of the designated custodians. They must limit their duty to this purpose: to safeguard what is said, not to say anything themselves. Until the questions have been brought to their conclusion and the truth, clearly established, has resolved every uncertainty, each draft, once written and signed, will be stamped both with my seal and with those of the eight custodians.*

*The Maximianists too are to be informed of the authority of this edict, so that they understand they must refrain from attending the council at which the religious instruction of our most clement emperors has ordered the entire dispute between the Catholics and Donatists to be settled and judged. Nor can they gain any assistance from flattering themselves with the name of Donatists, since they are said to have been condemned by the Donatists.*

*If anyone refuses in any way to observe these provisions, carefully devised, faithfully established, and diligently arranged against every ambush of manifold suspicion and contained in this published notice, he not only confesses what he thinks of his own case but also reveals what he is plotting. For the present, it is sufficient for the presiding judge to have this advantage of justice: he has made the people of Carthage witnesses to his admonition to all the bishops, and will shortly have them as his judge.*

*It remains for the bishops of both parties, before the day of the council and without any delay, to send letters to my office promising to carry out the entire tenor of this arrangement. This point especially must be attended to promptly: the letters declaring their agreement to everything in this edict are to be signed only by the primate bishops. For it suffices that the universal signatures, so to speak, of all the bishops be attached to the letters in which they promise to uphold the entire course of the debate conducted by the seven speakers on each side.*

**11.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*This edict too will be included in the body of the acts. To ensure that nothing seems lacking from the trustworthiness of the complete proceedings, let the court staff read through everything the parties have either written or submitted to the court in the meantime.*

**12.** Bishop Petilian said:

*You have performed your part, noble sir, by promising to be just toward the parties and open to the public’s hearing. But because all these matters precede the case and do not concern the case itself, we first ask Your Authority that the man who has troubled me by having me summoned through edicts, called away from my own see, and made to endure the hardship of travel should present the requests he wished to make, explaining why he desired my presence, so that I may know whether I should answer and what I should answer*. And in another hand: *I, Bishop Petilian, have checked this*.

**13.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*These matters will be handled more properly in their own place and order. In the meantime, let the court staff read through what I directed.*

**14.** Romulus, the recording clerk, read:

*Copy of the Donatists’ notarial statement: In the year after the consulship of the most distinguished Varanes, on the eighth day before the Kalends of June, Januarius, Primian, and the other bishops of sincere Christianity and Catholic truth, to Flavius Marcellinus, most distinguished and eminent tribune and notary.*

*We inform Your Integrity that, summoned by your edict, we hastened from different parts of Africa and entered Carthage on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of June. Everyone whom Carthage contains witnesses our arrival, and Your Integrity is not unaware of it. For the force of the oath expressed in your edict was so great that, apart from those whom bodily illness keeps in their sees or sickness overtook on the journey, neither the advanced age of the oldest men nor the labor of a long journey could prevent us. Although that edict alone sufficed before the case, you also saw fit to issue another. Many things in it trouble us, but this especially causes great concern: you determined that we ourselves must assent to your edict in letters of our own, although neither public practice nor the custom of judges requires this. Therefore we earnestly urge Your Integrity, preserving the good faith of the first edict, to order us all to come before you, so that our number may be established as soon as possible, since our opponents have often falsely claimed that we are few. Then, once everything relevant to our side has been properly arranged, let the debate follow. Let your edicts neither contradict each other nor prejudice us in any way, as would happen if, after ordering everyone to come, you now appeared to wish only a few to attend. For it is fitting and advantageous that a few should speak about our common case in everyone’s presence. We have retained a duplicate of this notarial statement.*

And in another hand: *I, Bishop Januarianus, have signed.* Likewise, in another hand: *I, Bishop Primian, have signed*.

**15.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*Let the other party’s letter also be read.*

**16.** Romulus, the recording clerk, read:

*Aurelius, Sylvanus, and all the Catholic bishops, to their honorable and most beloved son Marcellinus, the most distinguished and eminent tribune and notary.*

*As you kindly instructed, we inform you through this letter that we agree in everything to Your Eminence’s edict, which provides for preserving the peace and quiet of our conference and for revealing and safeguarding the truth. This concerns the place and time of the conference itself and the number of those who should be present. We also agree that those to whom we entrust the task of debate should sign their statements. In the document by which we assign them this duty and promise to uphold their actions, you will not only have all our signatures completed but will yourself witness their signing. With the Lord’s help, we will also admonish the Christian people, for the sake of quiet and peace, to refrain altogether from assembling at the place of the conference. They should not hurry to hear the proceedings while they take place, but wait to learn them from the written record, as you have promised to make them available to everyone.*

*Confident in the truth, we also bind ourselves by this condition: if those with whom we debate can demonstrate that, although Christian peoples were increasing everywhere according to God’s promises, had already filled a great part of the world, and were spreading to fill the rest, Christ’s Church suddenly perished through contamination by the sins of certain persons whom they accuse, and remained only in the party of Donatus—if, as stated, they can demonstrate this—we will seek no honors of the episcopal office among them. For the sake of eternal salvation alone, we will follow the counsel of those to whom we will owe gratitude for so great a benefit in coming to know the truth. But if we can instead show that Christ’s Church, already occupying the territories of all the provinces, not only African but overseas, and of many nations with its abundantly fruitful multitude of peoples, bearing fruit and growing throughout the world as Scripture says, could not have perished through the sins of any persons mingled within it; if, moreover, we demonstrate that the case of those whom they then wished to accuse rather than succeeded in convicting has been concluded—although the Church’s case does not depend on them—and that Caecilian was judged innocent and his opponents violent and slanderous by the emperor to whose examination they voluntarily submitted their accusations; finally, if, whatever they say about anyone’s sins, we prove through human or divine documents either that the innocence of those persons was attacked by false charges or that the Church of Christ, whose communion we share, was not destroyed by any of their offenses: then let them hold its unity with us in such a way that they not only find the path of salvation but also retain the honor of the episcopate. For it is not the sacraments of divine truth in them that we reject, but the inventions of human error. Once these are removed, we embrace a brotherly heart joined to us in Christian love, which we now grieve to see separated by diabolical discord.*

*Each of us, joined to a partner in his office, will be able to take turns occupying the higher seat, as when a visiting bishop sits beside his colleague. When this is mutually granted in alternating basilicas, each will outdo the other in mutual honor. For where the command of love has enlarged hearts, the possession of peace is not cramped. When one of them dies, thereafter one bishop will succeed one bishop according to the former custom. Nor will anything new be done, for Catholic love has observed this since the very beginning of the separation in the case of those who condemned the error of the wicked division and tasted the sweetness of unity, even if late. Or if Christian peoples prefer one bishop each and cannot tolerate the unfamiliar arrangement of two sharing the office, let both of us withdraw. Once the cause of schism has been condemned and each church established in peaceful unity, those found to be sole bishops in their respective churches should, after unity is restored, appoint one bishop in each place where needed. Why should we hesitate to offer our Redeemer this sacrifice of humility? Did he descend from heaven into human limbs so that we might be his members, while we fear to descend from our episcopal chairs to prevent those very members of his from being torn apart by cruel division? For our own sake, nothing is more sufficient than to be faithful and obedient Christians; therefore let us always be that. But we are ordained bishops for the sake of Christian peoples. Therefore let us make whatever use of our episcopate benefits Christian peoples in achieving Christian peace. If we are useful servants, why begrudge the Lord eternal gains for the sake of our temporary eminence? The episcopal dignity will be more fruitful for us if, by laying it down, we gather Christ’s flock rather than disperse it by retaining it. For with what face shall we hope for the honor promised by Christ in the age to come, if our honor in this age obstructs Christian unity?*

*We have taken care to write these things to Your Excellency so that through you they may become known to everyone. We ask that, with the help of the Lord our God, at whose prompting we make these promises and with whose assistance we trust we can fulfill them, devout love may heal weak human hearts or subdue hard ones, even before the conference if possible. Thus, with peaceful minds, let us no longer resist the most manifest truth, but either precede our debate with harmony or follow it with harmony. For we must not despair that, if they remember that the peacemakers are blessed because they will be called children of God, they will prefer the far more fitting and easier course of reconciling the party of Donatus with the whole Christian world, rather than having the whole Christian world rebaptized by the party of Donatus. Especially so, since they sought with such love those coming from Maximian’s sacrilegious and condemned schism, whom they also took care to correct by pursuing them through the orders of earthly authorities, that they did not dare invalidate the baptism administered by them. They received some who had been condemned without any loss of their honors, while judging others to have remained untainted within the fellowship of that division. We do not begrudge their harmony with one another. But surely they ought to observe how devoutly and earnestly the Catholic root seeks the branch broken from it, if that branch itself labored in this way to gather up a little fragment likewise cut off from itself.*

And in another hand: *We wish you good health in the Lord, son. I, Aurelius, bishop of the Catholic church of Carthage, have signed this letter.* Likewise, in another hand: *I, Silvanus, senior bishop of the church of Summa, have signed.* (Letter 128, 1–5).

He also read:

**17.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished and eminent tribune and notary, said:

*Since I remember that, at the prompting of justice and good faith, I once pledged to make publicly known whatever I did with the bishops of both parties, I have reason to issue another edict. The text of the previous proclamation contains detailed provisions by which an orderly hearing may follow an unobstructed course of judgment. I therefore thought it necessary to demand by edict from all the bishops what each party thought of it. Replying to my office, partly through a notarial statement and partly through a letter, they informed me of their views on the conditions recently proposed. I have judged that the contents of both the letter and the notarial statement should be placed before the public’s eyes. Thus, when the whole people has considered what the preceding edict required and what each party’s response conveyed, it may clearly understand the intention of the person conducting the examination*.

When this had been entered, the same clerk read:

**18.** *Aurelius, Silvanus, and all the bishops of the Catholic Church, to their honorable and most beloved son Marcellinus, the most distinguished and eminent tribune and notary.*

*The notarial statement, or letter, of our brothers, whom we desire to see turn from destructive division to Catholic peace, has caused us great concern. They refused to agree to Your Nobility’s edict providing for the peace and quiet of our debate. We fear that, even if not all of them, at least some may hinder through the commotion or noise of a crowd a conference that ought to be peaceful and calm. Would that this thought does not tempt them, and that our suspicion instead proves mistaken! May their desire for everyone to be present have this purpose: when they wish, we too may gather there, and together we may leave in harmony and peace, with the schismatic division corrected and ourselves bound by the brotherly bond of Christ’s unity. Then, to the wonder and joy of all good people, with only the devil and those like him grieving, may we proceed together to church in the most fervent and radiant love, to give thanks and render praise to God.*

*For what is difficult, if a peaceful eye pays attention and Christian reflection does not fail, about considering and seeing that, once human accusations, whether true or false, are set aside, the Church must be sought in those Scriptures in which Christ her Redeemer became known? Just as we do not listen to those who say against Christ that his body was stolen from the tomb by the disciples, so we must not listen to those who say against his Church that it exists only among Africans and their very few associates. For the apostle says* that true Christians are members of Christ*. Therefore, just as we do not believe that Christ’s dead flesh disappeared from the tomb through anyone’s theft, so we must not believe that his living members disappeared from the world through anyone’s sin. Since Christ is the head and the Church the body, it is not difficult to see both commended together in the gospel: the head against the slanders of the Jews, and the body against the accusations of heretics. For the passage,* Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead on the third day*, is against those who say that his dead body was removed from the tomb; what follows,* and that repentance and forgiveness of sins should be preached in his name among all nations, beginning from Jerusalem*, is against those who say that the Church does not exist throughout the world. Thus, in one short passage and a few words, both the enemy of the head and the enemy of the body are repelled and, if they attend faithfully, corrected.*

*We grieve all the more that our brothers maintain these hostilities because it is so clear that they share with us the same Scriptures containing these most explicit testimonies. The Jews who deny Christ’s resurrection at least do not accept the gospel. But these brothers of ours are bound by the authority of both Testaments, yet they wish to accuse us of handing over the gospel and refuse to believe it when it is read. Perhaps now, however, having undertaken preparations for this conference, they have searched the sacred Scriptures more carefully. In them they have found innumerable testimonies promising that the Church would exist among all nations and throughout the whole world, just as we see it beginning to be fulfilled and made present in the gospel, the apostolic letters, and the Acts of the Apostles. There we read the very places, cities, and provinces through which it grew, beginning from Jerusalem, to spread from there into Africa too, by growth rather than migration. Yet they found no testimony in the divine words saying that it would perish from the other parts of the world and remain only in the African party of Donatus. They saw how absurd it is that so many divine testimonies should be read concerning a Church destined to perish, while no testimony from the Lord can be found concerning the Church that, as they suppose, was destined to please him. Perhaps, reflecting on these things, they wished to assemble together at the place of our conference to end empty and destructive hostilities contrary to eternal salvation, so that longstanding discord might end rather than a new disturbance arise.*

*We believe that they may at last have reflected that even the point over which they are usually so bitterly angered with us is not to be blamed: the kings of the earth, who were foretold so long ago as destined to serve Christ the Lord, establish laws against heretics and schismatics for the sake of Catholic peace. For ancient kings, not only of the Hebrew nation but also foreigners, issued most threatening commands to all the peoples of their kingdoms, forbidding anyone not only to do but even to say anything against the God of Israel, that is, the true God. Their own predecessors sent the very case of Caecilian, from which this division arose, as an accusation to the emperor Constantine through the proconsul Anulinus. Surely they appear to have done this for no other reason than that the emperor Constantine might decree something by royal authority against those defeated and in favor of those victorious. By searching the public archives, they could have discovered—and perhaps the necessity of this conference led them to do so—that the whole case was concluded long ago. After the ecclesiastical judgments acquitting Caecilian, judgment was also given by the emperor to whose examination they first submitted and afterward brought the entire matter. There they could also have found that the case of Felix of Aptunga, Caecilian’s ordainer, whom they had called the source of all evils in their council, was cleared through an inquiry conducted by the proconsul Aelianus at that same emperor’s command.*

*Moreover, if they attended to and recognized what was easy to see—that in the holy Scriptures Christ’s Church is promised as continuing with weeds, chaff, and bad fish mingled within it until the time of harvest, winnowing, and arrival at the shore—they could surely have reflected that, even if Caecilian and his fellow bishops had a bad case, they could not have prejudiced the Christian world. God promised that world so long ago, when few believed, and has now fulfilled his promise before the eyes of many. Unless, indeed, a sinning man had greater power against the Church than God’s oath had for the Church, and what iniquity lost outweighed what truth promised! Perhaps they have now seen how foolish and impious it is to think this. They have also reflected that, after condemning the Maximianists who had condemned Primian, they took care to have them expelled from the basilicas through the earthly authorities. In their own more recent example they learned more certainly that it is no sin for the Church to request such action from authorities of this kind against its rebels. Moreover, they afterward received some of those condemned. When condemning them, they had granted a period of grace to many others belonging to the same schismatic fellowship, saying that these had remained untainted in communion with Maximian, that sacrilegious offshoot. They did not dare invalidate or repeat baptism administered either by the condemned men or by their associates, although it had been given outside, in schism. They have surely judged sufficiently that their own example condemns their arguments against us. We should believe that they now understand how unworthy and intolerable it is that, while they sit in episcopal chairs with these men, and Primian himself sits together with those who condemned him and were condemned in his case, so that there may be peace in the party of Donatus, the Christian world should be defamed on account of Caecilian so that Christ’s unity may not live in peace.*

*Perhaps, reflecting on all these things and moved by the fear of God, they wished everyone to be present at the conference with an intention of peace rather than disorder. As for their saying that they are all present to make their number evident because their opponents have often falsely claimed they are few: if our people have ever said this, it could most truthfully have been said of places where our fellow bishops, clergy, and laity are far more numerous, especially in the Proconsular Province. Indeed, with the exception of consular Numidia, our people easily outnumber them in the other African provinces too. Or, certainly, in comparison with all the nations through which Catholic communion extends, we most rightly say that they are very few. But if they now wished their number to be known, would it not become known more orderly and peacefully through their signatures, which your edict instructed them to add to their mandate in your sight? What, then, does their desire to have everyone present at the conference mean? If they do not have peace in mind, what will they not disturb if they speak, and what will they do there if they remain silent? Even if there is no shouting, the mere whispering of so many will make enough noise to hinder the conference.*

*What does it mean that they thought fit to state in their notarial notice that they rightly demanded everyone’s attendance because everyone had been summoned to come? As though the few who should attend could have been chosen except by all those who came, so that they might sign their selection in your presence! Thus everyone would be present in the few, because the few had been chosen by everyone. Therefore they are contemplating either disorder or peace. We desire the one and guard against the other. Accordingly, lest—God forbid—they should be preparing what we guard against rather than what we desire, we agree that all of them may attend, while only the number from our side that Your Eminence considered sufficient will be present. Thus, if any disorder arises through the crowds, it may rightly be attributed only to those whose side includes a multitude entirely unnecessary for business to be conducted by a few. But if that multitude is needed to bring about unity, as we desire with all our prayers, ardently long for, and humbly beseech the Lord to grant, we will all be present whenever they wish. With the help of him who grants this gift, we will hasten eagerly toward so great a good, saying:* You are our brothers*, no longer to those who hate us, but to those who embrace us now that hatred has ended*, so that the Lord’s name may be honored and he may appear to them in joy*, as they now experience with us* how good and pleasant it is for brothers to dwell together in unity*.* (Ps 133 [132], 1).

And in another hand: *We wish you good health in God, son*. Likewise, in another hand*: Aurelius, bishop of the Catholic church of Carthage*, *I have signed*. Likewise, in another hand: *I, Silvanus of the primatial see of the province of Numidia, have signed.*

*The case of the Catholic Church is more than adequately defended by divine testimonies against all the false accusations with which schismatics and heretics, themselves divided and separated in their respective regions or provinces, attack her, spread everywhere, through their empty and sacrilegious errors. In Africa, moreover, the case against the Donatists has been concluded by ecclesiastical judgments and an imperial judgment, as was fitting and necessary. Nevertheless, this must be made known and explained more insistently to people who do not know it, so that we may gain them for the eternal salvation of Christian love. Therefore, by the will of God the Lord, we have resolved to assemble in this church of the city of Carthage and select suitable brothers and colleagues to undertake the defense of this case and refute the utterly false accusations that the party of Donatus has brought against the Catholic Church, which spreads throughout the whole world, as the divine words testify of her.*

*For the Church of Christ was promised by the divine and truthful voice to our holy fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and to all peoples who become their descendants and children by imitating their faith, in such a way that no human and slanderous voice can convict her. For God spoke to Abraham in these words:* By myself I have sworn, says the Lord, because you have done this and have not spared your beloved son for my sake, I will surely bless you and abundantly multiply your offspring like the stars of heaven and the sand beside the seashore. Your offspring will possess the cities of their enemies as an inheritance, and in your offspring all the nations of the earth will be blessed, because you have obeyed my voice (Gen 22:16–18). *To Isaac too the Lord spoke these words on the same subject:* I will establish my oath that I swore to your father Abraham. I will multiply your offspring like the stars of heaven, and I will give you and your offspring all this land, and in your name all the nations of the earth will be blessed, because your father Abraham obeyed my voice and kept my commands, my righteous ordinances, and my laws (Gen 26:3–5). *Speaking also to Jacob, he promises the same thing with the clearest assurance:* Your offspring will be like the sand of the earth and will spread toward the sea, toward the south, toward the north, and toward the east, and in you and in your offspring all the tribes of the earth will be blessed (Gen 28:14). *Accordingly, the prophet Isaiah, harmonizing with these words and foreseeing in the Holy Spirit the Church that was to come, addresses her thus:* Rejoice, barren woman who does not bear; break forth and cry aloud, you who are not in labor, for the children of the deserted woman are more numerous than those of her who has a husband. For the Lord has said: Enlarge the place of your tent and your courts; plant your stakes, do not hold back; lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes. You will spread further to the right and left, and your offspring will possess nations, and you will inhabit deserted cities. Do not fear, for you will prevail; do not be ashamed because you were despised, for you will forget your shame forever and will not remember the disgrace of your widowhood. For I am the Lord who made you; the Lord is his name. And he who delivered you, the God of Israel, will be called the God of the whole earth (Isa 54:1–5). *Again, in another place:* The Lord will reveal his holy arm in the sight of all nations, and all nations, to the farthest reaches of the earth, will see the salvation that comes from God (Isa 52:10). *Again he says:* I have appointed you as a covenant for the people, as a light to the nations, that salvation may reach the ends of the earth (Isa 49:6). *The prophet Malachi too, foreseeing that the sacrifice of the first people would cease and that throughout the whole world, that is, from the rising of the sun to its setting, an everlasting sacrifice would be celebrated with the Lord’s name glorified among the nations, speaks thus to the Jews:* I take no pleasure in you, says the Lord Almighty, and I will not accept sacrifice from your hands. For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is glorified among the nations, and in every place incense and a pure sacrifice are offered to my name, for my name is great among the nations, says the Lord Almighty (Mal 1:10–11). *Jeremiah too foretells that the nations will come to the Lord from the ends of the earth, condemning the superstitions of idols:* Lord, my strength, my help, and my refuge in the day of troubles, the nations will come to you from the ends of the earth and say: How false were the idols our fathers possessed, and there is no profit in them (Jer 16:19). *It was foretold, however, not that the nations would come to God from other places into one particular place on earth, but that in their own places they would worship him in whom they would believe after condemning idols. Thus the prophet Zephaniah testifies:* The Lord will prevail against them and destroy all the gods of the nations of the earth, and all the islands of the nations will worship him, each from its own place (Zeph 2:11).

*With these and many more testimonies of the Law and the Prophets affirming that the Church spreads throughout the whole world, the divine Psalms also harmonize:* The Lord said to me: You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance and the ends of the earth as your possession (Ps 2:7–8). *Again, in another psalm, concerning Christ the Lord under the figure of Solomon:* He will rule from sea to sea and from the river to the ends of the earth. Ethiopians will fall before him, and his enemies will lick the earth. The kings of Tarshish and the islands will offer gifts; the kings of Arabia and Sheba will bring presents. All the kings of the earth will worship him; all nations will serve him (Ps 72 [71]:8–11). *Again, in another psalm*: The Lord, the God of gods, has spoken and called the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting; from Zion is the splendor of his beauty (Ps 50 [49]:1–2).

*Therefore the Lord Jesus himself, who had previously spoken through the mouths of the prophets, after rising from the dead promised the Church through his own mouth:* Everything written about me in the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms had to be fulfilled, *he said*. Then he opened their understanding so that they might understand the Scriptures, and said to them: Thus it is written, and thus Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead on the third day, and repentance and forgiveness of sins had to be preached in his name among all nations, beginning from Jerusalem (Luke 24:44–47). *It was as though he were explaining in other words the testimony of the psalm that we quoted last. For what is written there,* he called the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting, *is what the Lord expressed by* among all nations*; and what is written there,* from Zion is the splendor of his beauty*, is what the Lord expressed by*: beginning from Jerusalem. *For who does not know that Zion and Jerusalem are one city, from which the suffering and resurrection of the Lord Jesus Christ began to be proclaimed throughout the world in a most glorious proclamation to purify and save the peoples? Therefore, when he was about to ascend into heaven, foreseeing the grounds on which carnal people would dispute in defense of their divisions and separations, and when the disciples asked to hear something from him about the end of the world, he said:* It is not *for you, he said,* to know the times that the Father has placed under his own authority. But you will receive the power of the Holy Spirit, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, throughout Judea and Samaria, and to the whole earth (Acts 1:7–8). *Following the authority of this heavenly teaching, the apostles commend the Church as bearing fruit and growing throughout the world* (Col 1:6)*. And to prevent Christ’s members from dividing themselves into parties under human names, the apostle addresses the carnal people who had already begun to do this:* I appeal to you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all speak in agreement and that there be no schisms among you, but that you be united in the same understanding and the same judgment. For it has been reported to me, brothers, by Chloe’s people that there are quarrels among you, and each of you says: I belong to Paul; I to Apollos; I to Cephas; and I to Christ. Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in Paul’s name? (1 Cor 1:10–13). *Accordingly, we recognize as our brothers in Christ’s sacraments these very people against whose error and for whose salvation we have selected, in Christ’s name, men to answer their false and empty accusations. Nor do we detest their perversity, but we approve Christ’s own sacraments in them and do not dare invalidate them. For we remember the command given through the prophet:* Hear the word of the Lord, you who tremble; say, You are our brothers, to those who hate you and detest you, so that the Lord’s name may be honored and he may appear to them in joy (Isa 66:5, according to the Septuagint). *Let them speak what follows to themselves for their own salvation, or hear it from the prophet rather than from us. We speak more gently, so that we may have peace with them when corrected rather than a quarrel with them in their perversity. We call them our brothers so that the Lord’s name may be honored. They hate and detest us and deny that they are our brothers precisely because they honor human names more than the Lord’s name.*

*Therefore let our brothers and fellow bishops whom we have selected to refute their accusations defend the holy Church against their objections in the conference to be held with them. For they say that this Church, which we have received as foretold in the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms, manifested through the Savior’s work and redeemed by his blood, spread and planted through the apostles’ labors,* beginning from Jerusalem, *where the Holy Spirit came upon one hundred and twenty souls gathered together and, by making those upon whom he came speak in all the languages* *of the nations, taught by a most evident sign that the Church would exist among all nations and speak in the languages of every nation—this Church, then, which thus began at Jerusalem, as foretold and fulfilled, and spread from there through Judea, Samaria, and the other parts of the world by the apostles’ preaching, whose letters and acts name cities and provinces extending far and wide, into* *which they brought her* with their beautiful feet*, and from which she also grew into Africa—this Church, as we have said, is alleged by those against whose error we entrust the conference and defense to our colleagues to have perished, polluted by unspecified sins of Caecilian, formerly bishop of the church of Carthage, and his associates, leaving its remnants in the party of Donatus. Let those whom we commission expose and refute this empty and sacrilegious accusation by first distinguishing and separating the Church’s case from that of certain individuals. For it was foretold that the Church would have evil people mingled within her until, at harvest time, the weeds are separated from the wheat, the chaff is winnowed away from the grain, the sheep are separated to the right and the goats to the left, though now mingled in the same pastures, or the bad fish are divided from the good on the shore, that is, at the end of the world, though now drawn together toward it in the same nets. Any people in the Church, if good, are her wheat or grain, her sheep, her good fish; if evil, they are her weeds, chaff, goats, and bad fish. Therefore the entire harvest, threshing floor, flock, or catch is not condemned because of them. Otherwise, by rashly daring to judge supposedly evil people before the time and charging some with the sins of others, we might abandon the good because of the evil through sacrilegious separation, when we ought instead to tolerate the evil because of the good in devout unity. Therefore these enemies of the gospel’s comparisons and of such evident truth must be pressed to prove their allegation. Let them first show, if they can, how the Church, which we receive and hold from the holy Scriptures as beginning at Jerusalem and bearing fruit and growing near and far, perished through contamination by any of the people they accuse and remained in the party of Donatus.*

*If they demonstrate that this could have happened, then it will indeed be relevant to the Church’s case perhaps to undertake and examine another question: whether Caecilian and his colleagues were such* *men as they are alleged to have been, or could* *have become so notorious to the Christian unity already* *spread everywhere at that time that they stained and destroyed it to the farthest lands through the contagion of that knowledge. For our opponents themselves acknowledge that no one can be stained by another’s sins of which he is ignorant. Therefore, if they wish to demonstrate that the communion of Christian nations, extending so widely through so many lands, could have been contaminated and destroyed by the sins of Caecilian and his associates, it is not enough to show that these men were such as their accusations portray them. They must also show that their actual crimes became known to everyone whom they claim perished through that contagion.*

*But if they cannot demonstrate that the Catholic Church among all the nations in which the divine words commend her could have been destroyed by anyone else’s sins, let the case of Caecilian and those whom they thought should be accused with him be set aside for a while. It has no bearing at all on the Church’s case, since, even if it had been a bad case, it could not have contaminated her. Let those to whom we assign the duty of this defense demonstrate with utmost clarity and abundant examples what we say to repel their charges: the Church of Christ cannot be polluted and destroyed, as by a deadly corruption, by any evil people mingled within her until the end, whether unknown or even known. First, let them use the examples of the prophets, who rebuked with such stern words a sinful people offending God through many detestable abominations and, by the scourges of correction, stirred their hard and untamed minds to dreadful hostility against themselves. Yet they did not separate themselves bodily from that people, among whom there were certainly good people amid the evil. They judged it better to endure the evil through patience than to abandon the good through false accusation.*

*The Lord Jesus Christ himself, the unique example of patience, not only endured his utterly wicked betrayer, once known, among* *his disciples*, *but admitted him to their number with foreknowledge. He also said to his disciples:* You are clean, but not all of you (John 13:10*), so that they might thus know that their cleanness could not be stained by another’s uncleanness. For the apostles’ time, the apostle Paul is a witness, groaning among false brothers* (2 Cor 11:26), *and noting in his letter some who preached Christ out of envy, which is certainly a diabolical vice* (Phil 1:15), *showing how the good, in their careful devotion to preserving Christ’s unity, tolerated the evil in the communion of the sacraments without any destruction of their own, while utterly recoiling from their wickedness. As for how good bishops after the apostles also tolerated, for the sake of Christ’s unity, not only evil laypeople but evil fellow bishops, the letters of the martyr Cyprian bear witness. He laments some of his colleagues’ greed, extending to cruel usury and the plundering of estates. Yet he did not become like them through contamination by the evil, but endured such men through love of the good, teaching this not only by example but also by precept. For in one of his letters he says:* Even if weeds appear to be in the Church, our faith or love must not therefore be hindered, so that, because we see weeds in the Church, we ourselves leave the Church (Letter to Maximus, now 54).

*The truth of this must also be proved from the judgment of the very people who oppose it. After condemning Maximian for his sacrilegious schism, they allowed his associates, who had gone out from them with him and separated themselves from their communion, a period in which to return, saying that they had not been polluted by the shoots of that sacrilegious offshoot* [Judgment of the Council of Bagai.]. *We cannot see how this could rightly have been said, since the fellowship of one schism held them all united. Nevertheless, let them be compelled to understand from this, even if late, that in Catholic unity the evil can far less harm the good, like chaff harming grain, if in a schismatic division, where there is only chaff, Maximian was thought unable to stain his associates in that same division merely because they were not present at his ordination. Let them see how thoughtlessly they say that Caecilian, whatever sort of man he was, and any of his colleagues could destroy Christian unity among so many distant nations, when they suppose that Maximian did not destroy even his African partners in schism. Thus it will appear that Caecilian’s case, whatever question it involves, has no bearing on the case of the Church whose defense we enjoin.*

*As for their accusation against our baptism—or rather Christ’s baptism, which we possess and administer—and their invalidating it, through a deplorable error, in those whom they deceive: it must be defended as the Church herself is defended, whose gift it is. For they will no longer dare accuse the baptism we administer if the Church to which we belong is cleared of their charges. But if they think they should raise their customary objection that we recognize rather than reject Christ’s baptism even when administered outside our communion, and do not dare invalidate it, let them be answered with as many arguments and testimonies as possible: the Church’s divine gifts, found even among thieves, usurpers, or robbers, must be recognized rather than denied. For just as the apostle says of some that* they hold the truth in unrighteousness (Rom 1:18), *so all who possess or administer Christ’s baptism outside Christ’s Church hold the truth of a divine sacrament in the unrighteousness of human error. Therefore, when human unrighteousness is corrected, divine truth must not be violated. Those who think Christ’s baptism should be denied because heretics also administer it could think Christ himself should be denied because demons also confess him. Thus those outside the Church’s communion who possess her baptism must come to her, not so that what was lacking may be present, but so that what was present may benefit them.*

*Nor should it be thought that they will now sustain a lengthy dispute on this point, since here too they can be instructed by their own judgment and example. For in those whom they received from Maximian’s schism together with the men they had condemned, they preferred to recognize rather than invalidate Christ’s baptism administered outside their own communion. Accordingly, the passage they habitually quote without understanding it correctly,* Do not share in the sins of others (1 Tim 5:22)*—which is to be observed through difference of conduct, not bodily separation—they are compelled to understand by considering those whom they declared unpolluted while sharing the sacraments with the condemned Maximian. And the saying that they habitually apply to baptism,* One who is baptized by a dead man (Sir 34:30), *and other passages of this kind, they are compelled to understand correctly in those same persons, since they did not dare reject or repeat baptism administered in schism by the very men whom, in condemning them, they had called dead. As for their habitual accusation that we are compelled to take action with earthly authorities for their correction and for Christ’s unity, they must again be compelled to approve this in the case of the Maximianists themselves. When they accused these men before earthly judges as their own schismatics or heretics, to have them expelled from the basilicas, they sometimes even cited the Council of Bagai, at which they had condemned them. All these things must be said so that their own judgment may admonish them at last to lay aside dissension and, with the animosity of false and empty accusations exhausted or cast away, to love Catholic peace and embrace undivided love. Without it no human being can be good, whatever other good he may possess.*

*When these matters have been completed, we do not wish them to abandon Caecilian’s case itself. Let them undertake it, however, not as the case of the Church our mother, but as that of a bishop our brother; not to examine it again for a new conclusion, but to show through ecclesiastical and public documents that it was concluded long ago. Some of these documents will suffice if our opponents are willing to trust them when we present them. If they are unwilling, they can be authenticated by the archives themselves. They will show that our opponents submitted Caecilian’s case as an accusation to the emperor Constantine through the proconsul Anulinus, and that this same emperor, after the ecclesiastical judgments acquitting Caecilian, heard the whole case with the parties present and judged Caecilian innocent and his accusers utterly slanderous. They will also show that the innocence of Felix of Aptunga, Caecilian’s ordainer, whom they themselves called the source of evils in their council, became clear in an inquiry conducted by the proconsul Aelianus at the same emperor’s command. Whatever accusations they think should be brought against any of our colleagues, no longer in the manner of conferring or debating but of reviling and quarreling, they must be answered that these persons’ cases too do not pertain to the Church’s case, whose defense we now enjoin. Nor are they denied an examination of those cases, but it is postponed because of the demands of the present occasion. Once the Church’s case, which the most clement emperor has now ordered by a recent instruction to be examined and made clear, has been concluded, whatever charges they think fit to bring against any of our people may, before judges of both our side and theirs whom they choose, after they have joined Catholic unity, either be shown to have been cleared or be proved and punished.*

*We commission our brothers and fellow bishops Aurelius, Alypius, Augustine, Vincent, Fortunatus, Fortunatianus, and Possidius to carry out these matters as written, together with whatever fuller or stronger arguments the Lord may grant them. They attest their acceptance of this mandate by their signatures, just as we attest our granting of it by ours, promising to uphold whatever, with the Lord’s help, they accomplish in this defense. We associate with them Novatus, Florentius, Maurentius, Priscus, Serenianus, Boniface, and Scillatius, with whom they may consult whenever necessary. To safeguard the transcription of the proceedings, we appoint Deuterius, Leo, Asterius, and Restitutus.*

**222.** Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said:

*As the parties have agreed, let the business resume after an intervening day. Both parties must ensure that they have the designated custodians watch over the clerks and notebooks while the sheets are transcribed, so that everything is faithfully written down. They must also assign two more custodians each, making four each as arranged, to those who have recently taken notes*.

**223.** Bishop Alypius of the Catholic Church said:

*The other two are present, as the staff has already reported: our brothers Leo and Asterius.* And in another hand: *I have checked*. Bishop Primian said: *We too provide four custodians of the tablets. We have already provided two; now we appoint two more, our colleagues Veratianus and Victor, who are present*. And in another hand: *I, Bishop Petilian, have checked*. Marcellinus, the most distinguished tribune and notary, said: *Even with these men opposing, what was recorded afterward will be transcribed tomorrow, the tablets now being sealed*. And in another hand: *Let them be issued*. Also in another hand: *We, Hilarus and Martialis, clerks, have issued them.*

Here End the Proceedings of the First Hearing.
