# Book II

## The authority of Cyprian is not for the Donatists, but for the Catholics.

**1. 1.** I have set out to show, with the Lord's help, how much the things which the party of Donatus brings forth against us, as though from the authority of the blessed Cyprian, make for us—that is, for Catholic peace—and how much they are against those by whom they are brought forth. If, then, the necessity of replying compels me to recall again the things I have already set down in other books (though I shall do this sparingly), it ought not to be burdensome to those who have already read and hold them: for the things necessary for instruction must be conveyed rather often to the slower, and when the same things are handled and treated in manifold and various ways, they help even those endowed with more capacious understanding, both toward ease of knowing and toward abundance in discussion.

I also know how it tends to sadden a reader when, from the book he holds in his hands, when he has come upon some knotty point of a question, he is sent to another in which to seek its solution—one which perhaps he does not have. Therefore, whatever we have already said in other books, if the necessity of the present questions compels us to repeat briefly, let those who know it pardon us, and those who do not know it not be offended; for it is better to offer it to one who has it than to put off one who has it not.

## The example of the apostle Peter.

**1. 2.** What, then, do these men say, when they are choked by the force of the truth to which they are unwilling to consent? *Cyprian,* they say, *whose great merit and great learning we know, together with many of his fellow bishops bringing their own opinions, decreed in a council that heretics or schismatics—that is, all who are outside the communion of the one Church—do not have baptism; and therefore anyone baptized by them who comes to the Church is to be baptized in the Church.* The authority of Cyprian does not frighten me, because the humility of Cyprian restores me.

We know indeed the great merit of Cyprian, bishop and martyr; but is it greater than that of Peter, apostle and martyr? Of whom the same Cyprian, in his letter to Quintus, speaks thus: *For not even Peter,* he says, *whom the Lord chose first, and upon whom he built his Church*[^1], *when Paul afterward disputed with him about circumcision, claimed anything for himself insolently or assumed anything arrogantly, so as to say that he held the primacy and that he ought rather to be obeyed by those newly come and later; nor did he despise Paul because he had earlier been a persecutor of the Church: but he admitted the counsel of truth, and readily assented to the lawful reasoning which Paul defended—giving us, of course, a lesson both of concord and of patience, that we should not obstinately love our own opinions, but rather count as our own whatever is at any time usefully and wholesomely suggested by our brothers and colleagues, if it be true and lawful*[^2].

See where Cyprian recalls what we too have learned in the holy Scriptures: that the apostle Peter, in whom the primacy of the apostles stands out with such excellent grace, was accustomed to act about circumcision otherwise than the truth demanded, and was corrected by the later apostle Paul.

If, then, Peter could in some matter walk not rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, so as to compel the gentiles to judaize—which Paul writes in that letter in which he called God to witness that he was not lying; for he says, *In what I write to you, behold, before God, I do not lie*[^3]; and after this so holy and awesome attestation of God, he narrated all these things, among which he said, *When I saw that they were not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before them all: If you, being a Jew, live like the gentiles and not like the Jews, how do you compel the gentiles to judaize?*[^4]—if Peter, I say, could against the rule of truth, which the Church afterward held, compel the gentiles to judaize, why could not Cyprian, against the rule of truth, which the whole Church afterward held, compel heretics or schismatics to be baptized again?

I think that Cyprian the bishop is compared to Peter the apostle without any insult to himself, as far as concerns the crown of martyrdom. For the rest, I ought rather to fear lest I be insulting to Peter. For who does not know that the primacy of his apostolate is to be preferred to any episcopate whatever?

But even if the grace of their chairs differs, the glory of the martyrs is nevertheless one; and if perhaps they outstrip one another in something—the hearts of those who confess, and who die in the unity of love for the true faith—the Lord knows, by whose hidden and wonderful dispensation of graces the thief hanging on the cross confesses him once and is sent that very day into paradise[^5], while Peter, following the Lord, denies him three times[^6] and is deferred from the crown: it is rash for us to judge this matter.

Nevertheless, if anyone now were to compel someone to be circumcised after the Jewish manner and so to be baptized, the human race would detest this kind of thing far more than if someone were compelled to be rebaptized.

Therefore, since Peter, in doing that, is corrected by the later Paul, and, kept by the bond of peace and unity, is advanced to martyrdom, how much more easily and forcibly is what has been confirmed by the decrees of the universal Church to be preferred either to the authority of one bishop or to the council of one province? Since this same Cyprian so stated what seemed to him that he willed to be in the peace of unity even with those who held a different opinion on this matter—which his own first address indicates, at the beginning of that same council which is brought forth by these men. For it runs thus:

## The prologue of the Council of Carthage.

**2. 3.** "When very many bishops from the province of Africa, Numidia, and Mauretania had come together as one at Carthage on the first of September, with their presbyters and deacons, in the presence also of a very large part of the people, and the letter of Jubaian written to Cyprian had been read, likewise Cyprian's reply to Jubaian about baptizing heretics, and what Jubaian afterward wrote back to Cyprian, Cyprian said: You have heard, most beloved colleagues, what Jubaian our fellow bishop has written to me, consulting our littleness about the unlawful and profane baptism of heretics; and what I wrote back to him, judging, of course, what once and again and often we have judged: that heretics coming to the Church ought to be baptized and sanctified with the baptism of the Church.

Likewise there has been read to you another letter of Jubaian, in which, out of his sincere and religious devotion, replying to our letter, he not only agreed but also gave thanks that he had been instructed. It remains that we each set forth what we think on this very matter, judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently. For none of us has set himself up to be a bishop of bishops, or by tyrannical terror to drive his colleagues to the necessity of obeying, since every bishop, according to the license of his liberty and power, has his own free judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another; but let us all await the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ, who alone has the power both of setting us over the governance of his Church and of judging our conduct"[^7].

## The writings of Cyprian are not only to be read; his example is to be followed.

**3. 4.** Now let the proud and swollen necks of the heretics, if they dare, lift themselves up against the holy humility of this address. Insane Donatists, whom we desire and wish to return to the peace and unity of the holy Church and to be healed in it, what do you say to these things? You certainly are accustomed to set against us the letters of Cyprian, the opinion of Cyprian, the council of Cyprian: why do you take up the authority of Cyprian for your schism, and reject his example for the peace of the Church?

And who does not know that the holy canonical Scripture, both of the Old and of the New Testament, is contained within its own fixed limits, and that it is so set before all the later writings of bishops that there can be no doubt or dispute at all about it, whether what is established to be written in it is true or right; but that the letters of bishops, which were or are written after the confirmation of the canon, may lawfully be censured both by the discourse, perhaps wiser, of anyone more skilled in that matter, and by the weightier authority and more learned prudence of other bishops, and by councils, if anything in them has perhaps deviated from the truth;

and that the councils themselves which are held in the several regions or provinces yield, without any quibbling, to the authority of the plenary councils which are held from the whole Christian world; and that the plenary councils themselves, the earlier, are often amended by the later, when by some experience of things what was closed is opened and what lay hidden is recognized—without any pride of sacrilegious arrogance, without any inflated neck of presumption, without any contention of livid envy, but with holy humility, with Catholic peace, with Christian love?

## Certainly Cyprian too would have yielded to the truth, had this been declared.

**4. 5.** Therefore the holy Cyprian, the more exalted the more humble, who so loved the lesson of Peter that he said, *giving us, of course, a lesson both of concord and of patience, that we should not obstinately love our own opinions, but rather count as our own whatever is at any time usefully and wholesomely suggested by our brothers and colleagues, if it be true and lawful*[^8], sufficiently shows that he would most readily have corrected his opinion, if anyone had shown him that the baptism of Christ could be given by those who went outside in the same way that it could not be lost when they went outside; of which we have already said much.

Nor would we ourselves dare to assert any such thing, were we not confirmed by the most harmonious authority of the universal Church; to which he too would without doubt have yielded, if already at that time the truth of this question, refined and declared, had been made firm through a plenary council.

For if he praises and proclaims Peter as corrected patiently and harmoniously by one later colleague, how much more quickly would he himself, with the council of his province, have yielded to the authority of the whole world once the truth was made plain? Indeed, so holy a soul, so peaceable, could most readily have consented even to one man speaking and demonstrating the truth:

and perhaps it was done, but we do not know. For not all the things that were done among the bishops at that time could be committed to memory and to writing, nor do we know all the things that were committed to writing.

For how could this matter, enveloped in such great clouds of altercation, be brought to the clear illustration and confirmation of a plenary council, unless first it stood, treated for a rather long time through the regions of the world by many disputations and conferences of bishops on this side and on that? And this is what the soundness of peace brings about: that when some rather obscure things are sought for a longer time, and, on account of the difficulty of finding them, give birth to diverse opinions in brotherly debate, until one comes to the clear truth, the bond of unity nevertheless remains, lest in a part cut off there remain the incurable wound of error.

## Perhaps he did so, but the letter is not found.

**5. 6.** And so very often something is revealed less to the more learned, that their patient and humble love, in which the fruit is greater, may be proved—either how they keep unity when they think differently in more obscure matters, or how they accept the truth when they recognize that it has been declared against what they thought. Of these two we hold one made manifest in the blessed Cyprian, that is, how he kept unity with those from whom he thought differently. For he says: *judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^9].

But the other, that is, how he could have accepted the truth once it was found, against what he had thought—even if the letters are silent, his merits cry out; if the letter is not found, his crown bears witness; if the council of bishops does not show it, the fellowship of the angels shows it. For it is no small proof of a most peaceable soul, to have deserved martyrdom in that unity from which, though thinking differently, he was unwilling to separate himself.

For we are men. Hence to be wise about something otherwise than the matter stands is a human temptation. But to love one's own opinion too much, or to envy those who are better, to the point of arriving at the sacrilege of cutting off communion and founding a schism or heresy, is a diabolical presumption. But in nothing to be wise otherwise than the matter stands is an angelic perfection. Since, then, we are men, but in hope are angels of God, to whom we shall be equal in the resurrection[^10], as long as we do not have the perfection of the angel, let us not have the presumption of the devil. Therefore the Apostle says: *Let no temptation take hold of you but such as is human*[^11].

It is human, then, to be wise about something otherwise. For this reason he says in another place: *As many of us, therefore, as are perfect, let us be of this mind; and if you are of any other mind, this too God will reveal to you*[^12]. But to whom does he reveal it, when he wills, whether in this life or after this life, except to those walking in the way of peace and straying into no severance? Not such as these men are, who have not known the way of peace[^13], and who have broken the bond of unity for no other reason.

And therefore the Apostle, when he had said, *And if you are of any other mind, this too God will reveal to you*[^14], lest they should think that what they thought otherwise could be revealed to them apart from the way of peace, at once added: *Nevertheless, in that to which we have attained, let us walk in it*[^15].

Walking in this, Cyprian, by most persevering endurance—not with blood shed, but shed in unity (for if he handed over his body to be burned and had not love, it would profit him nothing[^16])—attained through the confession of martyrdom to the angelic light; so that, if not before, there at least he might recognize as revealed what, while he was of another mind, he did not set the opinion of a different view above the bond of unity.

## The Donatists, who separated themselves, are in the greatest self-contradiction.

**6. 7.** You, then, Donatists, what do you say to these things? If our opinion about baptism is true, all who were of another mind in Cyprian's times are not separated from the unity of the Church, until God should reveal to them that about which they thought differently: why then have you broken the bond of peace by a sacrilegious separation? But if your opinion about baptism is true, Cyprian and the rest, with whom you report that he held such a council, remained in unity with those who thought differently. Why have you broken the bond of peace?

Whichever of these you choose, you are compelled to pass sentence against your own severance. Answer: why did you separate? Why did you erect an altar against the whole world? Why are you not in communion with the Churches to which you hold and read the apostolic letters were sent, and according to which you say you live? Answer: why did you separate?

Certainly for this reason: lest you perish by communion with the wicked. How then did Cyprian and so many of his colleagues not perish? For although they believed that heretics and schismatics did not have baptism, yet, with these received without baptism—when they believed sins so monstrous and so sacrilegious to be upon them—they nevertheless chose to be in communion with them rather than to be separated from unity, Cyprian saying: *judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^17].

## On account of communion with the wicked, they do not perish.

**6. 8.** If, then, the just perish by such communion with the wicked, already in the times of Cyprian the Church had perished. Whence, then, arose the origin of Donatus—where was he catechized, where baptized, where ordained—when the contagion of communion had already extinguished the Church? But if the Church existed, the wicked could in no way harm the good in one communion. Why did you separate?

Behold, in unity I see Cyprian and his other colleagues, who, having held a council, judged that all who had been baptized outside the communion of the Church do not have baptism, and therefore that it must be given to them when they come. But behold again, in the same unity I see certain men think differently on this matter, and not dare, once the baptism of Christ has been recognized in them, to baptize again those who have come from heretics or schismatics.

All these the Catholic unity embraces in its maternal bosom, bearing one another's burdens[^18], and striving to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace[^19], until the Lord should reveal to whichever of them, if they thought anything otherwise[^20]. If these thought true things, were they not contaminated by those, or were they contaminated? If those thought true things, were they not contaminated by these, or were they contaminated?

Choose what you will. If they were contaminated, then already there was no Church: answer, whence did you come forth to this place? But if the Church remained, in no way are the good contaminated by the wicked through such communion: answer, why did you break the bond?

## Heretics coming to the Church do not contaminate the good.

**6. 9.** Or do schismatics, received without baptism, perhaps not contaminate, while betrayers of the holy Books do contaminate? That your own people were betrayers, the most manifest records of the deeds bear witness. And if at that time you had spoken the truth against those whom you were accusing, you would have persuaded the unity of the world of your case, so that, you being retained, they would be excluded.

But if you tried to do this and did not obtain it, the world is innocent, which trusted ecclesiastical judges rather than defeated litigants; while if you were unwilling to plead your case, the world is innocent, which could not condemn men unheard. Why, then, have you separated yourselves from the innocent? You cannot defend the sacrilege of your schism. But I pass over these things; this I say: if betrayers could contaminate you—men who have not been convicted by you, and by whom you have been defeated—much more could the sacrileges of schismatics and heretics, received without baptism according to your opinion, contaminate Cyprian. Yet he did not separate himself. And because the Church remained, it is manifest that it could not be contaminated.

Why, then, have you separated yourselves—I do not say from the innocent, which is proved, but from those very betrayers, which is not proved? Or, as I had begun to say, are the crimes of betrayers graver than those of schismatics? Let us not bring forward deceitful scales, on which we may weigh what we wish, and as we wish, saying at our own discretion, This is grave, this is light; but let us bring forward the divine scale from the holy Scriptures, as from the Lord's treasures, and weigh on it what is graver; nay, let us not weigh it, but recognize what has been weighed by the Lord.

At that time in which the Lord showed that former offenses were to be guarded against by recent examples of punishments, an idol was both fashioned and adored, a prophetic book was burned by the anger of a contemptuous king, and a schism was attempted; idolatry was punished by the sword[^21], the burning of the book by slaughter in war and foreign captivity[^22], the schism by the gaping of the earth, with its authors buried alive and the rest consumed by heavenly fire[^23].

Who now would doubt that what was more grievously avenged was the more wickedly committed? Coming from such sacrilegious men, without baptism, as you say, if they did not contaminate Cyprian, how could betrayers—not convicted, but invented—contaminate you?

And if these men had not handed over the Books to be burned, but had burned them themselves with their own hands, they would certainly have been guilty of a lesser crime than if they committed schism; because the one was more mildly, the other more grievously avenged—not by human discretion, but by divine judgment.

## The reply of the Donatists.

**7. 10.** Why, then, have you separated yourselves? If there is any sense in you,

you certainly see that you can find nothing to reply. *Not to such an extent,* they say, *have all things failed, that we cannot reply: It is our will. Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own master he stands or falls*[^24].

They do not understand that this was said to those who wished to judge not about manifest deeds, but about the hearts of others. For how does Paul himself speak so much about the crimes of schisms and heresies? Or how is it sung in the Psalms: *If you truly love justice, judge what is right, you sons of men*[^25]? And why does the Lord himself say: *Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment*[^26], if it is not permitted to judge about anyone? Finally, those very men, in the case of the betrayers about whom they judged falsely, why did they judge at all about others' servants? They stood or fell to their own master.

Why, in short, about the recent Maximianists did they not hesitate, *by the truthful mouth of a plenary council,* as they say, to pronounce such a judgment as to compare them to those first schismatics whom the earth swallowed up alive[^27]? Yet some of these—which they cannot deny—they either condemned as innocent or received back as guilty.

But when the truth is spoken to which they cannot reply, they gnaw with rough mutterings: *It is our will. Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own master he stands or falls*[^28]. But when a weak sheep is spied in the wilderness, where the shepherd who might cry out against them seems to be absent, their teeth are bared, the feeble throat is broken: *You would be a good man, if you were not a betrayer. Take counsel for your soul; become a Christian.* O wicked madness! When it is said to a Christian, *Become a Christian,* what else is he taught but to deny that he is a Christian? Was there anything else that those persecutors of the Christians desired to teach, those whom whoever resisted became martyrs? Or is what is committed by a threatening sword thought lighter than what is committed by a treacherous tongue?

## The Donatists clothe themselves in sheep's skins when they use the words of Cyprian.

**7. 11.** Answer these things, you ravenous wolves, who, desiring to be clothed in sheep's skins[^29], suppose that the letters of the blessed Cyprian are for you. Did the sacrilege of schismatics contaminate Cyprian, or did it not contaminate him? If it contaminated him, then already at that time the Church perished, and there was nothing from which you could be propagated.

But if it did not contaminate him, by what crime of another can the innocent be contaminated in unity, if they cannot be contaminated by the sacrilege of schism? Why, then, have you separated yourselves? Why, while you flee the lighter things that you invent, have you committed the very sacrilege of schism, which is graver than all? Or does it perhaps now please you to confess that those were no longer either schismatics or heretics who had been baptized outside the communion of the Church, whether in some schism or in heresy, because, by crossing over to the Church and anathematizing their former errors, they had ceased to be what they were?

How, then, without baptism, had their crimes not remained upon them? Or was it the baptism of Christ, but it could not profit them outside the communion of the Church; and when they came, and, having anathematized their former error, were received into the peace of the Church through the laying on of hands, then, rooted and grounded in love—without which the rest are fruitless—it began to profit them, both unto the forgiveness of sins and unto the sanctification of life, what they had carried outside without fruit?

## The most wholesome custom of the Church not to rebaptize is ancient.

**7. 12.** Do not, then, set against us the authority of Cyprian for the repetition of baptism; but hold with us the example of Cyprian for the preservation of unity. For that question of baptism had not yet been carefully treated, and yet the Church held the most wholesome custom, even in schismatics and heretics themselves, of correcting what is wrong, not repeating what has been given; of healing what is wounded, not curing what is sound.

This custom—coming, I believe, from apostolic tradition, as many things are not found in their writings, nor in the councils of those who came after, and yet, because they are kept throughout the whole Church, are believed to have been handed down and commended by none but the apostles themselves—this most wholesome custom, then, the holy Cyprian says began, as it were, to be corrected through Agrippinus his predecessor[^30].

But as the more carefully investigated truth taught—the truth which, after great waves of doubt, was brought to the confirmation of a plenary council—it is more truly believed to have begun through Agrippinus to be corrupted, not corrected. And so, when so great a question rushed in, since it was difficult to give a reason about the forgiveness of sins and the spiritual regeneration of man, whether it could take place among heretics or among schismatics, and the authority of Agrippinus went before, along with that of some men who had agreed with him—men failing in this question, who had preferred to contrive something new rather than to keep a custom whose defense they did not understand—plausible reasonings rushed into the eyes of the soul and blocked the road to tracking down the truth.

## The reason why Cyprian spoke against this custom.

**8. 13.** Nor do I think that the blessed Cyprian, for any other reason, set forth rather freely and stated first whatever he thought against the custom, except that he might most gladly accept another, if perhaps one should arise to whom it had been better revealed; and that he might show that not only diligence in teaching but also modesty in learning is to be imitated; and that, if no one should arise who might bring forward something such as would refute all those plausible reasonings by which he was moved, he might remain in the same opinion, well aware in himself both that the truth he supposed had not been concealed, and that the unity he loved had been retained.

For he understood thus also what the Apostle says: *Let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh; but if anything is revealed to another sitting by, let the first be silent*[^31]. *In which passage,* he says, *he taught and showed that many things are revealed to individuals for the better, and that each one ought not obstinately to contend on behalf of what he had once imbibed and held, but, if anything better and more useful should appear, gladly to embrace it*[^32].

By these words, certainly, he not only admonished those to agree with him who did not see anything better, but also exhorted those, if any could, to bring forward something by which the former custom should rather be confirmed as worth keeping: so that, if it were such as could not be refuted, he himself too might show how truthfully he had said that *each one ought not obstinately to contend on behalf of what he had once imbibed and held, but, if anything better and more useful should appear, gladly to embrace it*[^33].

But because at that time there were none except those who opposed the custom to him, while they brought forward defenses of the custom itself not such as would move so great a soul, that most weighty man was unwilling that his own reasonings—even if not true (which lay hidden from him), yet not defeated—should yield to a custom that was indeed truthful, yet not yet asserted. And yet, had not Agrippinus before him, and some of his fellow bishops throughout Africa, tried even by the sentences of a council to abandon this custom, he would not have dared even to reason against it; but, troubled in so obscure a question, and everywhere beholding the universal and robust custom, he would rather have constrained himself, both by prayer and by intention of mind, toward God, that he might perceive and teach to be true what afterward seemed good to a plenary council. But when, wearied, he had taken up the authority of the preceding council which was held through Agrippinus, he preferred to defend the view of his predecessors as though it were established, rather than to labor further in inquiry. For at the end of his letter to Quintus he shows thus on what couch of authority, as though weary, he came to rest.

## The custom of rebaptizing was introduced by Agrippinus.

**9. 14.** *This indeed,* he says, *Agrippinus too, a man of good memory, together with the rest of his fellow bishops who at that time governed the Church of the Lord in the province of Africa and Numidia, decreed, and confirmed by the balanced examination of a common council: whose opinion, religious and lawful, salutary, and agreeable to the faith of the Catholic Church, we too have followed*[^34].

By this attestation he sufficiently shows that he would much rather have recalled some council held about this matter overseas or universally, had one been held. But none had yet been held, because the world was held by the strength of custom, and this alone was set against those wishing to introduce a novelty, because they could not grasp the truth. Yet afterward, while it was treated and inquired into among many on both sides, it was not only found but also brought to the authority and strength of a plenary council—after the passion of Cyprian, indeed, but before we were born.

That this was the custom of the Church—which afterward, when many windings had been discussed and the truth perceived, was confirmed by a plenary council—is sufficiently shown also by the words of the blessed Cyprian himself in that same letter to Jubaian, which is recalled to have been read in the council. For he says: *But someone says: What then will become of those who in the past, coming from heresy to the Church, were admitted without baptism?*[^35]

Here he certainly shows sufficiently what used to be done, even if he did not wish it to be done; and by the very fact that he recalls the council of Agrippinus, he plainly indicates that there was another custom of the Church. For there would have been no need for this council to wish to decree it, if it was already held by custom; and in the council itself certain opinions plainly declare that they decreed against the custom of the Church what they judged ought to be decreed. Therefore let these men consider this one thing, which is plain to all: if the authority of Cyprian is to be followed, it is rather to be followed in preserving unity than in changing the custom of the Church; but if his council is regarded, to it is to be preferred the later council of the universal Church, of which he rejoiced to be a member; and he often admonished the rest to imitate him in keeping themselves within the framework of the whole body. For both later councils are preferred to earlier ones among those who come after, and the whole is always, by the best right, preferred to its parts.

## How the Donatists reply.

**10. 15.** But what do these men do, when the holy Cyprian is shown to have been in communion with those who admitted men baptized in heresy or in schism, even if he himself did not admit them—which he most plainly declared, saying: *judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^36]? If he was stained by communion with such men, why do they follow his authority in repeating baptism? But if he was not stained by communion with such men, why do they not imitate his example in retaining unity?

Surely nothing remains here except that they say: It is our will? What else do all disgraceful and criminal men reply to the discourse of truth and justice—the lustful, the drunken, adulterers, and those shameless in every way, thieves, robbers, murderers, brigands, sorcerers, idolaters[^37]—what else do they reply when the truth convicts them, except: This I will, this delights me?

And if they have been washed with the Christian name, they even say: *Who are you to judge another's servant?*[^38] Nevertheless, these men are more modest in this, that when they suffer some punishments by the divine and human laws for their lost morals and deeds, they do not call themselves martyrs; but these others wish to have at once both the life of the sacrilegious and the reputation of the innocent, and in wicked deeds no punishment, and in just punishments the glory of martyrs:

as though the mercy and patience of God toward them were not the greater, the more, by reproving them in part, he gives place for repentance[^39], and does not cease to redouble his scourges in this life, that, considering what they suffer and why they suffer it, they may at some time come to their senses:

and that those who have already received the baptism of the Maximianists for the unity of Donatus may rather embrace the baptism of the world for the peace of Christ, may be restored to the root, may be reconciled to unity, may see that nothing has remained for them to say, but that nevertheless something has remained for them to do—that for their past deeds the sacrifice of love may be offered to the God who is appeasable, whose unity they broke by a wicked crime, to whose sacraments they inflicted such long-lasting injuries. For the Lord is merciful and compassionate, long-suffering and very merciful and truthful[^40]. Let them embrace, in the present life, the merciful and long-suffering one, and let them fear, in the future, the truthful one. For he does not will the death of the impious, so much as that he should turn back and live[^41]; for he bends his sentence against the injuries inflicted. This is our exhortation.

## The Donatists do not fear to violate the baptism of the whole world.

**11. 16.** For this reason we have them as enemies, because we speak the truth, because we are afraid to be silent, because we dread to cease from urging as much as we can, because we obey the Apostle, who says: *Preach the word, be urgent in season, out of season; reprove, exhort, rebuke*[^42]. But, as the Gospel says, *they love the glory of men more than that of God*[^43], and they fear to be reproved for a time, but do not fear to be condemned forever.

They themselves also see what evil they do, they see that there is nothing at all for them to reply; but they spread clouds before the unlearned, while they themselves are swallowed up alive—that is, they perish knowing and recognizing it[^44]. They have seen men shudder at, and gravely detest, the fact that they too have divided themselves into many schisms, and especially in the head of Africa and the most renowned city, Carthage:

they tried to patch up the disgrace of their rags. Thinking that they could carry off the Maximianists, with great effort they pressed forward strongly through Optatus the Gildonian, and inflicted on them many evils and most savage persecutions. They received some back from there, thinking that they could convert them all by the same terror; but to those whom they received back they were unwilling to do the injury of baptizing again those baptized by them in schism, or rather of having them rebaptized within by those very men by whom they had been baptized outside, and in this way they laid down a precedent against their own wicked custom. They perceive how wickedly they now act, in that, having received the baptism of the Maximianists, they violate the baptism of the whole world[^45]. But they fear their own rebaptized ones, lest perhaps these not spare them, if they themselves spare the rest; lest they exact their souls from them, if they cease to slaughter the souls of others.

## They themselves, for the peace of Donatus, received back the schismatic Maximianists.

**12. 17.** They find nothing to reply about the Maximianists they received back. If they say, We received them back as innocent, it is answered them: Then you had condemned the innocent. If they say, We did not know, then you judged rashly (so too you pronounced a rash sentence about the betrayers); and you spoke falsely: *Know that they have been condemned by the truthful mouth of a plenary council.* For the innocent could not be condemned by a truthful mouth.

If they say, We did not condemn them, the council is read out, the names both of the bishops and of the cities are read out. If they say, That council is not ours, the proconsular records are read out, in which more than once they alleged that same council, in order to exclude those very Maximianists from the basilicas, and to drive them out by the uproar of lawsuits and the onset of armed aid.

If they say, Felician of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuras, whom they afterward received back, were not with Maximian, the records are read out by which they demanded, from the public courts, on the basis of their own council which they held against the Maximianists, that these men be excluded from the basilicas. If they say, They were received for the sake of peace, it is answered: Why, then, do you not recognize the true and full peace? Who drove you, who compelled you, for the peace of Donatus to receive back a condemned schismatic, and against the peace of Christ to condemn the world unheard?

The truth certainly presses them; they see that they have nothing to reply, and they think they have nothing to do; they find nothing to say. They are not permitted to be silent; they prefer to struggle against the truth with perverse voices than, their errors confessed, to be restored to peace.

## Those who have already been rebaptized, let them return to the Church, and let prayer be made for them.

**13. 18.** But who does not understand what they can say in their heart? What, then, do we do, they say, about those whom we have already rebaptized? It is answered: Return with them to the Church; offer for healing, by the medicine of peace, those whom you have wounded; offer for raising, by the love of life, those whom you have slain. Brotherly concord avails much to propitiate God. *If two of you,* says the Lord, *agree on earth, whatever you ask shall be done for you*[^46]. If for two men, how much more for two peoples? Let us together prostrate ourselves before the Lord; share unity with us, let us share grief with you, and let love cover a multitude of sins[^47].

Seek counsel from the blessed Cyprian himself; attend to how much he presumed upon the good of unity, from which he did not break himself away from those who thought differently; and although he judged that those who were baptized outside the communion of the Church did not have baptism, he nevertheless believed that they, once simply admitted into the Church, could, on account of that very bond of unity, attain to pardon.

For thus he resolves the question which he himself set, writing to Jubaian as follows: *But someone says: What then will become of those who in the past, coming from heresy to the Church, were admitted without baptism? The Lord is able by his mercy to grant pardon, and not to separate from the gifts of his Church those who, simply admitted into the Church, have fallen asleep in the Church*[^48].

## Whether it is more pernicious not to be baptized than to be rebaptized.

**14. 19.** But which is the more pernicious—to be not baptized at all, or to be rebaptized—is difficult to judge. I see indeed what men more detest and shudder at; but nevertheless, returning to that scale of the Lord, where the weights of things are weighed not by human sense but by divine authority, I find a sentence of the Lord about each matter. For he said to Peter: *He who has been washed has no need of washing again*[^49]; and to Nicodemus: *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he will not enter the kingdom of heaven*[^50]. What the more secret discernment of God may hold, it is perhaps difficult for men such as we are to know; yet, as far as concerns the very words, it is plain to any man how great is the distance between *he has no need of washing* and *he will not enter the kingdom of heaven.*

The Church itself, finally, holds it as a tradition that it cannot at all admit to the altar a man without baptism; but since a rebaptized man may be admitted after penance, what else is shown except that he does not lack baptism?

If, then, Cyprian presumed that those whom he judged to be without baptism nevertheless attained to pardon on account of the bond of unity, the Lord is able, through that very bond of unity and peace, to be appeased toward the rebaptized as well, and to grow gentle toward those by whom they were rebaptized, out of that very recompense of peace, and to pardon all the offenses they had committed in error, when they offer the sacrifice of love, which covers a multitude of sins[^51];

so that he may regard not how many were wounded by their departure, but how many more were freed by their return. For in whatever bond of peace Cyprian believed that, through the mercy of God, those whom he supposed admitted into the Church without baptism could not be separated from the gifts of the Church, in that same bond of peace we believe that, through the same mercy of God, the rebaptized can merit pardon.

## The doubts about rebaptizing heretics being resolved, let the Donatists return to the Catholic Church.

**15. 20.** For the Catholic Church, in the times of the blessed Cyprian, and before him in past time, held within the bosom of unity either the rebaptized or those not having baptism; either the one or the other obtained salvation only by the merit of unity itself. For if those who came from heretics did not have baptism, as Cyprian asserts, they were not rightly admitted; and yet he himself did not despair of pardon for them from the mercy of the Lord on account of the unity of the Church. So then, if they did have baptism, they were not rightly rebaptized; what, therefore, helped them except that same love of unity, so that the divine mercy did not impute to those who loved peace what, in the handling of the sacrament, lay hidden from human weakness?

Why, then, when you fear for your own rebaptized ones, do you begrudge both them and yourselves the entrance to salvation? There was once a doubt about baptism; those who thought differently remained in unity. That doubt, as time went on and the truth was perceived, was removed: a question which, not yet ended, did not deter Cyprian from withdrawing, now ended, invites you to return.

Come to the Catholic Church now in concord, which Cyprian did not desert when it was wavering; or, if the example of Cyprian now displeases you—he who was in communion with those who received men back with the baptism of heretics, openly saying, *judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^52]—where are you going, wretched men? What are you doing? Flee even yourselves, because you have proceeded from where he remained. But if to him, on account of the abundance of love and his affection for the brotherhood and the bond of peace, neither his own sins nor those of others could do harm, return here, where things that your people have invented will do far less harm both to us and to you.
