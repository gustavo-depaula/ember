# Book I

## The end of this work.

**1. 1.** In those books which we wrote against the letter of Parmenian, which he sent to Tyconius, we promised that we would treat the question of baptism more carefully; and even if we had not promised it there, we remember and acknowledge that it is a debt owed by us, since our brothers demand it. Therefore in this work, with the Lord's help, we have undertaken not only to refute the things that the Donatists are accustomed to bring against us on this matter, but also to say what the Lord may grant us to say concerning the authority of the most blessed martyr Cyprian, by which they try to prop up their perversity, lest it fall under the force of the truth, so that all who judge without being blinded by partisan zeal may understand that they are not only not helped by the authority of Cyprian, but are above all convicted and overthrown by it.

## In schism neither baptism nor the right to confer it is lost.

**1. 2.** It has already been said in the books mentioned above that baptism can be given outside the Catholic communion in the same way that it can also be possessed outside it. Now none of them denies that even apostates have baptism, since indeed, when they return and are converted through penance, it is judged that the baptism, not being conferred again, could not have been lost. So too those who depart from the communion of the Church through the sacrilege of schism nevertheless have the baptism which they received before they departed; for if they too return, it is not given to them again, whereby it is shown that what they had received while established in unity they could not lose once separated.

But if it can be possessed outside, why can it not also be given there? If you say, It is not rightly given outside, we reply: Just as it is not rightly possessed outside, and yet is possessed, so it is not rightly given outside, but is nevertheless given. And just as, through reconciliation to unity, what was uselessly possessed outside unity begins to be usefully possessed, so through that same reconciliation what was uselessly given outside it begins to be useful. Yet it is not permitted to say that what has been given was not given, or for anyone to allege as a slander that someone did not give it, when he confesses that this man gave what he had received. For the sacrament of baptism is what he who is baptized possesses, and the sacrament of conferring baptism is what he who is ordained possesses.

And just as the baptized person, if he departs from unity, does not lose the sacrament of baptism, so too the ordained person, if he departs from unity, does not lose the sacrament of conferring baptism. For no injury is to be done to any sacrament: if a man departs from his evils, he departs from both; if he remains in his evils, he remains with both.

As therefore that baptism is accepted which one who had departed from unity could not lose, so the baptism is to be accepted which was given by one who, when he departed, had not lost the sacrament of conferring it. For just as those returning who were baptized before they withdrew are not rebaptized, so those returning who were ordained before they withdrew are certainly not ordained again; rather, either they administer what they used to administer, if the welfare of the Church requires it, or, if they do not administer it, they nevertheless bear the sacrament of their ordination; and for that reason hands are not laid upon them as upon laymen.

For Felician lost neither the sacrament of baptism nor the sacrament of conferring baptism when he departed from them along with Maximian. For now they have him among them, together with those very people whom, while he was outside, he baptized in the schism of Maximian. Therefore the rest could receive from them what those men had not lost when they withdrew from our fellowship, even though they had not joined our fellowship. By all this it is shown both that they act impiously who try to rebaptize the unity of the world, and that we act rightly who do not presume to disapprove of the sacraments of God even in the schism itself.

For in whatever they agree with us, in that they are also with us; but in that they have withdrawn from us in which they disagree with us. For this approach and this withdrawal are to be measured not by bodily movements but by spiritual ones. For just as the joining of bodies comes about through continuity of place, so a certain contact of minds is the agreement of wills. If, then, one who has withdrawn from unity wishes to do something other than what he received in unity, in that he withdraws and is separated; but as for what he wishes to do just as it is done in unity, where he received and learned it, in that he remains and is joined.

## Baptism is to be received where it is rightly received.

**2. 3.** These people, then, are with us in certain matters, but in certain others they have gone out from us. Accordingly, we do not forbid them to do the things in which they are with us; but in those in which they are not with us, we urge them to receive them by coming over, or to take them up again by returning; and by whatever means we can, we strive with love that, amended and corrected, they may choose this. We therefore do not say to them, Do not give, but, Do not give in schism.

Nor do we say to those whom they intend to baptize, Do not receive, but, Do not receive in schism. For if extreme necessity should perhaps compel someone, where he cannot find a Catholic through whom to receive it, and, keeping Catholic peace in his soul, he receives through someone established outside Catholic unity that which he was about to receive within Catholic unity itself, if he then at once departs from this life, we reckon him none other than a Catholic.

But if he has been delivered from bodily death, when he restores himself even by bodily presence to the Catholic congregation, from which he had never departed in heart, we not only do not disapprove of what he did, but we even most confidently and most truly praise it; because he believed that God was present to his heart, where he was keeping unity, and he was unwilling to depart from this life without the sacrament of holy baptism, which, wherever he found it, he recognized to be not of men but of God.

But if anyone, although he could receive it within the Catholic Church itself, through some perversity of mind chooses to be baptized in schism, even if he afterward intends to come to the Catholic Church because he is certain that the sacrament profits there, since elsewhere it can indeed be received but cannot profit,

he is beyond doubt perverse and wicked, and the more perniciously so the more knowingly he acts. For he does not doubt that it is rightly received there, just as he does not doubt that what he received elsewhere profits there.

## What the Donatists and the Catholics hold concerning baptism.

**3. 4.** For there are two things that we say: both that baptism is in the Catholic Church and that it is rightly received only there. The Donatists deny both of these. Likewise we say two other things: that baptism exists among the Donatists, but is not rightly received there. Of these two, they strongly affirm the one, namely, that baptism is there; but they are unwilling to admit that it is not rightly received there. Of these four propositions, three are ours alone, but one we both assert. For that baptism is in the Catholic Church, and is rightly received there, and is not rightly received among the Donatists, we alone say; but that baptism exists also among the Donatists, both they assert and we concede.

Whoever, then, wishes to be baptized, and is already certain that our Church is to be chosen by him for Christian salvation, and that in it alone the baptism of Christ profits even if it was received elsewhere, but who for this reason wishes to be baptized in the party of Donatus, that baptism is there—which not they alone nor we alone, but both of us, affirm—let him attend to the other three points. For if he has chosen to follow us in those things which they do not say, and yet prefers the things that both of us say to those which we alone say, it is enough for us that he prefers the things which they do not say, and which we alone say, to the things which they alone say.

That baptism is in the Catholic Church, we say; they do not. That baptism is rightly received in the Catholic Church, we say; they do not. That baptism is not rightly received in the party of Donatus, we say; they do not. As, then, he rather believes what we alone say is to be believed, so let him rather do what we alone say is to be done. But that which we say both parties hold is to be believed—let him, if he thinks so, believe it more firmly than what is said by us alone. For he is going to believe more firmly that the baptism of Christ is in the party of Donatus, which is said by both of us, than that baptism is in the Catholic Church, which is said by Catholics alone.

But again he is going to believe more firmly that the baptism of Christ is also among us, which we alone say, than that it is not among us, which they alone say. For he has already decided and is certain that, in the things wherein we disagree, we are to be preferred to them. So then, what we alone say—that it is rightly received among us—he is going to believe more firmly than that it is not rightly received among us, since this they alone say. By the same rule, what we alone say—that it is not rightly received among them—he is going to believe more firmly than what they alone say, that is, that it is rightly received among them.

In vain, therefore, does he think himself secure in receiving there what we both say is indeed there, but what we do not both say is to be received there. He himself, however, has chosen, in that which we do not both say, to cleave rather to us. Here, then, let him securely receive, where he is certain both that it is and that it is rightly received; but let him not receive there what they say is indeed there, but do not say is to be received there—they whose opinion he has decided to choose.

And yet, even if he held it doubtful whether what he held it certain is rightly received in the Catholic Church is rightly received there, he would sin gravely, in matters pertaining to the salvation of the soul, if only on the ground that he preferred uncertain things to certain ones.

For he is certain that a man is rightly baptized in the Catholic Church, by the very fact that, though baptized elsewhere, he has decided to come over here. But that a man is not rightly baptized among the Donatists, let him at least hold as uncertain, since this is said by those whose opinion he holds it certain is to be set before the Donatists'.

And so, by preferring certain things to uncertain ones, let him be baptized here, where for this reason he is certain that it is rightly done, because, even when he was thinking of doing it elsewhere, he had decided that he must come over here.

## The analogy of the military brand.

**4. 5.** But now if anyone does not understand how it can come about that what we confess to be there we say is not rightly given there, let him attend to this: that we do not say it is rightly there either—a point that even they make in the case of those who depart from their own communion.

Let them also consider the analogy of the military brand, which can be both possessed and received outside military service by deserters; yet outside military service it is neither to be possessed nor to be received, and once a man has been brought or led back to military service, it is neither to be changed nor to be repeated.

Nevertheless, the case is different for those who fall in among these heretics imprudently, supposing it to be the very Church of Christ, and different for those who know that the Catholic Church is none other than the one which, as it was promised, is spread and extended throughout the whole world even to the ends of the earth—the Church which, growing up among the weeds and longing for the rest to come amid the weariness of scandals, says in the Psalms: *From the ends of the earth I have cried to you; when my soul was weary, you exalted me upon a rock*[^1]*.* Now the rock was Christ[^2], in whom the Apostle says[^3] that we are already raised up and seated in heaven—not yet in fact, but in hope. And so the Psalm continues and says: *You have led me, because you have become my hope, a tower of strength against the face of the enemy*[^4].

For from his promises, as from weapons and darts set in a most fortified tower, the enemy is not only guarded against but even routed—the enemy who clothes his wolves in sheep's skins[^5], that they may say, *Behold, here is Christ; behold, there*[^6]; and who, from the wholeness of the city set upon a mountain[^7], draws many away to the little portions of his snares, and devours them once their throats are cut.

And when they know this, they choose to receive the baptism of Christ outside the communion of the unity of the body of Christ, intending afterward to migrate to that same communion together with what they have received elsewhere. For knowingly they will hold the baptism of Christ against the Church of Christ, even on the very day on which they receive it. But if this is wicked, who is there who would say, Let me be permitted to commit a wickedness for one day? For if he is going to come over to the Catholic Church, I ask the reason. What else will he answer, except that it is an evil to be in the party of Donatus and not to be in Catholic unity? This evil, then, for as many days as you do it, for so many days' worth of evil you will be doing; and it can be said that a greater evil is done over more days, and a lesser over fewer; but it cannot be said that no evil is done. And what need is there to commit so detestable an evil even for a single day or a single hour? For one who wants this conceded to him might ask the Church, or God himself, to grant him to be an apostate even for a single day. For there is no reason why he should fear to be an apostate for one day, and not fear to be a schismatic or a heretic for one day.

## In baptism the true is to be preferred to the false.

**5. 6.** I chose, he says, to receive it there, where both parties agree that the baptism of Christ exists. But that it is not rightly received there, those to whom you are going to migrate say; while that it is rightly received there, those from whom you are going to migrate say. That, then, which these men say—men whom you yourself place behind, when those whom you yourself prefer contradict them—is either false or, to put it more mildly, uncertain. Prefer, therefore, true things to false, or certain things to uncertain. For that what you desire can be rightly received there, where you are going to migrate after receiving it elsewhere, not only do those to whom you are going to cross over confess, but you yourself who are going to cross over confess it. For if you doubted whether it could be rightly received there, you would also doubt whether you ought to cross over there.

To receive it, then, in the party of Donatus, if it is uncertain whether it is a sin—who would doubt that it is a certain sin not rather to receive it where it is certain there is no sin? But those who are baptized there through ignorance, supposing it to be the very Church of Christ, sin less in comparison with these others; yet they are wounded by the sacrilege of schism—not, on that account, lightly, merely because others sin more gravely. For when it was said to certain people, *It will be more tolerable for Sodom in the day of judgment than for you*[^8], it was not said because the Sodomites will not be tormented, but because the others will be tormented more grievously.

## The condemnation and the reception of the Maximianists settles the question.

**5. 7.** And yet perhaps this was once hidden and uncertain. But the very thing whereby those who take heed and are corrected are healed is that whereby those are weighed down who, when they are no longer permitted to be ignorant, obstinately rage to their own destruction.

For the condemnation of the Maximianists, and the reception of those condemned together with the people whom they baptized outside their own communion in a sacrilegious schism—as their own council proclaims—settles this whole question and removes the whole dispute. It leaves nothing at all between us and those Donatists who are in communion with Primian about which there could be any doubt that the baptism of Christ can not only be possessed but also be given by those who are separated from the Church.

For just as they themselves are compelled to confess that those whom Felician baptized in schism received true baptism, because they now have them among themselves with the very baptism that they received in schism, so we say that the baptism of Christ exists even outside the Catholic communion—the baptism which those give who have been cut off from that communion, because they did not lose it when they were cut off. And what they themselves suppose they conferred upon those whom Felician baptized in schism, when they reconciled them to themselves—that is, not that they might receive what they did not have, but that what they had uselessly received and possessed in schism might profit them—this God truly confers and bestows through the Catholic communion upon those who come from any heresy or schism in which they received the baptism of Christ: that is, not that they may begin to have the sacrament of baptism which they did not have, but that what they had may begin to profit them.

## The Donatists are divided into many factions.

**6. 8.** Therefore, between us and those, as it were, cardinal Donatists, whose bishop at Carthage is Primian, there is now no controversy on this matter. For God willed it to be ended through the Maximianists, so that what they refused to confess at the urging of love they might confess under the compulsion of their own example. But we still dispute the matter on this account, lest those should think they have something to say who are not even in communion with these Donatists—those who contend that the truer Donatists have remained with themselves, the fewer they are the more genuine. If these were the only Maximianists, we ought not to despise even their salvation. How much more, then, since that same party of Donatus has been chopped into many tiny fragments—all which tiny particles censure this one much larger party, in which Primian is, for the baptism of the Maximianists it received, and each tries to assert that true baptism has remained only with itself, and is nowhere else at all, neither in the whole world over which the Catholic Church is spread out, nor in that larger party of Donatus, nor among the rest besides its own one of the tiniest parties? If all these particles were willing to heed the voice not of a man but of the most manifest truth itself, and to tame the spirited temper of their own perversity, they would surely return, from their own dryness, not to the larger party of Donatus, from which, itself cut off, they were cut off, but to the very greenness of the Catholic root. For all these people, where they are not against us[^9], are for us; but where they do not gather with us, they scatter[^10].

## The ancient custom of the Church.

**7. 9.** Now, lest I should seem to do this by human arguments—since the obscurity of this question, in the earlier times of the Church before the schism of Donatus, so drove great men, and fathers and bishops endowed with great love, to debate among themselves and to waver while preserving peace, that for a long time the diverse decrees of councils in their various regions fluctuated, until it was confirmed—doubts also being set aside—by a plenary council of the whole world, what was being held with most wholesome judgment:

from the Gospel I bring forward sure proofs, by which, with the Lord's help, I show how rightly it was decided, and truly according to God, that the medicine of the Church should heal in any schismatic or heretic the wound in which he was separated, while what remained sound should rather be acknowledged and approved than wounded by being condemned. The Lord certainly says in the Gospel: *He who is not with me is against me; and he who does not gather with me scatters*[^11]*.*

Yet when the disciples reported to him that they had seen someone casting out demons in his name and had forbidden him, because he did not follow him along with them, he said: *Do not forbid him. He who is not against you is for you. For no one can do anything in my name and speak evil of me*[^12]. If there was nothing to be corrected in that man, then let anyone be secure who, established outside the communion of the Church, gathers in the name of Christ, cut off from Christian society; and thus that saying will be false: *He who is not with me is against me; and he who does not gather with me scatters.* But if what the Lord's disciples wished to do out of ignorance is to be corrected in him, since the Lord said, *Do not forbid him,* why did he himself forbid this forbidding?

And how will that be true which he there says: *He who is not against you is for you*? For in this deed he was not against them but for them, where he was working healings through the name of Christ. Therefore, in order that both sayings may be true, as indeed both are true—both that where he says, *He who is not with me is against me; and he who does not gather with me scatters,* and that where he says, *Do not forbid him; for he who is not against you is for you*—what remains to be understood except that the man was to be confirmed in his veneration of so great a name, where he was not against the Church but for the Church; and yet was to be blamed in that separation, where, if he gathered, he would scatter; so that, if he should perhaps come to the Church, he would not there receive what he already had, but would correct that in which he had gone astray?

## The Donatists are joined to the Church in their sound part.

**8. 10.** For neither were the prayers of Cornelius, a gentile, unheard, nor were his almsgivings unaccepted; indeed, he deserved both that an angel should be sent to him and to behold the one sent, through whom he could certainly, without the approach of any man, learn all things necessary. But because whatever good he had in his prayers and almsgivings could not profit him unless he were incorporated into the Church through the bond of Christian society and peace, he is bidden to send to Peter, and through him he learns of Christ; through him also, having been baptized, he is joined to the Christian people by the fellowship of communion as well—the people to which he was joined only by the likeness of good works[^13]. For he would have despised, to his own ruin, the good he did not yet have, growing proud over the good he did have.

So too those who, separating themselves from the society of the rest, break the bond of unity with love violated, if they do nothing of the things they received in that society, are separated in all things; and therefore anyone they have joined to themselves, if he wishes to come to the Church, must receive everything that he did not receive.

But if they do some of the same things, they have not separated themselves in those things; and from that part they are held within the fabric of the weaving, while in the rest they are torn away.

Accordingly, if they have joined anyone to themselves, he is bound to the Church from that part in which they too are not separated; and therefore, if he wishes to come to the Church, he is healed in that wherein he was wandering, mangled; but where he was joined sound, he is not cured but acknowledged—lest, when we wish to cure what is sound, we rather wound it.

And so those whom they baptize, they heal from the wound of idolatry or unbelief, but they strike them more grievously with the wound of schism. For the sword slew the idolaters among the people of God[^14], but the gaping of the earth swallowed up the schismatics[^15]. And the Apostle says: *If I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing*[^16]*.*

## Not only the sound parts are to be regarded, but also the weak.

**8. 11.** If someone were brought to a physician wounded with a grave wound in some necessary part of the body, and the physician said, He dies of it unless it is cured, I do not suppose that those who brought him would be so foolish that, having considered and counted his other sound members, they would answer the physician and say: Then are these many sound members not enough for his life, and is that one wounded one enough for his death? They certainly do not say this, but offer it to be cured; nor, however, because they offer it to be cured, do they ask the physician to cure also those parts that are sound, but to apply the medicine urgently to that one place alone, from which death threatens even though the rest are safe, and will come unless it is healed.

What good, then, is either a sound faith, or perhaps only the sound sacrament of faith, to a man in whom the soundness of love has been destroyed by the lethal wound of schism—by the destruction of that one thing alone, those other intact things too are drawn to death? That this may not happen, the mercy of God does not cease, through the unity of the holy Church, to call them to come and be cured by the medicine of reconciliation, through the bond of peace.

Nor let them think themselves sound because we say they have something sound; nor again let them think that what is sound must be cured because we point out that something is wounded. And so, in the soundness of the sacrament, because they are not against us, they are for us; but in the wound of schism, whatever they do not gather with Christ, they scatter. Let them not be exalted by the things they have. Why do they so lift up proud eyes over the things that are sound? Let them deign to look humbly upon their own wound, and attend not only to what is present but also to what is lacking.

## Without love the other gifts profit nothing.

**9. 12.** Let them see how many and how great things profit nothing if some one thing is lacking, and let them see what that one thing is. And let them not heed me in this, but the Apostle: *If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass or a tinkling cymbal. And if I have prophecy, and know all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing*[^17]. What good, then, does it do them if they have an angelic tongue in the sacred mysteries, and prophecy like Caiaphas[^18] and Saul[^19], so that they sometimes prophesy—men whom holy Scripture testifies to have been damnable?

If they not only know the sacraments but also have them, as Simon Magus had them[^20]; if they have faith, as the demons confessed Christ—for they did not disbelieve when they said, *What have we to do with you, Son of God? We know who you are*[^21]. If they distribute even their own substance to the poor, as many do, not only in the Catholic Church but in various heresies. If, when some persecution presses, they hand over their body to the flames along with us for the faith which they likewise confess[^22]—yet because they do these things separated, not bearing with one another in love, nor striving to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace[^23], since they certainly do not have love, even with all those things which profit them nothing, they cannot attain to eternal salvation.

## Whether baptism in the party of Donatus begets children.

**10. 13.** But they think they ask a most clever question, *whether the baptism of Christ in the party of Donatus begets children or does not beget them*: so that, if we grant that it begets them, they may assert that their Church is the mother who could beget children from the baptism of Christ; and since there must be one Church, they may on this ground charge that ours is therefore not the Church. But if we say it does not beget, *Why then,* they say, *are those who pass over from us to you, when they have been baptized among us, not reborn through baptism among you, if they have not yet been born?*

## The Church begets children not in the separated part, but in the united part, that is, in baptism. But not all the children belong to its unity.

**10. 14.** As though it begot from that in which it is separated, and not from that in which it is joined. For it is separated from the bond of love and peace, but joined in the one baptism. And so there is one Church, which alone is named Catholic; and whatever of its own it has in the communions of those separated from its unity, it is precisely through what it has of its own in them that it begets, and not they.

For it is not their separation that begets, but what they have kept with them of the Church; and if they give up this too, they do not beget at all. This Church, then, begets in all whose sacraments are retained, whereby something of the kind can be begotten anywhere—although not all whom it begets belong to its unity, which will save those who persevere to the end. For not only do those not belong to it who are manifest by the open sacrilege of separation, but also those who, mingled bodily in its unity, are separated by a most wicked life. For it had borne Simon Magus through baptism; and yet to him it was said that he had no part in the inheritance of Christ[^24].

Did he lack baptism? Did he lack the Gospel? Did he lack the sacraments? But because he lacked love, he was born in vain, and it would perhaps have been better for him not to be born. Were those not born to whom the Apostle says, *As to little ones in Christ, I gave you milk to drink, not solid food*[^25]? Yet he recalls them from the sacrilege of schism, into which they were rushing precisely because they were carnal: *As to little ones in Christ,* he says, *I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able to bear it; but neither indeed are you now able, for you are still carnal. For while there is jealousy and strife among you, are you not carnal, and walking according to man? For when someone says, I am of Paul, and another, I am of Apollos, are you not men?*[^26]

For about these he says above: *Now I beseech you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all say the same thing, and that there be no schisms among you; but that you be perfected in the same mind and in the same judgment. For it has been reported to me concerning you, my brothers, by those who are of Chloe, that there are contentions among you. Now I say this, because each of you says, I indeed am of Paul, but I of Apollos, but I of Cephas, but I of Christ. Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you, or were you baptized in the name of Paul?*[^27] These men, then, if they remained in this obstinacy and perversity, were certainly born, and yet did not belong, through the bond of peace and unity, to the very Church from which they were born.

Therefore the Church itself begets, both through its own womb and through the wombs of handmaids, from the same sacraments, as from the seed of its husband. For not in vain does the Apostle say that *all those things happened as a figure*[^28]. But those who are proud and are not joined to their lawful mother are like Ishmael, of whom it was said: *Cast out the handmaid and her son; for the son of the handmaid shall not be heir with my son Isaac*[^29].

But those who peaceably love their father's lawful wife, by whose lawful right they were begotten, are like the sons of Jacob, although born of handmaids and yet receiving the same inheritance[^30]. But those who, born within unity from the womb of that same mother, neglect the grace they received, are like Esau the son of Isaac, who was rejected, God bearing witness and saying: *Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated*—though both were conceived from one act of intercourse and born from one womb[^31].

## It is asked whether baptism in the party of Donatus remits sins.

**11. 15.** They also ask whether sins are forgiven through baptism in the party of Donatus: so that, if we say they are forgiven, they may answer: Then the Holy Spirit is there; for when, with the Lord breathing upon them, the Spirit had been given to the disciples, he then went on and said: *Baptize the nations in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*[^32]; *if you forgive anyone's sins, they shall be forgiven him; if you retain anyone's, they shall be retained*[^33]. And if it is so, they say, our communion is the Church of Christ; for the Holy Spirit does not work the forgiveness of sins apart from the Church.

And if our communion is the Church of Christ, your communion is not the Church of Christ. For it is one, whatever it is, of which it was said: *One is my dove, the only one of her mother*[^34]; nor can there be as many Churches as there are schisms. But if we say that sins are not forgiven there: Then, they say, there is not true baptism there; and therefore you ought to baptize those whom you receive from us; and because you do not do this, you confess that you are not in the Church of Christ.

## Sins are not forgiven where there is no love.

**11. 16.** We resist them in this way, according to the Scriptures, questioning them so that they may answer for themselves what they ask of us. For I ask them to say whether sins are forgiven where there is no love. For sins are the darkness of souls. We hear John saying: *He who hates his brother is in darkness even until now*[^35]. But no one would make schisms unless they were blinded by hatred of a brother. If, then, we say that sins are not forgiven there, how is one who is baptized among them reborn? For what is it to be reborn through baptism, except to be renewed from one's old state? But how is one renewed from his old state whose past sins are not forgiven him?

But if he is not reborn, neither has he put on Christ, from which it follows that he must apparently be baptized again. For the Apostle says: *As many of you as were baptized in Christ have put on Christ*[^36]; and if a man has not put him on, neither is he to be held baptized in Christ. Further, because we say he was baptized in Christ, we confess that he has put on Christ; and if we confess this, we confess that he was regenerated. And if this is so, his sins also have been forgiven. How then does John say, *He who hates his brother remains in darkness even until now*[^37], if the forgiveness of sins has already taken place? Or is there not in schism a hatred of one's brother? Who would say this, since both the origin and the obstinacy of schism is nothing else but hatred of a brother?

## Whether Simon Magus's sins were forgiven.

**11. 17.** They think they solve this question for themselves when they say: *There is, then, no forgiveness of sins in schism, and therefore no regeneration of the new man, and consequently no baptism of Christ.* But we, because we confess that the baptism of Christ is there, set this question before them to be solved: whether that Simon Magus was washed with the true baptism of Christ. They will answer, Yes; for they are compelled by the authority of holy Scripture. I ask, then, whether they confess that his sins were forgiven him? Surely they will confess it. Again I ask, why did Peter say to him that he had no part in the lot of the saints[^38]? Because, they say, he sinned afterward, wishing to buy the gift of God with money, of which he believed the apostles to be sellers.

## Baptism is true even without the forgiveness of sins.

**12. 18.** What if he approached baptism itself feignedly? Were his sins forgiven him, or were they not forgiven? Let them choose what they will; whichever they choose, it is enough for us. If they say they were forgiven, how then will *the Holy Spirit of discipline flee from the feigner*[^39], if in this feigner he worked the forgiveness of sins? If they say they were not forgiven, I ask: if he afterward, with his heart shaken and with true sorrow, were to confess his feigning, would he be judged to need baptizing again? But if it is most insane to say this, let them confess that a man can be baptized with the true baptism of Christ, and yet his heart, persevering in malice or sacrilege, does not allow the abolition of his sins to take place;

and thus let them understand that men can be baptized in communions separated from the Church, where the baptism of Christ is given and received in the same celebration of the sacrament, yet which then profits unto the forgiveness of sins when each one, reconciled to unity, is stripped of the sacrilege of dissension, by which his sins were held bound and not allowed to be forgiven. For just as, in the one who had approached feignedly, it comes about that he is not baptized again, but is purified by that very pious correction and truthful confession—which he could not be without baptism—but so that what was given before then begins to avail unto salvation, when that feigning has departed through truthful confession: so too this man who, an enemy of the love and peace of Christ, received the baptism of Christ (which those who separated themselves did not lose) in some heresy or schism, by which sacrilegious crime his sins were not forgiven—when he has corrected himself and has come to the society and unity of the Church, is not to be baptized again; because by that very reconciliation and peace it is granted that the sacrament, which when received in schism could not profit, now begins in unity to profit unto the forgiveness of his sins.

## Baptism forgives sins even in the feigner, but they return at once because of his feigning.

**12. 19.** But if they say that, in the one who approached feignedly, his sins were indeed forgiven him at that very point of time through the holy power of so great a sacrament, but returned at once through his own feigning—so that the Holy Spirit was both present to the baptized to make his sins depart, and fled from the persistence of his feigning so that they returned;

so that both this is true: *As many of you as were baptized in Christ have put on Christ*[^40]; and this: *For the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee from the feigner*[^41]—that is, so that the holiness of baptism clothes him with Christ, and the ruin of feigning strips him of Christ:

as happens when someone passes from darkness through light back to darkness; his eyes are indeed directed perpetually toward the darkness, but the light cannot but flood over him as he passes through—if, then, they say this, let them understand that this also happens in those who are baptized outside the communion of the Church, yet with the baptism of the Church, which, wherever it is, is holy in itself:

and therefore it does not belong to those who separate themselves, but to the source from which they separate themselves; yet it avails among them at least to this extent, that through its light they pass over to the darkness of their own dissension, with the sins returning at once which the holiness of baptism had forgiven at that point of time, just as the obscurity returns which the light had shaken off in passing.

## Baptism always forgives sins at the very instant it is received.

**12. 20.** For the Lord teaches most plainly in the Gospel that forgiven sins return where there is no brotherly love, in the case of that servant whom, when he found him a debtor of ten thousand talents, he released from everything at his entreaty. But when that servant had not had mercy on his fellow servant, who owed him a hundred denarii, the master ordered him to repay what he had forgiven him[^42]. The time, then, in which pardon is received through baptism is, as it were, the time of rendering an account, so that all the debts that are found may be forgiven.

Yet that servant did not afterward lend his fellow servant the money which, when the other could not repay it, he refused to pity him for; rather, his fellow servant already owed it to him at the time when he himself, rendering his account to his master, was released from a debt of so great a sum; nor had he forgiven his fellow servant what he owed him, and so came to have his master forgive him. The fellow servant's words indicate this when he says: *Have patience with me, and I will repay you*[^43]. Otherwise he would say: You had already forgiven me this; why do you demand it again?

The Lord's own words open this up more plainly. For he says: *But that servant, going out, found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii*[^44]. He did not say, To whom he had already forgiven a debt of a hundred denarii. For if he had forgiven it, the other did not owe it him. Because, then, he said, *he owed him,* it is plain that he had not forgiven it. And it would indeed have been better, and this rather befitted so great a debtor about to render an account and awaiting the mercy of his master, that he himself should first forgive his fellow servant what was owed to him, and so come to render his account, where the mercy of his master was to be implored. And yet the fact that he had not yet forgiven his fellow servant did not hinder his master from forgiving him, at that time of receiving the account, all that he owed. But what good did it do, since, on account of his persistence in hatred, everything was at once heaped back upon his head?

So the grace of baptism is not hindered from forgiving all sins, even if hatred of a brother persists in the soul of the one to whom they are forgiven. For yesterday is released, and whatever is before it is released, even the very hour and moment before baptism and in baptism. But thereafter he at once begins to be guilty, not only of the following days, hours, and moments, but also of the past ones, with all returning that had been forgiven; and these things often happen in the Church.

## Whether in baptism sins are forgiven to a man who hates.

**13. 21.** For it very often happens that a man has an enemy whom he hates most unjustly—although we are commanded to love even unjust enemies and to pray for them[^45]. Suddenly, by the danger of death, he begins to be alarmed, and he asks for baptism, which he receives with such haste that the time of danger scarcely admits the necessary questioning of a few words (how much less a very long discourse?) so that the hatred might be driven from his heart, even if it is known to the one baptizing. Certainly these things do not cease to occur not only among us but also among them. What, then, do we say? Are this man's sins forgiven, or are they not forgiven?

Let them by all means choose what they will. For if they are forgiven, they return at once: the Gospel says it, the truth proclaims it. Whether, then, they are forgiven or are not forgiven, medicine is afterward necessary; and yet if he lives, and has learned that this is to be corrected, and has corrected it, he is not baptized again, whether among them or among us. So too the things which schismatics or heretics have no differently, and do no differently, than the true Church does, when they come to us, we do not amend but rather approve.

For in the things in which they do not disagree with us, in those they are not separated from us. But nevertheless, because these things profit them nothing as long as they are schismatics or heretics—on account of the other things in which they disagree with the truth, and on account of the most monstrous crime of separation itself, whether their sins have remained in them or, forgiven at once, have returned—we urge them to come to the soundness of peace and love; not only that they may have something they did not have, but that what they had may also begin to profit them.

## The baptism which heretics and schismatics hold is not theirs, but Christ's.

**14. 22.** In vain, then, do they say to us: *If you accept our baptism, what do we have less, that you should think we must take counsel about your communion?* For we reply: We do not accept your baptism; because that is not the baptism of schismatics or heretics, but of God and the Church, wherever it has been found and wherever it has been carried. What is yours, however, is only that you hold wrong opinions, and act sacrilegiously, and impiously separate yourselves. For even if you held or had all other things true, and yet continued in that same separation against the bond of brotherly peace, against the unity of all the brothers who, throughout the whole world, have been presented just as they were promised—whose causes and hearts you could in no way ever know and examine so as to condemn them deservedly, and who cannot on that account be guilty, because they trusted ecclesiastical judges rather than litigants:

this alone you have less, which he has less who does not have love[^46]. Now what need is there for us to weave it out again? Look yourselves in the Apostle at how great is what you have less. And what does it matter for one who does not have love, whether he flies outward, carried off by some wind of temptation, or within does not depart from the Lord's harvest, only to be separated at the final winnowing? And yet even such people, if they have once already been born through baptism, ought not to be born again.

## The Church bears all from baptism as from the seed of its husband.

**15. 23.** For the Church bears all through baptism, whether within itself, that is, from its own womb, or outside itself from the seed of its husband. But Esau too, though born of a wife, was separated from the people of God on account of brotherly discord[^47]. And Asher, though born indeed by the right of a wife, yet of a handmaid, received the land of promise on account of brotherly concord[^48].

Hence too it did not harm Ishmael, that he should be separated from the people of God, that his mother was a handmaid, but brotherly discord harmed him; and it did not profit him that he belonged to a wife's authority, whose son he rather was, because by her own conjugal rights he had been both sown in a handmaid and received from a handmaid[^49].

Just so, among these people, by the right of the Church which is in baptism, all are born who are born; but if they agree with the brothers, through the unity of peace they will come to the land of promise, not to be cast out again from the maternal womb, but to be recognized in the paternal seed; while if they persist in discord, they will belong to the lot of Ishmael. But Ishmael was first, and Isaac afterward; and Esau first, and Jacob later: not because heresy bore before the Church, or because the Church itself bore first the carnal or natural, and afterward the spiritual; but because in the very lot of our mortality, by which we are born of Adam, *that which is spiritual is not first, but that which is natural, and afterward the spiritual*[^50].

And from that very natural mind, because *the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^51], all dissensions and schisms are begotten. Those who persist in this mind the Apostle says belong to the Old Testament[^52], that is, to the desire for earthly promises, in which spiritual things are indeed prefigured, but *the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^53].

## Those who belong to the Old Testament and those who belong to the New.

**15. 24.** At whatever time, then, such men begin to be in this life that, though already imbued with the divine sacraments according to the distribution of the ages, they still think carnally, and hope for and desire carnal things from God, whether in this life or after this life, they are natural men. But the Church, which is the people of God, is even in the pilgrimage of this life an ancient thing, having in some men a natural portion, and in others a spiritual one. To the natural belongs the Old Testament, to the spiritual the New. But in the first times both were hidden, from Adam to Moses. From Moses, however, the Old was made manifest, and in it the New was hidden, because it was secretly signified. But after the Lord came in the flesh[^54], the New was revealed; and the sacraments of the Old ceased, but such concupiscences did not cease. For they are in those whom the Apostle, though already born through the sacrament of the New Testament, yet calls natural men unable to perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God[^55].

For just as in the sacraments of the Old Testament certain spiritual men lived, belonging secretly to the New Testament, which was then hidden, so now too in the sacrament of the New Testament, which has already been revealed, very many live as natural men. And if these are unwilling to make progress toward perceiving the things that are of the Spirit of God, to which the apostolic word exhorts them, they will belong to the Old Testament.

But if they make progress, then even before they fully grasp it, by their very progress and approach they belong to the New; and if, before they become spiritual, they are snatched from this life, kept safe by the holiness of the sacrament, they are counted in the land of the living, where our hope and portion is the Lord[^56]. Nor do I find what truer meaning may be understood in what is written: *Your eyes saw my unformed substance*; since there follows: *And in your book they shall all be written*[^57].

## Spiritual men and carnal men.

**16. 25.** The same Church that bore Abel and Enoch and Noah and Abraham also bore Moses and the later Prophets before the coming of the Lord; and the same that bore these bore also the apostles and our martyrs and all good Christians. For all, though born at different times, have appeared, but are contained in the fellowship of one people; and as citizens of the same city they have experienced the labors of this pilgrimage, and some of them experience them now, and the rest will experience them until the end. Likewise, the same Church that bore Cain and Ham and Ishmael and Esau bore also Dathan and others like them in the same people; and the same that bore these bore also Judas the false apostle, and Simon Magus, and the rest down to these times—false Christians obstinately hardened in a natural disposition, whether they are mingled within unity or dissent by open severance.

But when such men are evangelized and imbued with the sacraments by spiritual men, Rebekah, as it were, bears them by herself, like Esau[^58]; but when such men are begotten in the people of God through those who do not chastely announce the Gospel[^59], it is Sarah indeed, but through Hagar[^60]. Likewise good spiritual men, when they are begotten by carnal men who evangelize or baptize, are borne by Leah or Rachel by conjugal right, but through a handmaid's womb[^61].

But when good faithful are begotten through spiritual men in the Gospel—those who either come to the disposition of spiritual age, or do not cease to strive toward it, or fail to do so only because they cannot—they are born into the new life and the New Testament, as Isaac from the womb of Sarah, or Jacob from the womb of Rebekah.

## Who are the true children of the Church.

**17. 26.** And so, whether they seem to dwell within or are openly outside, what is flesh is flesh[^62]; whether it persists on the threshing floor in its barrenness, or is carried outside by the occasion of temptation as by a wind, what is chaff is chaff[^63].

And he is always divided from the unity of that Church which is without spot or wrinkle[^64], even he who is mingled with the congregation of the saints in carnal hardness. Yet no one is to be despaired of, whether one who appears such within, or one who more openly opposes from outside.

But spiritual men, or those making progress toward this very thing with pious zeal, do not go outside; because even when they seem to be expelled by some perversity or constraint of men,

they prove there all the more that they remain within, since they in no way rise up against the Church, but are rooted in the solid rock of unity with the strongest strength of love.

For to this pertains what is said in that sacrifice of Abraham: *But the birds he did not divide*[^65].

## The schism of the Donatists is named after Donatus.

**18. 27.** On the question of baptism, then, I have now, as I judge, treated enough; and because this is the most manifest schism that is named with the name of the Donatists, it remains that we believe piously concerning baptism what the universal Church, removed from the sacrilege of schism, keeps. Yet within it, if some held one opinion and others another on this question while preserving peace, until by a universal council some one clear and pure thing should be settled, the love of unity would cover over the error of human weakness, as it is written: *For love covers a multitude of sins*[^66]. For in whose absence the other things are had in vain, in whose presence certain things are venially not had.

## On the authority of Cyprian and the testimony of his life.

**18. 28.** There exist in the letters of the blessed martyr Cyprian great proofs—that I may now come to him, on whose authority these men flatter themselves carnally, while they are destroyed spiritually by his love. For in those times, before the consensus of the whole Church confirmed by the sentence of a plenary council what was to be followed in this matter, it seemed to him, together with nearly eighty of his fellow bishops of the African Churches, that every person who had been baptized outside the communion of the Catholic Church ought, when coming to the Church, to be baptized again. That this was not rightly done the Lord did not, of course, reveal to so great a man—so that his pious humility and love in wholesomely keeping the peace of the Church might be made plain, and might be marked, as it were, for the medicinal instruction not only of the Christians of that time but also of those to come.

For when a bishop of such merit, of such great service to the Church, of such a heart, of such eloquence, of such virtue, thought otherwise about baptism than the truth, more carefully investigated, was going to confirm; and when many of his colleagues held, though it was not yet clearly manifest, that which both the past custom of the Church and afterward the whole Catholic world embraced—he nevertheless did not cut himself off from the rest, who held a different opinion, by a separated communion, and did not cease to persuade the rest of this also, that they should bear with one another in love, striving to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace[^67].

For thus, with the framework of the body remaining intact, if anything in certain members was weak, it would rather be restored to health by their soundness than, deadened by severance, admit the care of no healing. And if he had separated himself, how many would have followed him? What a name he would have made for himself among men? How much more widely would they have been called Cyprianists than Donatists?

But he was not a son of perdition[^68], of the sort of whom it was said: *You cast them down while they were lifted up*[^69]; rather, he was a son of the peace of the Church, who, endowed with so great an illumination of heart, on that account did not see one thing, so that through him something more surpassing might be seen. *And I show you,* says the Apostle, *a still more surpassing way: if I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass or a tinkling cymbal*[^70]. He therefore penetrated less far, so as to discern the hidden secret of the sacrament; but if he knew all the sacraments, yet had not love, he would be nothing[^71].

But since, perceiving that one thing less, he nevertheless kept this one humbly, faithfully, and bravely, he deserved to attain the crown of martyrdom: so that if any cloud from the human condition had crept into his lucid mind, it might be put to flight by the glorious serenity of his shining blood.

For not in vain did the Lord Jesus Christ himself, when he called himself the vine, and his own people as branches in the vine, say that they were cut off and taken away from the vine as useless branches that bore no fruit[^72]. And what is the fruit, except that new offspring, of which he likewise says: *A new commandment I give you, that you love one another*[^73]? This is that very love, without which the rest profit nothing. The Apostle also says: *But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control*[^74]. All these begin from love, and through the rest of the joining make, as it were, a wondrous cluster.

Nor yet in vain did the same Lord add: *But the branches in me that bear fruit, my Father prunes, that they may bear more fruit*[^75]; except that even those who excel in the fruit of love can nevertheless have something to be pruned, which the husbandman does not leave untended. Therefore, as for that holy man, who held an opinion about baptism otherwise than the matter stood—which was afterward thoroughly handled and confirmed by most careful consideration—because he remained in Catholic unity, it was both made up for by the abundance of his love, and pruned away by the sickle of his passion.

## The Donatists set his authority against the unlearned.

**19. 29.** But lest I should seem, for the sake of evading proof, to say these things in praise of the most blessed martyr—praise which is indeed not his, but his by whose grace he became such—let us now bring forward proofs from his letters, by which above all the mouths of the Donatists may be shut. For they set his authority against the unlearned, so as to show that they act, as it were, rightly in baptizing again the faithful who come to them. Too wretched are they, and, unless they correct themselves, altogether condemned by their own selves, who choose to imitate in so great a man that which did not harm him precisely because he walked, with steps most persevering to the end, in that peace from which these men have strayed, *who have not known the way of peace*[^76].

It is indeed the case that the baptism of Christ is holy everywhere, and though it be among heretics or schismatics, it nevertheless does not belong to the heresy or schism itself, and therefore those who come from there to the Catholic Church ought not to be baptized again; but this is one thing, and it is another to judge that those who stray from Catholic peace and are cast headlong into the wicked pit of severance must moreover be rebaptized. For in his case the breasts of love covered over that blemish on the brightness of a holy soul; but in the case of these men an unpeaceable countenance flaunts this soot in their hellish foulness.

But the things we are next going to treat, as far as concerns the authority of the blessed Cyprian, we shall take up from another beginning.
