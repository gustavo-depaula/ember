# Book III

## Augustine follows the example of Cyprian in preserving unity.

**1. 1.** I think it is now manifest to all that the authority of the blessed Cyprian, for retaining the bond of peace and for the most wholesome love of the unity of the Church which is in no way to be violated, is to be set forth rather for us than for the Donatists.

For if they wish to use the example of Cyprian for rebaptizing Catholics, because he judged that heretics were to be baptized in the Catholic Church, we rather use his example, by which he most plainly determined that one must in no way withdraw, by separation of communion, from the Catholic communion—that is, from the Christians spread throughout the whole world—even when the wicked and sacrilegious are admitted; since he was unwilling to remove even from the right of communion those who, as it seemed to him, received unbaptized sacrilegious men into the Catholic communion, saying: *judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^1].

## The question about the custom introduced by Agrippinus is answered.

**2. 2.** Yet I see what can still be asked of me, namely, that I should reply to those plausible reasonings by which either Agrippinus before, or Cyprian himself, or those who agreed with them in Africa, or perhaps some from overseas and far-distant lands, were moved—not indeed by any council, whether plenary or even regional, but by epistolary conversation—to think that this ought to be done, which both the former custom of the Church did not have, and which afterward the Catholic world excluded with the most robust firmness of consensus: so that what, through disputations of this kind, had begun to creep into the minds of some, the more powerful truth, coming from the salvation of unity, and the universal medicine, might heal.

Let them see, then, how securely I approach this discourse. If I cannot attain and show how to refute what they affirm from the council of Cyprian or his letters—that the baptism of Christ cannot be given through heretics—I shall remain secure in that Church, in whose communion with those who had not agreed with him Cyprian himself remained.

## The Church could not be stained if it did not rebaptize.

**2. 3.** But if they say that the Catholic Church existed then for this reason, that there were there a few—or, if they think so, even many—who disapproved of the baptism given among heretics, and baptized those who came from there: What then? Before Agrippinus, with whom this, as it were, new reasoning began, struggling against the custom, was there not a Church?

And finally, after Agrippinus, when—unless there had been a return to the former custom—it would not have been necessary for Cyprian to contrive another council, was there not a Church, because such a custom was held throughout, that the baptism of Christ was held to be none but the baptism of Christ, even if it were proved to have been given among heretics or schismatics? But if even then there was a Church, and the inheritance of Christ had not perished by interruption, but, taking increase through all the nations, remained; the safest course is to remain in that same custom, which then bore the good and the wicked in one embrace. But if then there was no Church, because sacrilegious heretics were received without baptism, and this was held by universal custom, whence did Donatus appear? From what earth did he sprout? From what sea did he emerge? From what sky did he fall?

We therefore, as I had begun to say, are secure in the communion of that Church through whose universality there is now done what, both before Agrippinus and between Agrippinus and Cyprian, was likewise done through its universality; and whose universality neither Agrippinus deserted, nor Cyprian, nor those who had agreed with them, although they were of another mind than the rest; but with those very men from whom they thought differently they remained in the same communion of unity.

But let them themselves consider where they are, who cannot even say from what they were propagated, if already then the Church had lost heretics and schismatics received without baptism by the contagion of communion:

nor do they themselves agree with Cyprian; for he professed that he remained in communion with the receivers of heretics and schismatics, and through this even with those received; but these men, on account of the name of betrayers whom they defamed in Africa, and did not convict in the overseas judgment, have separated themselves from the communion of the whole world: although, even if they brought forward true crimes, the crimes of heresies and schisms are far greater, from which those coming without baptism, as he himself held, and received into the Catholic communion not through baptism, could not stain Cyprian.

Nor, in the very thing in which they say they imitate Cyprian, do they find what to reply about the baptism of the Maximianists which they received, since they recalled into their communion—into the very same episcopate in which they had condemned them—those whom, of that number whom they condemned by their plenary council, they even persecuted by the judgment of earthly powers.

Therefore, if in the times of Cyprian communion with the wicked destroyed the Church, these men have no origin for their own communion; but if it did not destroy it, they have no defense for their severance: moreover, they neither follow the example of Cyprian, because they broke the bond of unity, nor his very council, because they received the baptism of the Maximianists.

## The letter of Cyprian to Jubaian.

**3. 4.** Now, then, we, remaining in the example of Cyprian, let us also consider the council of Cyprian. What does Cyprian say? *You have heard,* he says, *most beloved colleagues, what Jubaian our fellow bishop has written to me, consulting our littleness about the unlawful and profane baptism of heretics; and what therefore I wrote back to him, judging, of course, what once and again and often we have judged: that heretics coming to the Church ought to be baptized and sanctified with the baptism of the Church. Likewise there has been read to you another letter of Jubaian, in which, out of his sincere and religious devotion, replying to our letter, he not only agreed but also gave thanks that he had been instructed*[^2].

From these words of the blessed Cyprian, we have learned that he was consulted by Jubaian, and what he replied to the one consulting him, and that the other gave thanks that he had been instructed. Are we now to be considered obstinate, if we are willing to consider that very letter by which this was persuaded to Jubaian? For until it is persuaded to us also (if this can be persuaded by true reasonings), Cyprian himself makes us secure about the right of Catholic communion.

## Still about this letter.

**3. 5.** For it follows, and he says: *It remains that we each set forth what we think on this very matter, judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion if he should think differently*[^3]. He therefore grants me, with the right of communion preserved, not only still to seek the truth, but even to think differently. *For none of us,* he says, *has set himself up to be a bishop of bishops, or by tyrannical terror drives his colleagues to the necessity of obeying*[^4]. What more gentle? What more humble? Certainly no authority deters us from seeking what is true. *Since every bishop,* he says, *according to the license of his liberty and power, has his own free judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another*[^5]:

in those questions, I suppose, certainly, which have not yet been discussed with the most refined perception. For he knew how great a profundity of the sacrament the whole Church was then turning over in varied disputation, and he made the judgment of inquiry free, so that the truth, once examined, might be laid open.

For he was not lying, and thinking to catch his simpler colleagues in a word, so that when they had betrayed that they thought differently, he might then, contrary to what he had promised, judge them to be excommunicated. Far be this wicked treachery from so holy a soul; those who think this of such a man, as though to his praise, profess nothing else than that they themselves are such.

I could in no way at all believe that Cyprian, a Catholic bishop, a Catholic martyr, and the greater he was, the more humbling himself in all things, that he might find grace before God[^6], brought forth from his mouth—especially in a holy council of his colleagues—anything other than what he bore in his heart, especially since he adds and says: *But let us all await the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ, who alone has the power both of setting us over the governance of his Church and of judging our conduct*[^7]. Under the recollection, then, of so great a judgment, awaiting to hear the truth from his colleagues, would he be the first to set them an example of lying? May God turn this madness away from any Christian whatever, how much more from Cyprian! We have, therefore, the free choice of inquiry, granted to us by Cyprian's own most gentle and most truthful discourse.

## The reading of this letter does not persuade.

**4. 6.** Now indeed his colleagues begin to bring forward their own opinions; but they had heard the letter given to Jubaian. For it was read out, as was recalled in the opening. Let it therefore be read out to us also, that we too may from it track down, as far as the Lord helps, what ought to be thought.

Perhaps someone says: What, then? Are you only now learning what Cyprian wrote to Jubaian? I have already read it, I confess; and I would assuredly have gone over to the same opinion, had not so great an authority of others recalled me to a more diligent consideration—men whom the Church spread throughout the whole world, the Church which had also borne him himself, could bear, men either equal in the grace of learning, or even perhaps more learned, through so many nations, Latin, Greek, barbarian, and the Hebrew itself,

who could in no way seem to me to have been unwilling to hold this opinion in vain: not because it could not happen that, in a most obscure question, one or a few should think more truly than the many; but because a sentence is not easily to be passed for one or a few against innumerable men of the same religion and unity, endowed both with great talent and with abundant learning, unless the matters have been handled and perceived to the best of one's power.

And so, to one who inquires more anxiously, how much even the letters of Cyprian himself have supplied to me in favor of this opinion which the Catholic Church now holds—that the baptism of Christ is to be recognized and approved not from the merit of those through whom it is given, but from the merit of him of whom it was said, *This is he who baptizes*[^8]—the matter itself will show in the course of our discourse.

Let us therefore now suppose that letter, which was given to Jubaian by him, read out to us, as it was read out in the council. Let him first read it, who is going to read these things that I am about to say, lest he should perhaps think that I have left out something necessary. For it is too long, and does not pertain to the explanation of the matter undertaken, that all the words of this letter should now be recalled by us.

## What Augustine holds while treating the question.

**5. 7.** But if anyone asks what I hold in the meantime while I treat these things, I answer: first, the letters of Cyprian have supplied me with what to hold, until I perceive what sort of thing it is that began afterward to be disputed. For Cyprian himself says: *But someone says: What then will become of those who in the past, coming from heresy to the Church, were admitted without baptism?*[^9] Whether these were truly without baptism, or whether they were admitted for the reason that those who admitted them understood them to have baptism, we shall presently consider. Nevertheless, what the custom of the Church used to be, the same Cyprian sufficiently shows, who says that in the past those coming from heresy to the Church were admitted without baptism.

## The truth is not to be despised.

**5. 8.** Castus of Sicca also says in that same council: *He who, despising the truth, presumes to follow custom is either envious and malicious toward the brothers, to whom the truth is revealed, or ungrateful toward God, by whose inspiration his Church is instructed*[^10].

Whether the truth has been revealed, we shall presently inquire; yet that there was another custom of the Church, even this man has confessed.

## Let custom yield to truth.

**6. 9.** Libosus of Vaga also says: *In the Gospel the Lord says, I am the truth*[^11]; *he did not say, I am custom. And so, the truth being made manifest, let custom yield to truth*[^12].

Clearly, who would doubt that custom ought to yield to manifest truth? But about the manifested truth we shall see; yet now this man too makes manifest that there was another custom.

## When the revelation of truth has been made, let error yield.

**7. 10.** Likewise Zosimus of Tharassa said: *When the revelation of truth has been made, let error yield to truth: for Peter too, who formerly circumcised, yielded to Paul preaching the truth*[^13].

This man indeed was unwilling to say "custom," but "error"; yet when he says, *For Peter too, who formerly circumcised, yielded to Paul preaching the truth,* he sufficiently indicates that something else about baptism also used to be done. At the same time he reminds us that it was not impossible for Cyprian to be of another mind about baptism than the truth demanded—the truth which was retained by the Church both before him and after him—if even Peter could be of another mind about something which the truth did not hold, as we learned with the apostle Paul as our teacher[^14].

## Custom is not to be set before reason.

**8. 11.** Likewise Felix of Buslacenum said: *In admitting heretics without the baptism of the Church, let no one set custom before reason and truth; because reason and truth always exclude custom*[^15].

If it is reason, if it is truth, excellent; but this we shall presently see. Meanwhile, that there was another custom appears even from this man's words.

## Truth is to be set before custom.

**9. 12.** Likewise Honoratus of Tucca said: *Since Christ is the truth, we ought to follow truth rather than custom*[^16].

By all these opinions it is declared that we are not established outside the communion of the Church, until the truth, which they say is to be set before this custom, has shone clearly. But if the truth shall reveal that what that custom had prescribed is to be retained, it will be manifest both that the custom was not instituted and strengthened in vain, and that, even after these disputations, the most wholesome observance of so great a sacrament could not be changed in the Catholic Church, but, confirmed by the still greater strength of councils, is most religiously kept.

## Heretics do not have power and right over baptism.

**10. 13.** Cyprian therefore writes to Jubaian *about the baptism of heretics, who,* as it seems to him, *being placed outside and established outside the Church, claim for themselves a thing belonging neither to their right nor to their power; which we,* he says, *can think neither valid nor lawful, since it is established that this is unlawful among them*[^17].

Nor do we deny that one who is baptized among heretics or in some schism outside the communion of the Church does not profit by it insofar as he consents to the perversity of heretics and schismatics; nor that those who baptize, although they give the very and true sacrament of baptism, nevertheless do not act rightly in gathering outside the Church and thinking against the Church.

But it is one thing not to have something, another not to have it by right or to usurp it unlawfully. The sacraments of Christ and the Church are therefore not less his sacraments because not only heretics, but also all the wicked and impious, use them unlawfully. Yet these men are to be corrected or punished; but those sacraments are to be acknowledged and venerated.

## On this question two councils were held in Africa.

**10. 14.** He says, to be sure, that on this matter not one, but two or more councils were held, yet African ones. For he recalls that in a certain one there were seventy-one bishops[^18], to whose authority, all of them, we do not doubt—with the peace of Cyprian himself—to prefer the authority of the universal Church spread throughout the whole world with far more bishops, of which universal Church he rejoiced to be an unpluckable member.

## The water of baptism is not profane and adulterous.

**10. 15.** But the water over which the name of God is invoked is not *profane and adulterous,* even if it is invoked by the profane and adulterous; because neither is the creation itself adulterous, nor the name itself. But the baptism of Christ, consecrated by the words of the Gospel, is holy both through adulterers and in adulterers, though they be shameless and unclean; because its holiness itself cannot be polluted, and the divine power assists its sacrament, whether for the salvation of those who use it well, or for the ruin of those who use it ill.

Or does the light of the sun, or even of a lamp, when it is spread over filthy places, contract no defilement thence, and can the baptism of Christ be befouled by anyone's crimes? For if we direct our mind to the very visible things by which the sacraments are administered, who does not know that they are corruptible? But if to that which is done through them, who does not see that it cannot be corrupted, although the men through whom it is done, according to their morals, either receive rewards or pay penalties?

## The example of the Novatianists is of no avail.

**11. 16.** But rightly was Cyprian not moved by what Jubaian wrote: *that the Novatianists rebaptize those who came to them from the Catholic Church*[^19]. For whatever heretics have perversely imitated is not therefore not to be done by Catholics because those men do it likewise.

But there is one reason why heretics ought not to rebaptize, another why the Catholic Church ought not. For heretics ought not to do it even if it were to be done in the Catholic Church; because they say that this does not exist among Catholics, which they themselves received when they were there, and took away when they departed. But the Catholic Church ought not to repeat the baptism given among heretics, lest it seem to judge that what is Christ's belongs to them, or that they do not have what, since they received it within, they certainly could not lose by going outside.

For this Cyprian himself too decreed with the rest[^20]: that if any who had been baptized in any heresy returned to the Church, they should be received not now through baptism, but through penance; whence it is established that they cannot lose by departing what they do not receive by returning. But just as their heresy is theirs, as their error is theirs, as the sacrilege of dissension is theirs, so the baptism which is Christ's ought not to be called theirs. And so, when those evils of theirs are corrected when they return, in that which is not theirs he is to be acknowledged whose it is.

## Cyprian did not decree a new or sudden thing.

**12. 17.** But the blessed Cyprian shows that he did not decree a new or sudden thing, because it had already begun to be done under Agrippinus. *It is many years,* he says, *and a long age, since under Agrippinus, a man of good memory, very many bishops, coming together, decreed this*[^21].

Accordingly, by Agrippinus himself the new thing was done. But how he can say, *And from then to this day so many thousands of heretics in our provinces, converted to the Church, have not scorned, nor hesitated, but rather both reasonably and gladly embraced it, so that they might obtain the grace of the life-giving and saving washing of baptism*[^22], I do not see; unless perhaps he says this, *from then to this day,* because no question of any excommunication arose about those who, from the time they were baptized in the Church through the council of Agrippinus, were baptized.

But if the custom of baptizing those coming from heretics had continued from Agrippinus down to Cyprian, why were councils held by Cyprian on this matter? Why does he say to that same Jubaian that he is not doing a new or sudden thing, but one instituted by Agrippinus? For why would Jubaian be disturbed about a novelty, so that he had to be healed by the authority of Agrippinus, if the Church had held this from Agrippinus down to Cyprian? Why, finally, did so many of his colleagues in the council say that reason and truth are to be set before custom, and not rather say that those who wished to do otherwise were acting both against truth and against custom?

## Still the question is about the forgiveness of sins through baptism.

**13. 18.** But about the forgiveness of sins, whether it takes place through baptism among heretics, what seemed to me I have already written in another volume; but here too I recall it briefly.

If forgiveness of debts takes place there through the holiness of baptism, the debts return again through the obstinacy of heresy or schism; and therefore men of this kind have it as necessary to come to Catholic peace, that they may now cease to be heretics and schismatics, and may merit, by love working in the bond of unity, the purgation of those sins which had returned upon them.

But if, although among heretics or schismatics it is the same baptism of Christ, yet it does not there work the forgiveness of sins, on account of that same foulness of discord and iniquity of dissension: then the same baptism begins to avail for the forgiveness of sins when they have come to the peace of the Church; so that what was truly forgiven is not retained; and not so that that baptism, as though foreign or another, is disapproved, that another may be administered; but so that the very same baptism, which on account of discord was working death outside, may on account of peace work salvation within.

For it was the same odor of which the Apostle says: *We are the good odor of Christ in every place*; and yet, *both in those who are being saved,* he says, *and in those who are perishing: to some indeed an odor of life unto life, but to others an odor of death unto death*[^23]. And even though he said this about another matter, I have set it down for this purpose, that it may be understood that some good can work not only life for those who use it well, but also death for those who use it ill.

## On imperfect faith and baptism.

**14. 19.** Nor does it matter, when the integrity and holiness of the sacrament is treated, what he believes and with what sort of faith he is imbued who receives the sacrament. It matters indeed very much for the way of salvation, but for the question of the sacrament it matters nothing.

For it can happen that a man has an intact sacrament and a perverse faith: just as it can happen that he holds the words of the Creed intact, and yet does not rightly believe, whether about the Trinity itself, or about the resurrection, or something else. For it is no small thing, within the Catholic Church itself, to hold an intact faith, so as to believe nothing otherwise than the truth holds, not about any creation at all, but about God himself.

Is a man, then, if baptized in the Catholic Church itself, who afterward, by reading, hearing, and peaceably discussing, with the Lord himself revealing it, has recognized that he had previously believed otherwise than he ought, to be baptized again? But what carnal and natural man does not wander through the phantasms of his own heart, and set up for himself a God such as pleases him according to his carnal sense, and so believe him to be as far otherwise than God is as vanity differs from truth?

For the Apostle, full of the light of truth, spoke a most true sentence: *The natural man,* he says, *does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^24]. And yet he was saying this about those whom he himself makes plain to have been already baptized. For he says to them: *Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?*[^25] These men, then, had the sacrament of baptism, and yet, thinking carnally, what could they believe about God except according to the sense of their flesh, where *the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*? To such men he says: *I could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal. As to little ones in Christ, I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able; but neither indeed are you now able, for you are still carnal*[^26].

Such men are also carried about by every wind of doctrine, of whom he says: *That we may no longer be little ones, tossed and carried about by every wind of doctrine*[^27]. Are these men, then, if they have advanced to the spiritual age of the interior man, and by the sincerity of understanding have recognized how far otherwise, through the deceit of their phantasms, they believed about God than the truth demanded, to be baptized again?

So too it can happen that a Catholic catechumen comes upon the book of some heretic, and, not knowing how to distinguish error from truth, believes something against the Catholic faith; an error which, nevertheless, the words of the Creed do not contradict: for under these same words innumerable errors of heretics have arisen.

Thinking, then, that this book belongs to some great and learned Catholic, if, believing such things, he is baptized in the Catholic Church, and by later diligence recognizes how he ought to believe, and, having seized the Catholic faith, rejects that error from his soul, is he, if he confesses this, to be baptized again?

Or if, before he learns and confesses, he is found to hold this opinion, and is taught what he ought to cast off and what to believe, and it becomes manifest that he was baptized in that falsity of faith, is he to be baptized again? Why so? Because that holiness of the sacrament, consecrated by the words of the Gospel, remained intact over him, just as he had received it handed down; although he himself, established in the vanity of a carnal mind, believed, when he was baptized, something other than he ought to have believed.

Therefore it is manifest that it can happen that, with faith not intact, the sacrament of baptism remains intact in someone; and therefore the things that are said about the variety of the different heretics do not pertain to this question. For in each one that is to be corrected which the one through whom he is corrected perceives to be wrong. That is to be healed which is sick; that is to be given which is not had, and above all the love of peace, without which the rest cannot profit. But nevertheless, when those things are present, they are not to be administered as though they were absent; but, so that they may be had fruitfully and not perniciously, one must act through the very bond of peace and the preeminence of love.

## Baptism consecrated by the words of the Gospel is intact even if the faith is imperfect.

**15. 20.** Wherefore, if *Marcion consecrated baptism* with the words of the Gospel, *In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*[^28], the sacrament was intact; although his faith, holding under those same words something other than the Catholic truth teaches, was not intact, but defiled by fabulous falsehoods. For under those same words, that is, *In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,* not only Marcion, or Valentinus, or Arius, or Eunomius, but the very carnal little ones of the Church (to whom the Apostle said, *I could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal*[^29]), if each could be carefully questioned, would be counted as having perhaps as many diversities of opinions as there are men; for *the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^30]. Do they on that account not receive an intact sacrament? Or, if they make progress and amend the vanity of their carnal opinions, must what they received be on that account repeated?

Each one receives according to his faith[^31], but as much as he grasps under the governance of that mercy of God, presuming on which the same Apostle says: *If you are of any other mind, this too God will reveal to you*[^32]. Yet the snares of heretics and schismatics are for this reason exceedingly pernicious to the carnal, because their very advancement is blocked, with the opinion of vanity confirmed against the Catholic truth, and the animosity of dissension confirmed against Catholic peace. Yet the sacraments, if they are the same, are everywhere intact, even if they are wrongly understood and discordantly handled: just as the writing of the Gospel itself, if it is the very same, is everywhere intact, even if it is maintained with an innumerable variety of false opinions.

For as to what Jeremiah says[^33]: *Why do those who afflict me prevail? My wound is grievous; whence shall I be healed? It has become to me like deceitful water that has no faithfulness*[^34]; if water were nowhere set down in the figurative and allegorical speech of prophecy except such as signified baptism, we should labor to seek what these words of Jeremiah mean: but now, since in the Apocalypse the waters are most plainly explained as set for peoples[^35], why I should not understand deceitful and faithless water to mean a deceitful and faithless people, I do not know.

## Outside the Church love is not found, without which the rest are of no avail.

**16. 21.** But as for what is said—that *the Holy Spirit is given through the laying on of hands only in the Catholic Church*—our elders surely wished this to be understood as what the Apostle says: *For the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^36]. For that is the very love which those do not have who are cut off from the communion of the Catholic Church; and on this account, even if they speak with the tongues of men and of angels, if they know all mysteries and all knowledge, and if they have all prophecy and all faith, so as to remove mountains, and distribute all their goods to the poor, and hand over their body to be burned, it profits them nothing[^37].

But he does not have the love of God who does not love the unity of the Church; and on this account it is rightly understood to be said that the Holy Spirit is received only in the Catholic Church. For the Holy Spirit is now given through the laying on of hands not with temporal and sensible miracles bearing witness, as it was formerly given for the commendation of a fledgling faith and for enlarging the beginnings of the Church. For who now expects that those upon whom hands are laid for receiving the Holy Spirit should suddenly begin to speak with tongues? But it is understood that, invisibly and latently, on account of the bond of peace, divine love is breathed into their hearts, so that they can say: *For the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^38]. But many are the operations of the Holy Spirit, which the same Apostle, when in a certain place he had recalled as many as he judged sufficient[^39], concluded thus: *But all these the one and the same Spirit works, dividing to each his own as he wills*[^40].

Since, then, the sacrament is one thing, which even Simon Magus could have[^41]; the operation of the Spirit another, which is wont to take place even in wicked men, as Saul had prophecy[^42]; the operation of the same Spirit another, which none but the good can have, such as is the end of the commandment, love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned[^43]: whatever heretics and schismatics receive, the love which covers a multitude of sins[^44] is the proper gift of Catholic unity and peace; nor is it the gift of all within it, because not all belong to it, as we shall see in its place. Yet apart from that love those other things cannot exist, without which the rest, even if they can be acknowledged and approved, nevertheless cannot profit and set free. But the laying on of hands cannot, like baptism, fail to be repeated. For what else is it but a prayer over a man?

## The integrity of the sacrament is recognized everywhere, but does not avail for the forgiveness of sins.

**17. 22.** For as to what the Lord gave to Peter, in a figure of unity, that there should be loosed in heaven what he had loosed on earth[^45], it is manifest that that unity was also called the one perfect dove[^46]. Do, then, all the greedy belong to that same dove, about whom the same Cyprian grievously groaned within the Catholic Church itself[^47]? For, as I think, the rapacious can be called not doves but hawks. How, then, did those baptize who seized estates by treacherous frauds, and increased their interest by multiplying usuries, if that dove alone—that is, that unity which cannot be understood except among the good—simple and chaste and perfect, baptizes? Or is it perhaps that, through the prayers of the spiritual saints who are in the Church, as through the most frequent groaning of the dove, a great sacrament is carried on, and the hidden dispensation of God's mercy, so that even the sins of those are loosed who are baptized not by the dove but by the hawk, if they approach that sacrament with the peace of Catholic unity? But if it is so, why, then, are not a man's sins loosed through their prayers when he comes from heresy or schism to Catholic peace? But the integrity of the sacrament is recognized everywhere, yet for that irrevocable forgiveness of sins it will not avail outside the unity of the Church. Nor can the prayers of the saints—that is, the groanings of that one dove—help a man established in heresy or schism; just as they cannot help one placed within, if he holds against himself, through a most wicked life, the debts of his sins, not only if he is baptized through the hawk, but even if through the pious ministry of the dove itself.

## The peace of unity is among the good alone.

**18. 23.** *As the Father has sent me,* he says, *so I send you. When he had said this, he breathed upon them and said to them: Receive the Holy Spirit: if you forgive anyone's sins, they shall be forgiven him; if you retain anyone's, they shall be retained*[^48]. Therefore, if they bore the person of the Church, and this was said to them as though it were said to the Church itself, the peace of the Church forgives sins, and estrangement from the peace of the Church retains sins—not according to the discretion of men, but according to the discretion of God and the prayers of the spiritual saints, who judge all things, but are themselves judged by no one[^49].

For the rock retains, the rock forgives; the dove retains, the dove forgives; unity retains, unity forgives. But the peace of this unity is among the good alone—either those already spiritual, or those making progress toward spiritual things by concordant obedience: among the wicked, however, it is not, whether they make tumult outside, or are tolerated within with groaning, and both baptize and are baptized.

But just as those who are tolerated within with groaning, although they do not belong to that same unity of the dove, and to that glorious Church not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing[^50], nevertheless, if they are corrected and confess that they approached baptism as most wicked men, are not rebaptized, but begin to belong to the dove, through whose groanings their sins are loosed, by being estranged from whose peace they were held bound: so too those who are more openly outside, if they have received the same sacraments, when, corrected, they come to the unity of the Church, are set free not by a repeated baptism, but by the same law of love and bond of unity.

For if *it is permitted to baptize only to those set over the Church, and grounded in the evangelical law and the Lord's ordinance*[^51], were they such, who seized estates by treacherous frauds and increased their interest by multiplying usuries[^52]? For I think that those are grounded in the Lord's ordinance to whom the Apostle, in giving the rule, said: *Not greedy, not eager for base gain*[^53].

Yet such men baptized in the times of Cyprian himself, whom he confesses with great groaning to have been his fellow bishops, and whom he endures with the great reward of toleration. Yet they did not give forgiveness of sins, which is given through the prayers of the saints—that is, through the groanings of the dove—whoever baptizes, if those to whom it is given belong to its peace. For the Lord would not say to robbers and usurers: *If you forgive anyone's sins, they shall be forgiven him; if you retain anyone's, they shall be retained*[^54]. *Outside,* indeed, *nothing can be either bound or loosed, where there is no one who can either bind or loose*[^55]: but he is loosed who has made peace with the dove, and he is bound who does not have peace with the dove, whether he is openly outside, or seems to be within.

## The examples of Dathan and Abiram.

**18. 24.** But as for *Dathan and Abiram, who tried to usurp for themselves the license of sacrificing against the unity of the people of God, and the sons of Aaron who placed strange fire on the altar,* we know that *they did not do this with impunity*[^56]. Nor do we say that such crimes remain unpunished, unless such men have corrected themselves, if the patience of God, leading to repentance[^57], grants time for correcting them.

## The example of Philip the deacon.

**19. 25.** Those, indeed, who say that *baptism is not to be repeated because hands only were laid upon those whom Philip the deacon had baptized*[^58], say nothing pertaining to the matter; and far be it that, when we seek the truth, we should use such arguments. Wherefore we rather *do not yield to the heretics* if we say that what they have of the Church of Christ is not theirs, and if, on account of the crimes of the deserters, we are unwilling not to recognize the standards of our Emperor[^59]: rather, because *the Lord our God is a jealous God*[^60], whatever of his we recognize in anyone, let us in no way concede to that man that he should think it his own. God himself certainly reproaches the woman who fornicates away from him, in a figure of the prevaricating people, and says that the things which were his he gave to her lovers, and received again from them what was not theirs but his[^61]. Between the adulterous woman and the adulterous lovers, God nevertheless, as one jealous and angry, recognizes his own gifts[^62]: and shall we say that the baptism consecrated by the words of the Gospel is the heretics', moved by their deeds to attribute to them even the things that are God's, as though they could contaminate them, or make their own the things that are God's, because they themselves were unwilling to be God's?

## The gifts which heretics have are God's.

**19. 26.** Who is that adulterous woman whom the prophet Hosea designates, who said: *I will go after my lovers, who give me my bread and my water, my garments and my linens, and all things that befit me*[^63]? It is permitted indeed to understand this also of the prevaricating nation of the Jews: but yet whom else do the false Christians imitate (for this is what all heretics and schismatics are), except false Israelites?

For there were also true Israelites, as the Lord himself bears witness to Nathanael, saying: *Behold a true Israelite, in whom there is no guile*[^64]. But who are the true Christians, except those of whom the same Lord says: *He who loves me keeps my commandments*[^65]? But what is it to keep his commandments, except to persist in love? Hence he also says: *A new commandment I give you, that you love one another*[^66]; and again: *By this all will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another*[^67]. But who would doubt that this was said not to those alone who then, through his flesh, heard the words of the one present, but also to those who now, through the Gospel, know the words of the one seated in heaven?

For he came not to abolish the law, but to fulfill it[^68]. But the fullness of the law is love[^69]. In which Cyprian flourished greatly, so that, thinking otherwise about baptism, he nevertheless did not desert unity, and was a fruitful branch rooted in the Lord's vine, which the heavenly husbandman would even prune with the iron of his passion, that it might bear more fruit[^70].

But the enemies of this brotherly love, whether they are openly outside or seem to be within, are false Christians and antichrists. For, having found occasions, they go outside, as it is written: *He who wishes to depart from friends seeks occasions*[^71]. But even if occasions are lacking, when they seem to be within, they are separated from that invisible framework of love. Hence John says: *They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would surely have remained with us*[^72]. He did not say that by going out they became alien; but that they were alien, on this account he declared that they went out.

The Apostle also speaks of certain men who had wandered from the truth and were subverting the faith of some, whose talk crept like a cancer, whom, when he said they were to be avoided, he nevertheless indicates to have been in one great house, but as vessels for dishonor. I believe that they had not yet gone outside. Or if they had already gone outside, how does he say they were in that same great house with the honorable vessels—unless perhaps, on account of the very sacraments, which were not changed even in the separated assemblies of the heretics, he says that all belong to one great house, but with different merits, some for honor, some for dishonor?

For thus he speaks, writing to Timothy: *But avoid profane novelties of words; for they will advance much toward impiety, and their talk creeps like a cancer: among whom are Hymenaeus and Philetus, who have wandered from the truth, saying that the resurrection has already taken place, and they subvert the faith of some. But the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: The Lord knows those who are his; and let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity. But in a great house there are not only gold or silver vessels, but also wooden and earthen; and some indeed are for honor, but others for dishonor. If anyone, then, cleanses himself from such things, he will be a vessel for honor, sanctified, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work*[^73]. But what is it to cleanse oneself from such men, except what he said a little before: *Let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity*?

And lest anyone should think he could perish, as though in one great house, together with such men, he most vigilantly foretold: *The Lord knows those who are his*—namely, those who, by departing from iniquity, cleanse themselves from the vessels made for dishonor, lest they perish along with those whom they are compelled to tolerate in the great house.

## The gifts of God are not to be attributed to heretics.

**19. 27.** The wicked, then, and the criminal, the carnal, the natural, the diabolical, suppose that the things which are nothing but the gifts of God—whether the sacraments, or certain spiritual operations concerning present salvation—are given to them by their seducers. But these men do not have love toward God, but are taken up with those by whose pride they are seduced, and they are likened to the fornicating woman whom the prophet recalls saying: *I will go after my lovers, who give me my bread and my water, my garments and my linens and my oil, and all things that befit me*[^74]. For thus heresies and schisms come about, when the carnal people, not grounded in the love of God, says: *I will go after my lovers*—with whom, certainly, whether through corruption of faith or through the elation of pride, it shamefully fornicates. But on account of those who, having suffered difficulties and straits and obstructions in the empty reasonings of those by whom they are seduced, are pricked by fears, and return to the way of peace and to seeking God sincerely; therefore it follows and says: *Therefore, behold, I will hedge her way with stakes, and I will build up thorns in her path, and she shall not find her track; and she shall pursue her lovers, but shall not overtake them, and shall seek them, but shall not find them; and she shall say: I will go and return to my former husband, for it was better with me then than now*[^75].

Then, lest the things which the seducers have should be thought intact and coming from the doctrine of truth—the things through which they seduce men toward the falsehoods of their dogmas and dissensions—lest, then, the intact things they have should be thought theirs, he at once added: *And she did not know that I gave her grain and wine and oil, and multiplied silver for her; but she made gold and silver for Baal*[^76].

For she had said above: *I will go after my lovers, who give me my bread,* and so on—not at all understanding that these things are God's, not men's, whatever even the seducers hold intact and lawful: nor would they arrogate these things to themselves and claim them as their own, were they not in turn seduced by the seduced peoples, when they are believed and such honors are paid them, so that through them they may have the power of saying such things and claiming such things for themselves; so that their error may be called truth, and their crime thought justice, on account of the sacraments and Scriptures which they hold for appearance, not for salvation.

And therefore through Ezekiel too it is said to the same fornicatress: *And you took from the vessels of your glory, of my gold and of my silver, which I gave you, and you made for yourself male images, and fornicated with them. And you took my many-colored garment, and covered your idols; and my oil and my incense you set before the face of your idols, and my bread which I gave you; and with fine flour and honey and oil I fed you; and you set these before the face of your idols as a sweet-smelling odor. And you accomplished these things*[^77]. For to the images of its own phantasms, with which the carnal soul delights to wallow, it turns all the sacraments and the words of the holy Books. Yet not because those images are false, and are doctrines of demons, in the hypocrisy of liars[^78], are those sacraments too, and the divine words, to be so dishonored that they be reckoned theirs: since the Lord says: *Of my gold and of my silver and my many-colored garment, and my oil and incense and my bread,* and the rest. Or because those erring ones think these things belong to those by whom they are seduced, are we therefore not to recognize whose they are; when he himself says: *And she did not know that I gave her grain and wine and oil, and multiplied silver for her*[^79]? For he did not say that she did not have these things, because she was a fornicatress: rather, she is said both to have had them, and that they were not hers nor her lovers', but God's, whose alone these things are. Therefore, although she had her fornication, yet the things with which she adorned that fornication—whether seduced or seducing—were not hers, but God's.

If these things were prefigured about the Jewish nation, where the scribes and Pharisees rejected the commandment of God in order to establish their own traditions, so that with the people forsaking God they fornicated, as it were, in a certain manner; and yet such fornication in the people of that time, which the Lord by reproving brought to light, did not bring it about that the sacraments were theirs, which were not theirs but God's, who, speaking to the fornicatress, says that all those things are his; whence the Lord himself even sent those whom he cleansed from leprosy to those same sacraments, that they might offer sacrifice for themselves to the priests, because there had not yet succeeded for them the sacrifice which he afterward willed to be celebrated in the Church in place of all those, because in all those he himself was foretold: how much more ought we, finding the sacraments of the New Testament among any heretics or schismatics, not to attribute them to those men, nor to reject them as though unrecognized; but, although it be in a fornicating woman, to recognize the gifts of the lawful husband, and by the word of truth to amend that fornication which is proper to the unchaste woman, not to blame those gifts which are proper to the merciful Lord?

## The fathers always held the custom of not rebaptizing heretics.

**19. 28.** By the consideration of these and such matters our fathers, not only before Cyprian or Agrippinus, but also afterward, held the most wholesome custom, that whatever divine and lawful thing they found intact in any heresy or schism, they should rather approve than deny; but whatever was alien, and proper to that error or dissension, they should truthfully reprove and heal.

But the things which remain to be considered from that letter which was written to Jubaian, on account of the length of this volume, are, as I think, to be taken up and treated from another beginning.
