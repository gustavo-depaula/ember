# Book 3

## The medicine of punishment ought to preserve unity.

**1. 1.** Since every pious account and measure of ecclesiastical discipline ought above all to regard the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace[^1]—which the Apostle commanded to be preserved by bearing with one another, and which not being preserved, the medicine of punishment is convicted of being not only superfluous but even ruinous, and therefore no longer a medicine at all—those evil sons, who, not out of hatred of the iniquities of others, but out of zeal for their own contentions, seek either to draw away entirely or at least to divide the weak peoples ensnared by the boasting of their own name, swollen with pride, mad with stubbornness, treacherous with slanders, turbulent with seditions,

lest they be shown to lack the light of truth, hold out the shadow of a rigid severity; and the things that in the holy Scriptures were commanded to be done—with the sincerity of love preserved and the unity of peace kept—for correcting the faults of brothers by a more biting cure, they usurp for the sacrilege of schism and as an occasion of cutting off, saying, 'Behold, the Apostle says, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*[^2]. Which evil, surely,' they say, 'if it did not harm the whole and uncorrupt, would not be commanded to be driven out.'

## If the wicked cannot be removed, let the evil be removed from oneself.

**1. 2.** For the moment let us see whether perhaps it was not without reason that the Apostle did not say, 'Drive out the wicked from your congregation,' but, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*—because, even when anyone is hindered from separating wicked men from the congregation of the Church, if he drives out the evil from himself, he is not mingled with them in heart, and thus spiritually he is not only joined to the good but also separated from the wicked.

For just as in that passage to Timothy, when he had said, *Do not commune with the sins of others*[^3]—as if Timothy were saying that it could happen that he could not separate some wicked men from the congregation of the Church and was therefore compelled to tolerate them—as if giving counsel how he should not commune with their sins, he said, *Keep yourself chaste*—for none can be mingled with the wicked except a wicked man, but a good man in no way, although he dwell in one congregation with them—

so too here, to the Corinthians, when he had said, *For what business is it of mine to judge those who are outside? Is it not those who are within whom you judge?*[^4]—lest perhaps they be troubled by some great number of the wicked so mingled with the wheat that it could not be gathered and separated without harm to them—he said, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*, so that, if perhaps they could not drive out the wicked from their congregation, by driving out the evil from themselves—that is, by not sinning with them, nor consenting to them in sinning, nor favoring them—they might dwell among them most whole and most uncorrupted; because through his own evil each one consents to the wicked, but if he drives out the evil from himself, there is nothing by which he might consent to another's evil.

Wherefore whoever even despises the discipline of the Church, so that he ceases to admonish, rebuke, and arraign the wicked with whom he does not sin and whom he does not favor—even, if he bears such an office and the peace of the Church permits it, to separate them from the participation of the sacraments—sins not by another's evil but by his own. For in so great a matter that very negligence is a grave evil.

And therefore, as the Apostle admonishes, if he drives out the evil from himself, he will drive out not only the boldness of committing or the pestilence of consenting, but also the sloth of correcting and the negligence of punishing—prudence and obedience being applied in that which the Lord commanded, lest the wheat be harmed[^5].

For with that intention whoever tolerates weeds among the wheat, by driving out the evil from himself, neither communes with them and distinguishes and judges them, for the time being, until the day[^6]. For he does not know what will be tomorrow. And therefore, with love preserved, whatever necessary severity compels to be punished is to be punished not without hope of correction. And that this may sufficiently appear, let us retrace the whole passage of the apostolic epistle more carefully.

## The example of Paul in punishing the wicked.

**1. 3.** *What do you wish?* he says. *Shall I come to you with a rod, or in love and a spirit of gentleness?*[^7] Here already it appears that he speaks of punishment, to signify which he named the rod. Yet is even the rod without love, because he so connected it, *Shall I come to you with a rod, or in love?* But what follows, *a spirit of gentleness*, admonishes us to understand that the rod too has love. But the love of severity is one thing, the love of gentleness another. There is indeed one love, but it works diversely in diverse cases. *It is altogether heard*, he says, *that there is fornication among you, and such fornication as is not even among the gentiles, so that someone has his father's wife*[^8].

Let us see how, over so monstrous a deed, he commands them to be severe. *And you*, he says, *are puffed up and have not rather mourned, that he who did this work might be taken from your midst*[^9]. Why *rather mourned* and not been angry, except because *if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it*[^10]? And not 'mourned because he was being taken away,' but *mourned*, he says, *that he might be taken away*—that is, that the grief of the mourners might ascend to God, and he himself might take him who did this work from their midst, as he himself should know how, lest perhaps they, through human ignorance, root up the wheat together with it.

When, then, necessity compels to such a punishment, the humility of the mourners ought to obtain the mercy that the pride of the raging repels. Nor should the salvation even of him who is taken from the midst of the brothers be neglected, but it must be done in such a way that such a punishment is useful to him, and it must be done with vow and prayers, if he cannot be corrected by rebukes.

And therefore he goes on and says, *I indeed, as absent in body but present in spirit, have already judged, as if present, him who has so acted: in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, you being gathered together and my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus, to hand over such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus*[^11].

What, then, was the Apostle doing, except providing, through the destruction of the flesh, for spiritual salvation—so that, whether by some punishment or even by bodily death (as Ananias and his wife fell before the feet of the apostle Peter[^12]), or through penance, since he had been handed over to Satan, the man might destroy in himself the criminal concupiscence of the flesh—because the Apostle himself likewise says, *Mortify your members that are upon the earth*[^13], among which he mentions also *fornication*, and again, *For if you live according to the flesh, you will die; but if by the spirit you mortify the deeds of the flesh, you will live*[^14]?

Yet he does not separate brotherly love from him whom he commanded to be separated from the brotherly congregation. For this he says more plainly to the Thessalonians: *But if anyone does not obey our word through this epistle, mark that man, that you may not associate with him, so that he may be ashamed. And do not regard him as an enemy, but reprove him as a brother*[^15]. Let these men at some time hear and understand how apostolic love takes pains that we, bearing with one another, may strive to preserve the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace[^16].

For here too, when he had said, *And do not regard him as an enemy, but reprove him as a brother*, as if showing why he said this, he immediately subjoined, *But may the God of peace himself give you peace always in every way*[^17]. So too about that man who had his father's wife, he enjoins mourning rather, and everywhere commends peaceful love, as he says also of himself, *Lest, when I come again, God humble me before you, and I mourn for many of those who sinned before and did not repent of the uncleanness and licentiousness and fornication that they committed*[^18]. And a little later he says, *I have foretold and I foretell, as when present the second time, and now absent, to those who sinned before and to all the rest, that if I come again I will not spare*[^19]. Therefore by mourning he was judging, that the mercy of God, without the corruption of the bond of peace—in which all salvation consists—might crush and correct the sinners, as he is understood to have done concerning that very man who had fornicated with his father's wife.

For it is not found of what other man he means in the second Epistle to the same Corinthians, when he says, *For out of much tribulation and anguish of heart I wrote to you through many tears, not that you should be made sorrowful, but that you might know the love that I have more abundantly toward you. But if anyone has caused sorrow, he has not grieved me, but in part, that I may not burden all of you.*

*This reproof, which is made by many, suffices for him, so that, on the contrary, you should rather forgive and console him, lest somehow he who is such be swallowed up by greater sorrow. Wherefore I beseech you to confirm your love toward him. For to this end also I wrote, that I might know your proof, whether you are obedient in all things. But if you forgive anything to anyone, I also; for I too, if I have forgiven anything to anyone, have done it for your sake in the person of Christ, that we may not be taken advantage of by Satan. For we are not ignorant of his designs*[^20].

What more moderate, what more diligent, what fuller of pious solicitude and of paternal and maternal love can be done or said? Just as he applies amendment to the one sinning, so to the one corrected and crushing and humbling his heart in penance he wishes consolation to be rendered, *lest*, he says, *he be swallowed up by greater sorrow*. But what of that with which he concluded this sentence? *That we may not be taken advantage of*, he says, *by Satan. For we are not ignorant of his designs*[^21]. For it is he who, through the image of as it were a just severity, persuades to a cruel savagery, seeking by his most venomous craft nothing else than to corrupt and break the bond of peace and love—which being preserved among Christians, all his powers become weak to harm, and the snares of his ambushes are crushed, and his counsels of overthrow vanish.

## Ecclesiastical punishment ought to proceed from love.

**2. 4.** But even if the Apostle spoke of some other man in the Second Epistle to the Corinthians, there too he signified with how great love ecclesiastical punishment ought to proceed against anyone. This is that which these men, not understanding it, are accustomed to hold as chief among their slanders: *The just man shall amend me in mercy and reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head*[^22].

But because these men have not known how to amend in mercy, they have both pursued the innocence of Caecilian with savage suspicions and anointed the power of Optatus the Gildonian with the oil of deceitful flattery.

For if, on account of the bond of peace, they tolerated the iniquity of Optatus, groaning and mourning, they would surely not break the true and catholic peace in the holy unity of the world,

or at any rate they would so grieve that it had been broken by their elders in a wicked blindness that, taught at least by experience among themselves how many wicked men they are compelled to bear for the peace of Donatus,

they would extinguish those men's unpeaceful slanders by the peace of their own correction.

## Christ, by his mercy, is the physician of sinners.

**2. 5.** But let us return to what follows in that First Epistle to the Corinthians. When the Apostle had said, *To hand over such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus*[^23], again and again commending that this ought to be done with the humility of the mourning, not the pride of the raging, he immediately subjoins, *Your boasting is not good*[^24]—or, by way of reproachful pronouncement, *Your boasting is good*.

For so some, and especially the Latin manuscripts, have it, though the same meaning is held in both. For there is no fear that anyone should understand him to have said in praise, *Your boasting is good*, since above he said, *You are puffed up and have not rather mourned*, and here he immediately subjoins, *Do you not know that a little leaven corrupts the whole lump?*[^25]—which can more fittingly be referred to the very corruption of empty boasting. For pride, as if from the oldness of the first man, who fell by pride, with a mind as it were leavened and corrupted, makes those puffed up into one lump who consent to it among themselves with a like vanity of boasting.

And to boast indeed not of one's own sins, but over another's sins, as it were by comparison with one's own innocence, seems a little leaven, because to boast even of one's own iniquities is much leaven; but even that little corrupts the whole lump. For the proud man falls by the desert of his pride and begins, by defending his sins, even to wish to boast of them. Foreseeing which, the same Apostle says, *Wherefore let him who thinks he stands beware lest he fall*[^26], and again, *If a man be overtaken in any fault, you who are spiritual instruct such a one in a spirit of gentleness, attending to yourself, lest you too be tempted. Bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*[^27].

What is the law of Christ but, *A new commandment I give you, that you love one another*[^28]? What is the law of Christ but, *My peace I give to you, my peace I leave to you*[^29]? What, then, he said here, *Bear one another's burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ*, this he says in another place, *Bearing with one another in love, striving to preserve the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*[^30]. For in that Pharisee too there seemed to be a little leaven, because he not only did not grieve over the sinner, but over his sins extolled himself for his own merits. But the one confessing his sins went down justified more than that Pharisee boasting of his merits, *for he who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted*[^31].

The Apostle therefore goes on and says, *Purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new lump, as you are unleavened*[^32]. What is *that you may be* and what is *as you are*, except that there were there men of one sort, and there were there men not of that sort, whom, that they might be of that sort, he was admonishing by the example of those who were?

Yet he admonishes all in one, as it were the same men, lest those who were of that sort despair of those who were not yet of that sort and think that they did not pertain to the framework of his body; for he admonishes them as if the same, when he says, *That you may be, as you are*.

For those too who already were such knew, and, the Apostle admonishing, ought more and more to know, that they should bear with those who were not yet such, so that, bearing with one another in love, they might preserve the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace[^33] and, bearing one another's burdens, might surely fulfill the law of Christ[^34]. And since our Lord Jesus Christ, to teach the way of humility, deigned to be humbled even to the death of the cross, and, like a physician, so bore with love the sick who sinned—of whom he had said, *The healthy have no need of a physician, but the sick*[^35]—he at once set forth the chief example, saying, *For Christ our Passover has been sacrificed*[^36], that by the example of so great a humility they might learn to purge out the old leaven, that is, whatever of pride had remained in them from the old man. *Therefore*, he says, *let us celebrate the feast*—not indeed one day, but the whole life—*not in the old leaven, nor in the leaven of malice and wickedness, but in the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth*[^37].

For it is malice and wickedness, as it were, to boast of another's sin, as though each ought then to rejoice in his own justice when he has seen that another is not just. But it is sincerity and truth, even if anyone makes progress, to remember what he was, and much more to have mercy on the fallen, since he himself was raised from his own fall through the mercy of Christ, who without any sin of his own humbled himself for sinners.

## The sins of others are not to be neglected.

**2. 6.** But lest the sins of others be ruinously left and as it were dissemblingly neglected—which is of no less cruelty than that of pride—he goes on and says, *I wrote to you in an epistle not to associate with fornicators—not at all with the fornicators of this world, or the avaricious, or robbers, or those who serve idols. Otherwise you would have had to go out of this world*[^38]—that is, if you keep yourselves from the sinners of this world who are outside the Church, you would have had to go out of this world. For your work in this world is to win sinners to Christ unto salvation; which cannot be done if you flee their conversation and company. *But now*, he says, *I have written to you not to associate, if anyone called a brother is either a fornicator, or serving idols, or avaricious, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or rapacious—with such a one not even to take food together. For what business is it of mine to judge those who are outside? Is it not those who are within whom you judge? But those who are outside God will judge. Drive out the evil from among yourselves*[^39].

## Parmenian does not cite the last particle of Paul's sentence.

**2. 7.** Behold how the Apostle comes to this sentence, the last particle of which Parmenian believed should be set down, saying that it is written, '*Drive out the evil from among yourselves*[^40]. Which surely,' he says, 'if it did not harm the good and the whole, would not be commanded to be driven out.' But the things said above, from which it came to this, he passed over—by which surely he could be helped toward what he desired to persuade, that separation from the wicked ought to be made bodily, since the Apostle says, *With such a one not even to take food together*[^41]. Why, then, did he not mention what seemed able most of all to help his intention?

For when he vehemently insists in persuading that a bodily separation must be made from those who live wickedly, why does he not adduce the testimony of the Apostle saying, *If anyone called a brother is either a fornicator, or serving idols, or avaricious, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or rapacious—with such a one not even to take food together*, except because he saw that, if he said this, it could be answered him: 'So, then, even if you either do not have fornicators and idol-servers or are ignorant of them, do you see and know no avaricious man, or reviler, or drunkard, or rapacious man among you? Why, then, with such men, against the precept of the Apostle, do you not only take the food of your table, but even commune in the supper of the Lord's table?' This, then, Parmenian, as I judge, tried to avoid, lest it be answered him, so that he should not set down what would seem to sound most strongly for his cause. For if this chapter of the apostolic epistle had escaped him and not occurred to him, he would not set down from its last part, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*[^42].

## The example of Cyprian, who remained among certain wicked colleagues.

**2. 8.** But now, because we have said this, perhaps these men will dare to deny that they have among themselves avaricious, or reviling, or drunken, or rapacious men, and perhaps will try to defend Optatus himself, who was most notorious to all Africa, whom they so long bore with. Let them say, then, if they can, that they now have a Church better and more purged than the very unity was in the times of the most blessed Cyprian, who—calling none of them by name, but prudently and soberly applying the medicine of a most salutary biting—gravely arraigned his colleagues, from whom nevertheless he was separated by no bodily disjunction, with these words: that, while their brothers in the Church went hungry, they wished to have silver in abundance, seized estates by treacherous frauds, and increased their gain by multiplying usuries.

And to show most plainly that he was speaking of those with whom he lived in the communion of one Church, he consequently added, 'What should we not deserve to suffer for sins of this kind?' For he did not say, 'should they deserve,' but, 'should we deserve'—which he would in no way have said, since he himself was surely not such, unless he wished to make manifest that he groaned over the deeds of those who were joined to him not only in the unity of the Church but also in the fellowship of the college, although he differed from them in life, morals, heart, and purpose.

Let these men say, then, that their church is now better and that they do not have such colleagues as Cyprian had in the very unity. Let those believe them who will, and close their eyes against those evils which, from the morals of those who disguise their face, strike it; I will call them back to those very earlier times of unity and ask them whether, when that so great man Cyprian, bishop of the Church of Carthage, groaned over so wicked a college by the testimony of a free voice, even in those writings that would profit posterity too, the Church of Christ existed or did not exist.

If it existed, I ask how Cyprian and the rest like him fulfilled what the Apostle commanded, *If anyone called a brother is either a fornicator, or serving idols, or avaricious, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or rapacious—with such a one not even to take food together*[^43], when, together with these avaricious and rapacious men—who, while their brothers in the Church went hungry, wished to have silver in abundance, seized estates by treacherous frauds, and increased their gain by multiplying usuries—they ate the bread of the Lord and drank the cup of the Lord.

## Open, O Donatists, the eyes of your heart!

**2. 9.** Or are these crimes perhaps small and to be reckoned small? For these men are accustomed to say even this, weighing them not in the just balance of the divine Scriptures, but in the deceitful balance of their own customs. For whatever crime and iniquity intoxicates the multitude loses the truth of examination. But for this reason the oracles of the heavenly pages are set before men like a most sincere mirror, that there each may see how great any sin is which is perhaps great and is despised through the blind custom of those who live wickedly.

Could avarice be more gravely accused by the divine eloquence than that it should be shown equal to idolatry and called by its name, the Apostle saying, *And avarice, which is the service of idols*[^44]? Could it be judged worthy of a greater penalty than that it should be placed among those crimes by which the ones beset will not possess the kingdom of God? Let them open the eyes of their heart, lest the eyes of their body be open in vain, and let them read the free preacher of truth writing in that same First Epistle to the Corinthians: *Do not be deceived. Neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor those who lie with men, nor thieves, nor the avaricious, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor the rapacious will possess the kingdom of God*[^45].

How, then, did Cyprian and the other wheat of the Lord, in that Church of unity at that time, eat the bread of the Lord and drink the cup of the Lord together with the avaricious and rapacious, with those who will not possess the kingdom of God—not laypeople or any clerics, but bishops themselves—when the same Apostle commands *not to associate with them* and cries out, *with such a one not even to take food*[^46]? Was it because they could not be separated from them bodily, lest the wheat be rooted up together, and it sufficed for them to be sundered from such men in heart, distinguished from them in life and morals, for the sake of the compensating consideration of preserving peace and unity, for the sake of the salvation of the weak and as it were suckling wheat, lest they tear the members of the body of Christ by sacrilegious schisms?

## How the Church could be holy in the time of Cyprian.

**2. 10.** But behold, I do not press any of them to understand it thus; let them themselves explain how that Church could then be glorious, without spot or wrinkle[^47], where, while their brothers in the Church went hungry, they wished to have silver in abundance, where they seized estates by treacherous frauds, where they increased their gain by multiplying usuries, where they were involved in such great iniquities that through them they would not possess the kingdom of God.

But if the glorious Church without spot or wrinkle was reckoned only in those who grieved and groaned over those iniquities which were committed in their midst—whence too, according to the prophecy of holy Ezekiel, such men deserved to be marked with a special sign, so that they might escape most safely from the devastation and destruction of the iniquitous[^48]—let them cease to slander the good who do not do evils through a diseased cupidity, but tolerate them through peaceful love, to whom it was said, *Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*[^49].

For this reason the Holy Spirit, through the aforementioned prophet Ezekiel, designated by such a word the wicked whom the good tolerate in unity, so as to say that they were set in the midst of the good; because, if it said that the good were in the midst of the wicked, the good would seem to be as it were outside and external. *They groan*, he says, *and grieve over the iniquities of my people that are committed in their midst*[^50], so that we should think those iniquitous men not only not excluded, but even enclosed.

## The Church has remained, remains, and will remain in the good faithful.

**2. 11.** But if even then there was no Church, because Cyprian and all who with him knew those avaricious and rapacious men—such as they themselves were not—although they accused them with the gravest groans and the most illustrious words, yet, entering one Church with them and handling equal sacraments in one congregation, were by this kind of communion made of equal lot, because they did not obey the Apostle commanding *with such a one not even to take food together* and, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*[^51]—why do we still labor?

Why do they themselves boast that they have any Church, if it already ceased to exist in those times? Let them say from where Majorinus or Donatus was born, that through them Parmenian and Primian might be born.

For what does it profit them that they now lie that they either do not have avaricious and rapacious men—with whom the Apostle forbids food to be taken—in their congregation, or that they are unknown to them, since there were such men in that Church of unity, from which these men so boast that they sprang that they try to persuade us that it has remained in their fellowship alone, that is, in the communion of Donatus? For if they say that through the communion of such men the Church perishes, why do they not say that it already perished in the times of Cyprian? And so, not finding even from where they themselves arose, let them cease to say that the Church has remained among them—the Church which they say wholly perished in earlier times.

But if the Church has always remained, and remains, and will remain in the good to whom such things are displeasing, let these men at some time learn not so to understand what the Apostle says, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*, that, trying through schisms to gather the weeds, they root up the wheat together with them.

All these things we so argue that those who read or hear them may remember that they could never have shown—either in those times, when the events were most recent, or now, when, with a far firmer conscience of innocence, the whole world is bound together for itself in the catholic Church in Christian peace—that Caecilian or the others who concordantly adhered to him were weeds. But we say these things so that each one may remain secure in the unity of the holy Church, and may not follow the deserters of that same unity, lest he perish with them; because, if those men had been weeds, they ought rather to have been tolerated until the harvest than separated, the wheat being rooted up, by the ruin of schism.

## Augustine imagines some difficulty on someone's part.

**2. 12.** But someone will say: 'How, then, will we be able to obey the Apostle commanding, who says, *With such a one not even to take food together*[^52]? For if he were commanding that only a separation of heart should be made from them, he would not say, *I wrote to you in an epistle not to associate with fornicators—not at all with the fornicators of this world*[^53], that is, with those who are not Christians, of whom he afterward says, *For what business is it of mine to judge those who are outside? Is it not those who are within whom you judge? But those who are outside God will judge*[^54].

Since, then, he commands such a separation, which is made not from those wicked who are not Christians, but from those who are Christians—but a separation of heart is to be made from all the wicked, and through this we ought to be separated in heart even from those wicked who are not Christians—what remains except that we understand that this is commanded us through the Apostle: that with certain wicked Christians, such as he designates, we should not be mingled by such a conjunction as that by which we are mingled with the pagans in the very fellowship of human life? And therefore in another place, *If any of the unbelievers invites you and you wish to go, eat everything that is set before you, asking no questions*[^55]; but here he says, *With such a one not even to take food together*[^56]. He permits, then, food to be taken with unbelievers, that is, with those who have not yet believed in Christ, among whom he says one must eat what they set before him, and about whom, since they are outside, *God will judge*[^57]; but with those who are within—that is, *if anyone called a brother is either a fornicator, or serving idols, or avaricious, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or rapacious*[^58]—he forbids even food to be taken. He persuades, then, that the weeds be separated from the wheat before the time of harvest. But if we are unwilling to do this, since the Lord forbids it, we shall tolerate them and persevere in only a separation of will and heart from them, and through this shall even take food with such men, which the Apostle forbids.'

## He replies by bringing forward the health of the Church.

**2. 13.** In this, as it were, strait of the question, I will say nothing new or unusual, but what the health of the Church observes: that, when any of the brothers—that is, of the Christians established within in the fellowship of the Church—has been caught in some such sin that he is held worthy of anathema, this be done where there is no danger of schism, and with that love of which the Apostle himself elsewhere commands, saying, *That you regard him not as an enemy, but reprove him as a brother*[^59]. For it is not done to root out, but to correct.

But if he does not acknowledge himself and correct himself by repenting, he himself will go out and, by his own will, will be sundered from the communion of the Church. For the Lord himself too, when he said to the servants who wished to gather the weeds, *Let both grow until the harvest*, prefaced the cause, saying, *Lest perhaps, when you wish to gather the weeds, you root up the wheat together with them*[^60].

Where he sufficiently shows that, when this fear is not present, but a wholly certain security about the stability of the wheat remains—that is, when anyone's crime is so known and appears so execrable to all that it has either no defenders at all or none such through whom a schism could come about—the severity of discipline should not sleep, in which the amendment of depravity is the more effective the more diligent is the preservation of love.

But this can then be done without the stain of peace and unity and without harm to the wheat, when the multitude of the Church's congregation is a stranger to that crime which is anathematized. For then it helps the prelate reproving rather than the criminal resisting; then it salutarily keeps itself from his company, so that no one even takes food with him—not with hostile rage, but with brotherly coercion; then too that man is both struck with fear and healed by shame, when, seeing himself anathematized by the whole Church, he cannot find a companion crowd with which to rejoice in his offense and insult the good.

## Exegesis of the text: If anyone called a brother.

**2. 14.** For to this end the Apostle himself says, *If anyone called a brother*[^61]. For in that he says, *If anyone*, he seems to have wished to signify nothing else than that one can be salutarily corrected in such a manner who sins among unlike men, that is, among those whom the pestilence of like sins does not corrupt. But in that he says, *called*, he doubtless wished this to be understood: that it is too little for anyone to be such, unless he is also so called—that is, appears notorious—so that the sentence of anathema pronounced against him may seem to all most worthy. For thus he is both corrected with peace preserved, and is struck not lethally but burned medicinally. Therefore he said also of that man whom he had wished to be healed by such a medicine, *This reproof, which is made by many, suffices for him*[^62]. For a reproof by many cannot be salutary except when he is reproved who does not have a companion multitude.

But when the same disease has seized very many, nothing else remains for the good but grief and groaning, that through that sign which is revealed to holy Ezekiel they may deserve to escape unharmed from the devastation of those men[^63]—for they cry out to him who cannot err, *Do not destroy my soul with the impious, and my life with men of blood*[^64]—lest, when they wish to gather the weeds, they root up the wheat together, and, instead of purging the Lord's crop by diligence, they themselves rather be numbered among the refuse through rashness.

And therefore the same Apostle, when he had already learned that many were defiled with unclean licentiousness and fornications, writing to the same Corinthians in the Second Epistle, does not again command that they should not take food with such men—for they were many, nor could it be said of them, *If anyone called a brother is a fornicator*, or avaricious, or anything such, *with such a one not even to take food together*[^65]—but he says, *Lest, when I come again, God humble me before you, and I mourn for many of those who sinned before and did not repent of the uncleanness and licentiousness and fornication that they committed*[^66]. By his own mourning he rather threatens that they are to be coerced by the divine scourge than by that reproof, that the rest should keep themselves from their company.

For he consequently says, *Behold, this is the third time I am coming to you. In the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall stand. I have foretold and I foretell, as when present the second time and now absent, to those who sinned before and to all the rest, that if I come again I will not spare, since you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me*[^67].

What else did he say here, *I will not spare*, but what he said above, *And I mourn*—so that his mourning might obtain a scourge from the Lord by which those might be corrected who, now on account of their multitude, could not be corrected in such a way that the rest should keep themselves from their company and make them ashamed, as must be done if any brother is called by name in some crime unlike that of the rest?

And indeed, when the contagion of sinning has invaded the multitude, the severe mercy of divine discipline is necessary. For counsels of separation are both empty and ruinous and sacrilegious, because they are done with impious pride and disturb the weak good more than they correct the headstrong wicked. So that most faithful witness of the avarice of his colleagues, when he attributed to divine censure and discipline the evils of the tribulations that the Church suffered at that time, having mentioned the worst morals he knew of certain bishops—who, while their brothers went hungry, wished to have silver in abundance, seized estates by treacherous frauds, increased their gain by multiplying usuries—said, 'What should we not deserve to suffer for sins of this kind?' And then he adduces a testimony from the Psalms, saying, 'Since the divine censure has long since forewarned and said, *If his sons forsake my law and do not walk in my judgments, if they profane my justifications and do not observe my precepts, I will visit their crimes with the rod and their offenses with scourges. But my mercy I will not scatter from them*[^68].'

## The sinner is to be mercifully reproved, patiently tolerated.

**2. 15.** Let a man, then, mercifully reprove what he can; but what he cannot, let him patiently bear and, with love, groan or mourn, until either God from above amends and corrects it, or until he defers, until the harvest, to root up the weeds and winnow the chaff. Yet, that Christians of good hope, secure about their own salvation, may dwell in unity among the desperate whom they cannot reprove, let them drive out the evil from themselves—that is, that there be not found in them what displeases them in the morals of others. For when the Apostle had said, *For what business is it of mine to judge those who are outside? Is it not those who are within whom you judge? But those who are outside God will judge*[^69], as if they answered, 'What do we do, when we are so pressed by the multitude of the wicked that we cannot exert our judgment in any coercion?', he says, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*—that is, 'If you cannot drive out the wicked from your midst, drive out the evil itself from yourselves.'

But if anyone wishes to understand what was said, *Drive out the evil from among yourselves*, in such a way that, through the reproof of separation, every wicked man is to be driven out from the congregation of the brothers, no one will doubt that it must be done with the zeal of healing, not with the hatred of destroying.

And what measure must be applied, and what times observed, lest the peace of the Church be violated—in which especially the wheat must be spared, lest it be rooted up together with the weeds—we have discussed, as far as seemed necessary at present. Whoever considers these things diligently and peacefully neither neglects the severity of discipline in the preservation of unity, nor breaks the bond of fellowship by the immoderation of coercion.

## Exegesis of Paul, 1 Cor 5:11.

**2. 16.** For this very thing that the Apostle says, *With such a one not even to take food together*[^70], how many good Christians do regarding those for whom they care more closely—so that, from the company of those whom they could separate, whom they perceive can be corrected by such a reproof, or whom they wholly despair of being able to correct, lest they corrupt others by the contagion of evil conversations[^71], they do not hesitate to do it. But he does this well—that is, with humble love and kindly severity—who so presides over his brothers that he remembers he is their servant, as is both the precept and the example of the Lord himself[^72]. For then it is done both without the swelling of pride against the man and with the mourning of supplication to God.

But the multitude of the wicked in any order of the Church cannot be shut off and expelled from the mingling with the good as easily as any one man is removed by a bishop from the grade of the clergy, or from the number of the poor whom the Church feeds, or from the very congregation of laypeople—whether by a bishop or a cleric or any prelate who has the power—so that with such a one no food is taken by the rest to whom this can be commanded.

For in their own houses too any good faithful so moderate and govern the discipline of their household that there too they obey the Apostle commanding, *With such a one not even to take food together*[^73], when they do this, or order it to be done, regarding their sons and their domestics, when they see them living in such a way that the very love which is held toward them suggests that this must be done to them.

But the crowd of the iniquitous, when there is the opportunity of delivering a sermon among the peoples, must be struck by a general rebuke, and especially if some scourge of the Lord from above has furnished the occasion and opportunity, by which it appears that they are being beaten for their deserts. For then the calamity of the hearers offers humble ears to the amending sermon, and compels afflicted hearts more easily into the groan of confessing than into the murmurs of resisting—just as blessed Cyprian then perhaps would not have said those things about his colleagues, unless the divine severity from above had helped him.

For he said those things at a time so troublesome, so calamitous, and so mournful that they not only did not dare to be angry, but even perceived that they could scarcely obtain pardon from those who were angry. And yet, even if no calamity of tribulation presses, when the opportunity is given, a multitude is usefully reproved in the multitude; for just as, separated, it is accustomed to rage, so, rebuked within the very congregation, it is accustomed to groan.

Wherefore that precept of the Apostle too[^74] is in no way to be neglected, when it can be done without danger of violating peace—because he himself did not wish the wicked man to be separated from the congregation of the good in any other way—and that other precept of his is especially to be held: that, bearing with one another, we should strive to preserve the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace[^75].

Likewise we ought to obey the Lord speaking in the Gospel, both in that where he says, *If he will not hear even the Church, let him be to you as a heathen and a publican*[^76], and in that where he forbade the weeds to be gathered, lest the wheat be rooted up together[^77]. For both can be kept by those of whom it was said, *Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God*[^78].

## Other testimonies set forth by Parmenian are explained.

**3. 17.** But now let us see the other testimonies that Parmenian set forth. Among all the things in which their sacrilegious swelling appears, in no place did he reveal himself more than in this, that he dared to set down even that passage from the prophet Jeremiah, by which he would persuade blind men that the communion of the Donatists is not only the true Church, but even such already at this time as the holy Church will be after the final winnowing. To which sacrilegious presumption and unspeakable arrogance I do not know what could be added. And in many places of their discourses they are understood to presume nothing else; yet sometimes they are abashed, when the truth begins to press them, when they are asked whether they either do not have or are not themselves sinners. But in this prophetic testimony they have most plainly betrayed their impious vanity and excessive perversity.

For when holy Jeremiah wished to show to the good and the wicked that, even if for a time there is one fellowship of the congregation, yet the weights of their morals and the end of their deserts are far disparate, he said, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*[^79]

But Parmenian, when he wished to refute Tyconius—who said that the wicked are for the time being to be tolerated by the good in unity for the sake of peace, and to be separated at the end of the last divine judgment—set down this testimony of Jeremiah, by which, perverse and erring, he might inflame the perverse and erring to most turbulent and most criminal seditions; so that whoever, puffed up in the mind of his own flesh, seems to himself to be something when he is nothing[^80], esteeming himself and those like him to be as it were the most purged grains, may think that he need no longer come to the congregation of the Church, in which it is necessary that those who pertain to eternal life should, until a certain end, tolerate, like the wheat its chaff, those who pertain to the eternal fire. No other blast shook the lightest chaff from the threshing floor of Christ before the time of winnowing; no other presumption made the sacrilegious schisms, whatever and wherever they are.

## The three horns of the Donatists' winnowing fork.

**3. 18.** 'The most holy Jeremiah,' he says, 'admonishes, and distinguishes the unfruitful and barren crowds of sinners from the honored fruit of the just, saying, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*[^81]' O trumpet of frenzy, O voice of execrable pestilence! Does the human race perhaps so err that it does not recognize Parmenian the winnower? Or does he too give place to Donatus and boast that he has come to the lump purged through Donatus? For whether he deigns to recognize Majorinus before himself, I do not know. Or were these three, as it were the three horns of a certain winnowing fork in the hand of the Lord, through whom the harvest of the whole world was to be cleansed, and was Africa chosen, where the purged lump should stand, while the rest, the chaff, separated, should clothe all the earth?

Whence, then, so great bands of Circumcellions? Whence so great crowds of banquets, of drunkards, and the innumerable debaucheries of women unwed but not uncorrupt? Whence so great a crowd of robbers, of the avaricious, of usurers? Whence so many men, each most notorious throughout his own region, wishing as much but not having as much power as Optatus? What is answered to these things? Do these things not exist? Or is this too wheat? Woe to the most shameless denial, if they answer that these things do not exist among them; woe to the most criminal perversity, if they answer that they are wheat.

Finally, why did even Primian dare once again to winnow the heap of cleansed wheat after a trident of such great authority—that is, after Maiorinus, Donatus, and Parmenian—in order to separate the Maximianists from his own communion? Or did he perhaps throw out grain? What, then, is he himself, along with those of his party who threw out such grain?

Or has this grain been so cleansed that its members do not recognize one another and try to winnow one another by condemning one another? Could chaff baptize wheat? If it could, then why do they say, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?* But if it could not, then why is it that, when Felician had flown out among the chaff of the Maximianists, all those whom he baptized while he was with them came back again to that most thoroughly cleansed heap, and they now have all of them within, and they do not say to themselves, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*

## How Jeremiah 23:28 is to be understood

**3. 19.** Let them, then, at long last wake up and understand how the prophet meant the words, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*[^82] For let them first consider where it is said, if any human sense at all is in them. Can it be said in the field, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat,* when they are borne by the same root?

Can it be said on the threshing floor, where they are threshed together? Surely it is said in the barn: *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?* For that householder will come bearing *his winnowing fork in his hand, and he will clear his threshing floor and gather the wheat into the barn, but the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire*[^83].

For by another comparison all the wheat is signified by the name of sheep and all the chaff by the name of goats, two kinds of animals that for the time being are pastured together, mixed under one shepherd. *But the Son of man will come*, he says, *with his angels, and all the nations will be gathered before him, and he will separate them one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats. And he will place the sheep at his right hand, but the goats at his left. And he will say to those who are at his right hand: Come, blessed of my Father, take possession of the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. But to those who are at his left hand he will say: Go into the eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels*[^84]. Then, therefore, the prophecy will be fulfilled, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat,* when the pasture cannot be shared by the goats and the sheep.

If the good fish can say to the bad fish, within that net to which the Lord said the *kingdom of heaven* is *like*[^85], "Depart from us, or let us depart from you, until all are brought to the shore and the good are placed by the angels into vessels, while the bad are thrown away," then what was said can be fulfilled at this present time: *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?* But those who already think their own congregation to be cleansed wheat have flown away from the mingling of wheat and chaff like pure chaff; and those who do not perceive that they are still being pastured under one shepherd along with the goats have been separated from the Lord's flock by the snares of wolves; and those who do not think they have been gathered together with the bad fish are not only bad fish but have also torn apart the nets of unity.

But if we are to understand that what Jeremiah says, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat,* takes place already at this present time, we can rightly understand it in no other way than this: that they are indeed in one congregation, until at the end of the winnowing they are separated even bodily, and yet the heart of the wheat is above, but that of the chaff is below. For the chaff seeks its own things, not the things of Jesus Christ[^86], while the wheat stores up treasure in heaven, and where its treasure is, there is its heart also[^87].

## How Isaiah 52:11 is to be understood

**4. 20.** So too the words of blessed Isaiah are to be understood, which he likewise, failing to understand them, tried to twist toward the same opinion of his error. For what does Isaiah say? *Depart, depart, go out from there, and touch nothing unclean. Go out from her midst and be separated, you who bear the vessels of the Lord*[^88]. Must the same things be repeated so often, telling how the separation of the heart from the wicked is to be made? For one who consents to no one for sin does not touch what is unclean.

And he goes out from there, so that his cause may be safe before God, if he also does not neglect the discipline of correcting and reproving while keeping the peace intact. For one who wishes to forsake bodily, as it were, those who are manifestly wicked, forsakes spiritually the hidden good, whom he is compelled to accuse though he has neither tested nor known them, while he tries to defend his own separation.

## Certain examples are cited

**4. 21.** But let these people answer: If Felician is clean, why did he go out from their midst? But if he is unclean, why do they now touch what is unclean? But if he was unclean then, when he was outside, those whom he baptized there are unclean, because they touched what was unclean. Or were they all cleansed by coming back with him? Can these people, then, cleanse those baptized outside, whom they have not baptized in their own communion? Why, then, do they rebaptize others? Or is some special dignity perhaps conferred by being condemned by the three hundred and ten who sat at Bagai, and is it therefore the case that whoever comes to them from the whole world is rebaptized, because the whole world did not deserve that dignity of being condemned by the Council of Bagai? What, then? When any baptized people come from Maximian and from the rest of his associates who have not returned to Primian's communion, are they rebaptized, or are they spared?

If they are rebaptized, the dignity of Bagai is violated—for in it the very ones who baptized these people were also condemned; but if they are spared, these people ought to be asked to assemble again at the town of Bagai and, if perhaps that number of three hundred and ten has already been consecrated, to assemble just as many and there pronounce a sentence against the whole world, just as they pronounced one against the Maximianists, so that, when they wish to rebaptize someone coming from some part of the world, he may defend himself by an equal privilege, asserting that he ought to be spared just as the one whom a Maximianist baptizes is spared, because by now it is not the Maximianists alone but the whole world that has deserved to be condemned by the Council of Bagai.

Next, they will free themselves from such great odium, so that, when they have begun no longer to rebaptize those baptized in the Church that is established throughout all lands, if anyone raises the question with them why they no longer do what they did before, they may answer: "When we did those things, we had not yet condemned the whole world by the Council of Bagai; but now, having been asked, we have mercifully granted that we would condemn them by such a council as we condemned the Maximianists by, whose baptism we do not repeat." What great or difficult thing is it, then, to grant to all the nations so great a dignity of condemnation? Or is it permitted to repeat the baptism of the whole world, and not permitted to repeat the condemnation of the whole world? On this score too let them be at ease; we do not find by what council they have condemned so many nations and provinces.

They condemned a few people in Africa, by whom they were overcome through the judgment of the whole world, nor did they afterward dare to condemn the judges before whom they were overcome. For what could be more shameless, what more insane? Far less were they able to condemn those established also in the other parts of the world, who chose to trust such ecclesiastical judges rather than defeated litigants. And yet the baptism of the Maximianists, whom three hundred and ten Donatists condemned at the Council of Bagai, is acknowledged, accepted, admitted; but the baptism of the whole world—through which the inheritance of Christ has been displayed just as it was promised, in which they themselves were a few years ago, which they could by no right condemn, which they have not condemned even by any perversity of a council—is rejected, blown away, repeated. O holy condemnation, which the Maximianists have deserved, and toilsome innocence of the nations, which has on this account lost the Christian name among them, because it could not find such a place of condemnation!

## The Donatists contradict themselves

**4. 22.** But if those alone who come from the Maximianists are not rebaptized who return along with the ones who baptized them—as those did who returned with Praetextatus and Felician—let them first see how they confirm in some cases, and blow away in others, a baptism of the same schism conferred equally outside their communion: in part they honor it, in part they violate it. For where they violate it, they become guilty; but where they confirm it, they are found to be witnesses of their own guilt.

For if they confirmed it afterward in such a way that they now ceased to violate it, this would be called not inconsistency but correction; but when they reject in some cases what they approve in others, in the former they are accused, in the latter they speak testimony against themselves. For I ask: Why do you not baptize those whom Felician baptized in the schism of Maximian—because they received the baptism of Christ, or because they received the baptism of Felician?

If because of Felician's, he conferred it among the Maximianists as a condemned man, he conferred it outside, beyond your communion; this baptism is the same as that of Salvius of Membressa and of others like them. But if because of Christ's, then Felician among the people of Mustis counts for more with you, as a baptism of Christ, than Christ himself in all lands,

and there counts for more with you, as a baptism of Christ, one who sits at your side, having come from a man condemned by you, than the one who sits at the right hand of the Father, having come from the one crucified for you. In a very few cases the baptism of Christ is spared, lest Felician be offended, and it is not spared, with the result that Christ himself is blown away in so many thousands of the nations.

## The Donatists fail to see this in the deeds of the prophets

**4. 23.** But the blindness of these people is incredible, and I simply do not know how it could be believed that there is in human beings such great perversity, were it not laid bare by the evidence of their own words and deeds that the eyes of their heart are closed to such a degree that, when they call to mind the testimonies of holy Scripture, they do not observe in the deeds of the prophets how the words of the prophets are to be understood. Jeremiah said, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*[^89] Did he withdraw from the chaff of his own people, against whom he said such true things? Isaiah said, *Depart, depart, go out from there, and touch nothing unclean*[^90].

Why did he himself, in one congregation together with them, touch the uncleanness that he so gravely reproved among that people? Let them read how many and how vehement and true things he said against the wicked of his own people, from whom nevertheless he separated himself by no bodily division. David said, *I have not sat in the council of vanity, and I will not go in with evildoers. I have hated the assembly of the wicked, and I will not sit with the impious*[^91].

Let them read what sort of people he himself tolerated among that people in his own times—he who paid such honor to the mystical sacrament of anointing that he did not despise it even in the utterly wicked Saul, but rather venerated it so highly that he could not possibly have done so any more.

If we set their words against their deeds, would they not answer us: "We plainly had no fellowship in our heart with such people, nor did we touch the unclean where contact can defile, that is, by consent and by the approval of conscience we withdrew and went out from them, because not only did we not do such things, but neither did we keep silent toward those who did them"?

But these seditious and insane people, who seek defenses for their breaking away from the oracles of the prophets, have only this left: to charge, in their raving impiety, the conduct of the prophets on the basis of the prophets' own words. Or are they going to say that in those times the just were not permitted to withdraw from a wicked people, but that in these times it is permitted? What could be said more perversely than that in that time the good ought not to have been separated bodily from the wicked, when many sacraments were commanded to be observed bodily, but that now bodily separation is necessary, when those things are now observed spiritually?

## Christians who were far from Africa are not guilty of the crimes committed there

**4. 24.** Woe to the blind who lead and the blind who follow! Do they not, in saying such things, fear that perhaps, throughout the great breadth of the world over which the faith and name of Christ has been spread, in some region of the earth most remote from the African territories, certain just people had already done this very thing before these people separated themselves, while these people were still living in the contagion of the uncleanness that the others had fled? For who guarantees them, who makes them secure, that—if such a separation from the wicked must be made—it was never made before they made it, so far away that the Africans were wholly unaware of it, just as in the remotest of those lands the party of Donatus is utterly unknown?

Perhaps they say that what they did not know could not have harmed them. So, then, neither can what was done in Africa harm those lands that are unaware of it—even if these people were telling the truth in what they lie about the crimes of the Africans. But if they say that, had it happened, it could not have been hidden from them, then let them tell us how many schisms have been made through all the parts of the world. I am asking too much: in Africa itself let the Donatists of Carthage, or whoever are in the vicinity of Carthage, tell how many factions have arisen throughout Numidia and Mauretania out of the party of Donatus itself.

They ought, of course, to know the causes of all these, lest perhaps certain just people in their own regions avoided the society and congregation of the wicked and went out from there, so as not to touch what is unclean[^92], so as not to go in with evildoers[^93], lest perhaps already some years ago these people remained as chaff in some corner of Numidia or Mauretania when the wheat was separating itself, and they do not know it.

But on what ground are they secure, except that they hold it for certain that those could not have been good who separated themselves from the unity of the communion of Donatus, which is spread throughout all Africa—because, if they were enduring some wicked people in their own vicinity whom they could not point out to the rest, they ought rather to have tolerated them than to divide themselves from so many innocent people whom they could not persuade of the sins of others, even if they themselves knew those sins perfectly well?

Why, then, is this innocence not granted to the whole world, throughout so great a multitude and breadth of nations wherever the inheritance of Christ extends, so that it may be certain and secure that those who call themselves good and cut themselves off from the unity of all the earth thereby show what sort of people they are? For they seem just to themselves and despise the rest, and therefore they do not sing the new song, because they are puffed up by the pride of the old man. For they are separated from that communion to which it was said, *Sing to the Lord a new song; sing to the Lord, all the earth*[^94].

If they were truly just, they would also be humble; but if they were humble, then even if they were enduring truly wicked people in the congregation of their own vicinity whom they could not expel from the unity of Christ, they would love to tolerate them out of the love of Christ. But how can they have a just judgment about the very people whom they reprove as wicked in their own vicinity, when with reckless blindness they bring charges against utterly unknown people set so far from themselves?

For whether they have known that even their own fellow citizens or neighbors whom they reprove are wicked is uncertain to the world;

but that they are separated, with reckless blindness, from those whose life, set far away, they cannot know, is certain to the world; and that the known wicked are tolerated with the praise of patience, lest the unknown good be condemned, is certain to the world.

Therefore the whole world judges with security that those are not good who divide themselves from the whole world in whatever part of the earth.

## The examples of Paul and Cyprian in preserving unity, although they lived among sinners

**4. 25.** Finally, if the prophets warned those who came after them to separate themselves bodily from the chaff before the time of the final winnowing, and by such a separation to beware of touching what is unclean[^95] and of going in with evildoers[^96], why did the apostle Paul not do this? Or were those not chaff who proclaimed Christ not out of truth but out of envy?

Or were those not unclean who preached the Gospel not chastely[^97], whom he testifies to have been in the Church of that time, and whose most excellent love, which bears all things[^98], those who came after also imitated? Or is avarice not an uncleanness, which Cyprian did not touch in his heart and yet most peacefully lived among avaricious colleagues? He had grown deaf, no doubt, to the words of the psalms, so as to sit in the council of vanity and go in with evildoers, not to hate the assembly of the wicked, and to sit with the impious[^99]. Was there not a council of vanity among them[^100] who desired to gleam with the most lavish silver while their brothers in the Church went hungry? Were they not evildoers who seized estates by treacherous frauds? Were they not most wicked and impious who increased their gain by compounding usury? But he washed his hands among the innocent and went about the altar of the Lord[^101]. For he tolerated the guilty precisely so as not to forsake the innocent with whom he washed his hands, because he loved the beauty of the house of the Lord, which beauty was in the honorable vessels. *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and of silver, but also of wood and of earthenware; and some indeed are for honor, but others for dishonor*[^102]. And he cleansed himself from such things, so that he himself too might be *a vessel sanctified for honor*, useful to the Lord*, prepared for every good work*[^103], nor did he separate himself from the great house on account of the vessels that were for dishonor, but, reproving them, he tolerated within the unity of that house those from whom he cleansed himself by not imitating them.

## Parmenian wrongly interprets Psalm 26:4-10 (25:4-10)

**5. 26.** But Parmenian knows how to set down the words of the prophet who says, *I have not sat in the council of vanity, and I will not go in with evildoers. I have hated the assembly of the wicked. I will wash my hands among the innocent, and I will go about your altar, O Lord, that I may hear the voice of praise and tell of all your wondrous works. O Lord, I have loved the beauty of your house and the place where your glory dwells. Do not destroy my soul together with the sinners, nor my life with men of blood, in whose hands are crimes, whose right hand is filled with bribes*[^104]. Parmenian knows how to set these things down, and he does not care to notice how these words may be served without any sacrilege of wicked division.

For the beauty of the house and the place where God's glory dwells[^105] is, as I have said, not in all the vessels that are nevertheless in the one great house, but in those that are sanctified for honor, useful to the Lord, always prepared for every good work[^106].

Among these, whoever has loved the beauty of the house of God and the place where his glory dwells[^107] tolerates the things that are for dishonor and does not on their account abandon the house, lest he himself become not a vessel for dishonor—which is nonetheless tolerated in the house—but the dung that is thrown out of the house; and on account of this temporal congregation with the wicked in one house, he prays, saying, *Do not destroy my soul together with the sinners, nor my life with men of blood, in whose hands are crimes, whose right hand is filled with bribes*[^108], praying precisely this: that he not perish together with those with whom love bids him to live together.

His sacrifice he set first, saying, *O Lord, I have loved the beauty of your house and the place where your glory dwells*[^109]. For because I have loved the beauty of your house, and on account of that very love I tolerate the vessels that are for dishonor, because love tolerates all things[^110], do not destroy my soul together with them.

Is there not apparent in these words the voice of those who were foretold through Ezekiel as groaning and mourning over the iniquities of the people that were done in their midst, and who, because they were vessels for honor[^111], deserved to receive their own sign, so that, when all those others began to be laid waste and destroyed, God would not destroy their souls together with the sinners?

But these wretched people, who presume that they have been cleansed from the congregation of all the wicked as wheat from chaff, have through this vanity prejudged their own case, so that, in the peoples over whom they preside, they do not dare to correct the most wicked and most disgraceful throngs so that they may be corrected, lest by this they be forced to confess that they are wicked, and it be said to them, "Surely you are speaking to cleansed wheat; why do you confess by these utterances that so much chaff has been mixed in with it?"

And therefore, because they are not just, they do not amend and reprove in mercy, but with the oil of flattery they anoint the heads[^112] of those over whom they themselves wish to be heads, since they refuse to be under the one head that is in heaven, in the unity of the body that is throughout all the earth, so that it may deservedly be said to their congregations, *Those who call you happy lead you into error and trouble the paths of your feet*[^113].

## The just groan and mourn throughout the whole city, which is the Church, over the iniquities of the people

**5. 27.** Therefore, let one who does not wish to sit in the council of vanity[^114] not vanish in the swelling of pride by seeking councils of the just separated from the unity of the whole world, which he cannot find. For the just, throughout the universal city that cannot be hidden because it is set upon a mountain[^115]—I mean that mountain of Daniel into which that stone, cut without hands, grew and filled the whole earth[^116]—throughout, then, this whole city spread over the whole world, the just groan and mourn over the iniquities that are done in their midst.

Let him not, therefore, seek the just in separation, but rather let him groan in concord with them in the temporal mingling with the wicked. For he will not sit in the council of vanity, because he will sit where he has his life's conversation, and he will hear the Apostle saying, *Our conversation is in heaven*[^117]. There he will not be with evildoers, there he will not endure the assembly of the wicked, there he will not sit with the impious[^118]. Let him dwell in such hope that he may deserve at some time to attain the thing of which he now has hope.

For we have not yet risen as Christ has, nor do we yet sit with him in the heavens, and yet, because he has given us such hope and through that very hope we already in a certain way have our conversation there, the Apostle says, *But if you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God. Set your minds on the things that are above, not on the things that are on the earth. For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God*[^119]. In that life of ours, which is hidden with Christ in God, we do not sit in the council of vanity, because, as the same Apostle says, *he raised us up together and made us sit together in the heavens*[^120]—but in hope, not yet in reality. *But hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it through patience*[^121].

This patience these wretched people have lost, and by hastening to separate themselves from the chaff before the time, they have shown that they themselves are the lightest chaff, carried off from the threshing floor by the wind. Let us, then, hold to what Wisdom says: *But he who listens to me will dwell in hope and will rest secure, without fear of any evil*[^122].

While, then, we dwell in hope, thinking not of what we are but of what we shall be—because *we are* indeed *the children of God, but what we shall be has not yet appeared; for when it appears, we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is*[^123]—dwelling in such hope, because this dwelling of thought has no wicked people, we endure neither councils of vanity, nor evildoers, nor the unjust, nor the impious; and yet we endure them not in hope but in the reality that is seen, in the Catholic congregation spread over the whole world, until iniquity passes away, until at the time of harvest the weeds are gathered, until at the final winnowing the chaff is separated from the wheat[^124], until the good fish are picked out on the shore from the bad fish with which they were gathered within the same nets[^125], until the goats are at last set apart at the left from the sheep, with which they pastured so long under one shepherd in the same pastures[^126].

## No good people can divide themselves from the Church

**5. 28.** There is, therefore, no security of unity except in the Church made manifest by the promises of God, which, as has been said, is set upon a mountain and cannot be hidden, and therefore it must of necessity be known in all the parts of the earth.

Let us hold unshaken and firm that no good people can divide themselves from her—that is, that no good people, even if they endure wicked people known to them wherever they live, may on their account separate themselves by the reckless sacrilege of schism from good people who are set far away and unknown to them—

so that, in whatever part of the earth these things either have been done, or are being done, or are to be done, the firm security of the rest of the parts of the earth—which are set far away, and which do not know whether these things were done or why they were done, and which nevertheless remain with the whole world in the bond of unity—lies in this: that no one could have done such a thing except those made furious by the swelling of pride, or made insane by the venom of envy, or corrupted by worldly advantage, or made perverse by carnal fear.

By all these causes it comes about that either good people are defamed by false charges, or false things are rashly believed about the good, or even the wicked—who, tolerated for the sake of the bond of unity, do the good no harm—are most perversely fled from, with the peace of the good broken, when no mercy is shown to the harassment of the wheat, while people usurp for themselves before the harvest what the angels will do at the harvest.

## When the Donatists suffer, they are certainly not martyrs. An account of Salvius

**6. 29.** Since this is so, sacrilegious schisms and impious heresies even dare, if by some scourge they are admonished to correct themselves, to count the penalties of their own madness among the sufferings of martyrs. Thinking this, Parmenian too at the end of his letter exhorts Tychonius to remain in the party of Donatus and to endure persecutions, saying that "they ought not to join by their own will those to whom they did not join when persecution compelled them," and he even adduces the testimony of Scripture, which says, *Woe to those who have lost endurance and have turned aside into crooked ways! And what will they do when the Lord begins to look upon them?*[^127]

After his manner, he sets forth everything from the divine Books against himself. For who have lost endurance, if not those who were unwilling to tolerate, for the peace of Christ, the people whom they could not convict of the crimes they charged—so that, thinking later, too late, that their own party ought not to be divided into many fragments, they tolerated for the false peace of Donatus even the most manifest sacrilegious people whom they themselves had condemned, taking them back again? And let them now at last at some time recognize and correct what they have impiously done, since they have learned it at least from what they have suffered at the hands of Maximian.

But they blush, of course, to be corrected by the most manifest reasons, because they resisted the commands of the emperors, lest, once corrected, they should seem to have lost those very things that they complain they suffered. As though they do not lose those things better than they perish! For if it has any shadow—and, however false and deceptive, still some image—of fortitude not to yield to an emperor's command, what allurement even of human praise is there in contradicting the most manifest truth? Why, with closed eyes, do they bring forth so many testimonies from the Scriptures, which, once understood and turned against them, they reject—testimonies that, even if they are taken according to their own understanding, nonetheless convict them of their own perversity? Is it not written, *Do not in any way contradict the truth*[^128]? But whom does one contradict, if not the truth, when one resists even a king commanding something out of the truth? But a king who is a man—what he threatens or what he punishes is troublesome only for a time;

not so that King who is also called the Truth, who even now cries out to them through the prophet, *In vain have I struck your children; they have not received instruction*[^129], who for this reason now mercifully admonishes them through human powers, lest at the end he take such vengeance that the proud cannot boast about their own condemnation. For under the vengeance of kings the obstinacy of human beings can wish to be praised under the false name of virtue; but to be burned up by eternal fires neither will be nor will be called fortitude.

For on that day there will not be those who anoint the head with the oil of flattery, or who, deceived by empty deceit, dream of crowns for the condemned, saying, "Well done, well done," and swearing by the gray hairs of those who had no sound head, and by the boots of those *who have not known the way of peace*[^130].

Turning such throngs away from the unity of Christ and desiring to convert them to their own name, these people meanwhile dare to count the temporal punishments of their schism among the sufferings of martyrs, so that the anniversaries of their own penalties may be celebrated for them by a great gathering of frenzied people—of whose number are those who, even with no one persecuting them, throw themselves headlong of their own accord over the cliffs of the mountains, so as to end a bad life by a worse death.

On that day there will not be foolish crowds to whom it is said, "We are the just who suffer persecution," and to the blind there will not be sold a rejected stone for a precious gem—that is, carnal hardness for spiritual patience. There will not be those who recite at the altars the names of the leaders of their madness, whether of those whom they divided from the unity of Christ or of those whom they set up under the name of Christ against the Church of Christ.

For these are the prices that, in order to receive them, while they wish to have something to sell, they stir up the severity of the powers against themselves by their headstrong perversity,

nor do those who are seduced by them, and who think them just for this reason, that they pay the penalties of iniquity, return to their heart and consider why they suffer the things they glory in suffering. For how easy it would have been for them to notice, even a little, that Parmenian says such things to Tychonius about enduring persecutions and the glory of endurance as all heretics say, about whose coercion or punishment similar commands of kings are issued—

such things, certainly, as even Salvius now says to his own people of Membressa—Salvius, on whom the people of Abitina inflicted such great blows and insults, the people through whom these had earned that he be expelled from the Church, so that they bound to his neck even the carcasses of dead dogs, so that finally they danced with him to shameful shouts and songs. What sort of sermon do we suppose he delivered to his own people—the wretches whom he deceived—after he had suffered so much, that they might build him another basilica?

How much did he say about his own righteousness, for which he had earned to suffer such things, persuading himself that he was holy because he had suffered, and that they were most wicked because they had done these things! The ancient cruelty of the Tuscan tyrants is recalled, that they used to bind dead bodies to the living—but human bodies to human; whether anyone recalls having ever either heard or read of canine bodies bound to human, and these the limbs of a bishop, I do not know.

It is known to all that trifling and disgraceful dances are usually checked by bishops; who has ever remembered men whom bishops sought for help dancing together with the bishops? Or was Salvius perhaps not a bishop at that time, since his condemnation is recited in the sentence of the Council of Bagai? What then? If he too were afterward reconciled to Primian—just as Felician was reconciled to him, Felician who was condemned "by the truthful mouth of that same plenary council," as their words are recited there—would he then be a bishop, or would he perhaps not be admitted, on the ground that the sacrilege of schism can be cleansed in the man it has defiled, just as Felician could be cleansed, but the man whom dead dogs hung from his neck have made unclean cannot be expiated?

I should like to know what those people say to these things, so manifest, so public, so recent—those who fling at us their ancient slanders as if they were our crimes. Whoever among them thinks that I am boasting of false things, what great or difficult thing is it that, taking care of his own soul, he go to Membressa, ask whether these things were done, and defend them, if he can?

For if he says that these things are rightly done to the schismatics whom three hundred and ten Donatist bishops condemned, then let them not murmur when they suffer anything, although they have never suffered such things—they whom we convict of having made a schism from the unity of Christ not by the authority of three hundred and ten, but of the whole world.

But if he says that what Salvius suffered was trivial, I ask whether, if the emperor were to compel one of the Donatist bishops to dance and were to threaten the beasts and the fires to him if he refused, he would not rather endure those things than admit this—and whether, once he had suffered, these people would not recite him among the holy martyrs. Salvius, then, with whom they danced, suffered graver things than if he had been burned alive.

For if these two were set before any one of them—whether he would rather not dance himself, but have dancing done with him, or be burned alive—there is no doubt what they would answer that he would choose. But if he says that the Primianists obtained nothing else from the proconsul except that Salvius be expelled from his basilica by the people of Abitina, but that those people of their own accord did whatever they afterward did to him cruelly and disgracefully, why does he not say to himself that the Catholics too can in this way ask nothing else of the emperors except that these people be shut out from the basilicas that they hold under a sacrilegious name, but that those people of their own accord—with royal power and honor preserved—take vengeance on the sacrilegious far more mildly than the people of Abitina took vengeance on Salvius of Membressa, with no imperial law, with no judicial command?

Having considered these things, let them attend first to what they do and afterward to what they suffer, lest, when they close their eyes to their own deeds and open their eyes to their own penalties, they both suffer temporal things in vain here and, at the last judgment of God, be punished with eternal torment even for this very thing—that he admonished them in vain by such troubles in order to amend them.

I do not rehearse old matters, where they deceive whom they can; I speak briefly, I press present matters, I point with my finger: the condemned Maximianists are received, unknown nations are charged; the baptism of the Maximianists is accepted, the baptism of the whole world is blown away. Behold the people of Assuras, behold the people of Mustis, behold Praetextatus, dead not long ago, behold Felician, still alive, behold their names cited among the condemned in the records of the proconsul at the Council of Bagai;

by such recent and present deeds they sufficiently show what sort of people they were from the beginning. If they suffer anything for perversities and iniquities of this kind, then, if they are unwilling to be corrected, let them at least not dare to boast.
