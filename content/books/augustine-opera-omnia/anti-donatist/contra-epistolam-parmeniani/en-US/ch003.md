# Book 2

## The Donatists call good evil, and the reverse.

**1. 1.** What force, at last, but the blindness and emptiness of mind, compels men—as it were with eyes closed, as the saying goes—to throw against another what immediately returns upon the one who threw it and strikes him with a recoiling blow, leaving untouched the one he wished to wound? So these Donatists do with almost all the testimonies of the Scriptures, which, when they suppose they are bringing them forward against us, seem rather to warn us what sort of men the very Scripture convicts them to be. For what else does what Parmenian thinks to be for himself or against us accomplish, namely what is written, *Woe to those who say that what is evil is good and what is good is evil, who put light for darkness and darkness for light, who put what is bitter for sweet and sweet for bitter*[^1]? O most foolish blindness! For what is so good and so pleasant as brothers dwelling together in unity[^2]?

This those men call evil and put for bitter, who have separated themselves from all their brothers, while they were unwilling either not to stir up their empty suspicions—not to say their factious slanders—or, once stirred up, to tolerate them. For if they hated the true chaff and were not themselves chaff, they would not, on its account, sunder themselves from the Lord's wheat, sown and growing throughout the whole field, that is, this world.

## They call their own presumption light.

**1. 2.** Let them therefore cry out as much as they can, *Woe to those who say that what is evil is good and what is good is evil*[^3]. We reply, 'This is true,' and we add another: *Woe to those who have lost endurance*[^4], while they put *light for darkness and darkness for light*[^5]. For what is more luminous than the promises of God, who has in our times exhibited what he foretold thousands of years before—that in the seed of Abraham, which is Christ, all the nations would have a blessing[^6]—and what is more darksome than the presumptions of men, who, on account of the rashly alleged and never proved crimes of traitors (which, even if they were true, would never prejudice God so that he should not fulfill what he promised), say that the Christian name has perished from so many nations in the world and remained in Africa alone? And this presumption of theirs they call light, while they strive to cover with the darkness of their lies the promises of God, now illumined by the very effect of events; and besides they cry out their own deeds against us, saying, *Woe to those who put light for darkness and darkness for light*.

Was Optatus, then, light, and did all Africa call him darkness? Or did all Africa rather perceive that he was darkness, while these men called him light—they who do not put light for darkness and darkness for light? 'But Optatus,' they say, 'displeased all the good in our communion.' You did not, then, call him light, and yet you communed with him.

Choose, therefore, what you will: either that darkness does not harm light in one communion, but it suffices for the light to disapprove the darkness and, for the sake of unity, to tolerate it if it cannot expel it—and so that there was no cause why you should be sundered from innocent brothers, to whom you certainly could not show the wicked, even if you say they were known to you, by the darksome sacrilege of schism;

or, if it does not suffice for the light to disapprove the darkness it cannot expel—that is, if it does not suffice for the good to disapprove the wicked whom they cannot exclude or amend—then one Optatus, most notorious and most evident in the one province of Africa, more easily stained the party of Donatus than any African traitor stained so many nations throughout the world—a traitor not, let me say, accused of false crimes, but, what is most shamelessly denied, unknown to them.

## They receive the Scriptures with a perverse heart.

**1. 3.** Receiving the Scriptures, therefore, with a perverse heart, they make them harm not us but themselves, when they think it was written for this purpose, *Woe to those who say that what is evil is good and what is good is evil*[^7]—namely, that the wheat should not tolerate the chaff among them until the time of winnowing; so that, while they ill understand *Woe to those who say that what is evil is good and what is good is evil*, there is fulfilled in them, *Woe to those who have lost endurance*[^8].

But if they understand that this was said against those who, supposing what is evil to be good, commit evils, or against those who consent to such men by praising and approving them—two kinds that Scripture mentions in one place, saying, *For the sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquity is blessed*[^9]—

they will understand rightly and will not be troubled, since the wicked are found even among themselves—except that for the party of Donatus they tolerate those whom they ought to have tolerated for the unity of Christ; and through this obstinacy of their contentiousness the wretched men are forced, in their schism, to bear those whom they know, and in the world to accuse those whom they do not know.

Whoever, then, either corrects by reproof what he can, or excludes—with the bond of peace preserved—what he cannot correct, or, with the bond of peace preserved, disapproves in fairness what he cannot exclude, and bears it in firmness—this man is a peacemaker, and is altogether free, wholly secure, and entirely a stranger from that curse by which Scripture says, *Woe to those who say that what is evil is good and what is good is evil, who put light for darkness and darkness for light, who put what is bitter for sweet and what is sweet for bitter*[^10].

## They say: God did not speak the truth.

**2. 4.** Again, with their usual blindness, they bring forward something similar. 'Of you,' they say, 'Scripture says, *He who judges the just unjust, and the unjust just, is abominable before God*[^11].' Why does this abomination not rather fall upon those who dared to condemn the whole Christian world unheard, in which, among so vast a number of men, there were and are, without any doubt, men innocent of their crimes?

In this way, then, they judged what is just to be unjust, and what is unjust they judged just, when they held in communion, honoring him as a priest and a colleague, Optatus the Gildonian, for ten years the groan of all Africa.

Or if they disapproved him in their heart, but tolerated him for the sake of peace, let them learn that the patience of no peacemaker, not consenting to evil, can be stained by any evils; and so let them perceive in what perdition they lie who, on account of the crimes of the Africans—whether false or true, for that is not now the question—do not keep *the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*[^12] with the whole world.

And if they were to say, 'We do not know whether there are good Christians throughout so many nations of the lands across the sea,' they would speak most shamelessly. For God bears witness to his wheat, which, sowing it throughout the whole field, he foretold would grow until the harvest, although with weeds sown over it by the devil; whence, even if we know the men themselves less, we nevertheless know that they exist, because we hold by a most certain faith that God could not have lied.

When, therefore, with sacrilegious shamelessness they say, 'We do not know whether there are good Christians in the rest of the world,' let them see with what insanity they dare to say—for daily they do not hesitate to say—'We know that there are no Christians there.' It is one thing, surely, to say, 'We do not know whether there are,' and another to say, 'We know that there are not'—both are faithless and impious. But if he is to be detested who says, 'I do not know whether God spoke the truth,' what is he who says, 'I know that God did not speak the truth'?

## They falsely boast that they saved the Gospel from the fire.

**2. 5.** Certainly I think I ought to offend none of these men if I set God before Donatus. For however much they love Donatus, they fear God more; and finally, however much they themselves love Donatus, we know that *God alone is true, but every man a liar*[^13]. Christ, therefore, *who is God over all, blessed forever*[^14], and who said most truly of himself, *I am the truth*[^15], when his servants asked him whether he wished them to go and gather the weeds, said, *Let both grow until the harvest*[^16]; but Donatus says that the weeds indeed have grown, while the wheat has been diminished.

Let these men choose whom to believe. Christ, that is, the truth, says, *The field is this world*[^17]; but Donatus says that the field of God has remained Africa alone. Let them choose whom to believe. Christ, that is, the truth, says, *At the time of harvest I will say to the reapers: First gather the weeds*[^18]; and he explains it, saying, *The harvest is the end of the age*[^19]; but Donatus says that by the cutting off of his party the weeds were separated from the wheat before the harvest.

Let them choose whom to believe. Christ, that is, the truth, says, *The reapers are the angels*[^20]; but Donatus says that he and his colleagues did, before the harvest, what the Lord said the angels would do at the harvest.

Let them choose whom to believe. They certainly call themselves Christians: we set before them Christ and Donatus. If they give words to Christ but their heart to Donatus, let them consider who they are. I spare them, I do not inveigh, I do not exaggerate; I press down my grief rather than bring it out.

But if they say they give their heart to Christ, then let them believe Christ when he says that throughout the whole world both the sons of the kingdom and the sons of the evil one grow[^21], not Donatus when he says that throughout the world only the sons of the evil one have grown, while the good sons have been diminished down to Africa alone. But if they believe Christ, let them now have peace—we do not say with the Churches of the world, but with the Gospel itself—which they falsely boast they preserved from the fire, since they do not prove it by their deeds.

## They cannot see a most manifest thing.

**3. 6.** Now to what end is that which the same Parmenian thought should be objected to us from the prophet Isaiah: *Is the hand of the Lord not strong enough to save, or has he made his ear heavy that he should not hear? But your offenses separate between you and God, and on account of your sins he has turned away his face from you that he should not have mercy. For your hands are defiled with blood and your fingers with sins; but your lips have spoken iniquity, and your tongue meditates injustice.*

*No one speaks just things, nor is there true judgment. They trust in vain things and speak empty things, for they conceive pain and bring forth iniquity. They have hatched the eggs of asps and weave the spider's web; and he who was about to eat of those eggs, crushing one, found it rotten, and in it a basilisk. Their web, therefore, will not be for clothing, nor will they cover themselves with their works. For their works are works of iniquity, and their feet run swift to wickedness, swift to shed blood, and their thoughts are the thoughts of fools. Destruction and misery are in their ways, and the way of peace they have not known*[^22].

Those whom Scripture describes in this passage, wherever they are—even among the good—do not harm the good, just as chaff does not harm the wheat, until the Lord of the threshing floor comes, bearing a winnowing fork in his hand, and cleanses his floor, and stores the wheat in the barn, but burns the chaff with unquenchable fire[^23]; just as so great a multitude of criminals did not harm the good—which we learned through the prophet Ezekiel—namely those who groaned and grieved over the crimes of the people that were being committed in their midst[^24]. Hence, because they could not correct them, and ought in no way to separate themselves from the unity of the people of God, by the merit of their most innocent endurance they deserved to be marked and, in the devastation and destruction of those lost ones, to be delivered.

But why do these men, who object these things to the catholics, not look at themselves—whose hordes of raging bands, armed with iron and clubs, dart here and there, and are by no means sated, in such monstrousness, with the frequent slaughters they work wherever they can—where, amid the pyres of their corpses, with the women who, mingled with them everywhere against the order of things divine and human, roam day and night, such drunkenness rages that from it they conceive a daily madness not only of pursuing others but even of hurling themselves headlong? Do not their feet run to malice, and are they not swift to shed blood[^25]? Does judgment not depart from these men, who usurp for themselves even the most unjust licenses of a disordered power? Does not darkness come upon them, while they wait for light in a false martyrdom[^26]? Do they not walk in the midst of night even by day, since the Apostle says, *Those who are drunk are drunk at night*[^27]? Do not these men fall at noon as if in the middle of the night[^28]? This indeed belongs to all heretics, who cannot see a most manifest thing set in the light of all the nations—outside whose unity, whatever they do, however much it may seem to be done with great skill and diligence, profits them as little against the wrath of God as spiders' webs can shield from the cold.

## Much favor is shown for the peace of Donatus.

**3. 7.** What, then, do they put forward from this prophetic chapter that cannot be turned back upon themselves—except perhaps about the eggs of asps, of which the framer of the sentence of the plenary council of three hundred and ten made much mention, when they came together from all the provinces of Africa? This, certainly, if Parmenian were living now, he would not say, nor would he put forward from the prophet Isaiah against us, *They have hatched the eggs of asps*[^29].

For he would look at Felician of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuras, joined to his college, whom, from the number of the condemned, they received afresh as if whole, for the supposed good of peace—not of Christ, however, but of Donatus—men who both were the eggs of asps and had hatched. For so do the three hundred and ten describe them, 'by the truthful mouth of the plenary council,' as their records, appended even to the proconsular records, testify.

For these are certainly the words of their council: 'For although the channel of a venomous womb long hid the harmful offspring of viperous seed, and the moist curds of the conceived crime warmed themselves with slow heat into the limbs of asps, yet the conceived poison could not be concealed by a vanishing shade.

For although late, their vows pregnant with crimes brought forth a public outrage and their own parricide.' These eggs of asps had already hatched and, now horrible and foul-smelling, with their venomous offspring, had already been cast out of their communion; but inside there was Optatus the basilisk, who, by a certain royal nod—by which it is said to excel among serpents—might recall even the asps that had been cast out.

Or if these things do not harm them for the sake of peace, why do they object to the catholics things they could not prove, when they themselves have received men whom they condemned with their own mouth?

If these things do not harm the peace of Christ, yet they harm it for the sake of the peace of Donatus, to which much favor is shown to the injury of the peace of Christ; and for this sacrilege, whatever they suffer in bodily hardships is God's admonition to beware of eternal damnation. For they themselves truly shed blood, not only bodily through the frenzies of the Circumcellions, but also spiritually—they who try, if they could, to rebaptize the whole world.

Or if he alone sheds blood who either wounds mortal flesh or destroys it by a wound, but he does not shed it who destroys seduced souls by the sacrilege of schism, why did they say such things against their own schismatics the Maximianists, through the same sentence of their plenary council, *Their feet are swift to shed blood*[^30], when it is established that no one was killed by them bodily, no one wounded, but that, moreover, those men suffered many things from these, when they were shut out of the basilicas through the judicial powers?

Such things they did, in many cases, before the separation of the Maximianists, when they were together in one body, to those who had made the earlier schisms from the party of Donatus. For what schismatic of their own did they ever spare—they who wish, most shamelessly, to be spared by the world, of which they themselves are schismatics—when schisms are most justly punished by that one true unity alone, if these things are to be punished in that way?

## In the sharing of the sacraments not all are contaminated.

**4. 8.** And that which is written, *As the prince of the people is, so also are his ministers; and as the ruler of the city is, such also are its inhabitants*[^31]—if they understood to what it refers, they would neither object it to us nor be proud with empty boasting.

For we, so that we may place men's hope—which is safe and rightly secure in God alone—not in men, since we remember that it is written, *Cursed is he who puts his hope in man*[^32], do not understand in this passage the prince of the people and the ruler of the city to signify a bishop—not because we cannot behold innumerable holy bishops in the catholic Church, but lest, as I said, anyone's hope as a man be placed in a man, and lest, if it should perhaps befall him to live in a city where there is no good bishop, he should think he may become wicked with impunity, usurping for his defense this Scripture, as perversely understood as it is understood by these men, and saying that he therefore cannot be good, because *as the prince of the people is, so also are his ministers, and as the ruler of the city is, such also are its inhabitants*[^33].

For this error is contradicted by the mouth of Truth, saying, *Do what they say, but do not do what they do; for they say and do not do*[^34]. And so, when peoples have such bishops, who say good things from the chair of Moses and do not do the things they say[^35] out of their own pestilence[^36]—if those peoples do the good things that the bishops say and do not do the evil things that the bishops do, as the Lord commanded, do they not sufficiently show that the prince of the people and the ruler of the city is not to be understood as these men understand it, since peoples can be good even where the bishops have been wicked, just as the people could be wicked where Moses was prince and a good ruler?

For erring in the words of the holy Scriptures and, as the Apostle says, *understanding neither what they say nor the things about which they make assertions*[^37], they themselves rather, according to the perversity of their understanding, suffer great straits, when it is said to them: 'Then as Optatus was, so was also the people of Thamugadi; and if by the sharing of the sacraments, as you say, even those are contaminated who disapprove the evil deeds—even the deeds they tolerate for the peace of unity—then such are all of you too, who communed both with that colleague of yours and with that people, when all Africa with a manifest groan declared him a satellite of Gildo.' Him I often name for this reason, that he appeared so manifestly that, wherever he has been named, no one answers that he is ignorant of him.

## The separation of the peoples takes place at the final winnowing.

**4. 9.** For let them look back and recall how many like themselves they have among them, whose malice is equal but whose notoriety is unequal, and let them at last come to the true meaning of these words and understand that the one prince of the people is our Lord Jesus Christ, whose ministers are good, and that he himself is the ruler of that city of Jerusalem, *which is our eternal mother in heaven*[^38].

To the dignity of this ruler the inhabitants are conformed, not to an equality, but each according to his measure, because it was said to them, *You shall be holy, for I also am holy*[^39], according, namely, to a certain likeness of the image into which *we are transformed from glory to glory as by the Spirit of the Lord*[^40], by the gift of him who makes us conformed to the image of his Son[^41]. There is also another, wicked people, whose prince is the devil and the ruler of that city which is mystically called Babylon[^42], since the apostle Paul calls him and his angels the princes and rulers of this darkness[^43], that is, of sins; and his ministers are like him, because they transfigure themselves into ministers of justice, as he into an angel of light[^44], and the inhabitants are conformed to that worst ruler in like deeds. But the separation of these peoples and cities will be open at the time when this harvest has been winnowed; and until that is done, the love of the wheat tolerates all things, lest, while the grains too hastily flee the chaff, they be impiously separated from their fellow grains.

## Sacrifices do not harm the good, but the wicked who offer them.

**5. 10.** What weight, then, does it now have for the case, what these men object to us, that through Isaiah the Lord says of wicked sacrificers, *The criminal who sacrifices to me a calf is as one who slays a dog, and he who offers fine flour is as one who offers swine's blood; and he who offers incense in memorial is as a blasphemer*[^45]? All these things are said far more fittingly of those who, against the Church of God spread—as it was promised—throughout the whole world, have erected the altar of their schism; which sacrilege wraps them all up, and whoever offers sacrifice anywhere with such a heart or such deeds as to deserve to hear these things brings ruin upon himself, not upon those good men who, according to the prophet Ezekiel, groan and grieve over the sins and iniquities committed in their midst[^46], although they do not bodily separate themselves from them; for the Lord renders to each according to his heart[^47].

For if in earlier times wicked priests did not harm either their good colleagues, such as was Zechariah[^48], or the good people, such as was Nathanael, in whom there was no guile[^49], how much more does a wicked bishop, in Christian unity, in no way harm either good fellow bishops or good laypeople, since now that priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek, our high priest seated at the right hand of the Father, intercedes for us[^50], *who was handed over for our offenses and rose again for our justification*[^51]. Therefore that too does not harm the good, but the wicked who offer, which was said most truly, *The Most High does not approve the gifts of the wicked*[^52].

For he did not say, 'The Most High does not approve the gifts of those who tolerate the wicked for the sake of peace'—although these men could not even prove the things they objected at the time when they made the schism. Otherwise, with those men excluded, the inheritance of Christ, spread throughout the whole world, would hold these men in catholic communion.

## If the Donatists were good, they ought to tolerate the wicked for the peace of Christ.

**6. 11.** *The sacrifices*, he says, *of the impious are an abomination to the Lord; for they offer them iniquitously*[^53]. It has already been answered above that Christ is not iniquitous, who offered himself for us and is our mediator in heaven[^54]; and while he governs his Church, the wicked, who are either unknown or tolerated for the sake of peace, will not harm the good, until he himself comes and, having sent ahead the reapers, separates the weeds from the harvest and, with the winnowing fork, the chaff from the wheat[^55]—

although these men, as must often be said, objected false crimes, which, even if they were true, would do no harm to the love of the good, which for the sake of unity tolerates all things in unity[^56], if perhaps they could not, even when these were known, persuade the ecclesiastical judges of them. The sacrifices of the impious, therefore, will harm those very men who offer impiously.

For one and the same sacrifice, on account of the name of God, which is there invoked and is always holy, becomes for each such as the heart with which he approaches to receive it. *For he who eats and drinks unworthily, eats and drinks judgment to himself*[^57]. He did not say 'to others,' but *to himself*. He, then, who eats and drinks worthily, eats and drinks grace to himself. Let those men, then, see whether they eat worthily, who, among so many parents and children, husbands and wives, among so many—what is most important—heirs of God and coheirs of Christ[^58] spread throughout the whole world, have divided by a wicked schism, when they could certainly, if they were good and truly arraigned the wicked, fruitfully tolerate for the peace of Christ what they ruinously tolerate for the party of Donatus.

## Each one bears his own burden.

**7. 12.** 'In Exodus,' he says, 'it is written, *Let the priests who approach the Lord God be sanctified, lest perhaps the Lord forsake them*[^59]; and again, *And when the ministers approach the altar of the holy place, let them not bring sin upon themselves, lest they die*[^60]; and that in Leviticus, *A man who has a blemish and a defect, let him not approach to offer gifts to God*[^61].'

It is well that they bring forward these testimonies from the old books. Let them tell me, then, whom—established either among the priests or among the people—a wicked or blemished priest has ever harmed in respect of spiritual salvation. Where Moses and Aaron were, there were sacrilegious murmurers, whom God always threatened to destroy from before his face; where Caiaphas and others like him were, there was Zechariah, there Simeon and other good men; where Saul was, there was David; where Jeremiah, where Isaiah, where Daniel, where Ezekiel, there were wicked priests and wicked peoples; but each one bore his own burden.

## They too have iniquitous men among them.

**7. 13.** I forbear to say with what criminal pride it is said that there is no one among their colleagues, or that they themselves are not, with some blemish or defect—not of body, but, what is worse, of character. And when we begin to press this with them, they reply that it makes a difference what kind of blemish and what kind of defect it is, as though Scripture had made the distinction when it said, *A man who has a blemish and a defect, let him not approach to offer gifts to God*[^62].

Had no blemish and defect—I do not say Optatus, but Parmenian himself or Donatus himself? But these men are so blinded by love of men, and with an unchaste heart do not hesitate to make the adulterers of their mind equal to the one lawful husband, that they maintain that what could be said of the Lord Jesus Christ alone was perfectly fulfilled even in Donatus. *Who will give my eyes a fountain of tears?*[^63] What groan fit for this crime can be wrung from a stricken breast?

But meanwhile let them attend to whether at least Optatus had some blemish or some defect; they are not so blind as to answer that even his life was wholly unblemished and free of every defect. Why, then, did he approach to offer gifts to God, and from him did the rest, with joined hands, receive what one blemished and defective had offered? Let them attend, among their other men, to whether drunkenness is no blemish—but first let them read with what criminals the apostle Paul joined drunkards[^64]—whether avarice is no blemish, which the same Apostle so detests that he compares it to the servitude of idols[^65].

## A man, even the best, cannot be without some defect.

**7. 14.** But those who think most rightly understand that any man—although, in respect of the fellowship of human society, it can not absurdly be said that he lives most justly—cannot, however, be without some defect, so long as *the flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*[^66]. And, *He who is born of God does not sin*[^67]; and, *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^68].

For although, insofar as we are born of God, we do not sin, yet there is still in us also what we are from Adam, because *death is not yet swallowed up in victory*[^69]—which is promised also in the resurrection of the bodies[^70]—so that we who already, according to faith, *are children of God*, may in every way be blessed and unblemished and incorrupt; but according to sight *it does not yet appear what we shall be*[^71].

For not yet in reality, *but in hope we have been saved. But hope that is seen is not hope. For what a man sees, why does he hope for it? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience*[^72]. But so long as *with patience we await the redemption of our body*[^73], let us not dare to say that we are free of every defect, lest that very pride be a most monstrous defect; and let us at some time awake and see that, in the priests of that time, when bodily defects were avoided, there was prefigured that one who, being God, became man for our sake, alone truly the unblemished lamb[^74] and the priest without defect. And therefore then too the priest alone entered into the Holy of Holies[^75], while the people stood outside, just as now that priest, after the resurrection, has entered into the secret places of the heavens, to intercede for us at the right hand of the Father, while the people whose priest he is still groan outside.

For with the bishop the people are within, and they pray with him and, as if subscribing to his words, answer, 'Amen.' To such a degree, then, when—because the souls could not be without any blemish and without any defect—the bodies of priests were required to be so[^76], was that one alone prefigured, not these proud and impious men, who, while their soul plays the harlot, are not jealous for the bridegroom, but dare to display themselves to the people in place of the bridegroom.

## In prayer, the wicked among the good can fail to be heard, and the good among the wicked can be heard.

**8. 15.** 'In the Gospel,' he says, 'it is written, *God will not hear sinners; but if anyone worships the Lord and does his will, him he will hear*[^77].' And here too the answer is conclusive. For if two pray together, one a sinner and the other one who worships the Lord and does his will, surely he hears the one, he does not hear the other.

What, then, does this testimony mean, or how do they suppose it should be brought forward on their behalf, when by these very words the good become especially secure among the wicked, and no cause of bodily separation arises—so that by a wicked schism men should be separated even from the good through spiritual discord—since the wicked among the good can fail to be heard on account of their faithlessness, and the good among the wicked can be heard on account of their faith?

For God, *who is the inspector of the heart*[^78], does not err, so as to hear or reject one for another. Or do they say these things so that it may be understood that a wicked bishop is not heard when he prays for the people? Even if this were so, yet the people, if they are good and faithful, ought not on that account to be anxious. For that Scripture makes them secure which says, *Brothers, these things I write to you, that you may not sin; and if anyone has sinned, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just, and he is the propitiation for our sins*[^79].

With what truthful and pious humility this was said, let them hear, if they have ears to hear with. For John had said, *These things I have written to you, that you may not sin*. If it had then followed that he said, 'And if anyone has sinned, you have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just, and he is the propitiation for your sins,' he would have seemed to have set himself apart, as it were, from sinners, so that he no longer needed the propitiation that is made through the mediator seated at the right hand of the Father and interceding for us[^80]. Which surely would have been said not only proudly but also falsely. But if he had even said this, 'These things I have written to you, that you may not sin; and if anyone has sinned, you have me as a mediator with the Father, I entreat for your sins'—as Parmenian in a certain place set the bishop as mediator between the people and God—who among good and faithful Christians would bear him, who would look upon him as an apostle of Christ and not as an antichrist? And yet these broken cisterns bear the smoking pride of these men, and cannot hold the Holy Spirit[^81], so as to keep *the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace* and to be secure, in all their prayers, about the one mediator[^82].

## Christ is the one and true Mediator.

**8. 16.** For all Christian men commend one another by their prayers. But he for whom no one intercedes, while he himself intercedes for all, this is the one and true mediator[^83]; and since his type was prefigured in the priest of the Old Testament, there too no one is found to have prayed for the priest.

But the apostle Paul—although under the head a chief member[^84], yet because he is a member of the body of Christ, and knew that the greatest and truest priest entered for us, not through a figure into the inner part of the veil to the Holy of Holies, but through the expressed and rendered truth into the inner part of heaven, to a holiness not imaginary but eternal[^85]—commends even himself to the prayers of the Church and does not make himself a mediator between the people and God, so that all the members of the body of Christ may pray for one another, since the members are anxious for one another and, if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it[^86], and, if one member is glorified, all the members rejoice together; and thus the prayer of all the members for one another, still laboring on earth, will ascend to the head that has gone before into heaven, in which is *the propitiation for our sins*[^87].

For if Paul were a mediator, so surely would his fellow apostles be, and thus there would be many mediators, nor would the account hold for Paul himself by which he had said, *For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus*[^88]. In him we too are one, if we keep *the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace*[^89]: let us not desert the good on account of the wicked, but for the sake of the good let us endure the wicked; lest, while we wish to defend what by rash presumption we abandon, men unknown to us, we be compelled by a greater crime to condemn the unheard.

## Even wicked bishops can be heard on account of the devotion of the peoples.

**8. 17.** And what of this: that there is also found in the Scriptures the prophet Balaam, not of the people of Israel but a foreigner, hired by an enemy to curse the people of God, turned by the Lord to blessing—whose words, all of one praying well, we hear and read[^90]—and, though he had something else in his wish, yet the words of one praying are both good and, on behalf of the people, are heard by the Lord.

Hence it is no wonder that good words which are said in prayers on behalf of the people, even if they are said by wicked bishops, are nevertheless heard—not on account of the perversity of the prelates, but on account of the devotion of the peoples. Yet what is written in the Gospel, *God does not hear sinners; but if anyone worships the Lord and does his will, him he will hear*[^91], was not said by the Lord, but by that man who had indeed already had the eyes of his body restored, but to whom the eyes of his heart were not yet open—whence he still thought the Lord himself to be a prophet. For afterward, when he had recognized him, he adored the Son of God as a suppliant[^92]. But the Lord himself, when a publican and a Pharisee prayed in one temple, says that the sinner confessing his sins was more justified than the Pharisee boasting of his merits[^93]—and these men are like that Pharisee. For although, once justified, he ceased to be a sinner, yet, in order to be justified, the sinner was praying and confessing his sins, and, being heard, he was justified, so that he ceased to be a sinner. He would not, then, have ceased to be a sinner, unless the sinner were first heard. Wherefore the truth is witness that not indeed every sinner is heard, but yet that not every sinner is unheard.

## The words of God do not profit sinners who do not do them, but they profit those who hear them.

**9. 18.** They object also what is said in the Psalms: *But to the sinner God says: Why do you expound my justifications and take up my testament through your mouth? But you hated discipline and cast my words behind you. If you saw a thief, you ran with him, and with adulterers you placed your portion. Sitting, you spoke against your brother and against the son of your mother you laid a stumbling block*[^94]. But let them at last open the ears of their heart and cease to be *understanding neither what they say nor the things about which they make assertions*[^95].

For they attend to what is said to the sinner, *Why do you expound my justifications and take up my testament through your mouth?*[^96] And they do not understand that it was said for this purpose: that he should know that the words of God which he utters with his mouth profit him nothing, if he himself does not do what he says, but that they profit others who, even through the wicked, hear and do. What the Lord commands, the Lord himself teaches in the Gospel, saying of the Pharisees, *They sit on the chair of Moses. Do what they say, but do not do what they do; for they say and do not do*[^97].

## No one is contaminated by the evils of others; therefore there was no cause for departing from unity.

**9. 19.** And would that in these words, which they mentioned from the psalm, they were willing to behold themselves as in a mirror: how they cast the words of God behind them[^98] who announce peace to the peoples and do not love peace; how they hate discipline who dare to condemn the world unheard and who, because—deservedly, nay, with far less than they deserve for the audacity of so great a frenzy—they suffer temporal hardships according to the discipline of divine mercy, do not confess that their sins are being punished, but boast that their merits are being crowned.

I do not say that they ran with a thief[^99], because worse than a thief is a robber, which Optatus was proclaimed on every side to be. Or do they perhaps not place their portion with adulterers, who shamefully allow the drunken bands of their nuns to roam, mingled day and night with the drunken bands of the Circumcellions?

Or do they not, sitting, speak against their brothers, who, on account of certain men whom they could not convict, maintain that there are no Christians in the inheritance of Christ spread throughout the whole world, and thus against the son of their mother—that is, the one still a little child in the faith, to be nourished with the milk of the sacraments—lay a most ruinous stumbling block[^100], while the weak one, not yet knowing how to follow God the Father, follows a man and, enticed by a feigned and shadowy appearance of truth, is torn away by a cruel rending from the framework of unity?

But if to those who do not do evils in that party the evil deeds of the rest are displeasing, and they do not believe that the crimes of others harm them—crimes they groan and grieve to see done in their midst[^101]—why do they ruinously tolerate, in the common sacrilege of schism, what they could fruitfully have tolerated in the integrity of unity? For they can say, if at last, taught by the very facts, they awake, they can say that the evils of individuals do not harm the rest who neither do nor approve such things; but that the sacrilege of schism is not the evil of individuals, but pertains to all those who do not commune with catholic unity, they can most easily be convicted, even if they most difficultly confess it.

For the very reason that, in their communion, the crimes of some do not pertain to others, by that very reason schism is the crime of all—because, when they now say that among their own people they cannot be stained by the crimes of others, they must at the same time confess that they had no cause to depart from unity, where the crimes of others could not stain them; and therefore by the most manifest crime of schism they are bound together alike, as by one deadly bond.

## Even if they are sinners, the Donatists have true baptism.

**10. 20.** 'But Jeremiah prophesied'—what did Jeremiah prophesy? 'That those who forsake God,' he says, 'do not have true baptism. For he says, *The heaven was astonished at this and shuddered much more vehemently, says the Lord, for my people have done two evils. They have forsaken me, the fountain of life, and have dug for themselves broken cisterns that cannot hold water*[^102]; and again, *It has become to me*, he says, *like deceitful water that has no faith*[^103]; and that which is written, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit?*[^104]

And that in the Psalms, *Let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head*; and again elsewhere, *Dying flies destroy the sweetness of the oil*[^105]; and elsewhere, *For the holy Spirit of discipline will flee the deceitful one and will withdraw itself from thoughts that are without understanding*[^106].'

If all these things are to be understood in the way they are understood by these men, the account of truth squares neither with us nor with them. But if I show that they are to be understood in another way, they alone are troubled by their own perversity; and, in order not to be troubled, they flee back to the catholic understanding—and from where they find a way out of the reply, from there they are entangled in the crime of schism.

For they too certainly have—I will not say, 'they are such,' but I will say this, which they either confess or most insanely deny—they too, then, have men forsaking God, who is the fountain of living water[^107], that is, men living iniquitously. For God is forsaken not by the feet but by the heart. They have also liars and men having no faith[^108], that is, men professing one thing and behaving otherwise. They certainly have dead men[^109].

For if the Apostle does not grant delights even to the more delicate and weaker sex, saying of the widow, *But she who lives in delights is dead while living*[^110], let them seek whether no men among them—and, what is more, no prelates or ministers—live in delights, and so, if they dare, let them declare that they have no dead men and that they are better than that Church to whose angel, in the figure of prelates or souls, it is said that it does not live but is dead[^111], and which nevertheless is numbered among the seven Churches, and to which—not divided from the framework of the body of Christ, but persevering in unity—precepts of life are besides given.

I pass over what they said against the Maximianists in their council: 'Quite after the example of the Egyptians, the shores are full of the corpses of the perishing[^112].' Of which number of the dead Felician is now within, who, still dead, baptizes; or, if he has now come back to life, he has with him those whom, dead, he baptized in schism. They certainly have sinners[^113]. For if any who seem great among them be questioned, they do not deny that even they themselves are sinners. For either they do not strike their breasts, or, when they do it, they do it feignedly—and if this is so, then certainly even then they unhappily sin, deceiving their peoples with a feigned humility—or else they do not say in the Lord's Prayer, *Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors*[^114]. Which surely is not said of those sins that were forgiven in the regeneration of baptism, but of those which the weakness of human life daily contracts from the most bitter waves of the world, for the curing of which the medicines of almsgiving, fasting, and prayers are furnished, so that in prayer is said what is done in almsgiving. For even to forgive the sin that another has committed against you, so that God may forgive you too, is a great work of mercy. But if they say this in prayer feignedly, not truthfully, thinking that they have nothing for God to forgive them, that very thing is an inexpiable sacrilege, that very thing is an impious and insane pride, which is certainly a monstrous sin.

For what shall I say of the dying flies[^115], when we have shown that in them are found, in manifold ways—whether they confess it or are convicted of it—men not still about to die, but already dead?

But as for the deceitful, whom the holy Spirit of discipline flees, and who live in ruin without understanding[^116]—how many of them they have among themselves, let them consider according to what has been said above.

For all the wicked too who lie hidden there—which is certain even from this, that often, when betrayed and convicted, they are condemned, not only for recent deeds but also for an old habit of outrages, having been able by a most cunning pretense to be long concealed—these surely are the more deceitful, who deceive by feigning themselves good.

## The Donatists have feigned good men, that is, hidden wicked men.

**10. 21.** If, then, these things are to be understood as these men understand them, how will the account be able to stand for them—why, among them, those who by living wickedly forsake God, whether they lie hidden or are known, do not become broken cisterns that cannot hold the waters[^117], if in this passage the sacrament of baptism is to be understood? And why are their liars and faithless men believed either to give or to have water that is not deceitful[^118] but truthful? Why, from those of their dead by whom men are baptized, does their washing profit anything[^119]? Why do their sinners fatten the heads of others with oil[^120]? What have the dying or dead flies there[^121] deserved, that they should not destroy the sweetness of the oil? By what privilege are all those fortified who are feigned there—that is, who cover the wolf with the skin of justice—so that the holy Spirit of discipline does not flee them[^122]? Or if the holy Spirit flees them, how is it given to those baptized through them?

For here cannot be said what they are accustomed most ineptly and most shamelessly to say: 'that someone can then be baptized by a wicked man, if the wickedness of the one baptizing lies hidden.' For he who is feigned is the more feigned the more he lies hidden. When, therefore, the Holy Spirit withdraws itself from this man, what hope will there be for the one baptized, if the merit of the man baptizing is to be considered in that grace of God? Here they simply do not find what to answer, because they deny that they have open wicked men. In which indeed they are most manifestly convicted.

But what is that to us? It is enough for us, for the necessary point of the case, that they cannot deny that they have feigned good men, that is, hidden wicked men, among themselves. For they are refuted by the many who, when they were living there with ruined and criminal habits and had long lain hidden by that very pretense, were at some time betrayed and expelled.

If, then, they are unwilling for us to believe that there are still some such men there, those certainly who were cast out, when they lay hidden there in the same pretense and the holy Spirit fled them, of whom it is written, *For the holy Spirit of discipline will flee the deceitful one*[^123]—how could they baptize?

Why are not at least those reviewed who are alive, so that they may receive baptism—which surely they could not receive from the feigned men whom the Holy Spirit was deserting?

But if they say, 'The Holy Spirit indeed was absent from those feigning men who were to give it for justifying,

but it was present to those believing men who were to receive it for cleansing, through the ineffable efficacy of its power—easily able to do both, to flee the one and to cherish the other, to find the one guilty and to cleanse the other'—let them see that the very question they resolve for themselves is at the same time resolved for us.

## If the ministers are dead through iniquity, yet Christ lives: it is he who baptizes, and not...

**10. 22.** But what is understood in this sentence of holy Scripture—which certainly they themselves, not understanding it and as it were putting it forward on behalf of their party, thrust upon us, to their own conviction—we admonish that this is to be understood in almost all such questions: namely, that all the sacraments, although they harm those who handle them unworthily, nevertheless profit through them those who receive them worthily, just as does the word of God, whence it was said, *Do what they say, but do not do what they do*[^124].

For to dig broken cisterns that cannot hold the waters[^125]—what prevents me from understanding it thus: that this is to be turned toward earthly pleasure and to be unable to hold the Holy Spirit, who is signified by the name of water in the Gospel[^126], as what Christian in any way does not know? Likewise, deceitful water having no faith[^127] can be understood not as a false baptism, but as a people deceitful and faithless—not counting the truthful and faithful, but counting only those who are deceitful and faithless. For that peoples are sometimes signified by the term 'waters,' let them read the Apocalypse, and let them not slander themselves first and rather than us. For so it is said to John, *The waters that you saw, over which that harlot sits, are peoples and crowds and nations and tongues*[^128]. And what is written, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^129]—to postpone for the moment a more careful inquiry into these words—I take most safely as denoting the baptisms of the pagans, because they worship men dead both to justice and to this life, in whose name they baptize.

For although the priests too are said to be dead through impiety, yet it is understood not on account of them, but on account of their dead gods, in whose contrast it is said, *Our God is the living God*[^130]. Wherefore, even if among the Christians there are some prelates or ministers dead through their iniquity and impiety, yet he lives of whom it was said in the Gospel, *This is he who baptizes*[^131], because, as the Apostle says, *Christ, rising from the dead, dies no more, and death shall have dominion over him no longer*[^132].

But as for the oil of the sinner, the Psalm itself most plainly indicates how it is to be understood. For it says, *The just man shall correct me in mercy and shall reprove me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head*[^133]. Hence it is manifest that by the oil of the sinner are signified the flatteries of a fawner, which, rejected and detested, he chooses rather to be corrected and reproved by the just—because the just man, doing this not with the deceitful gentleness of flattery but with the truthful harshness of rebuke, does it far more mercifully.

Hence too is that in the Apocalypse, *Those whom I love I reprove and chastise*[^134], and that in Solomon, *Better are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy*[^135], and many such things are found. But *the sweetness of the oil*—namely, the good odor through the good repute of Christians—those destroy[^136] who, about to live wickedly and to return to the death of their iniquities, rush in in a great multitude, so that through baptism their sins may be forgiven them and they may turn back to them again.

For on account of their numerousness I judge that they are compared to flies. But for them *they destroy the sweetness of the oil* who look not at the very grace of God but at the morals of men; and since, just as grains are not seen amid the chaff, so those living piously do not easily appear amid the crowds of the iniquitous, they, carnally offended, are either delayed from receiving everlasting salvation or recoil from it altogether. But that there is a good odor in the good repute of rightly living Christians, the Apostle teaches, saying, *We are the good odor of Christ in every place*[^137]. On the contrary, those to whom it is said, *For the name of God is blasphemed among the nations because of you*[^138], surely *destroy the sweetness of the oil*.

Thus, then, these passages have other, sincerer meanings, which, if these men too follow them, they are freed from the straits of those questions. But their understanding, if it is not confirmed by us, entangles themselves alone; but if it is confirmed by us, it entangles both of us.

...an angel, or the Holy Spirit through his hands.

**11. 23.** What need is there now to discuss more? Unless perhaps this will move someone: that when Parmenian wished to prove that carnal men cannot beget spiritual sons, he added a testimony from the Gospel, *What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the Spirit is spirit*[^139]. As if we said that any man begets spiritual sons through himself and not through the Gospel, in whose preaching the Holy Spirit works to beget in baptism spiritual sons—even when it flees a feigned minister, as we learned above.

And therefore the Apostle, when he was speaking with such sons, if he had said, *I have begotten you*, and had not added, *in Christ Jesus through the Gospel*[^140], in no way would any of the faithful religiously confess himself to be born of him. But the Gospel even the thief Judas preached without harm to believers; and they assert that their feigned men—that is, hidden wicked men, as they themselves also concede—though the Holy Spirit flees them, yet through their ministry spiritual sons are begotten.

For who could bear so great a madness that, when he perhaps calls a man begetting sons of his wife carnal, he should call an adulterer spiritual? 'God forbid,' he says, 'that I should hold this.' How, then, could a hidden adulterer, when he was a prelate among them, propagate spiritual sons, if carnal men cannot do this? Or did Christ perhaps then, or the Holy Spirit, or perhaps an angel, baptize through his hands?

If, then, a man baptizes when the baptizer is manifestly good, but, when the baptizer is a hidden wicked man, then God baptizes, or an angel, and each one is born spiritually such as was the one by whom he is baptized, let those who desire baptism wish that the men through whom they are baptized be not manifestly good but hidden wicked men, so that thus, with God or an angel baptizing, they may deserve to be reborn more holily. If they think to avoid this absurdity, let them confess that, through whatever man, when anyone is baptized with the baptism of Christ, Christ baptizes—he of whom alone it was said, *This is he who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^141].

## Paul allows sinners to announce Christ, even if they did not do so chastely.

**11. 24.** For that passage from the Gospel, *As the Father has sent me, I also send you. When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them: Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive anyone's sins, they will be forgiven, and if you retain anyone's, they will be retained*[^142], would be against us, so that we should be compelled to confess that this is done by men, not through men, if, after he said, *I also send you*, he had immediately subjoined, *If you forgive anyone's sins, they will be forgiven, and if you retain anyone's, they will be retained*.

But since there was interposed, *When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them: Receive the Holy Spirit*, and then it was added that through them the remission or retention of sins is done, it is sufficiently shown that they themselves do not do this, but surely the Holy Spirit does it through them, as in another place he says, *For it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit who is in you*[^143].

But the Holy Spirit is in a prelate or minister of the Church in such a way that, if he is not feigned, the Spirit works through him both his reward unto everlasting salvation and the regeneration or edification of those who through him are either consecrated or evangelized; but if he is feigned, since it is most truly written, *For the holy Spirit of discipline will flee the deceitful one*[^144], the Spirit is indeed absent from his salvation and withdraws itself *from his thoughts that are without understanding*, yet does not desert his ministry, by which it works through him the salvation of others.

On account of this the Apostle says, *For if I do this willingly, I have a reward; but if unwillingly, a stewardship has been entrusted to me*[^145]—that is, 'it profits those to whom I dispense this, not me who am feigned.'

For he who does it unwillingly, on account of carnal advantages and joys—which, if he could have otherwise, he would desert that office—he himself is surely feigned; and therefore he does not say, 'If I do it unwillingly, I profit nothing to those toward whom I do it,' but he only sets himself apart from the reward of salvation, not also those to whom even a wicked servant dispenses the Lord's provisions.

But because the Apostle was not such, but rather such that he did it willingly—that is, dispensed the free grace with the piety of a chaste heart—the Holy Spirit worked through him even his reward, which, as he himself says, *the Lord, the just judge, will render to him on that day*[^146].

But those who announced the Gospel not chastely—who indeed announced nothing other than the truth, that is, Christ, yet announced it not with the truth of their heart but for the occasion of their own advantages—he allows them to announce, and he rejoices, not surely for them, but for those who through them were being saved, holding the precept of him who says, *Do what they say, but do not do what they do; for they say and do not do*[^147]. For thus the Apostle speaks to the Philippians: *Some indeed*, he says, *preach Christ through envy and contention, but some also through good will: some out of love, knowing that I am set for the defense of the Gospel; but some announce Christ through obstinacy, not chastely, thinking to stir up tribulation for my chains. What then? Provided that in every way, whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is announced. And in this I rejoice, yes, and I will rejoice*[^148].

Would he permit them to preach Christ, although they preached the truth of the Gospel not in the truthful chastity of their heart—would he rejoice at the preaching of such men—unless he knew that it was indeed ruinous for them to announce a chaste thing unchastely, but salutary for those who, hearing good and true things through them, advanced toward salvation?

For where Christ, who is the truth[^149], is not preached, but falsehood and lie, he most plainly forbids it, saying to the Galatians, *If anyone evangelizes to you beyond what you received, let him be anathema*[^150], and likewise to Timothy, *As I asked you*, he says, *to remain at Ephesus when I went into Macedonia, that you might charge certain men not to teach otherwise*[^151]. But those envious, contentious, obstinate, unchaste men, seeking an occasion to satisfy their evil will—because the Holy Spirit so fled their pretense that it did not desert their ministry by which through them Christ was preached—he not only permits them to do it, but even rejoices that they do it.

## There is, therefore, no necessity of schism, than which nothing is graver.

**11. 25.** We bring forward these proofs from the holy Scriptures, so that it may appear that scarcely anything is graver than the sacrilege of schism, because there is no just necessity for cutting off unity, since the good tolerate the wicked—who will in no way harm them spiritually—for this reason, that they may not be spiritually sundered from the good, when the consideration of preserving peace reins in or defers the severity of discipline; which, however, security exercises when it appears that, without the wound of schism, something can be punished by ecclesiastical judgment unto salutary correction.

For we rather say: The name of the Lord belongs to those who fear him[^152], although they are mingled with the crowds of those who do not fear him; and that which the Apostle says, *The Lord knows those who are his, and let everyone who invokes the name of the Lord depart from injustice*[^153].

For if, for the good of peace—lest before the time, when the weeds are gathered, the wheat be rooted up together with them—anyone is compelled by necessity to be among the unjust, let him nevertheless depart from injustice, and securely he invokes the name of the Lord.

For at the same time he both departs from the unjust and goes out from them and is separated, for the time being, in heart, so that he may deserve to be separated from such men in body too at the end.

## The praise of God is comely in the ears of those who hear and the heart of those who believe.

**12. 26.** And that which is written, *Praise is not comely in the mouth of a sinner*[^154], is certainly comely in the mouth of the faithful. But each one has his own mouth, just as each one has his own burden, nor is anyone wounded by another's mouth without the consent of his own heart. But when the preaching of God proceeds from the mouth of a sinner, it must be asked what kind of sinner Scripture wished to signify in this passage.

For the publican too, who was justified more than that Pharisee[^155], was surely a sinner. For if his sins were not real, his confession of sins was false; but if that confession was true, on account of which he even deserved to be justified, then without doubt his sins were real. This I would say most truly also of the prayer of holy Daniel, who indeed says truthfully, *While I was praying and confessing my sins and the sins of my people*[^156]. In whose mouth, then, is praise not comely, except especially the most lying and feigned man, whom the holy Spirit of discipline will flee[^157]? Yet when he speaks the truth, it is not comely in his mouth, because it is not attributed to him of which he is not a partaker—just as prophecy was not comely in the mouth of Caiaphas, the high priest of the priests, who did not know what he said, but, being high priest, prophesied[^158]. But yet in itself the praise of God is comely in the ears of those who hear and the heart of those who believe[^159].

## Schismatics do not lose the baptism they received within...

**13. 27.** Indeed, from a sentence of the Apostle, ill usurped, Parmenian even dares as it were to insult those whom he denies to have baptism and therefore says cannot give it. *For what do you have*, he says, *that you did not receive?*[^160]

To pass over from where and why the Apostle says this—which is declared by the connection of his epistle—if he wishes this to be understood of baptism, and if he cannot give who does not have, and he does not have who did not receive, so that what is written, *For what do you have that you did not receive?*, may in some way support him, I ask whether he does not have who received it among them. If they say, 'He has it,' I ask whether he loses it if he departs from them. If they say, 'He loses it,' he must be baptized again if he returns, so that what he lost may be restored to him. But if this is not done, nor does anyone say it should be done, then he had not lost it. Moreover, if he received it and did not lack it, he surely has what he received, and therefore it cannot be said to him, according to these men's understanding, *For what do you have that you did not receive?*

Now turn your mind to the very origin of the schism. Whatever sort they think Caecilian to be—whom we believe innocent—without doubt he had been baptized in unity; for this division had not yet been made. Let us suppose, as they wish, that he himself departed from unity; he surely did not lose what he had received. For if he returned, he would not be baptized again, so as to recover what he had lost. Moreover, if he had not lacked what he had received, he surely had it. Nor, then, could it be said even to him, from these men's perverse understanding, *For what do you have that you did not receive?*

Much less can this be said to him who received from the Lord through Caecilian. For why could a man, who is not joined to you, not receive through him—who did not lose it when he departed from you—what he did not have? How much more shamelessly, then, is it said to the whole world, *For what do you have that you did not receive?*—the world from which the connection of the sacrament itself came into Africa, which through no crime of any traitor could lose the promise of God, who said to Abraham with a most plain pledge, *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^161]—even if these men had been not impious slanderers of the brothers, but true examiners of crimes.

...nor do they lose the right of giving.

**13. 28.** For that which some of them, convicted by the truth, have begun to say—'He who departs from the Church does not indeed lose baptism, but he nevertheless loses the right of giving it'—appears in many ways to be said vainly and emptily. First, because no cause is shown why he who cannot lose baptism itself can lose the right of giving it. For each is a sacrament, and each is given to a man by a certain consecration—the one when he is baptized, the other when he is ordained—and therefore in the catholic Church neither is permitted to be repeated.

For if at any time, coming from that party, even prelates have been received, for the good of peace, with the error of schism corrected, then, even if it seemed necessary that they should hold the same offices they held, they have not been ordained again, but, as baptism in them, so ordination remained whole, because the defect had been in the cutting off, which was corrected by the peace of unity, not in the sacraments, which, wherever they are, are themselves. And when it is judged expedient for the Church that their prelates, coming into catholic fellowship, should not there administer their honors, yet the very sacraments of ordination are not taken from them, but remain upon them.

And therefore the hand is not laid on them again among the people, lest injury be done not to the man but to the sacrament itself. And if at any time it is done in ignorance, and the deed is not headstrongly defended but, when recognized, is piously corrected, pardon is easily obtained. For our God *is not the God of dissension, but of peace*[^162]; nor are the sacraments of the Church his enemies in those who have departed from the Church, but those very men who have departed are enemies.

But just as they have in baptism that which can be given through them, so in ordination they have the right of giving; both, indeed, to their own ruin, so long as they do not have the love of unity. But yet it is one thing not to have, another to have ruinously, another to have salutarily. What is not had must be given when there is need to give it; but what is had ruinously, the ruin being driven off through correction, must be brought about that it be had salutarily.

## Even if a Christian layperson baptizes, the sacrament is valid.

**13. 29.** And yet, even if a layperson, compelled by some necessity of one perishing, has given what he learned, when he himself received it, how it was to be given, I do not know whether anyone would piously say it must be repeated. For if it is done with no necessity compelling, it is the usurpation of another's office; but if necessity presses, it is either no offense or a venial one.

But even if it is usurped under no necessity and given by anyone to anyone, what has been given cannot be said not to have been given, although it can rightly be said to have been given unlawfully. The unlawful usurpation, therefore, is corrected by the disposition of one remembering and repenting.

But if he does not correct it, what has been given will remain to the punishment of the usurper—either of him who gave it unlawfully or of him who received it unlawfully—yet it will not be held as not given.

Nor in any way will the royal mark be violated, through a devoted soldier, by what has been usurped by private persons. For if some men have furtively and irregularly stamped gold or silver or bronze by striking it, not in the public mints, when it has been detected, will not the recognized royal mark—those men being punished or freed by indulgence—be gathered into the royal treasuries?

Or if anyone—whether a deserter or one who never served at all—marks some private person with the military mark, when it has been detected, is not the one so marked punished as a deserter, and the more severely the more he could prove that he had never served at all, the most audacious marker being punished along with him, if he betrays him? But if perhaps, not serving, he in fear shuddered at that character of military service in his body and fled to the clemency of the emperor, and, having poured out his prayer and obtained pardon, has now begun to serve, is that character, the man being freed and corrected, repeated, or rather, being recognized, approved?

Or do the Christian sacraments perhaps adhere less than this bodily mark, when we see that not even apostates lack baptism—which surely is not restored to them when they return through penance, and therefore is judged to have been unable to be lost? Or was a comparison from military service not to be drawn, when the Apostle draws one even from the contests of the games[^163] and openly cries out, *No one serving God as a soldier entangles himself in worldly affairs, that he may please him to whom he has proved himself*[^164]?

## Whether non-Christians can baptize.

**13. 30.** And this indeed is another question—whether baptism can be given also by those who were never Christians—nor is anything to be rashly affirmed here without the authority of a council as great as suffices for so great a matter.

But about those who are separated from the unity of the catholic Church, there is now no question that they both have it and can give it, and that they have it ruinously and hand it on ruinously outside the bond of peace. For this has now been discussed, considered, examined, and confirmed in the very unity of the whole world. But if we do wrong, let them explain how the sacrament of baptism cannot be lost and the sacrament of ordination can be lost, since they say, 'He who departs from the Church does not indeed lose baptism, yet he loses the right of giving it.'

For if each is a sacrament—which no one doubts—why is the one not lost and the other lost? Injury must be done to neither sacrament. If holy things flee the wicked, let both flee them; if holy things remain inviolably in the wicked, let both remain. If they say, 'Baptism is rightly given only in the true Church,' it is answered them, 'Baptism is rightly had only in the true Church.' Why can it not be given where it is not rightly given, when it can be had where it is not rightly had? Or is it because not to have is one thing, not to have rightly another?

So too not to give is one thing, not to give rightly another. Just as he who departs from unity does not rightly have it, yet has it, and therefore it is not given back to one returning, so too he who departs from unity does not rightly give it, yet gives it, and therefore what he received from him is not repeated for one coming to unity.

But they maintain that what is not rightly given has not been given. What if another maintained that what is not rightly had cannot be had—do we not protest against both and say that he who has departed from unity does indeed have it, but does not have it rightly?

If, then, they wish that the one should hear what we equally protest, let them themselves hear us protesting that he who departs from unity does indeed give it, but does not give it rightly. Wherefore, just as what he had even outside is not given back to one returning, so what he received even outside is not to be repeated for one coming. Whence it is consequently understood that the perversity of men must be corrected, but the holiness of the sacraments must be violated in no perverse man; for it is established that it remains unpolluted and inviolable in perverse and criminal men, whether in those who are within or in those who are outside.

And that the wicked are said to pollute them, they are said to do so as far as in them lies, while those things remain unpolluted. But in the good they remain unto reward, in the wicked they remain unto judgment. For of the Spirit too, which can in no way be quenched, it was nevertheless said, *Do not quench the Spirit*[^165]—that is, 'As far as in you lies, do not act as if you were trying to quench the Spirit, or as if you reckoned the Spirit quenched.' And the name of God can in no way be polluted, and yet it was said, *A son and a father went in to the same girl, that they might pollute the name of the Lord their God*[^166].

## Whether a criminal who is within can give holiness.

**13. 31.** Nor can these men in any way extricate themselves, when it is put to them why the holiness of the sacrament can be both had and given by one whom God has already condemned as a criminal within, and only then begins to be unable to be given by him when he has been condemned by men—although not even then can he lose it.

Finally, why did Felician—whom, with Maximian, the three hundred and ten condemned, and who was for a long time outside, in a sacrilegious schism, as they themselves pronounced in their council—not only not lose baptism itself, but also not lose the right of giving it?

For he was received in his honor, just as he had gone out, with all those whom, while outside, he baptized, not one of them being rebaptized; for if they judged that any of those whom he had baptized outside ought to be rebaptized, they would be judging that he had lost the right of giving while he was outside; and therefore it would follow that they should also ordain him again, if they baptized those men again.

But when they are called back to the peace of Christ they are slanderers; when they consult for the peace of Donatus they are dissemblers. What else is this than what their own Tyconius says of them: 'What we wish is holy'?

## The Lord raises the dead, heals the wounded...

**14. 32.** Why, then, does Parmenian exult with empty boasting and say, 'Never will the censure of the divine law allow that a dead man should be able to give life to anyone, a wounded man to heal, a blind man to enlighten, a naked man to clothe, a polluted man to cleanse'? For the Lord raises the dead, the Lord heals the wounded, the Lord enlightens the blind, the Lord clothes the naked, the Lord cleanses the polluted.

Why does he arrogate to himself what does not belong to a man? Are those among them who do not sin so alive that they are even said to be able to give life, when they cannot give the increase? *I*, he says, *planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. Neither is he who plants anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase*[^167].

How does he give life to a dead man, who cannot give increase to a living one? *For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so also the Son gives life to whom he will*[^168]. Are they so healthy among them that they can even heal those who are not healthy? What else do these men do than set themselves up to be blessed in place of the Lord? But they are in no way seduced by them who, hoping not in man but in God, sing, *Bless the Lord, O my soul, and do not forget all his recompenses; who is gracious to all your iniquities, who heals all your infirmities*[^169].

If he himself heals all infirmities, he leaves none which Parmenian may say he heals. Are there such great lights among them that they can even enlighten? Which not even John the Evangelist grants to that John the Baptist, than whom no one greater has arisen among those born of women[^170]—of whom he says, *He was not the light, but came to bear witness to the light. The true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world, was*[^171]. If that light enlightens every man, it leaves none whom Parmenian may say he enlightens; because, although holy men are in a certain manner called lights, yet enlightened lights are one thing, and the enlightening light another, which alone is that of which the same John the Baptist says, *We have all received of his fullness*[^172]. But who clothes the naked except him who says, *Bring forth for him the first robe*[^173], and who will clothe this corruptible with incorruption and this mortal with immortality[^174]? And is any man said to clothe anyone with the divine garment, for whom it is a great thing if he himself deserves to be clothed? Now further, let him dare to say that he cleanses the polluted, if he first dares to say that he is not polluted. For we have indeed been cleansed by the grace of God. But not even then shall we cleanse anyone, when our cleansing has been perfected; how much less now, when *the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul*[^175]! For *who shall boast that he has a chaste heart, or boast that he is clean from sin?*[^176]

For to cleanse and to heal, in spiritual matters, are of equal force. But just as we have been saved in hope[^177], so we have been cleansed in hope, in respect of perfect salvation and perfect cleanness. How, then, can we already heal and cleanse, who shall not be able to even then, when we shall in every way be clean and saved? 'But God,' he says, 'does these things through a man.' He does indeed, but he did them also through Judas, whom he sent with the rest to preach the Gospel[^178]; he did them also through the Pharisees, in those who themselves did the good things they heard through them, while those men did not do the things they said[^179].

Finally, why do their iniquitous and criminal men, whether when they lie hidden or when they are tolerated for the party of Donatus, give life, heal, enlighten, cleanse? Are they themselves not dead, wounded, blind, polluted? Or do not blind men leading the blind fall together among them into the pit[^180] for this reason, that those who follow do so by hearing and obeying not them, but the God whom they preach? Just so, indeed. But they then preach God, if they gather with Christ; but when they scatter what they do not gather with him[^181], by preaching Donatus, not God, then, blind men following the blind, they fall together into the pit.

But about the question of baptism we will treat something more at length, with the Lord's help, when we have replied to Parmenian on all the testimonies of the holy Scriptures that he believed should be objected to us—he who truly gives his neighbors drink, to their overthrow, from the cup of iniquity, who has poured out for them so evil an error that, not for peace but for division and rending, not only does he himself speak, but he even persuades them that the divine books speak.

## Baptism is given from heaven, but through men.

**15. 33.** But, among many things, I wonder that the man, when he was arguing that baptism cannot be had unless received, nor received without one giving, interposed a testimony from the Gospel: *A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from heaven*[^182]. For someone ignorant of these words, when this man had begun to say, *A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from heaven*, before he had said *from heaven*, might think that he was going to say, 'from Donatus,' or 'from Parmenian,' or 'from someone of the party of Donatus,' or altogether 'from the party of Donatus itself.' I acknowledge the Gospel and note that it is written there, *A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from heaven*.

But is Donatus heaven? Is Parmenian heaven? Is the party of Donatus itself? It would not be heaven, not even if it were in heaven. For he who said, *A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from heaven*, would never say, 'Unless it has been given him from the sun or from the moon or from the stars,' which are nevertheless in heaven; how much less would he say, 'Unless it has been given him from the party of Donatus,' which not only is not heaven nor in heaven, but does not even wish to be in the kingdom of heaven! Nor without doubt would he say, 'A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from the Church'; for the Church too receives from heaven.

But if he said, 'A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him by a just man,' these bold-faced men would indeed rush in and proclaim that they are just, so that whoever wished to receive might receive from them; nor would we raise the question whether they were just or not, but we would easily show, from certain men betrayed and expelled, that there are among them even hidden unjust men, and yet that they do not disapprove them nor say that what was given and received by such men was not given or not received.

But even this would be said falsely, if it were said, 'A man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him by a just man.' For that just man himself, from whom another receives—I ask from whom he received. If he too from a just man, I ask the same about him, until from the very head of the human order I come to someone who did not receive from a man, and so I teach that it is false that a man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him by a man.

## When a man desires to receive something holy, let him not regard the man.

**15. 34.** But what do these men accomplish, unwittingly, through the evangelical testimony, except that men are admonished to awake at last and observe that truly men are not to be regarded when a man desires to receive something holy, but only him who gives this to a man from heaven, because *a man cannot receive anything unless it has been given him from heaven*[^183]? But if they say, 'He receives from heaven indeed, not from the man, yet through the man,' I ask through what sort of man.

If only through a just man, then those do not have it who, among their very selves, received through hidden most iniquitous men; if also through an unjust man, what cause, then, is there that they should rebaptize anyone? If through an unjust man but only a hidden one, then they do not have it whom Optatus, the manifest satellite of Gildo, baptized;

if also through an unjust man even a manifest one, who nevertheless is not yet condemned and cast out from the communion of the Church, then those do not have it whom Felician of Musti, when he was outside their communion, baptized in the schism of Maximian—whom nevertheless now, returned with him, no one rebaptizes.

Finally, if a man, although from heaven, can nevertheless receive anything only through a man, I ask through what man John the Baptist himself, who said these things, had received what he surely had received from heaven—and none is found.

And so the evil cause is convicted by the testimonies brought forward by itself; because, even though the Son says that he has received from the Father and that the Holy Spirit receives of his own—not as it were by degrees, but as he himself explained, saying, *Because all that the Father has is mine, therefore I said: He will receive of mine*[^184]—

yet that a man can receive something divinely with no man interposed, John himself testifies by his own example, and so many holy men before the Son of God became man,

and, after he rose again and ascended into heaven, the hundred and twenty men who were gathered together at once, whom the Holy Spirit, coming from heaven with no man on earth laying on a hand, filled; and, the order of the Church being now established, Peter himself wondered that Cornelius the centurion, before baptism itself, before the laying on of the hand, was filled with the same Holy Spirit together with those who were with him.

No one, then, receives without one giving; but as regards the holiness of baptism, God is present to give and a man to receive, whether through God himself giving, or through an angel, or through a holy man, or through an iniquitous man—as through so many, whether hidden or manifest, whom the servants of the head of the household are forbidden to gather from the harvest before the time, and whom the Lord's wheat, separated for the time being in heart, like chaff, until the time of winnowing, does not desert bodily by rashness, but bears spiritually by piety.

## Even one who believes wrongly can receive baptism.

**16. 35.** 'He who has believed wrongly,' he says, 'cannot obtain the sacrament of baptism, because it is written, *The perverse cannot be adorned*[^185].' What? If, then, someone baptized among them, who, for example, had thought that Christ began to exist from the time when, according to the flesh, he was born of the Virgin Mary, afterward moved by the word of truth—when he had learned that he is the one of whom John says, *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God*, of whom he consequently says, *The Word was made flesh and dwelt among us*[^186]—were to confess to them his former error and to indicate, in his anxiety, that he had been in it when he was baptized, would they order him to be baptized again?

They would surely never do it, but they would rejoice over the man's unlearned simplicity—depraved before by a carnal opinion, afterward corrected by the reasoning of truth—

or if such a perversity seemed to him to be defended obstinately, and, having long resisted and not been corrected, he were even driven from the Church,

and afterward, the truth being known, were to return, they would surely judge that he must be cured by penance, not wounded by a repetition of baptism—although he confessed that he had had this in his heart even at that time when he had been baptized among them.

The perverse, then, *cannot be adorned*, because the sacrament he had received would avail him not for adornment, if he persisted in a perverse faith, but rather for punishment—although the sacrament itself, of itself, would remain whole even in the perverse man, whom it did not adorn but judged; and therefore in no way was the holiness of that sacrament to be violated, even when the perversity of that man was to be corrected.

## For receiving baptism, holy men are not to be sought out.

**17. 36.** Parmenian even dares to set before us divine examples, by which he thinks to show that a holy man must be sought, from whom the sacrament ought to be received. 'The Son of God himself,' he says, 'the Lord Jesus Christ himself, from whom the beginning of spiritual baptism arose, when according to the will of the Father he was to be baptized, is he shown to have come to the perfidious and profane Pharisees, or to the most holy John?' But if by this example we are invited to receive baptism, we ought to seek out one inferior to us by whom we may be baptized, since the Lord was baptized by one who had foretold that he himself ought to be baptized by the Lord, and who testified that he was unworthy to loose the strap of his sandal[^187].

Nay rather, to leave off inquiring more carefully why our Savior himself wished to be baptized—since he willed it for some certain cause—perhaps for this reason: that, although he could baptize himself, he who could even baptize his own baptizer, the Lord willed to be baptized by a servant, and he through whom all things were made[^188] by one who was made among all things, both to teach humility and to show that it does not matter who is baptized by whom, provided that he be baptized with that baptism with which he is to be baptized. Nor would he disdain to be baptized by the Pharisees, if the Pharisees had a baptism of this kind, with which he, for the grace of a certain sacrament, willed to be baptized.

For when he was also to be circumcised, was John sought, since this was already accustomed to be done by the Jews? And when the legal sacrifice was to be offered for him, was that temple shunned which is called by him a den of robbers[^189]? Into which certainly both the good and the wicked entered, nor did the wicked harm the good; since the Lord, who says, *Be holy, for I also am holy*[^190], so inviolably makes his holy ones dwell among the wicked, if they keep the holiness they receive, just as the Lord Jesus himself was polluted by no contagion of malignity in the nation of the Jews—neither when, according to the perfect way of humility, made under the law, he received those first sacraments, nor when afterward, the disciples being chosen, he lived with his own betrayer even to the final kiss.

For by his example—of one not only not doing evils, but also not consenting to any malice—the wheat is secure among the chaff, because it neither does such things nor consents to those who do, even if in the same crop until the harvest, in the same floor until the winnowing, within the same nets until the separation that will take place on the shore, the good tolerate the wicked[^191]. But these men are truly blind leaders of the blind[^192], who both see so many wicked men in the number of their own and do not see the way of peace[^193], and persuade men to follow them not for bearing with one another on account of the bond of unity[^194], but for dividing from one another on account of the sacrilege of schism.

## To baptize a sinner is not to help him to sin.

**18. 37.** 'But it was said through the prophet to King Jehoshaphat: *O King Jehoshaphat, do you help a sinner, or do you love one whom the Lord hates? On account of this the wrath of the Lord was upon you*[^195].' For which of us says that a sinner is to be helped in that, surely, in which he wishes to sin, as Ahab himself, whom Jehoshaphat helped by going with him into battle, when Ahab despised the truthful words of the prophet Micaiah?

And yet not even so did the most wicked desert of King Ahab harm the innocence of King Jehoshaphat, because the Lord delivered him, crying out to him from the danger of war, but permitted that sacrilegious despiser to come into the hands of the enemy[^196]. And if Jehoshaphat experienced any danger—which the prophet indicates to him was from the wrath of the Lord—he had merited it not by another's sin but by his own, because, as was said to him, he was helping one who sinned.

But with God his other good deeds prevailed. For so it is said to him: *Did you help that sinner, and are you a friend to those who hate the Lord? And in this there is wrath upon you from the face of the Lord. The good words of God are all with you, because you have taken away the groves from the land and have prepared your heart to seek God*[^197].

But he who dwells in the Church of God, where there are also those who *seek their own things, not the things of Jesus Christ*[^198], who *through envy and contention announce Christ not chastely*[^199], and who says, *Whether in pretense or in truth, let Christ be announced; and in this I rejoice, yes, and I will rejoice*[^200],

remains unpolluted and whole among them, because he does not consent to them in seeking their own things, since he blames and arraigns this, nor does he help them to sin, but to this: that Christ may be more widely preached and, by those who hear and do, through those who do not do what they say, Christ may be believed, hoped in Christ, Christ loved.

For to such men the same Apostle gave a precept, saying, *Do not be joined with unbelievers. For what partnership is there of justice with iniquity? What communion is there of light with darkness? And what agreement of Christ with Belial? Or what part has a believer with an unbeliever? And what concord has the temple of God with idols? For you are temples of the living God. For he says: I will dwell in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. Wherefore go out from their midst and be separated, says the Lord, and touch nothing unclean. And I will receive you and will be to you a Father, and you shall be to me sons and daughters, says the Lord almighty*[^201].

Understanding these words carnally, these men, through so many divisions, have cut themselves into bits within the one province of Africa. For they do not understand that no one is joined with unbelievers except one who does the sins of the pagans or favors those who do such things, nor that anyone becomes a partner of iniquity except one who either does or approves iniquitous things. But who communes with darkness except through the darkness of his own consent, when, leaving Christ, he follows Belial?

Who places his part with unbelievers except one who becomes a partner of their unbelief? For so too he ceases to be the temple of God, nor does he otherwise join himself to idols.

But those who are temples of the living God and appear in the midst of a crooked and perverse nation like luminaries in the world, holding the word of life[^202], are infected by nothing that they tolerate for the sake of unity, nor are they straitened, because in them God dwells and walks, and they go out from the midst of the wicked and are separated, for the time being, in heart—lest perhaps, when they wish to do this through the sedition of schism, they be separated from the good spiritually before they are separated from the wicked bodily.

## The Donatists do not glorify God.

**19. 38.** What, then, is written that God said, *Those who glorify me I will glorify, and he who despises me shall be despised*[^203], these men above all are unwilling to behold. For how do they glorify God, who say that his promises could not be fulfilled throughout the world—the promises he made to our fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, which, foretelling them so long beforehand through the Prophets, he exhibited through his only Son, who was made to him of the seed of David according to the flesh[^204], so that in him, that is, in the seed of Abraham, all the nations might be blessed; who say that the Son of God himself said in vain, *Let both grow until the harvest*[^205], as though he were false or deceived himself, when only weeds have grown throughout the world, but the wheat, except for the party of Donatus, has failed in the whole world?

How do those who hold these things glorify the Lord, when it is written, *In the breadth of a nation is the glory of a king, but in the diminution of the people is the ruin of the prince*[^206]? Or how do they not despise the Lord, who, with an incredible rashness of impiety, blow away his baptism in those whose case they have not heard and about whom they could in no way judge, and dare to arrogate to themselves what belongs to God,

who receive men condemned by themselves, in their honors whole, for the peace of Donatus, and detest men unheard by themselves, against the peace of the Lord; who maintain that baptism handed down by the Apostles has perished in those parts of the earth, and concede that baptism handed down by Felician among the Maximianists has not perished?

But now, how do the catholics not glorify the Lord, who are confident that his promises could be hindered by no crimes of men from being fulfilled, who follow up his sacraments with such due veneration that they show that these, even if handled by the unworthy, remain in their inviolate holiness, while those who handle them are condemned by their own perversity?

## To reprove, with peace preserved.

**20. 39.** 'Again,' they say, 'it is written, *Do not commune with the unfruitful works of darkness, but rather even reprove them. For the things that are done by them in secret it is shameful even to speak of*[^207].' We have already said how these very words are to be understood: that not to commune is not to consent; which, for the discipline of the Church, is too little, unless they are also reproved, so that they may be corrected. But these things must be done with peace preserved, and as far as the duty of preserving unity admits, lest the wheat be rooted up together with them.

## Not to commune with the sins of others is to keep oneself chaste.

**21. 40.** 'Again, to Timothy the same Apostle says, *Do not commune with the sins of others. Keep yourself chaste*[^208].' He taught by what follows how what he said before is to be understood. For he who keeps himself chaste does not commune with the sins of others. For if he communes, he consents; if he consents, he is corrupted; if he is corrupted, he does not keep himself chaste.

But at last, at length, Parmenian awoke and attended to what Tyconius said—yet in vain; for at once, out of love of his own opinion, he closed his eyes against the truth. For he says, 'Or do not, dearest brother, the sins of others pollute others? And this is not to commune with criminals: though you meet with them, not to do their deeds.'

And yet he did not say the whole. For it is too little not to do their deeds, unless they displease; too little that they displease, unless they be reproved. For it is one thing not to do, another not to commune—that is, to consent to those who do—another also to reprove. Why, then, where he had begun to see, did he at once turn himself away and refuse to come through to the end, and scarcely set down a third of the whole? Or did he think, as they did with the people, that the sentence of truth too must be cut short? We say that he who does not do evil, nor consents to one doing it, and reproves the one doing it, dwells firm and whole among the iniquitous, like wheat among the chaff; but he says only, 'Not to do their deeds.' And yet let us see how he refutes this third little particle of the whole sentence.

## Evil is at least to be avoided in the heart.

**21. 41.** 'That this is against the divine law,' he says, 'no one who venerates the law is ignorant.' Still these words can be said in common. For another too can say, 'That this is according to the divine law, no one who venerates the law is ignorant.' But one must prove, not assert. Let us attend, then, to how he proves it. 'For what does it profit,' he says, 'to have kept one's innocence, if you are mingled with the guilty and liable with them?' Indeed, if it is so, it is not that having kept one's innocence does not profit, but that innocence has not been kept.

For no one can rightly be said to be mingled with the guilty and liable except through the stained consent of conscience. But he who fulfills what is written, *To the just man nothing iniquitous is pleasing*[^209], wherever any necessity compels him to dwell, cannot be mingled with iniquities. 'Or how,' he says, 'will you be able to remain uncorrupted, you who are associated with the corrupt?'

Just so, plainly, if he is associated—that is, if he commits something evil with them or favors those committing it; but if he does neither, he is in no way associated.

Moreover, if he adds the third thing—that he be not slothful in punishing, but the just man either correct in mercy and reprove[^210], or even, if he bears that office and the consideration of preserving peace admits it, reprove sinners before all, that the rest may fear, and even remove them either from some grade of honor or from the very communion of the sacraments, and do all these things with the love of one correcting, not with the hatred of one persecuting—he has fulfilled the fullest duty not only of the most chaste innocence, but also of the most diligent severity. But where the other things are hindered, those two, always retained, keep him uncorrupted and chaste: that he neither do evil nor approve the deed.

## The Donatists are not a little leaven.

**22. 42.** But still let us see from where he teaches what he affirms. 'Since it is written,' he says, '*A little leaven corrupts the whole lump*[^211].' Parmenian said this and departed, nor can it now be shown to him that in the party of Donatus there is not only a little leaven, but much poison from those hatched eggs of asps[^212] and now full-grown serpents, condemned on behalf of Primian and again recalled to Primian. 'But they have been corrected,' he says.

Thanks be to God. If it is true, I do not begrudge it. And would that it were done perfectly. For if to return from the Maximianists to the party of Donatus is some grade of correction, how much truer and more perfect a correction is it to return from the party of Donatus itself to catholic unity!

And Tyconius indeed said many things, by which in those times—and, as he inwardly knew—they would be unwilling to confess that their whole lump was corrupted by their own leaven, not a little but much, who charged that the whole world had been leavened by the sins of the Africans. But I wonder at these men, if it still pleases them so to understand these apostolic words, and yet to wish so to defend Optatus the Gildonian that they do not even concede that he was a little leaven.

But if they concede even this, how great do they think their lump is, that it could not be wholly corrupted? Or if it was corrupted only in those whom Optatus pleased, let them, taught by experience, learn to understand what they read, because the whole lump is in those to whom pertains what is called the whole, whether good or evil[^213]. But it pertains to those who consent; to those who do not consent it does not pertain at all, and therefore it must be corrected by ecclesiastical discipline, lest by persuading it reach to many. And where the consideration of peace permits this to be done and it is not done, that very negligence draws guilt, and is in danger of consenting through the sloth of correcting.

## Let the wheat and the weeds grow together.

**23. 43.** From which rule that too is to be understood which he consequently objects, saying that it is written, *Let it be for you a perpetual statute throughout your generations to divide between the holy and the irreligious, and between the clean and the unclean*[^214]. For each one does this the better, the more he advances in the Church. *For when the herb had grown and made fruit, then the weeds appeared*[^215]. And although the servants of the head of the household already divided and distinguished between the two by knowledge, yet they are commanded to let them grow, and this until the harvest[^216].

But these things thus far. For what remains must be considered and treated somewhat more carefully from a fresh beginning.
