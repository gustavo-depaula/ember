# Book 1

## A reply is made to the letter that Parmenian writes to Tyconius.

**1. 1.** Indeed I have elsewhere, with the strength the Lord supplies, discussed much against the Donatists, partly in writing and partly also in oral discourse. But now, since there has come into our hands a certain letter of Parmenian, once their bishop, which he writes to Tyconius—a man endowed with keen intelligence and rich eloquence, yet a Donatist—since Parmenian thought him to be in error on the very point that Tyconius was compelled to confess as true, I have decided, at the request, or rather at the command, of the brothers, to reply here to this letter of Parmenian, especially because of certain testimonies from the Scriptures that he takes in a way other than they ought to be taken.

For Tyconius, beaten on all sides by the voices of the sacred pages, awoke and saw the Church of God spread throughout the whole world, just as so long beforehand it was foreseen and foretold concerning her through the hearts and mouths of the saints. Having perceived this, he undertook to demonstrate and maintain, against his own people, that no man's sin, however criminal and monstrous, sets a limit to the promises of God, and that the deeds of any persons whatever established within the Church, and any impiety, do not so act that the faith of God concerning the Church—that she would in the future be spread to the ends of the earth, a faith held in the promises to the fathers and now made manifest—should be made void.

Although Tyconius discussed this vigorously and copiously, and stopped the mouths of those who contradicted him with many great and clear testimonies of the holy Scriptures, he did not see what he should consequently have seen: namely, that those Christians in Africa belong to the Church spread throughout the whole world who are joined by communion not to those who are cut off from the communion and unity of that same world, but to the very world itself. Parmenian, however, and the rest of the Donatists saw that this followed, and they chose to take up a most obstinate spirit against the most evident truth that Tyconius was maintaining, rather than, by granting it, to be overcome by the African Churches that rejoiced in the communion of that unity which Tyconius maintained—the unity from which these men had separated themselves. And Parmenian indeed at first thought he should be corrected by a letter; but afterward they report that he was even condemned by their council. And so we have determined in this work to reply to the letter of Parmenian which he wrote to Tyconius, reproving him because he proclaimed the Church spread throughout the whole world, and warning him not to dare to do so.

## Parmenian does not believe the testament of God, but his colleagues.

**2. 2.** And so let us first see what sort of thing this is, that he says the Gauls and Spaniards and Italians and their associates—by whom he certainly wishes the whole world to be understood—are like the African traitors by commerce in crimes and fellowship in offenses. For to a man who brings forward from the holy Scriptures so many and so weighty proofs, he himself speaks without proofs and wishes to be believed though he proves nothing, inviting Tyconius, of course, to imitate himself—because he too believed certain of his fellow bishops, who merely talked, against so many Churches established throughout so vast an expanse of all lands. What more reckless thing could be found than such credulity? For he says that certain men, as he asserts, most faithful witnesses, having performed an embassy, came to these provinces; then that, by a twofold visitation of the Lord's most holy priests, as he himself says, the things they allege were published more clearly, more fully, and more truly.

O the man, who thinks that he ought to be believed more than God! Tyconius brings forth the thunders of the divine testament, which was made in the promise to Abraham and the promise to Isaac and the promise to Jacob, whose God he testifies himself to be, saying, *I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob; this is my name forever*[^1], and this man sets against it the narratives of his fellow priests. What was said to Abraham? *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^2]. What was said to Isaac? *And in your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because Abraham your father obeyed my voice*[^3].

What was said to Jacob? *I am the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac; fear not. For the land on which you sleep I will give to you and to your seed. And your seed shall be as the sand of the earth, and it shall spread abroad to the sea and to the south and to the north and to the east. And in you shall all the tribes of the earth be blessed, and in your seed*[^4].

But lest they think this was said of the Jews, let the Apostle explain what is the seed of Abraham in which all the nations were said to be blessed: *The promises*, he says, *were made to Abraham and to his seed. It does not say, 'And to seeds,' as of many, but as of one, 'And to your seed,' which is Christ*[^5]. It was therefore promised on such great authority, and fulfilled with such great truth, that all the nations would have a blessing in Christ—and those who wish to be called Christians contradict it!

And what do they set against these things? Certain most faithful witnesses, he says, having performed an embassy, came to these provinces; then, by a twofold visitation of the Lord's most holy priests, it was published more clearly, more fully, and more truly.

What, I ask you, what was published through these faithful witnesses, whom you wish to be more faithful than God? Was it that through the African traitors the seed of Abraham, which is Christ, was not permitted to reach all the nations, and dried up there where it had arrived? Say now that your colleagues are to be believed more than the testament of God, and while you say these things, boast that you preserved that very testament from the flames—the testament you are trying to blot out with your tongue.

## The Donatists shamelessly say: We alone are Christians.

**2. 3.** But let each one choose what he pleases, and if against the lightning bolts of heaven the smoke of an earthly lie has any power, let it vanish into the winds, leaving heaven behind. For if Parmenian were not currying favor with his own chair, he would rather choose to believe the Scripture of God than his colleagues. For God says to Jacob, *I will not leave you until I have done what I have spoken to you*[^6].

But it is surely far more credible about these men—since they had already been disapproved by a just judgment—that they were not admitted to share communion in those regions where God was already fulfilling what he had promised to our fathers, and that for this reason they spread such tales about the holy priests of God, by whom they had not deserved to be received, in order to stir up the weak spirits of the peoples they had deceived with false rumors, and to tear away from the peace of the whole world the rashly credulous minds ensnared by the pride of their name. What more insane thing is found than this folly, or rather this madness?

Among so many nations of the whole world God has in great part fulfilled, and still fulfills—until it comes to absolutely all—what he promised, he who said, *I will not leave you until I have done what I have spoken to you*; and yet these men already believe those who announce that what God promised is not being fulfilled, and that therefore from the regions of the earth in which it had already been fulfilled the seed of Abraham, which is Christ, has perished, and the promises of God have been made void, because they themselves were not admitted to the communion of those among whom the world already held this fulfilled. And it is not said to them: *God alone is true, but every man a liar*[^7].

You say these things out of your own resources, because *whoever speaks a lie speaks from his own*[^8]. You therefore lie like men, because like men you grow angry. This is not said to them; instead, they are even believed when they say that throughout the world, which Christ had already begun to possess, Christ has perished. And those who believe these things, while shamelessly saying, We are Christians, dare to say, We alone are.

## Parmenian does not prove that the world was made criminal.

**3. 4.** Parmenian also says that it is proved from this that the whole world was made criminal by the offenses of handing over the Scriptures and of other acts of sacrilege, because, although many such things were committed in the time of persecution, no subsequent separation of peoples occurred in those provinces. As if it could not have happened that some of the wicked lay hidden and were not accused, so that they could be condemned without any rashness; while others were also exposed and condemned, and, because they were convicted by manifest facts, ceased from disturbing and dividing the Churches;

and so it came about that, since some were not accused, and certain uncertain crimes were left to God for the sake of a sure peace, while others were so made manifest and convicted that no condemned man could deceive the peoples with a feigned innocence, the bond of unity was in no way broken.

For not even in Africa would this monstrous evil of cutting off have happened, had not a faction inventing falsehoods prevailed more than reason truly convicting.

## Caecilian is condemned in his absence on the basis of crimes merely alleged.

**3. 5.** Let those who wish read what Optatus, bishop of Milevis of venerable memory, of the catholic communion, relates and by what documents he establishes so many things—whether concerning Lucilla, then a most wealthy and most factious woman, whom Saint Caecilian, while still a deacon, had offended for the sake of the Church's discipline,

or concerning the other associates of her faction—whether the thieves of the Church's silver, or those grieving that they had not attained the episcopate and harassing with whatever intrigues they could Caecilian, who had been preferred to them—or concerning the Numidian bishops, whom this faction had convened for the ruin of Caecilian, so that, with him deposed, another might be ordained for them.

These men, coming with their primate at that time, Secundus of Tigisis, and others, to whom Secundus himself had pardoned the confessed crimes of handing over the Scriptures, as if consulting for peace—as the ecclesiastical records testify—judged Caecilian, who was absent, to be a traitor, without any delay for a more careful inquiry,

with no opportunity for a reply preserved—so that, namely, crimes of handing over the Scriptures merely alleged should weigh against him in his absence, while to themselves, present, they pardoned crimes that had been confessed.

Thus against the man seated in the chair, with whom the whole Christian world in lands across the sea and far removed, and among the Africans themselves the more weighty Churches and those stronger against deceptions of this kind, held communion, they ordained another bishop, so that they might be able by their own deed to contradict the promises of God—that all the nations should not be blessed in the seed of Abraham—and might call even those parts of the world defiled by the African traitors which had neither heard the name of Caecilian, or at any rate had heard, as of an innocent man, how his life stood, the life of one whom even these men convicted of no crime.

Moreover, against the whole world saying, What you charge against your fellow citizens I could neither know nor ought I to have condemned what was unknown, they bring forward the testimony of the Apostle saying, *Not only those who do these things, but also those who consent to those who do them*[^9]. For it was too little for them to condemn so many and so great Christian peoples unheard, unless they also dared to bring out, against the Apostle himself, his very words, but not his meaning.

For if this is to consent to evildoers—to be with them in the Church—then the Apostle himself also consented to false brothers[^10], among whom he testified that he was in danger, and whom, preaching the Gospel not chastely but through envy and without love, he nevertheless permits to preach[^11].

But if to consent to evildoers is nothing other than to approve and praise their evil deeds, then by the Apostle's example the whole world would not consent to the crimes of the Africans, even if it tolerated them, once known, for the sake of the Church's peace; and yet these men do not prove that the world knew of them, even if they had been able to prove that they were true.

## We must believe the promises of God more than the words of the Donatists.

**4. 6.** In vain, therefore, does Parmenian say that the traitors condemned in Africa were received into fellowship of holiness by the provinces across the sea. For this is what we ought not to believe, lest by a sacrilegious rashness we condemn the whole world, founded in the unity of Christ, on the basis of their false accusation, rather than love it on the basis of the true promise of God. For which is more to be believed: what God said, *In your seed all the nations shall be blessed*[^12], or what these men say, 'In the seed of the African traitors all the nations are cursed'—so that what iniquity committed should prevail more than what truth promised?

Why, then, do we not rather believe that those who were received into communion by the provinces across the sea were innocent and could not be overborne by slanderers—which can be more probably established by many documents—or at any rate that, since they could not be convicted, even if they were wicked, they were believed innocent and were received without any contamination of those who received them?

And what of this: that even if, through wicked brothers—such as the Apostle tolerated in the unity of the Church, such as the martyr Cyprian laments in his letter *On the Lapsed*[^13]—if, then, through such brothers, set even among the number of the judges, something had been done with some wickedness, so that the traitors could not be excluded and convicted, and the Christian world were deceived by their feigned innocence, the world would in no way have lost its own innocence?

## What he said about Hosius of Cordova must be proved.

**4. 7.** As for what he said about Hosius, once a catholic bishop of Cordova, they must be required to prove not only that he was such as they say, but that his being such was manifest to those with whom they assert he held communion. For unless they prove this, they say in vain that they knew what sort of man he was, because he could not harm those who did not know it—those innocent ones from whom these men, by separating themselves, cannot, on account of the sacrilegious iniquity of the separation itself, be innocent.

For this is more credible—if indeed Hosius, condemned by the Spaniards, was absolved by the Gauls—that it could have happened that the Spaniards, surrounded by false accusations and deceived by the cunning fraud of intrigues, passed sentence against an innocent man, and afterward peacefully, in Christian humility, yielded to the sentence of their colleagues, by whom his innocence had been proved,

lest by an obstinate and headstrong perversity, in defending their earlier sentences, they should rush, in the blindness of impiety, into the sacrilege of schism, which surpasses all crimes—as these wretched men did, who, so many times divided and cut apart, do not even belatedly perceive what they have done.

## How the Donatists became incurable.

**4. 8.** And so they sufficiently declare how they became incurable: namely, lest they should be compelled to condemn their earlier sentences, which they rashly passed against the absent Caecilian, if in the trial across the sea—where they were overcome by Caecilian present—they had acquiesced through consideration of truth and peace; whereas they would have carried off a greater victory had they overcome human contentiousness at least after the trial than if they had overcome the man in the trial itself. For that victory is more exalted and fuller in a loftier triumph than if it were won not over a single conquered man but over an entire subjugated city, as Scripture says, *Better is he who conquers anger than he who takes a city*[^14].

Those men, then, desired to conquer a man, while anger was conquering them; and because they could not conquer the man, they were overcome both by the man and by anger—by the man, because they were defeated in the trial; by anger, because not even when defeated did they rest, hearing or reading with a most depraved heart the saying of the Apostle, *For if I build again these same things that I destroyed, I prove myself a transgressor*[^15]. If indeed the Apostle himself had understood these words with so warped a mind, he would have become neither a Christian nor an apostle, nor would he as a preacher have built up the Churches that as a persecutor he had earlier been destroying.

Nowhere, then, did these men so openly reveal why even when overcome they refused to be corrected, as when they detest the Spaniards because, after their own sentences had been pronounced otherwise, they yielded to the later examination and judgment of their colleagues. For just as this was done out of Christian gentleness, so that was done out of diabolical contentiousness; and therefore it is no wonder that by this humility peace was preserved, by that haughtiness it was torn apart.

Accordingly, what they did is rightly repaid to them; for they taught such things to their descendants. For the Maximianists too, for no other reason, refused to yield to the council of their three hundred and ten colleagues, by whom Primian—whom they themselves had first condemned—was judged innocent, except by setting, with abandoned obstinacy, the apostolic sentence against the ignorant and saying, *For if I build again these same things that I destroyed, I prove myself a transgressor*. For a hundred colleagues had destroyed Primian, and for that reason they refused, together with the three hundred, to build him up again. Thus, while they pretend to take care not to build up a man whom they had destroyed, they destroyed themselves in another schism by a greater sacrilege.

## If Parmenian were living, he would not dare to reproach.

**4. 9.** But still, if Parmenian were living, he would no longer dare to reproach the Spaniards and call them transgressors because they bent their own sentence to the sentence of their colleagues, lest he himself offend his own colleagues, many of whom, after Primian was condemned by him, changed for the better and crossed over to the council of the three hundred, choosing rather to go against their own overhasty sentences than against the peace of unity, even within the party of Donatus. And Parmenian would above all spare Praetextatus of Assuras and Felician of Musti, who, even when condemned by their three hundred and ten fellow bishops, returned, out of zeal for concord, to those very men by whom they had been condemned, and by their own condemners, with the same zeal, were received for the sake of peace without any loss of their rank; nor did anyone think that those who had been baptized by them outside, in schism, should be rebaptized.

Or perhaps, because those who correct their own sentences greatly displease Parmenian, and, not understanding the Apostle, he calls them transgressors,

he would detest these men because they chose to return inside rather than remain outside, and, with some like-minded associates joined to him, he would even create Parmenianists, just as many fragments have already been made throughout all Africa from that great fragment?

So, so it must needs be: cut and chopped bit by bit, they perish utterly, who preferred the swelling of their own contentiousness to the most holy bond of catholic peace. For the things Parmenian charges are not so much to be feared as the things he confesses are to be observed.

## The Donatists accuse other lands with blind custom and frenzy.

**5. 10.** For when he said that through Hosius the Spaniard help was furnished to Caecilian, so that the company of the holy and undefiled was forced into communion with them, and that the unimpaired faith of the servants of God resisted this impiety, he confessed of his own accord that his own people had also approached Constantine, and that by Constantine's decision the case was examined by bishops as judges, over whom Miltiades, bishop of the city of Rome, presided. In this trial, since these men were defeated and Caecilian was found innocent—as the ecclesiastical records testify—they accuse this same Miltiades of the crime of handing over the Scriptures.

And I ask when they learned of this. For if before the trial, they ought not to have prejudged their own case by beginning to plead it before such a judge and, by the command of the emperor whom they themselves had approached, to undergo such a trial. But if they claim that they learned he was a traitor after the case was argued and the sentence pronounced, then men are mad to such a degree that, against the judges before whom they were defeated, they believe defeated litigants?

And yet in all these matters, though with great rashness they accuse the Italians and Gauls and Spaniards, they leave aside so many other provinces and nations from which by an unspeakable sacrilege they are separated—nations whom the crimes of the Italians, Spaniards, and Gauls could surely not harm, even if they were truly told. For they now arraign the other lands with blind custom, or rather frenzy, and are angry at them because, since there were two parties in Africa—one, they say, of traitors and the other of innocent men—they chose to join to themselves the traitors rather than the innocent.

This empty accusation is refuted most briefly and most truly thus: that when they had heard there were two parties in Africa, one of traitors, the other of innocent men, they believed that one to be innocent which had won its case before its neighboring ecclesiastical judges. Wherefore those always remained innocent who, not knowing how these things had been done in Africa, nevertheless believed what they ought peacefully and reverently to have believed—and from those innocent men separation could in no way be innocent.

## The bishops and judges met at the town of Arles.

**6. 11.** Parmenian also admits that at the town of Arles both bishops as judges and the parties from Africa met—namely, Caecilian and the Donatists. There he entrusted everything to his own people, who, being defeated, could do nothing but complain about the judges; nor does he deny, again, that they came to Constantine and, because there too they were overcome in a final judgment, he charges that Constantine himself was corrupted by a bribe.

When all these things are considered, let one who judges without partisan zeal choose whom to believe—whether the judges pronouncing their sentences or the litigants against whom the sentences were pronounced, who refused to end the suit. And the whole world certainly believed the judges. But those who agree with the Donatists and defend them confess that they believe men who, whatever their case may have been, nevertheless could not win it in so many disputes across the sea, and they take up with credulous emptiness their murmurs and charges against the judges.

In this matter, if they call themselves innocent and are unwilling rashly to believe that those whom they see defeated were defeated by the truth, how much more innocent are those who are unwilling rashly to believe anything evil about the judges themselves—about whom those who are defeated must of necessity complain! For not only does one who has lost a good case complain of an unjust or slow or negligent judge, but even one who has been most justly defeated murmurs against the innocent judge with the same blindness with which he was litigating against an innocent adversary.

Wherefore these men are criminal, not because they are unwilling rashly to believe anything about defeated men, but because by the frenzy of schism they have cut themselves off from those innocent men who, far more reasonably, are unwilling to believe any such thing about the judges themselves.

## A personification of the Church of Philadelphia.

**7. 12.** For let there come forward, in the name of Christ, some most noble Church of those parts, from among the seven, and, if it please, especially Philadelphia[^16], which by its mystical name in the Greek tongue signifies brotherly love. Let us therefore hear her voice—and let not her chaff speak, but her wheat.

If, then, she should say to these men: 'Why do you arraign me, brothers, why do you accuse me? At what distance, in respect of earthly place, I am removed from Africa, you have been able partly to know, partly to hear. What the traitors there did then, or the accusers or condemners of the traitors, or the slanderers or oppressors of the innocent, I am wholly ignorant.

But that Lord of ours, who bought the whole world at the price of his blood, whose holy purchase the prophet sang so long beforehand, saying, *They have pierced my hands and my feet, they have numbered all my bones. But they themselves have considered and gazed upon me; they divided my garments among them, and over my garment they cast lots*[^17], did not set between you and us spaces empty and bare of Christians; he filled all these with the sanctification of his name. For in that very Psalm of the Passion it was declared not only how many he bought, but also how much he bought. For there it is said a little later, *All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the Lord, and all the families of the nations shall worship in his sight, for the kingdom is his, and he shall rule over the nations*[^18]. About your case, then, I might perhaps be compelled to judge if I were either near, or so remote that between us and you no other Christians dwelt, sealed with the same name, made peaceful in the same unity.

Many families of the nations lie between, bought together with me by his blood, who in his sight worship together with me. Through them the report about you reached me; they, from their nearness, were able to examine your case. But if this was not done, you neglected it; for had you not been neglected by the rest, you would not have come even all the way to us. But if anything was judged, grant pardon: I do not dare rashly to believe you who were defeated, and to condemn your judges with the same rashness. There is added another thing that moves me greatly.

For if you, being innocent, had been oppressed, you would at least love us as brothers who did you no harm; but when you nevertheless try to tear us apart with curses and to pursue us with bitter hatreds—us who are well aware that your case was committed to your neighbors by the right order of the Church, and who know before God how they judged—and even try to rebaptize us, as though for your sake Christ had lost his inheritance among us, what good can we be able to think of that case of yours?

For you who do not in the least hesitate to condemn by rash suspicions brothers most far removed, show how justly you were condemned by your neighbors. For why should I not believe that one could rightly be condemned by a neighboring judge after a hearing, who does not hesitate to condemn me, a brother so far absent and unheard, charging against me this greatest crime—that I, who could not be present, believed the judges to whom the case was brought rather than the defeated litigants? And if I had not rather believed those judges, then even if those who were defeated were innocent, I myself could not be innocent.

For we should be entangled in a great offense if, since we could not discern human hearts, nor held to ecclesiastical discipline, we were unwilling to believe those judging—beyond whom the case could not pass, and through whom the report could run all the way to us. Tearing yourself away from these innocent men by an impious rending, do you call yourself innocent? Yet surely, if you were, when you saw in the holy Scriptures that the harvest of your Lord cannot, before the final separation and winnowing, be separated from the weeds and the chaff, you would choose rather to be strong in enduring the wicked than impious in deserting the good.'

For how many of the Churches far removed throughout the world do we think these are, and how most just are the words that I have represented in Philadelphia!

## Whoever suffers at the emperor's hands is not on that account at once a martyr.

**8. 13.** Indeed Parmenian even dares to complain that Constantine ordered those men to be led to the field, that is, to execution, who, defeated before ecclesiastical judges, could not even before him prove what they were saying, and were still being carried by a sacrilegious frenzy into rendings of the holy Church; and he charges that Constantine ordered this monstrously, at the suggestion of Hosius the Spaniard—condemning the unheard, as always, by his own suspicions.

As though, more humanely and more probably, another might believe that it was rather done at the suggestion of Hosius, as a bishop, that the emperor should bend his sentence toward a milder coercion of so monstrous a crime—that is, of sacrilegious schism. For what do these men not justly suffer, when they suffer by the most exalted judgment of God, who presides and admonishes by such scourges to beware the eternal fire, suffering both by the merit of their crimes and by the due order of the powers?

For let them first prove that they are not heretics or schismatics; only then let them raise a free voice about their unworthy punishments; only then, when they suffer something of the kind, let them dare to call themselves martyrs of the truth.

Otherwise, if whoever pays a penalty at the hands of the emperor or of the judges sent by him is at once a martyr, then all the prisons are full of martyrs, all the judicial chains drag martyrs, in all the mines there are toiling martyrs, to all the islands martyrs are deported, in all the places of punishment martyrs are struck by the magistrate's sword, all martyrs are exposed to the beasts, or by the orders of judges are burned alive in the flames. But it is rather as the Apostle says: *There is no power except from God, and the minister of God is an avenger in wrath against him who does evil; he does not bear the sword without cause. Do you wish not to fear the power? Do good, and you will have praise from it*[^19].

For if any good man suffers, praise comes to him from the power that acts; but let the wicked man not impute to the cruelty of the power what he deservedly suffers for his iniquity.

## The Donatists do not suffer anything like what they do.

**8. 14.** And yet what do these men suffer like what they do, except that the multitude of men keeps its heart not in the heart but in the eyes? For if blood flows from mortal flesh, whoever beholds it shudders; but if souls cut off and separated from the peace of Christ die in the sacrilege of heresy or schism, then, because it is not seen, it is not lamented—nay rather, a death fouler and more grievous and, to speak plainly, truer is, by the right of custom, even laughed at, while the authors of such great deaths publicly insult us and do not even deign, for the sake of making the truth manifest, to hold a conversation with us.

And if they suffer any temporal hardship through the most sure and most upright order of the powers—while they themselves, by the private bands of their madmen, everywhere and daily commit far graver things by no royal, no ecclesiastical law—they call us persecutors of bodies, and do not call themselves slayers of souls, although by their private license they do not spare even bodies. But because, through Christian gentleness, an eye torn out in a brawl is avenged far more severely than a soul blinded in schism, they speak out, and speak against us, and do not speak with us; and when truth compels them to fall silent, iniquity does not permit them to be silent.

## Otherwise even persecuted demons could be called martyrs.

**9. 15.** Or is it perhaps not lawful concerning religion that the emperor, or those whom the emperor has sent, should judge? Why then did your envoys come to the emperor? Why did they make him judge of their case, if they were not going to follow what he judged? But to what end are these things said? Even granting that they make good their claim that it does not pertain to the emperor to decree anything against those who follow what is depraved in religion, will they on that account, if he does so and punishes them, be martyrs?

For in this way this plea will lie open to all heretics, against whom, by the hidden command of God through the manifest command of men, many measures have been most severely established by which they might be coerced—and not only to heretics whitewashed in any way at least with the Christian name, but even to the pagans themselves.

For surely the pagans too are impious in a false religion, whose images recent laws have ordered to be overturned and shattered, and whose sacrifices are forbidden under pain of death. If, then, any of them has been condemned in such a crime, is he to be held a martyr because he pays the penalty under the laws for a superstition that he thought a pious religion? Surely no Christian in any way dares to say this.

Not everyone, then, who has been punished by the emperor in some question of religion is made a martyr. For those who think such things do not see that they advance to the point where they would have to maintain that even the demons themselves could claim for themselves the glory of martyrs, because they suffer this persecution through Christian emperors—so that throughout almost the whole world their temples are overturned, their idols broken in pieces, their sacrifices withdrawn, and those who honor them, if they are caught, punished.

But if it is utterly mad to say this, then it is not from the suffering that the justice is certain, but from the justice that the suffering is glorious. And therefore the Lord, lest anyone in this matter should spread mists before the ignorant and, in the condemnation of his own deserts, seek the praise of martyrs, did not say generally, 'Blessed are those who suffer persecution,' but added a great distinction by which piety is truly set apart from sacrilege. For he said, *Blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of justice*[^20].

But in no way do they suffer for the sake of justice who have divided the Church of Christ and who, while with a feigned justice they as it were attempt to separate her before the time from the chaff, and pursue her wheat with false accusations, have themselves rather been separated from her, like the very lightest chaff, by the various gusts of rumors. 'But,' they say, 'we did not do this.' Let them see, then, that they must first free themselves from this question, and only then dare, if they suffer any hardships or punishments at the hands of Christian emperors, either to lay them down in complaint or to take them up for glory. On this matter, that is, the question of the schism itself, if I said nothing else, what has been said above would suffice.

## It does not belong to emperors to punish schisms.

**10. 16.** Or will they perhaps say that, even if they are convicted in a sacrilegious dissension, so that for that madness they are no martyrs if they suffer anything, nevertheless the coercing or punishing of these things ought not to belong to the power of emperors? On this matter I ask what they say: is it that powers of this kind ought to take no care about a vicious or false religion? But we have already said much also about the pagans, and about the demons themselves, that they suffer persecutions from the emperors. Or does this too displease them? Why then do they themselves overturn temples where they can, and not cease, through the frenzies of the Circumcellions, to do or to avenge such things? Is private violence more just than royal diligence? But I pass these things over.

This I ask: since the Apostle enumerates the manifest works of the flesh—*which are*, he says, *fornication, uncleanness, licentiousness, idolatry, sorcery, enmities, strifes, jealousies, animosities, dissensions, heresies, envyings, drunkenness, carousings, and the like*[^21]—what do these men think? Do they hold that the crime of idolatry is justly avenged by emperors? Or, if they will not have even this, why do they admit that the rigor of the laws is justly exerted against sorcerers, but are unwilling to admit it against heretics and impious dissensions, when these are reckoned by apostolic authority among the same fruits of iniquity? Or do they perhaps not permit these powers of human institution to take care even of such things?

For what purpose, then, does he *bear the sword*, who was called *the minister of God, an avenger in wrath* against those who do evil[^22]? Unless perhaps, as some of them—truly most ignorant—are accustomed to understand this, it was said of ecclesiastical honors, so that the sword is understood as the spiritual punishment that works excommunication; though the most provident Apostle, by the connection of the passage that follows, sufficiently makes clear what he is speaking of. For there he added, *For on account of this you also pay tribute*, and then went on, *Render to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due, custom to whom custom, honor to whom honor, fear to whom fear*[^23].

It remains, then, that by these disputations of theirs they should forbid Christians to pay tribute—when even the Lord, to the Pharisees who thought such things (whom these men imitate), having looked at the coin, replied, *Render to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's*[^24].

But these men, disobedient and impious in both, render neither Christian love to God nor human fear to kings—so blind and insane that, although they themselves, through powers sent by the catholic emperors, shut their own schismatics the Maximianists out of the basilicas and, by the great force of orders and reinforcements, compelled them to yield, yet they arraign the catholic Church if for her sake catholic princes have commanded any such thing to be done. But let the Maximianists themselves, before they were Maximianists—that is, while they were still held together in one common body of the Donatists—recall what cruel and bitter things that Rogatus the Moor suffered at their hands through the barbarian king Firmus, and let them be silent and not dare to complain if they suffer anything of the kind, whether from the Primianists on account of their own schism, or together with the Primianists on account of the schism of the Donatists—not for a holy religion, but for a sacrilegious contentiousness.

## The things the Donatists do are graver than what they suffer.

**11. 17.** For perhaps they say that they have suffered graver things from the catholic emperors than they themselves did to the Rogatists through the kings of the barbarians, or to the Maximianists through the judges of the catholic emperors, or even now do, through the frenzy of the Circumcellions, to whomever they can.

As though the question were whether they suffer graver things than they do—which I would by no means concede. For many of their most savage and most bitter deeds are recounted, or rather cannot be recounted; and if these were fewer, or afflicted those against whom they are committed less, they would for that very reason certainly be graver, because they are not commanded by ordained powers but committed in lawless frenzies.

For the things they did against the Maximianists through judges of human institution are not so many. Into that class of actions let them put, if they will, what was also done by them, in persecuting Rogatus the Moor, through the barbarian Firmus, and let them reckon that man—though a most monstrous enemy of the Romans—among the lawful powers.

But these things are not so many as the many things they daily commit, through the raging bands of drunken young men over whom they set leaders—men who at first armed themselves only with clubs, but now have begun to arm themselves even with iron, who wander and rage throughout all Africa under the notorious name of Circumcellions, and act against all order of laws and powers.

When the crimes of these men are reported to them, they pretend to be ignorant of such a kind of men, or with a most shameless mouth they affirm that such men have nothing whatever to do with them—contrary to what all men know. Nor do they accept even this voice of the whole world, which says, far more probably and truly, that it does not know what was done in Africa, whether by the party of Donatus or against the party of Donatus, if in Africa itself it is permitted to the Donatist bishops either to be ignorant of the deeds of the Donatist Circumcellions or to say that they have nothing to do with them.

## It is permitted to coerce heretics and schismatics.

**11. 18.** But, as I had begun to say, the question is not in this way—whether they suffer graver things than they do—but whether it is lawful that any such thing be done against heretics and schismatics. For if they say it is not lawful, why do they themselves do it? But if they admit it is lawful, then even if they should show—what they can in no way do—that they suffer graver things from the catholic emperors than they themselves did to their own schismatics through those emperors' judges or through the kings of the barbarians, or than they do to every kind of men through the madness of the Circumcellions, neither is this to be wondered at, if princes can do more than the judges sent by princes, if Roman emperors can do more than barbarian kings, and if a robber deservedly suffers graver things by the laws than he himself commits against the laws. Hence the henchmen of the Circumcellions deservedly suffer, by just decrees, graver things than the Circumcellions do. And yet so great is Christian gentleness that their crimes incomparably surpass their punishments.

But behold, three hundred and ten Donatist bishops condemned the Maximianists in their council, while those men, by the obstinacy of their perversity, refused to yield the basilicas. The judges were approached; their council was entered into the proconsular records. Then it was ordered that those who had been condemned by so great a number of bishops should yield up the places. Those who yielded easily did not suffer much; but as for those who attempted to resist, who does not know how they were afflicted?

But still, if the monstrousness of those resisting had been so great that it reached the point of injuries to the judges, would they not have suffered far more bitter things by the Roman laws? So too at that time, when, after the conclusion of the case in which these men cut themselves off from the catholic Church, it consequently began to be pressed that they should not hold the basilicas, and they did hold them by resisting the imperial orders—and resisting in such a way that the notorious force of the Circumcellions prevailed, adding besides that they harassed with most turbulent and most savage seditions those whom the emperor had sent with gifts of the Church as they went through Africa—such laws were issued against them that they were not even allowed to retain those basilicas which did not belong to the unity but had been built by men separated and already established in their schism.

In this matter the royal power has now avenged its own injuries. For what avails it that the enemies of justice should justly possess anything?

## Several severe laws have been promulgated against them.

**12. 19.** Nor is anyone found to have promulgated anything on their behalf except the apostate Julian, to whom Christian peace and unity were exceedingly displeasing, since the very religion from which he had impiously fallen displeased him. And these Donatists, as the records of the judges testify—to which they appealed for what they had obtained—supplicated him in such words that perhaps some who consented to the worship of idols out of fear were gentler toward it than these men were in praising him out of frenzy. For they said that with him justice alone had a place.

What else, then, are they found to have said but either that Christian holiness is not justice—which had no place with him—or that the honoring of demons is justice, which had first place with him? But as for how vehement the laws of other emperors that were enacted against them, who does not know? Among them, one general law against all who wish to be called Christians and do not commune with the catholic Church, but gather in their own separated conventicles, contains this: that either the one who ordains a cleric or the one ordained should be fined ten pounds of gold, and the very place where the impious separation gathers should be confiscated to the treasury.

There are also other general decrees by which the faculty of making wills, or of conferring anything by donations, or of receiving anything from donations or wills, is taken away from them.

For in a certain case, when a man of nobility had supplicated the emperors—because his sister, who had belonged to the party of Donatus, had at her death conferred a great deal upon certain persons of their communion, and especially upon a certain Augustine, a bishop of theirs—it was commanded, on the basis of that general law, that everything be restored to the brother; and there too mention was made of the Circumcellions, in case they should, in their usual manner, violently oppose, by what kind of reinforcements and supports they were to be repelled.

For so well known are they, so proven in many battles, that about them neither the emperor's suppliant nor the emperor himself could keep silent.

## But great is Christian gentleness toward them.

**13. 20.** Since these things are so—since they are condemned by both divine and human laws—nevertheless so great is Christian gentleness that not only do they hold the basilicas that they built once they were already cut off, but the unity has not even taken back all those that it held from the beginning. And although they themselves urgently shut the Maximianists out of the basilicas belonging to the party of Donatus, through judges sent by the catholic emperors, yet from many places that the catholic unity formerly held they are not shut out even by the laws of those same catholic emperors.

Finally, if anything has perhaps at some time been done against them too immoderately, so as to exceed Christian leniency, it displeases all the wheat of the Lord's harvest—that is, the Christians praiseworthy in Christ—who, whether with hundredfold or sixtyfold or thirtyfold fruit[^25], grow up in the catholic Church throughout the whole world.

## Let them accuse the chaff in the Lord's harvest along with us, and tolerate it along with us.

**14. 21.** Wherefore let them most copiously accuse, along with us, the weeds or chaff of the catholic crop, but let them not refuse most patiently to bear them along with us. For he who was unwilling to root out the weeds before the time and to separate them from the mingling with the wheat said, *Let both grow until the harvest*[^26]. And when he explained the wrapping of that same parable to the disciples who asked, he did not say, 'The field is Africa,' but he said, *The field is this world*[^27]. Throughout the whole world, then, this crop was sown; throughout the whole, weeds were sown over it; throughout the whole, both grow until the harvest.

Was Donatus the chief of the reapers, or had the time of harvest come at the time when these men separated themselves from the world, when the Lord himself, lest anyone be permitted to interpret as he wished, said most plainly, *The harvest is the end of the age, and the reapers are the angels*[^28]? For such reapers cannot err, so as either to gather the wheat for weeds or to consign the weeds for wheat.

But these men, while they flee as it were the weeds, have shown that they themselves are the weeds, preaching in a most manifest sacrilege against the sentence of the Lord; so that, when he says, *Let both grow until the harvest*, they maintain that throughout so great a field, that is, the whole world, only weeds grow, but that the wheat has been diminished and remained in Africa alone, doing a sacrilegious injury to Christ our king and prince. For it is written, *In a numerous people is the glory of a king, but in the diminution of the people is the ruin of the prince*[^29].

But now it is time, as I judge, to attend diligently to the very testimonies of the Scriptures which, by misinterpreting them, they use to deceive the ignorant, and—as far as the Lord grants—to open them up according to the understanding of catholic truth.
