# Book II

**2. 1. 1.** I have received your reply, Gaudentius—if indeed that is to be called a reply which you wished to send me back for this reason, lest, had you been silent, we should say you were convicted. But to reply is not the same as not to be silent. For if it were, you have plainly replied; but [you have done so] in such a way that even those who could hope something of you may know that you found nothing you ought to reply, and yet replied so as not to be silent. And so, while you took care not to be called conquered, you brought it about that you were shown to be so. To show this, your very writings suffice, if they are read by intelligent people and compared by diligent examination with mine. But to teach this by other writings, by which satisfaction may be given even to slower wits, a somewhat more lengthy disputation is necessary: which indeed I will undertake, if there is need and the Lord wills.

**2. 2. 2.** For now, in the meantime, since you tried to affirm by the testimony of blessed Cyprian that you are rather the Catholics: attend a little to what Church he called Catholic, when he was defending its unity. "The Church," he says, "flooded with the light of the Lord, stretches out its rays through the whole world: yet there is one light that is diffused everywhere, and the unity of the body is not separated. With abundance of fruitfulness it extends its branches over all the earth, it spreads more widely its streams flowing copiously: yet there is one head, and one origin, and one mother, abounding in the successions of fruitfulness." Why, then, do you both deceive yourselves, and wish to deceive others by shameless lies? If, by the testimony of this martyr, your Church is Catholic, show that it stretches out its rays through the whole world; show that it extends its branches with abundance of fruitfulness over all the earth. For from this it is also named by the Greek word "Catholic." For what in Greek is called *holon*, in Latin is rendered "whole" or "universal." Through the whole, then, or according to the whole, is *kath'holon*, whence it is called "Catholic." If you know this, why do you pretend not to know it? But if you do not know it, why, before you speak what you do not know, do you not question those who do know? But if this displeases you, find some language—not Greek—in which you may teach that *kath'holon* does not signify "through the whole," or "according to the whole," or "according to the universal": and withdraw from the testimony of Cyprian. For he speaks against you, you who see what he says. He says that the Catholic [Church] is stretched out and extended through the whole world and over all the earth, agreeing with its Greek name and definition. You are found to hold, think, and say something else; and while you lean on Cyprian as witness, by Cyprian as witness you lie.

**2. 3. 3.** From this true and genuine Catholic [Church], then, which, flooded with the light of the Lord, stretches out its rays through the whole world, extends its branches with abundance of fruitfulness over all the earth, what cause you had for going out, when you are questioned, you find nothing just: utterly, by no cleansing of a plausible excuse do you wash away your departure from this Church. For what do you say, except: "Necessity compelled us, that we, the just, should leave the unjust"? The divine Scripture answers you: *The evil son calls himself just; but he does not wash away his departure* [^1]. *His departure,* it says—surely that of which the apostle John speaks: *They went out from us* [^2]—he utterly *does not wash away*, does not defend, does not excuse, does not cleanse. For it does not pertain to the just in the Catholic Church to do anything but most patiently tolerate the bad whom they cannot correct or condemn; and not, on account of the weeds, to go out from the Lord's field [^3], nor, on account of the chaff, from the Lord's threshing floor [^4], nor, on account of the dishonored vessels, from the Lord's house [^5], nor, on account of the bad fish, from the Lord's nets [^6], before the time; lest in vain they try to wash away their departure. If you wish, by any argument whatsoever, to twist these gospel sentences into another meaning, you contradict that same blessed Cyprian, whose testimonies you employ: as you did in our Conference. For these are the words of the aforesaid martyr on this question, in the letter which he wrote to Maximus and the companions of his confession: "Although," he says, "weeds seem to be in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we perceive weeds to be in the Church, we should ourselves withdraw from the Church. We must only labor that we may be able to be wheat: so that, when the wheat begins to be stored in the Lord's barns, we may receive fruit for our work and labor. The Apostle says in his letter: *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and earthenware; and some indeed honored, some dishonored* [^7]. Let us give our effort, and labor as much as we can, that we may be a vessel of gold or silver. But to break the earthenware vessels is granted to the Lord alone, to whom also the rod of iron is given [^8]. The servant cannot be greater than his lord [^9]: nor let anyone claim for himself what the Father granted to the Son alone—so as to think he can already bear the shovel or the winnowing fan to winnow and cleanse the threshing floor, or by human judgment separate all the weeds from the wheat. Proud is that obstinacy and sacrilegious that presumption which a depraved frenzy takes upon itself: and while certain people always claim for themselves more than gentle justice demands, they perish from the Church; and while they insolently exalt themselves, blinded by their own swelling, they lose the light of truth."

**2. 4. 4.** Surely now at least you see that against holy Cyprian you cried out in our Conference, and resisted him with contentions, by which you asserted that the field of Christ, of which he says, *The field is the world* [^10], is not the Church, but the world apart from the Church, so that the things which were perceived might have weeds? For you said that in the Church manifest weeds could not be. How often did we set down this testimony of Cyprian, and you neither dared openly to resist him, nor yet were willing to consent? Surely now at least you awake, you hear, you take note, "that although weeds seem to be in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we perceive weeds to be in the Church, we should ourselves withdraw from the Church." Why, then, do you depart from the unity of this Church by a nefarious schism, and persist by heretical presumption in this same rending? See, you have Cyprian: consent to him, or answer him. Do you see how, by these words, in which he says that weeds are and are perceived in the Church, and yet that it ought not therefore to be departed from, he blots out all the slanders of your writings? By which you also advanced into so great precipices, and, after your manner, cast yourself headlong, so as to say that even those who did not know who had sinned, throughout the whole world, could perish through others' sins—Christians, namely, because it is read in the holy Scriptures that one stole from the anathema, and for this sin another people who did not know it was punished [^11]: not knowing that those punishments of mortal bodies, that is, the deaths of men who would die, availed for the useful terror of the people; but yet that others' sins, especially those they did not know, were no detriment to the future life of those same dead. Would you, then, say this; would you dare to believe and say that anyone perishes unto God through others' sins, even unknown ones; nor did you fear even your own colleagues, who, while you were silent in our Conference—and perhaps, when you already thought this, not daring to say it—fought through such great bouts of disputation that the bad fish in the Lord's nets are not known by the fishermen, lest, namely, they perish by their contagion, if they knew them? Did it not come into your mind, when we disputed about the chaff of the Lord's threshing floor—that is, the Church—to be tolerated in one mixture until the time of winnowing, that, when Emeritus was pressed, he denied and said: "You do not read 'threshing floor'"? And when he was both more privately admonished by his own people, and more openly by us recalling from the Gospel that the Lord would come bearing the winnowing fan in his hand, who would cleanse his threshing floor and store the wheat in the barn, but burn the chaff with unquenchable fire [^12], he straightway corrected the error of forgetfulness by which he had denied it was written; nor yet did he turn back from his heretical or schismatic perversity, by which he denied that the bad ought to be borne by the good for the unity of the Church: and straightway he said that by the name of chaff the hidden bad are signified, so that you might keep this with special diligence for your cause—that unknown bad people could defile no one of the good. See, the excellent patron of your party lost his labors, with you opposing. For he, in order to safeguard the salvation of the good, asserts that the bad remaining in the Church are utterly unknown, lest they destroy them if they were known and tolerated: but you said that the good perish even by the contagion of unknown bad people. Nor did you fear so many of your own, hidden from the beginning—wicked, criminal, and impious—who surely, according to your sentence, destroyed you and all yours, without your knowing it. But now too you trembled with no dread, lest perhaps some one of you should sin without your knowing it, and destroy you while you say these things. Or do you perhaps, since you understand that you have perished by your known deeds, for this reason not fear to perish by others' unknown ones?

**2. 5. 5.** What should I wish for you, except that it be granted us to find you, lest it please you to perish? For what hope will remain—not only for us, who agree with the Lord Christ, the Prophets, the Apostles, and holy Cyprian, that even known bad people, if we can neither correct nor punish them, must be borne for the bond of unity, but also for you, to whom, before the time of harvest, of winnowing, and of the shore, a bodily separation from the bad is pleasing—if your opinion is true, by which you think that "each one perishes both by his own sins, the one who commits them, and by others', the one who does not know whether they were committed"? For if this is so, surely even your forebears, who separated themselves, as you think, from known bad people, perished by unknown ones. But not only to you—who, when you say that a man perishes by others' crimes, whether he knows them or does not know them, without doubt allow no one to remain innocent—but also to Emeritus himself, who, holding a far more tolerable view, in the communion of the sacraments binds men only by known sins, and looses them from unknown ones, the venerable Cyprian answers with a far truer voice, that the wheat growing together does not perish, since the weeds are established not outside the Church but in the Church, and are not hidden and unknown, but plainly known and conspicuous. For I think that he is blinded not in the flesh but in the very mind, who contends that what is perceived lies hidden. But when that most blessed man admonishes that the Church not be departed from on account of the weeds in it, he does not admonish on account of those which are hidden, but rather on account of those which are perceived. For these are the ones that can disturb those who perceive them, unless wisdom makes them patient. For how should we learn that we ought not to depart on account of hidden things, which we do not know to exist at all? "Although," he says, "weeds seem to be in the Church." "They seem," he says—they are not believed by suspicion. And lest anyone think it was said "seem to be" as though they were not, but only seemed to be, he opens up by the following words what he said: "Yet neither our faith," he says, "nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we perceive weeds to be in the Church, we should ourselves withdraw from the Church." He does not say, "We suspect, we think, we believe, we suppose"; but "we perceive." For those too believed these things to be not covered but open, who said to the father of the household, *Do you wish us to go and gather them?*—concerning which it had been said, *When the blade had grown, and had borne fruit, then appeared the weeds* [^13]. To whom he answered: *No; lest perhaps, while you wish to gather the weeds, you root up the wheat as well*; and: *Let both grow until the harvest* [^14]. And yet you say (wherein you do nothing but contradict the Lord) that "weeds alone have grown throughout the world, and that throughout almost the whole world the wheat has been diminished and has perished": when to some nations the Church has not yet, by growing, arrived. But it is necessary that it arrive, and that the Gospel be preached in the whole world, and then the end come [^15]. That this will be, the Lord foretold without any ambiguity.

**2. 6. 6.** You see that the Church, by the testimony of Cyprian, is both called Catholic from the whole, and is not without manifest bad people, on account of whom, nevertheless, he admonishes that it not be abandoned. In it are the good, in themselves many: but in comparison with the weeds or the chaff, surely few. For it is not apart from it, but in it, that what the Lord himself says comes to pass: *Because iniquity has abounded, the love of many will grow cold* [^16]. But there too is the people diffused everywhere, to whom it is said: *He who perseveres to the end, he will be saved* [^17]. But those who have faith as a grain of mustard seed, by which even mountains may be moved [^18], are altogether very rare. For concerning such faith the Lord said: *Do you think the Son of man will come, and find faith on the earth?* [^19]—not *concerning the apostasy of the whole world*, as you most perversely understand it.

**2. 7. 7.** Further, concerning baptism, which you think exists only in the Church, and on this account reprove us because we judge that those coming from heresies, if they are already baptized, are not to be baptized again: it suffices that you could by no means answer how the condemned Felician was able to baptize, established outside your Church—whom you wished in vain to place among those to whom you gave a delay. Read the Bagai sentence of your council, in which, namely, after the many most bitter things that were said against them, and far heavier than against Caecilian when your forebears condemned him absent and innocent, their manifest and undoubted condemnation is thus appended: "Therefore know that, by the will of the presiding God, by the truthful mouth of the universal council, the men guilty of an infamous crime have been condemned—Victorianus of Carcabia, Martianus of Sullectum, Beianus of Baiae, Salvius of Ausafa, Theodorus of Usula, Donatus of Sabrata, Miggin of Elephantaria, Praetextatus of Assuras, Salvius of Membressa, Valerius of Melzi, Felician of Mustis, and Martialis of Pertusa, who by the deadly work of perdition cemented the filthy vessel with the dregs they had collected; and also the clerics, once of the Church of Carthage, who, while they took part in the crime, lent their pandering to the unlawful incest." And then a delay begins to be extended to the rest in these words: "But those whom the sacrilegious shoots did not pollute as plantings—that is, those who withdrew their own hands from the head of Maximianus by a modest shame of faith—we have permitted to return to Mother Church." Now concerning these, what had to be said has been said sufficiently in the earlier letters of mine sent to you. That you could utterly not answer this point, whoever reads and takes note a little will not be able to doubt.

**2. 8. 8.** In vain, then, does it seem to you that *the councils of Agrippinus and Cyprian are to be followed on this question*; since you yourselves neglected them, when, without any repetition of baptism, you received those baptized outside your communion by condemned men, against whom you were litigating to have them excluded from the basilicas. But concerning the opinion of Cyprian, or of his colleagues, to whom it then seemed good that those coming from heretics ought to be baptized, it would take long for me to dispute as it deserves. But solve me this brief question, if you can. When Cyprian, bishop of the Church of Carthage, rebaptized those coming from heretics, at that time Stephen, bishop of the Church of Rome, received heretics in the same baptism which they had received outside; and both, doing these different things, remained in Catholic unity. Tell me, whether at that time the Church—when, according to you, through Stephen and his innumerable colleagues throughout the world, who shared in the same opinion, it received men guilty of all crimes without baptism—had perished by the contagion of the bad, or had not perished? For you cannot say that these bad people were hidden: although you assert that even hidden ones harm and put to death. Whoever, then—a homicide, or even a parricide, an adulterer, an incestuous man, an idolater, finally a fearless traitor of the sacred books, or a cruel torturer that they might be handed over, and a violent extorter, and at the last, by command or by his own hand, a burner—being baptized among heretics, came to Stephen and his associates, was, according to you, received without baptism. You see, then, that all the crimes of men, if what you think about baptism is true, were then gathered in the Church without baptism? Answer whether Cyprian was stained by these crimes in that same unity; answer whether the Church perished, or did not perish. Choose what you will think. If it had already then perished, what gave birth to Donatus?  
But if, with so many people gathered into it without baptism, it could not perish, answer, please: what madness persuaded Donatus’s party to separate from it, as though avoiding communion with the wicked?

**2. 8. 9.** But far be it that, because most blessed Cyprian thought otherwise about baptism than the truth, both formerly by custom and afterward by reason more diligently perceived, has shown, anyone of us therefore, who in this matter do not have his view, should dare to set himself before him. For his many other and great merits, and his mind most full of singular love, by which he remained most peaceable with colleagues thinking differently, and his glorious passion in the unity of the Church, have sufficiently shown that he was a fruitful branch in the root of Christ, which the Father would cleanse even of this reproof, that it might bear greater fruit. For thus says Jesus himself: *The branch that bears fruit in me, my Father cleanses it, that it may bear more fruit* [^20]—showing that even in the fruitful, the husbandman who is in heaven finds something to cleanse. For which of us can be compared to the apostle Peter—although we have never compelled the gentiles to Judaize, which he was doing when he was not walking rightly toward the truth of the Gospel? Whence, corrected by a wholesome admonition by Paul, his later fellow apostle [^21], he furnished posterity a more useful example of humility than if there had been nothing in him to be corrected.

**2. 9. 10.** Established, then, in this Church, which neither could nor will be able to perish by the contagion of the bad—whether hidden or even manifest—we dread no slanders concerning any men whatsoever. For if they are bad, without doubt either the good do not know them, or, when they are made manifest by their own evidence, they condemn them by ecclesiastical laws; or even if they know them, and cannot condemn them when not accused before them nor convicted, they tolerate them for the peace of the Church not only blamelessly but even laudably; nor do they damnably separate themselves, on account of the bad fish, by breaking the Lord's nets before the time of the shore. For if they should wish to do this, apart from the innumerable testimonies of the divine Scriptures, by which they are forbidden to do so, surely most blessed Cyprian, whom you have brought forward as witness, restrains them, crying out and attesting: "That although weeds seem to be in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we perceive weeds to be in the Church, we should ourselves withdraw from the Church." Admonishing us not only by his word but also by his own example—he who bore for the bond of unity colleagues such as these, seizing estates by treacherous frauds, increasing their gain by multiplying usury, whose avarice he understood, according to the Apostle, to be not some light vice, but idolatry [^22]—and was not by their contagion made such himself: he departed from them by unlikeness of conduct, not by division of sacraments; and did not touch the unclean, but by abhorring their deeds, not by gathering peoples apart. But you, while you understand carnally what was said through the prophet Isaiah—*Depart, and go out from the midst of them, and be separated, says the Lord, and touch not the unclean* [^23]—and other things that are similarly said in the Scriptures: while, I say, you understand these things not spiritually but carnally, you appear altogether such as those who, the same Prophet foretelling, are condemned saying: *Touch me not, for I am clean* [^24]. Finally, when you judged that others' sins were perversely to be avoided, you made others your own: dividing peoples by a sacrilegious schism, and, by a nefarious spirit, holding a sacrilegious heresy against the manifest and fulfilled promises of God concerning the Church spread throughout the whole world. For if, as you think and reprove us in word, one and the same society of the lost were not at once both schism and heresy, blessed Cyprian would not have said, in this very letter from which I set down this testimony about the weeds established in the Church, to those confessors whom he congratulated on having been freed from the division of the Novatians: "I grieved vehemently and was gravely distressed, that I could not communicate with those whom I had once begun to love, after a schismatic and heretical error had received you as you came forth from prison." Do not, then, against the most open truth, either deceitfully evade both, or choose one of these—whichever seems milder to you: since you are both a schismatic by sacrilegious departure, and a heretic by sacrilegious dogma.

**2. 10. 11.** Nor let you flatter yourselves that we do not rescind your baptism. It is not yours, but the Catholic Church's, which we hold, whence you took it with you when you departed—not, indeed, to your salvation, but to your ruin. For the Lord's vessels remained holy even among foreigners. Whence the king who dared to use them contemptuously was punished, with God angered [^25]. And the ark of the covenant, captured by enemies, by no means lost the power of its sanctification [^26]. If, then, those holy things, which then were among strangers in such a way that they ceased to be among their own, could nevertheless in no way lose the force of their holiness; how much more do the Christian sacraments not lose it, when they cross over to heretics in such a way that they also remain with us? This is what I said in writing to you, what we also said to you in the Conference, and what you thought rather should be evaded, because you could not dissolve it: that, as the Apostle says of certain people, *They hold the truth in iniquity* [^27]; so you too hold the truth of the divine baptism in the iniquity of human error. Which truth, not yours, we surely ought not to rescind on account of your iniquity. And because the same Apostle is understood to have said this of the idolatrous gentiles—*who hold the truth in iniquity*—you, as though answering me, demanded that I prove *what the Apostle does not rescind of the gentiles' sacrilege, what of their profane rite he does not condemn*. As though he could fail to rescind and condemn what is sacrilegious and profane, as we [fail to rescind] your schism and your heresy. Yet certain sentences of truth, which certain of the gentile philosophers hold concerning the unknown God, the Apostle not only did not destroy, but even from there, when there was need, employed a testimony. For, speaking to the Athenians, he says of God: *For in him we live, and move, and are, as also certain of your own have said* [^28]. This truth of wisdom, which most blessed Paul not only did not destroy, but even employed for instructing them, they held in the iniquity of their idolatry, which the apostolic teaching overthrew by apostolic devices. So we too do not rescind, but acknowledge, the true things which your forebears received in the Catholic Church and handed on to you; but your sacrilege we either rescind in your conversion, or detest in your obstinacy.

**2. 11. 12.** And yet you have altogether solved the whole question, and the whole matter at issue between us, by one word of the tribune, expounded carefully enough. For when I had said, "Neither is it read in the tribune's letter that the name of God was invoked by you in truth"—because I had not read that very word there—you answered: "You are deceived, or rather you deceive. For these are the words of the tribune: 'Nor let so great a work of the house of the Lord, where the name of God and of his Christ has been more frequently invoked by you, be said to have been burned up through your religion established there.' Understand that in truth it is called 'religion,' in falsehood it is named 'superstition.'" When would I have noticed that? when would I have reasoned so? when would I have so proved one thing from another? I confess, this escaped the edge of my poor wit, such as it is: and therefore believe me that I could there be deceived, not, as you said, that I wished to deceive in a word. And so the tribune, as a military man, erred, in that to one whom he knows or believes to be a heretic he said, "through your religion"; whereas heresy is not religion but superstition; but religion, by its proper expression, is said to be not in falsity but in truth. Accordingly, by this exposition of yours, the true worship of God is called "religion," but the false "superstition." Hear, then, your own self, obey your own self: and you will by no means refuse to follow us. For, writing to this same tribune, you set down in the very opening of your first letter, and said: "To the honorable Dulcitius, tribune and notary, longed for by us exceedingly, if you would have it so, Bishop Gaudentius"; and straightway you added: "I have received the writings of your religion." Why, then, do you still hesitate to come to us? See, the tribune Dulcitius, although he is a man of our communion, is nevertheless, according to your testimony, not superstitious but religious: and by this, according to your exposition, he holds not the false but the true worship of God. He, then, is in the Catholic [Church] rather than you: since you so far did not err in this word, that you even expounded that religion differs from superstition as much as truth differs from falsehood. But that man, as I have already said, being a military man and less learned in these proprieties of words, did not know what religion was. For far be it that I should say: He was deceiving you by flattery. But you, who reprove from the prophet those against whom you dispute, and cry out, "Woe to those who call bitter sweet, and sweet bitter; who call light darkness, and darkness light!" [^29]—if it is superstition which Dulcitius holds with us, why did you call it religion? But if you spoke the truth, why, holding heretical superstition, do you spurn the Catholic religion? Follow, then, your own testimony: especially because even your own people, when they learn of it, will perhaps not communicate with you; since by this word you communicated with the tribune Dulcitius. Come, brother Gaudentius, do not lose the occasion which the Lord has given you even through your own tongue. Or are you perhaps angry at this, that I call you brother? For this name you spurned from us even in our Conference: where you showed that we are those whom the Lord commanded through the prophet: *Say: You are our brothers, to those who hate you and detest you* [^30]; but that you are of the number of those who hate and detest these to whom the Lord says this. You surely cannot deny that you called it "religion," in which the tribune Dulcitius is. For this reason he sent you that letter, namely, that you should not kill yourself, and should communicate with that Church in which he himself is. If, then, his is religion, yours is superstition. For although both of you said this to each other in turn, you nevertheless choose that he rather than you spoke falsely.

**2. 12. 13.** Wherefore, according to this most true testimony of yours, and your most true exposition—since it is religion which the tribune Dulcitius holds—surely it is religion by which the emperor's command impels you to our communion. Whence it follows that it is also religion by which the Christian emperor judges it to pertain to his care that there be no sinning with impunity against divine matters: by which you do not wish to be cared for, except [in] those things which are contained in the earthly republic. Whence also you said, forgetting what you have read, that the king of the Ninevites did not command the people to do penance. For these are your words to me: "Why," you say, "do you deceive the wretched? God commanded Jonah, the Lord sent the prophet to the people; he commanded the king nothing of the kind." Take note, then, of what is written, and be angry with none but yourself, who either do not recall the divine writings, or rather you yourself deceive the wretched. *Jonah arose, and went to Nineveh, as the Lord had spoken. Now Nineveh was a great city, of about three days' journey. And Jonah began to enter the city, about one day's journey, and he preached and said: Yet three days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown. And the men of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least. And when the word had come to the king of the city of Nineveh, he arose from his seat, and took off his garments, and clothed himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes. And it was proclaimed by the king and by the powerful, saying: Let men and beasts, sheep and oxen, taste nothing, nor feed, and drink no water. And men were clothed with sackcloth, and the beasts cried out to the Lord earnestly: and each one turned away from his way of wickedness and from the iniquity that was in his hands* [^31]. Do you hear at last that the king took care of what you are displeased should pertain to the care of kings? Surely [he commanded] that what was being done less than it ought to be should be done earnestly. Therefore the Ninevites were not, by the king's command, constrained to penance by despoilings and proscriptions or by the terror of soldiers, because they did the commands obediently. So we do not show that the people suffered these things, since neither do you show that the king was despised. Accordingly, when anyone obeys kings commanding, with religious fear, the things that are according to God, beginning from fear and advancing to love, he receives peace from the Lord—not as the world gives peace [^32]; since the world gives peace for temporal advantage, but the Lord for eternal salvation. Therefore, since this is religion, in which the tribune Dulcitius is (for you will not deny your own words): the party of Donatus is superstition, from which the religion of this man wishes you to be rescued; it is superstition that you seek to kill yourself, which the religion of this man strongly forbids; it is superstition that you reprove the emperor's care concerning these things, which the religion of this man undertook to carry out.

**2. 13. 14.** And so, since by your own word the case is finished, I beseech you, through the God of religion, the God of truth, that your error too may at some time be finished. This very thing, brother, is the Church of Christ, which at this time, by growing, is spread abroad through the whole world, containing both the bad and the good, to be separated at the final winnowing. And, that I may address you at the last rather with the words of him whom you wished to bring forward as a witness of the Catholic name: "It is the very one which, flooded with the light of the Lord, stretches out its rays through the whole world: it is the very one which extends its branches, with abundance of fruitfulness, over all the earth." Accordingly, "although weeds seem to be in it, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we perceive weeds to be in the Church, we should ourselves withdraw from the Church. We must only labor that we may be able to be wheat, so that, when the wheat begins to be stored in the Lord's barns, we may receive fruit for our work and labor. The Apostle says in his letter: *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and earthenware; and some indeed honored, some dishonored* [^33]. Let us give our effort, and labor as much as we can, that we may be a vessel of gold or silver. But to break the earthenware vessels is granted to the Lord alone, to whom also the rod of iron is given [^34]. The servant cannot be greater than his lord [^35]: nor let anyone claim for himself what the Father granted to the Son alone, so as to think that he can already bear the shovel or the winnowing fan to winnow and cleanse the threshing floor, or by human judgment separate all the weeds from the wheat. Proud is that obstinacy and sacrilegious that presumption which a depraved frenzy takes upon itself. And while certain people always claim for themselves more than gentle justice demands, they perish from the Church: and while they insolently exalt themselves, blinded by their own swelling, they lose the light of truth." These are the words of most blessed Cyprian, not mine: the words, namely, of him whom you set down for us, in the opening of your writings, as a most choice witness of the Catholic name, and commended through many things most copiously; nay, through him (since the words are true and divine) they are God's. See what you should hear, see what you should beware, so that, with the mercy of the Savior helping, we may together hold Catholic love, together love the Catholic peace, together grow everywhere with his wheat, together tolerate the weeds until the end, together live in the barn without end. You see, surely, that without any defense of Caecilian, or of any men whatsoever whom you think must be accused, the Catholic [Church] stands by its own strength and firmness. Although our Conference cleared even the case of Caecilian: and concerning the others of whom you slander, either the accusation is doubtful, or the condemnation unjust. But it is very foolish that we should place the cause of the Church, supported and fortified by divine testimonies, upon the cases of men, with reason wandering off course: since, even if we saw those bad people most manifestly, and could no longer separate them from the sacraments of the Church, our faith or our love nevertheless ought not to be hindered, so that, because we perceived these too to be among the weeds in the Church, we should ourselves have to withdraw from the Church. If you think to answer, do not abandon the cause, and wander off into superfluous things. Look at the things that have been said: answer the things that have been said, not by deceitfully evading, but by reasonably disputing. For what you accomplished by that lengthy reply of yours—or rather, how nothing you accomplished—I will, if it seems necessary and the Lord grants it, show more diligently in another work.
