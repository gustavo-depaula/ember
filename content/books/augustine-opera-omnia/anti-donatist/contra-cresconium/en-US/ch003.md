# Book III

**3. 1. 1.** If I had not to care for the wits of the slow, who cannot understand that in the two preceding volumes I have so answered in many ways the whole cause which is contained in your letter, brother Cresconius, that even those things which remain there are already resolved and frustrated, I ought already to have put an end to this work. But because there are many whom it is fitting for us to serve, who do not think all things have been driven off unless even the individual points are treated in their own places, I shall briefly run through, in order, the rest of your discourse to be refuted.

**3. 1. 2.** Whatever you thought should be inserted from the letters of Cyprian, the venerable martyr, and of certain Easterners—that it pleased them to disapprove the sacrament of baptism given among heretics and schismatics—hinders our cause in nothing, if we retain that Church which Cyprian did not desert, even when many of his colleagues had refused to consent to this opinion. For even in that very council he said: "Judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion, if he has thought differently." And he so concluded the same letter to Jubaianus:

**3. 2. 2.** "These things, dearest one, we have answered you according to our mediocrity, prescribing to no one or prejudging, so as to keep each of the bishops from doing what he thinks, having the free power of his own judgment," and so on. Among these, then, reckon us for the present, to whom Cyprian could not persuade that opinion, from whom nevertheless, even though they thought differently in this matter, he by no means separated his communion. But your forebears, to whom you bore such testimony—"that they were separated from the communion of the Easterners for this reason, because those rescinded their judgment, by which it had pleased them that one ought to consent, about this question of baptism, to Cyprian and that African council"—acted against Cyprian. For they ought to have held the unity of communion with their colleagues even thinking differently in this question, which we read Cyprian did in his writings. They answer that "he wished so to speak for this reason, lest perhaps, deterred by fear of excommunication, they should not dare to say freely what they thought, not because he would remain in communion with them if they thought differently"; where they openly say that Cyprian lied. For if he said, "Judging no one, nor removing anyone from the right of communion, if he thinks differently"—which the recorded acts of the council indicate that he said; and yet, if any of those to whom he said these things showed himself to think differently, he would not commune in the sacraments of Christ with him: without doubt he was lying, who promised such things deceitfully, not truthfully; and—what is worse in that lie—he was deceiving the simplicity of the brethren with a double heart, especially since the things that were being said were also being written. For whoever from that council thought differently—by what means could he be condemned or excommunicated by them with their mouth, when he recited on his own behalf the beginning of that same council? Who, then, thinks more tolerably of Cyprian—whether we, who say that in the obscure question of baptism the man could have been deceived; or you, who say that, in promising Christian communion, the bishop wished to deceive, not some one of the brethren, but the whole episcopal society? But if it is wicked for you too to believe this of him; your forebears acted against his opinion, who separated themselves from the communion of the Easterners, because they thought differently about this matter.

**3. 3. 3.** Accordingly, if it is now to be believed at all that fifty bishops of the Easterners held the same view as seventy Africans, or even somewhat more, against so many thousands of bishops to whom this error displeased in the whole world; why should we not rather say that even those few Easterners corrected their judgment, not, as you say, rescinded it? For just as it is praiseworthy not to be moved from a true opinion, so it is blameworthy to persist in a false one: never to hold which is the first praise, to change it the second; so that either from the beginning the true one remains, or, when the false is changed, the true succeeds. Nor does it now pertain to our question what is the character of what the greatest part of the Christian world held with the Easterners. For if this is true, if this is to be held and observed, which we hold and observe about baptism, we object to you two evils of yours: one, that you err in the question of baptism; the other, that you separate yourselves from those who think truly about this matter. But if, to speak according to you, this is true in that question which you yourselves think; you are certainly stained with that crime, that you withdrew from the Church, for whose peace, as Cyprian both did and admonished, you ought to have borne even those thinking differently.

**3. 4. 4.** Then you cry out that I said: "You distinguish nothing between the faithful and the faithless; the pious and the impious seem the same to you"—which I did not say. But plainly I said this, from which you wished to cry out as though I said that, and to say what I did not say: "Whether," I said, "anyone receive the sacrament from a faithful or from a faithless dispenser." In which opinion I distinguished not nothing between the faithful and the faithless, nor did I command that the pious and the impious should seem the same to anyone: but that the same sacrament can be had by the pious and the impious; which not even you deny, who concede that one ought not to be baptized at least after hidden impious men. Accordingly without cause you join together and say: "It profits nothing to live by good morals, because whatever is lawful for the just, the unjust too can fulfill"; because it is both false, and was not said by me. For good morals distinguish the life of the good and the bad, and lead to different ends. Nor whatever is lawful for the just can the unjust too fulfill; because the just man fulfills the law of Christ through love, from which the unjust is a stranger; yet he can do something which the just man too fulfills—so that he too may baptize, if nothing else, at least when he is hidden: just as those too can preach the commandments of God as the just do, but not live according to them as the just do, of whom it was said, *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do*[^1].

**3. 5. 5.** But grant that an unjust man, not hidden and known to some of the good, cannot, through some sedition or faction, be separated from the Church. Hear Cyprian, tolerate the weeds, be wheat. How well did those things seem to you to sound, which in one matter you variously repeated: "What," you say, "can be said more iniquitous than this precept: Let the stained purify another, let the filthy wash, let the unclean cleanse, let the faithless give faith, let the criminal make innocent?" I answer briefly: Neither stained, nor filthy, nor unclean, nor faithless, nor criminal is Christ, who loved the Church, and gave himself for it, cleansing it by the washing of water in the word[^2], making us certain of his own goods, lest we be corrupted by another's evils. For behold, when the unjust minister is hidden, by whom nevertheless you do not rescind the baptism given, cannot all these your words be turned back on you—since the stained too purifies, and the filthy washes, and the unclean cleanses, and the faithless gives faith, and the criminal makes innocent? "Not he," you say, "but his good reputation, although empty and false." Hence do you not wish me to cry out: O crime, O monstrosity—not, as someone says, to be carried out into the farthest lands, but rather to be driven, if it could be done, outside every heaven and all lands! I do not mean you yourself, whom I wish corrected; but this error, from which I desire you to be corrected. So, for cleansing a man, when the true life of a good minister is lacking, then the false reputation of a bad minister suffices, to effect what a good life would effect—so that, for sanctifying a man, when the iniquity of the minister is hidden, falsehood serves God as a handmaid? All this, then, lest you should say what we say—that, whether anyone receive the sacrament from a faithful or from a faithless dispenser, none sanctifies but God.

**3. 5. 6.** Then you mention my words, that I said: "Let Christ always give faith, let Christ be the origin of the Christian; in Christ let the Christian fix his root, let Christ be the head of the Christian." I said it plainly, and I say it: nor were you able to answer at all. For you seem, as it were, to have succumbed to so great a weight of truth, when you subjoined: "This we too persuade, this we wish."

**3. 6. 6.** Then again you place under it a man in whom the one about to receive should set his hope: "But we ask," you say, "through whom this is better done?" And because we too do not say that a man can be baptized without a minister, you ask of me whether it is better that the minister be unjust, or just? Here I answer that it is better for this—that the minister be just, so that the weakness of a man, for whom what God commands is laborious and difficult without an example, may by the imitation of a good minister be more easily raised to a good life. Whence the apostle Paul says, *Be imitators of me, as I too am of Christ*[^3]. But for baptizing and sanctifying a man, if what is received is so much the better, the better he is through whom it is given; there is as great a variety of baptisms in the receivers as there is of merits in the ministers. For if, what is believed without controversy, Paul was better than Apollos, he gave a better baptism, according to that vain and perverse opinion of yours: and if he gave a better baptism, surely he envied those whom he congratulates as not baptized by himself[^4]. Moreover, if among good ministers, since one is better than another, the baptism which is given through the better is not better, in no way is that bad which is given even through the bad, when the same baptism is given. And therefore through unequal ministers the gift of God is equal, because it is not theirs, but his.

**3. 7. 6.** Not, then, as you vainly inveigh, do we distinguish nothing between the faithful and the faithless: but we distinguish human merits, not the divine sacraments; which you too, led by the force of truth and forgetful of your heretical intention, said are to us and to you not other, but one and the same.

**3. 7. 7.** How do you say, "This we too persuade and wish—that Christ always give faith, that Christ be the origin of the Christian; in Christ let the Christian fix his root, let Christ be the head of the Christian"—when you defend the writings of Petilian, where most openly, while he was commending the merit of the baptizer, and trying to show how much human righteousness availed for giving baptism, he, for the cause he had undertaken, said: "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver. For he who knowingly takes faith from a faithless man receives not faith, but guilt"? And as though it were said to him, "Whence do you prove this?" he followed and added: "For every thing consists by its origin and root; and if it does not have a head, it is nothing." Why, I beg you—because you have fallen into the defense of a rash falsehood—do you strive to pour mists over manifest things? The man plainly says that there can be no origin, root, and head of one to be regenerated through baptism, except him by whose ministry he is baptized; and you say: "This we too wish—that Christ be the origin and root and head of the Christian; but we ask through whom this is better done." This is one thing that you say, another that Petilian said: this which you say, even if it is true, is not what he said.

**3. 8. 8.** Accordingly, if you too wish this—that Christ be the origin and root and head of the Christian—resist Petilian, not me: since *neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters; but God who gives the increase*[^5]. Which, when I had set down from the Apostle in my letter, you wished to answer in this way, saying: "It is indeed God's to give the increase; but just as he who plants and waters is sought to be none but a faithful and diligent husbandman, so too in the sacrament of baptism none but a faithful and most just workman is employed." As though, forsooth, what a faithless husbandman planted should not, on account of his faithlessness, sprout. The force of the seed, and the fertility of the earth, and the temperateness of the sky have received this efficacy divinely—that, for propagating fruits, they await only a planter or waterer as workman, and do not care with what mind he works, with what intention he labors, whether he faithfully loves the master of the field, or seeks his own gains and not his master's. You add also a prophetic testimony, saying, *I will give you shepherds according to my heart, and they will feed you, feeding you with discipline*[^6]. I know, it was fulfilled: such were the Apostles, such even now—although, for the breadth of the Church, very few—are nonetheless not lacking. But what is said also through the prophet Ezekiel against the bad shepherds, you ought to have sought, read, considered. For there he says, *I will feed them, not the shepherds*[^7].

**3. 9. 9.** Accordingly both through good shepherds and through bad shepherds, when he dispenses his word and sacrament, he himself feeds; because of himself he says, *That there be one flock and one shepherd*[^8]. *For it is good to trust in the Lord, rather than to trust in man*[^9]; and: *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^10]. Which, mentioned by me in that letter, you show that you understand in such a way that you say you therefore the more seek out one who is just and faithful through whom this sacrament may be celebrated: "because you have the hope and trust of God, not of man; and that faith and righteousness are God's, which you always attend to in his ministers." This you say truly, because we have nothing of all goods which we have not received[^11]; and therefore faith and righteousness are to us from God. But when you say that God cannot give this to you, unless the man by whom you are baptized has it; you surely place your hope in a man, whether he is a partaker of righteousness you do not know; and if he is not, then you attend to his reputation, and when you have found a false good one about a hidden bad man, you believe it sufficient for your sanctification. I beg you, if you have the trust of God, not of man, and therefore the more seek that he be just and good and faithful through whom this sacrament is celebrated, because faith and righteousness are God's; is the falsehood of reputation too God's, which, if a bad minister has it as good, you say suffices you for your sanctification? I would now rather you trusted in a man, from which I most vehemently forbade you, than in the falsehood of his reputation. For a man, whatever sort he is, as far as concerns what he is as a man, is a creature of God: but no falsehood is a creature of God. Moreover, if *cursed is he who places his hope in man*[^12], how much more he who places his hope in the falsehood of human opinion, so as to fall into that other thing which is written, *He who trusts in false things, this man feeds the winds*[^13]—that is, becomes food for evil spirits?

**3. 10. 10.** "If baptism," you say, "given by whomsoever and in whatever way, ought not to be rescinded, why did the Apostles baptize after John?" How much more forcibly is it proposed thus: If the Apostles baptized after John, why does not one who is either better or equal baptize even after any saint?—so that from this at least you may be compelled to understand that the baptism of John does not pertain to this question? "And to the Jews," you say, "baptized by Moses, Peter said, *Repent, and let each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ*[^14]." If the Jews had already been baptized for this reason, because Moses, so long a time before, had baptized their parents through the Red Sea[^15]; those born of baptized Christians are baptized today without cause: and yet you say these things, and write these things; you are heard and read, and you are thought to be answering our writings: as though it were the same to have been able to answer as to have been unwilling to keep silent.

**3. 11. 11.** Nor did you refute, at the beginning of this letter, as it seems to you, that which you thought should be passed over—what was said by me: "If those who wished to be of Paul erred, what hope, then, is there for those who wish to be of Donatus?" For who does not see that this division was made from this, and persists in this plague even today, while hope is placed in the righteousness of a man, so that the baptism of Christ is then acceptable, if anyone is dipped by a just man? Against which error, against those who had already begun to make schisms on account of the differing merits of men, Paul himself cries out: *I thank God that I baptized none of you, lest anyone say that I baptized in my name*[^16]; insinuating what else than that the baptism of Christ is none but his in whose name it is given, and therefore it does not become better when it is given through a better man, nor worse through a worse one?

**3. 11. 12.** In vain, therefore, you next exult, and say: "It follows that I conclude all those things which were written by holy Petilian (whosesoever the discourse is) to have been rightly said." For those very words which you conclude as though rightly said do not prove themselves to have been rightly said; because neither is "the conscience of the one who gives holily attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver," when the stained conscience of the giver is hidden. There, conquered, when you ought to have acquiesced in the truth, you appealed to his false reputation, as to an unhappy judge deceived by a lie: because neither is the conscience attended to, when his reputation is attended to, and one can no more be cleansed by anyone's false reputation than by anyone's bad life; and no one takes the Christian faith from a man, neither faithless nor faithful, but from him of whom it is written, *Cleansing their hearts by faith*[^17]. And if he hears through a faithful man what is to be believed, he indeed imitates him, yet is not justified by him. For if a minister justifies the impious, it follows that belief should also rightly be placed in the minister; for there is a clear and certain apostolic sentence: *To one believing in him who justifies the impious, his faith is reckoned unto righteousness*[^18]. Accordingly, if a minister does not dare to say, "Believe in me," let him not dare to say, "You are justified by me."

**3. 11. 13.** But now as to what follows: "Every thing consists by its origin and root; and if it does not have a head, it is nothing": if the origin and root and head of the baptized is the baptizing minister, it is not Christ; if it is Christ, it is not he. Finally, when the minister is secretly bad, what is the origin, what the root, what the head of the baptized? Is it his false reputation? This indeed Cresconius says, but the truth contradicts. Therefore, if the origin and root and head is then Christ, he is so even when the minister is good, lest—what is most absurd—the condition of one baptized through a hidden bad man, when Christ is the head, be better than that of one baptized through a manifest good man, if then the minister is the head. This I would say also of the good seed. For there follows: "Nor does anything well regenerate, unless it is regenerated by good seed."

**3. 11. 14.** But the things which follow from the letter of Petilian set down by you, where he says: "Since these things are so, brethren, what perversity can there be, that one who is guilty by his own crimes should make another innocent, when it is written, *A good tree bears good fruit; and a bad tree bears bad fruit. Do men gather grapes from thorns?*[^19]"; and again: *Every good man brings forth good things out of the treasure of his heart; and a bad man brings forth bad things out of the treasure of his heart*[^20]—they sufficiently and openly convict that Petilian referred all these things to none but the man through whom baptism is ministered, so that he himself is understood, if he is innocent, to make innocent the one whom he baptizes; he himself is the good tree, whose fruit is the one baptized; he himself the good man, from the treasure of whose heart proceeds the sanctification of the baptized. And through this, when this man is secretly bad, tell me, by whom is that man made innocent? Tell me, of what tree will he be the fruit? Tell me, from the treasure of whose heart will the baptized be sanctified? Or if then he merits to have as the author of his innocence, then as the tree from which the good fruit is born, not the man minister of baptism, but Christ; he has more happily fallen upon a hidden bad minister than if he had fallen upon a manifest good one. But if it is most absurdly and most insanely said, it is Christ's fruit who is sanctified by his baptism, by the work of whatever minister he is baptized. Unless perhaps he is to have recourse to your counsel, when he falls upon a stained and hidden conscience of the minister, so that you may show him the tree from which he is born—the falsely good reputation of a bad man: whose root if you seek, you will find the cunning of a deceiver; from which if a good fruit can be born—which far be it—Christ lied, who said, *A bad tree cannot bear good fruits*[^21]. But because Christ said the truth, let a good man, as a good tree, have good fruit, a good work; just as a bad man, as a bad tree, has bad fruit, a bad work: but let him who is baptized be born not of any man, but of the Spirit of Christ, if he wishes to be a fruit not corrupted by the wind, and a tree not uprooted. Since these things are so, when you said, "Therefore it follows that I conclude all those things which were written by holy Petilian (whosesoever the discourse is) to have been rightly said," I think that I rather conclude all those things to have been not rightly said.

**3. 12. 15.** Now let us see what you next wove into your letter—how your people taught you the case of Optatus and of the Maximianists, or rather, that I may be made more learned by you, of the Maximianians. And about Optatus, indeed, about whom I can recite nothing written, whatever you think, I easily yield. This one thing I know: if the things which, I will not say were shown, but certainly were said, about him are true, he was neither good, nor had a good reputation; whence whoever were baptized by him could be cleansed neither by his conscience, according to Petilian, nor by his reputation, according to you: but if envious reputation, as it often is wont, bruited false bad things about him; see how rightly we do not easily believe what you could never prove to us about the betrayers whom you accuse: because a bad reputation is wont to lie even about the good. If, then, neither their innocence, nor finally—what is true and firm—the grace of God and our conscience makes baptism ratified to us, let at least your sentence make it so.

**3. 13. 16.** For you, when you spoke of Optatus, said: "I indeed neither absolve nor condemn Optatus." If I—indeed, not only I, but the whole African Catholic Church; how much more the overseas Church too, so far and wide diffused—should say of Caecilian and his ordainers: I neither absolve nor condemn them? Do you think it too little for those whom they baptized, of whom none ever saw Caecilian, what you believe suffices about Optatus for those whom he even baptized with his own hands? Or because you recite the council of your forebears about Caecilian, but no council is recited by us about Optatus; do you therefore think that none of us is permitted to say: I neither absolve nor condemn Caecilian—which was permitted to you about Optatus? But a later overseas judgment was made on behalf of Caecilian, with your people pressing before the emperor Constantine that it be made. Or if ecclesiastical judgments, which were made earlier, cannot now be uprooted; what will you do about Primian, your bishop of Carthage, against whom first a hundred—surely more than judged about Caecilian—gave judgment, and, his episcopate being abrogated, appointed Maximian in his place? Does not Primian rely on the later judgment which was made on his behalf in the town of Bagai; according to which judgment he does not wish there to be doubt about himself, but extorts from all of you his absolution? Whence we too, according to the later judgment about Caecilian, absolve him altogether without hesitation. Yet it suffices for the cause, if we say of him what you said of Optatus: We neither absolve nor condemn Caecilian. Let those, whether yours or ours, see for themselves how they judged about him; let them render the account of their own judgments, let them bear the burden of their own work, whether bad or good: permit us at least to doubt about the deeds of others, lest we be compelled to condemn in ourselves those sacraments about which it is not permitted to doubt. But I have already said: think of Optatus what you will: for there is nothing whereby we may convict him, who committed nothing before the acts; which nevertheless he so committed that among the satellites of Gildo he was held chief, was held captive, died. Or about Felicianus and Praetextatus, the associates of Maximian, whom three hundred and ten of your bishops, with the others likewise expressed by name, condemned by one and the same sentence of the Bagai council, and whom, after no long time, with all those whom the condemned had baptized, you received, just as they were, as bishops—are you permitted to prattle whatever you wish?

**3. 14. 17.** Wherefore, whatever you said against us or against our people, as though sparing and passing over, without names, without witnesses, without any proofs at all—partly blaming what is not to be blamed, partly not proving what is to be blamed—it is superfluous to wish to clear it away. These, these again attend to—Felicianus of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuras—gaze somewhat more diligently, whose whole cause I shall in its place, if the Lord permits, so explain that even if you wished to be too great an enemy of truth, you could neither defend nor deny the lie of your people: but now for the present it pleases me to deal according to what they told you; I do not yet discuss how untrue what they said was, I do not yet show with what marvelous blindness of shamelessness they lied. Certainly, "when you read in my letter about those whom I called Maximianists, both condemned by the council of your people and received," as you write, you were greatly moved: indeed, since (for I shall use your own words) you were ignorant of what the truth held. Finally, "at once," as you relate, "I more diligently inquired of your bishops; and with them informing me, I learned both the decree of the council, and the sentence pronounced against those who were condemned, and the order of the whole matter." And because you believed that I too was ignorant of what was done, you exhorted me to learn what the truth held, and so next you related everything. See that in this cause I set down your very words from your letter; for they are most necessary to me.

**3. 15. 18.** "When the error of Maximian," you say, "was striving to associate to itself very many bishops, a council being gathered by our people, a sentence was pronounced against all those who had persisted in his schism: which you too testify that you read. And when this sentence was confirmed by the consent of all, it nevertheless pleased," you say, "that by decree of the council a delay of time be given, within which, if anyone wished to be corrected, he would be held innocent. So it came about," you say, "that not only those two whom you mention, but also many others, purged and innocent, restored themselves to the Church. Whose baptism therefore ought not to have been rescinded, because, restored within the appointed day, they were not held by the peremptory sentence; nor, when they baptized, had they been separated from the Church, since they were not yet disjoined by the fixed limit of the elapsed time. But those who even after the foresaid day persisted obstinately with Maximian, held by the sentence of condemnation, lost at once both baptism and the Church." These words of yours, certainly, my Cresconius, you recognize as expressed from the context of your letter.

**3. 16. 19.** Accordingly I ask of you: If they had not yet been separated from the Church, how was "a sentence pronounced against all those who had persisted in the schism of Maximian, which, when it was confirmed by the consent of all, it nevertheless pleased that by decree of the council a delay of time be given, within which, if anyone wished to be corrected, he would be held innocent"? By which words you show that of those who had persisted in the schism of Maximian, if any within the delay of time wished to be corrected, he would be held innocent. He would, then, be corrected, if anyone wished to be corrected, from that schism in which he had persisted with Maximian: accordingly, before he was corrected, he was in the schism in which he had persisted; although in it, because he was corrected within the appointed time, he had not persisted obstinately. For in the later words too, a little after, you distinguish thus: "But those who even after the foresaid day persisted obstinately with Maximian, held by the sentence of condemnation, lost at once both baptism and the Church"; saying surely "persisted obstinately," you show that those too who were corrected persisted, but not obstinately. Against all of whom at once that sentence was pronounced, which, confirmed by the consent of all, you nevertheless say it pleased by decree of the council to give that delay of time. How, then, were those in the Church, who, before they were corrected, were persisting in schism with Maximian? But if they were not in the Church, because they were in schism, how did they baptize? Then, how did it come about, as you say, that not only those whom I mentioned, but also many others, purged and innocent, restored themselves to the Church, if they were not separated from the Church? To whom did they restore themselves, from whom they did not withdraw? Or if they had withdrawn, tell me, I beg, before they restored themselves to the Church, by what right did they baptize? "Whose baptism therefore," you say, "ought not to have been rescinded, because, restored within the appointed day, they were not held by the peremptory sentence." "Restored"—to where? Wake up, I beg, tell us, restored to where? Surely you are going to say: "To the Church," to which you said they restored themselves. Is anyone restored to the Church who was not separated from the Church? Does anyone not separated from the Church persist for however few days in schism? Does anyone not separated from the Church restore himself to the Church after however small a time?

**3. 17. 20.** I reckon, dearest one, that you not only did not consider your words before writing them, but did not even read them after they were written. Yet what would you do, on whom in this work the necessity was imposed of defending, against Maximian and his associates, a sentence not your own but spoken by others, as best you could? For even if you did not use these words, I would recite that same decree of the Bagai council, where it is written: "But those whom the slips of the sacrilegious shoot did not pollute the plantings—that is, those who withdrew their own hands from the head of Maximian out of a modest shame of faith—we have permitted to return to the mother Church."

**3. 18. 21.** Accordingly, even if I did not find such words of yours, here certainly I would say, here I would cry out for the truth: How are they permitted to return to the mother Church, who did not withdraw from the mother Church? Or if they withdrew, by what right, before they returned, could they baptize—except that in the cause of patching up this schism you forgot your own vanity, by which, leading down from the very seats of the Apostles an unshaken succession of bishops up to these times, you judge that not one man, not one house, not one city, not one nation, but the whole world must be rebaptized? Or certainly because the horror of this deed has always shaken the hearts even of those doing it, when so many peoples returned to your communion from the communion of Maximian, it pleased you indeed to restore so many, but you were ashamed to rebaptize so many: which nevertheless, however much any might detest and shudder at it, you would do for the salvation of men—which, the more numerous they were, the less surely it was to be despised, if you ever did this with a view to the truth, and not by the prejudice of error. You see surely that in this return of the Maximianians to your communion it was made plain that the things said by us about baptism are true. For if there is any understanding in men, either of saying anything or of hearing it, he who baptizes before he returns to the Church surely baptizes outside the Church: and yet the baptism is not to be rescinded, just as it was not rescinded by you in those men. Which baptism, unchanged, each one baptizes neither in his own name, nor in anyone else's, but in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Then, besides the sacrament of baptism, which, whether unto the punishment of the perverse or unto the salvation of the upright or the corrected, perseveres whole, I wish to say something about the very expiation or cleansing of the corrected. For you remember what you said against us—that we receive those coming to us from you, out of the sacrilegious error of the heretics, without any expiation. Tell us now, then, those returning to you from the schism of Maximian, when they were received even into their own honors; by what expiation, I ask, were they cleansed? Or, having shared in so great a crime, were they not stained by so nefarious a fellowship?

**3. 19. 22.** Attend to what that truth-speaking mouth of your bishops sounds forth, what it thunders, what it confesses in that illustrious council. "Maximian," it says, "the rival of faith, the adulterer of truth, the enemy of the mother Church, the minister of Dathan, Korah, and Abiram, the thunderbolt of the sentence has struck out from the bosom of peace: and because the gaping earth has not yet swallowed him[^22], it has reserved him for greater punishment to those above. For, snatched away, he would have gained the avoidance of his punishment by the shortcut of death: now he gathers the interest of a heavier loan, since, dead, he is among the living." So then, those persisting in his schism, as you yourself said, before, within the time of the delay, as you likewise said, they restored themselves to your Church, drew no stain, even a small one, from his fellowship? But hear next what follows; hear, I say, what the framer or speaker of that sentence connects. "Nor does just death condemn this man alone," it says, "for the chain of sacrilege drags also very many into the fellowship of the crime, of whom it is written: *The venom of asps is under their lips; whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness, their feet are swift to shed blood: ruin and misery are in their ways, and the way of peace they have not known*[^23]. We would indeed not wish them to be cut off as from the joint of our own body; but since the pestiferous rottenness of a festering wound has more of solace in cutting off than of remedy in remitting, a more salutary remedy has been found, lest the pestilent poison creep through all the members—that the newly arisen wound be lopped off with a brief pain. The defendants, then, of a notorious crime—Victorianus of Carcabia, Martianus of Sullectum, Beianus of Beia, Salvius of Ausafa, Theodorus of Usula, Donatus of Sabrata, Miggin of Elephantaria, Praetextatus of Assuras, Salvius of Membressa, Valerius of Melzita, Felicianus of Musti, and Martialis of Pertusa, who by a deadly work glued together a filthy vessel of perdition with collected dregs; and also the clergy at one time of the Church of Carthage, who, while they took part in the crime, lent procuring to an unlawful incest—know that they have been condemned, by the judgment of God presiding, by the truth-speaking mouth of a universal council." What graver things could you say against them? What graver things are you accustomed to say against us ourselves? "But within the time of delay," you say, "they were corrected from this so great evil." I shall see whether they were corrected: because they would indeed have been corrected, if they had returned to the true Church. But if yours is the true one, tell us how they were expiated from so monstrous a crime. For if they were not expiated, you are all, according to your own sentence, stained with their crime: but if they were expiated by the return alone, on account of the love which covers a multitude of sins[^24], you concede that they could be expiated; and then you accuse our people, when corrected they come to us, with empty slanders: unless perhaps, although they had already, as the words of the council indicate, perpetrated the sacrilege of schism, yet before the day of the granted delay they had not yet been defiled by that same sacrilege, and therefore were not thought to need expiation.

**3. 20. 23.** If this is so, who would dare to oppose you, who have received so marvelous a power over men? They sin when they will, and are defiled when you will. We are reciting nothing obscure, or less known and published. That is the sentence which, by the merit of so great eloquence, is in the hands of all, in the mouth of all who are zealous for reading such things: about which now at least you perceive how truly I said that "they ought not first to rejoice that it was eloquent, lest afterward they should bewail it as defamed." Attend to what it has, hear what it clamors.

**3. 21. 24.** "Maximian," it says, "the rival of faith, the adulterer of truth, the enemy of the mother Church, the minister of Dathan, Korah, and Abiram, the thunderbolt of the sentence has struck out from the bosom of peace." Therefore, if anyone had knowingly communed with him in the sacrament of the altar for one day, according to that most boastful severity of yours, would he not be so polluted by so great an evil that he would become his equal? What, then, were they, or what were they made by it, who not only approached the altars with him, but themselves, with altars erected against your Primian, brought him to be ordained bishop? But why should I question you about these things? Let the sentence itself speak, whose words so shine that, if you should wish to hide it, it would break through the darkness of whatever hiding places by its excessive splendor. Let us see with how great a sound of condemnation it rises up against the associates of Maximian.

**3. 22. 25.** "Nor does just death condemn this man alone," it says, "for the chain of sacrilege drags also very many into the fellowship of the crime, of whom it is written: *The venom of asps is under their lips; whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness, their feet are swift to shed blood: ruin and misery are in their ways, and the way of peace they have not known*[^25]." Then a little after, when it had enumerated by name all the ordainers of Maximian, among whom also these of whom I am dealing, Felicianus and Praetextatus, are read, it subjoins thus what they did, that such things should worthily be said against them: "Who," it says, "by a deadly work glued together a filthy vessel of perdition with collected dregs"; wishing it to be understood that they themselves were present, that they themselves ordained Maximian by laying on hands. Adding also about the clergy of Carthage: "And also the clergy," it says, "of the Church of Carthage, who, while they take part in the crime, lent procuring to an unlawful incest."

**3. 23. 26.** I ask you, Cresconius, do I in any words of mine exaggerate this crime? Which, had I wished to do, perhaps things—if not such, yet in some way sufficient—would not have been lacking to me. I ask you, then, before these two, of whom for the moment I am dealing, returned to the concord of your communion, set as they were in that chain of sacrilege, under whose lips was the venom of asps, with mouth full of cursing and bitterness, with feet swift to shed blood, how did they baptize? Was there in them the conscience of those giving holily, that it might cleanse that of the receivers? Did even a reputation good or most false commend them—which gave you, in those straits, an exit not by which you might escape, but by which you might hurl yourself headlong, when so notable a council cries out that they were "defendants of a notorious crime"? Then, returning—as you believed your bishops who told false things—before the day of delay, how are they received into their honors, with those whom, set in the chain of sacrilege, they, as schismatics outside the Church with Maximian, had baptized? Whence are they expiated from so great a sacrilege? From that chain whence are they loosed? Their lips, their mouth—from the venom of asps, from cursing and bitterness, whence are they cleansed? Whence are their feet washed from the shedding of spiritual blood, to which they ran swiftly? Whence are their hands cleansed from the deadly work of perdition? Whence is purged, from unlawful incest, not the members of the body, but the affection of the soul?

**3. 24. 27.** Surely, to defend this cause, willing or unwilling, you are compelled to come to the patronage of the truth, which says that the baptism of Christ—not only given through hidden, but even through manifest bad men; not only by the converted, but even by the perverse—has the unshaken firmness of its own strength; and that it can indeed be in them, but cannot profit except the corrected; but that the corrected are expiated even by fraternal prayers, through the love that covers a multitude of sins[^26]. Behold, before I show how shamelessly your bishops lied to you about the reception of the Maximianians, according to that very lie of theirs and your own narrative, how much your whole cause is overturned, I think you ought not now to seek, but to recognize, nor to prepare a contradiction, but rather to think of correction. For you see now at least how truly those things were said by me, to which you tried vainly and mendaciously to answer; how rightly I said: "If, for the unity of the party of Donatus, no one rebaptizes those baptized in a nefarious schism, why, for the unity of Christ, is the true and full law of that inheritance not acknowledged?" Since you yourself confess that those who had persisted in the schism of Maximian merited a sentence, by which they would not be held if within the day of delay they restored themselves to the Church. Whence it is established that, before they restored themselves, they baptized in the schism in which they had persisted, along with those with whom they were received into your communion. You see how dead men baptized: for about those who had persisted in the schism of Maximian, before they returned to you, it had been said in the sentence of the Bagai council: "After the manner of the Egyptians, the shores are full of the corpses of the perishing[^27]."

**3. 25. 28.** But as to what I said: "When it was recited among them as a thing to be decreed, they acclaimed with most ample mouth; but now, when it has been recited by us, they fall silent"—behold, now they would fall silent much better, when they say such things by which they would be the worse entangled. You see how true is what was said by me: "Now at last they ought to perceive how great things are to be tolerated for peace, and to return for the peace of Christ to the Church, which did not condemn things unknown, if for the peace of Donatus it pleased to recall things condemned." For this is much more true according to your narrative, who said that even those were recalled, with delay granted, about whom, expressed by name, it had already been said: "Know that they have been condemned, by the judgment of God presiding, by the truth-speaking mouth of a universal council." For since after these words that delay was established, how did it not please to recall things condemned? Or how can the things that could not be proved about Caecilian in the overseas judgment which was afterward made be other than unknown to us, born so long after, or even to the Christian world itself; when the things that were done about the Maximianians, both in Africa and in our own times, you—although you are an African, as you say—after so many years did not yet know? As, however, we shall be able to teach you, you do not yet know, because you believed your bishops lying.

**3. 26. 29.** But as to your saying that "I wished to retort the charge of handing over upon your forebears, as it were by counter-accusation, that it was done by our forebears," blaming me for having done this, "as though it were a matter dealt with in a school about kinds of cases or questions, not the truth being sought in the Church"; would you say this to the prophet Elijah, who, when it was objected to him by a most iniquitous king that he himself was overturning Israel, answered: *I do not overturn it, but you overturn it, you and your father's house*[^28]? For what does this kind of retorted objection pertain to us—by what name it is called by the Greeks in the art of rhetoric—when we find this in prophetic authority? When, therefore, anyone says: I did not do it, but you did it: it is necessary to say the truth, it is to be feared to say it. And you, how you may show that your forebears did not do what, by their own confessions when read, they are shown to have done, you ought, if you are able, to consider most vigilantly, not to deter the unskilled by a Greek word lest they hear us. And that our forebears were betrayers you did not teach: for not because you said that there are many writings by which this could be shown, are you therefore to be thought already to have shown it. But about your forebears there stands the council of Secundus of Tigisis, made indeed with very few at Cirta after the persecution of handing over the books, that there a bishop might be ordained in the place of one deceased.

**3. 27. 30.** There receive what was done: for the things that were necessary I have taken care to write below. "In the consulship of Diocletian for the eighth time and Maximian for the seventh, on the fourth day before the nones of March, at Cirta, when Secundus the bishop of Tigisis had taken his seat in the first chair in the house of Urbanus Donatus, the same said: Let us first prove ourselves, and so we shall be able to ordain a bishop here. Secundus said to Donatus of Mascula: It is said that you handed over. Donatus answered: You know how Florus sought me out to make me offer incense, and God did not give me into his hands, brother: but because God spared me, therefore do you too keep me for God. Secundus said: What, then, shall we do about the martyrs? Because they did not hand over, therefore they were also crowned. Donatus said: Send me to God, there I will render my account. Secundus said: Step aside to one side. Secundus said to Marinus of Aquae Tibilitanae: It is said that you too handed over. Marinus answered: I gave little papers to Pollus; but my codices are safe. Secundus said: Cross over to one side. Secundus said to Donatus of Calama: It is said that you handed over. Donatus answered: I gave over medical codices. Secundus said: Cross over to one side." And in another place: "Secundus said to Victor of Rusicade: It is said that you handed over four Gospels. Victor answered: Valentianus was the curator, he himself compelled me to throw them into the fire. I knew they were ones to be erased. Pardon me this fault, and God too pardons me. Secundus said: Cross over to one side." And in another place: "Secundus said to Purpurius of Limata: It is said that you killed two sons of your sister at Mileu. Purpurius answered: Do you think I am frightened by you, as the others were? What did you do, who were held by the curator and the council to give up the Scriptures? How did you free yourself from them, except that you gave, or ordered to be given, whatever it was? For they did not let you go just anywhere. For I killed, and I kill those who act against me. Therefore do not provoke me to say more. You know that I deal with no one. Secundus the younger said to his uncle Secundus: Do you hear what he says against you? He is ready to withdraw and make a schism, not only he, but all those whom you accuse: whom I know will leave you, and give sentence against you, and you will remain alone a heretic. Therefore what does it matter to you, who did what? He has to render account to God. Secundus said to Felix of Rotaria, Nabor of Centurio, and Victor of Garba: What seems good to you? They answered: They have God to whom they may render account. Secundus said: You know, and God. Sit down. And all answered: Thanks be to God."

**3. 27. 31.** These betrayers, who with others at Carthage pronounced sentences against Caecilian and his associates—among whom was also Silvanus of Cirta, about whose handing over I shall presently insert the acts—you will defend, of course, by a splendid reasoning: for this you are surely going to say about so many of these, which you thought should be dictated as something great about Silvanus alone, reckoning that from this you would manifestly demonstrate false the crime of handing over which is objected to him, because you inserted his sentence which he pronounced in the council among the other bishops against Caecilian and others sharing his communion; as though it could not happen that a betrayer should condemn betrayers. For you see these things more prudently than the apostle Paul: for he objected absurd things to certain people, and did not consider that what he said could not happen: *You who preach not to steal, do you steal? You who say not to commit adultery, do you commit adultery? You who abhor idols, do you commit sacrilege?*[^29] and especially that: *For in what you judge another, you condemn yourself; for you who judge do the same things*[^30]. These betrayers, then, whose confession frightened Secundus into pardoning them, he had with him in the Carthaginian council, and they pronounced sentences against the absent who had not confessed, who, present, were not condemned about their own confession. This council would not stand, nor endure into the memory of posterity, unless it were written out by others, while it was being kept by those who prepared to defend themselves by it, if anyone afterward objected to them the same crimes which were there released.

**3. 28. 32.** It was also in the interest of Secundus himself, that it might appear that he, lest a schism be made (which peaceable men are wont to guard against before others), dismissed all those things to the divine judgment: which he ought much more to have done in the Carthaginian council, where against the absent nothing was established, except that Lucilla, then a most powerful and very wealthy woman, kindled with furious hatreds, vehemently pressed that against Caecilian, as though condemned, another be ordained bishop. Which was afterward mentioned in the judgment of the consular Zenophilus, through a certain Nundinarius, deacon of Silvanus of Cirta, then your bishop, who, when he could not, as he wished, be reconciled with his said bishop, whom he had endured as an enemy. He had indeed dealt with his colleagues more terribly, lest he should betray everything, than suppliantly, that he might merit pardon.

**3. 29. 33.** From the acts of that judgment I have inserted these few things: "In the consulship of Constantine the Great Augustus and Constantine the younger, most noble Caesar, on the Ides of December, Victor the grammarian, the son of Tamogades, having been brought in and presented, with the deacon Nundinarius also standing by, Zenophilus the consular said: What are you called? He answered: Victor." And a little after, in another place: "Nundinarius answered: Let the acts be read. Zenophilus the consular said: Let them be read. And Nundinius the clerk read: In the consulship of Diocletian for the eighth time and Maximian for the seventh, on the fourteenth day before the calends of June, from the acts of Munatius Felix, perpetual flamen, curator of the Colony of Cirta: When they had come to the house in which the Christians used to assemble, Felix, perpetual flamen, curator, said to Paul the bishop: Bring forth the Scriptures of the law, and whatever else you have here, that you may be able to obey the precept and the command. Paul the bishop said: The readers have the Scriptures, but what we have here, we give. Felix, perpetual flamen, curator, said to Paul: Point out the readers, or send to them. Paul the bishop: You all know them. Felix, perpetual flamen, curator, said: We do not know them. Paul the bishop said: The public office knows them—that is, Edesius and Junius, the clerks. Felix, perpetual flamen, curator, said: The matter of the readers, whom the Office will point out, remaining, you too give what you have here. With Paul the bishop sitting, and Montanus and Victor of Castellum Memorianum, presbyters, standing by, with Mars together with Aelius, and Mars the deacon, with Marcuclius, Catulinus, Silvanus, and Carosus, subdeacons, bringing forth, and Januarius, Marcuclius, Fructuosus, Miggin, Saturninus, Victor of Samsuricum, and the other gravediggers, Victor son of Aufidius writing it down in a summary thus: Two gold chalices; likewise six silver chalices," and so on. And in another place: "After the library was opened, empty cupboards were found there; there Silvanus brought forth a little silver box and a silver lamp, which he said he had found behind a jar. Victor son of Aufidius said to Silvanus: You would have been a dead man if you had not found them. Felix, perpetual flamen, curator of the commonwealth, said to Silvanus: Search more diligently, lest anything have remained here. Silvanus said: Nothing has remained here, we have thrown out all this," and so on. And in another place: "Copy of the document handed over to the bishops by the deacon Nundinarius. Christ and his angels are witness, that you communed with betrayers: that is, Silvanus of Cirta is a betrayer, and a thief of the goods of the poor, which all you bishops, and presbyters, and deacons, and elders know; and about the four hundred folles of Lucilla, a most illustrious woman, for which you conspired together, that Maiorinus might be made bishop, and thence the schism was made. For Victor the fuller too, in the presence of you and the people, gave twenty folles, that he might be made a presbyter, which Christ and his angels know," and so on. And in another place: "When these things were read, Zenophilus the consular said: Both by the acts and by the letters which have been recited, it is established that Silvanus is a betrayer. Likewise in another place: Zenophilus the consular said: What did Silvanus administer then in the clergy? Victor answered: Under Paul the bishop, when the persecution arose, Silvanus was a subdeacon."

**3. 30. 34.** Have you, brother Cresconius, anything to say to these things? I think your brow has not so perished from human affairs that, for the clearing of Silvanus, you still think the sentence should be recited which he pronounced against Caecilian and his colleagues as though betrayers; lest you should the more admonish us to say something similar from the Epistle of the Apostle, as I mentioned a little before: You who preach not to hand over, do you hand over; and: *In what you judge another, you condemn yourself; for you do the same things which you judge*[^31]. "But afterward," you say, "when Ursacius and Zenophilus were persecuting, since he had refused to commune with them, he was driven into exile." Nay rather, he who was already a betrayer wished to remain also a heretic, so that he might have in the very party of Donatus a false honor, which he could have none of in the Catholic Church, the acts of his handing over being laid open so manifestly by a public judgment. These things you are surely going to say are false, and on behalf of your forebears, as far as you can, you are going to bring forward other things against ours. Which perhaps you will not be able to do, finding nothing to bring forward. But grant that you have found, grant that you have brought it forward: is the shamelessness of your fury so great that you would contend that the things brought forward by you ought to be believed more than those which are brought forward by us? For either there were betrayers both here and there, if you too recite something from the confession of our people: or if you think something has been forged by our people against yours, why do you not concede that we may think the same of what was done by your people against ours? Therefore, concerning human deeds—either manifested on this side and that, or uncertain on both sides—lest we who believe in one God should quarrel, let us, in the grace of Christ, be in concord about what is a certain and divine gift. For when the acts of our forebears and yours, contrary on this side and that, are recited to us born so long after, if it is not permitted even to doubt, what is more iniquitous? But if it is permitted, what is more sufficient? For just as it is uncertain from whom the evil of handing over arose, so it is not uncertain from whom the good of peace is commanded to be rendered.

**3. 31. 35.** And through this, he who, for an uncertain evil of another, rejects the peace of Christ, is most certainly bad: since Cyprian did not desert the peace of the wheat even for the certain malice of the weeds mingled in, who, writing to Maximus, said: "And even if there seem to be weeds in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we discern that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church." He did not say: We suspect, we opine, we reckon, we conjecture, we believe; but he said: "We discern." O word, by which all doubt would be removed, that the body of Christ might not be divided! If you desire wheat alone, groan in the labor of the field, rejoice in the hope of the barn, tolerate the bad in the communion of the sacraments of Christ, lest, by breaking the nets before the time of the shore, you become what you were unwilling to tolerate. These things I would say, if you had proved anything about the betrayers whom you accuse. Indeed, at this present time I would not now say even these things: for I am not commanded to tolerate those with whom I am no longer compelled to live. But if even now a betrayer were demonstrated to me, with what conscience do I desert so many Christian nations, to whom none is demonstrated? Then, if I myself now learn what a little before I did not know, why do you rescind in me what I knew? For I knew that I had received the baptism of Christ: you yourselves too teach that another's crime, when unknown, harms no one.

**3. 32. 36.** Why, then, do you rebaptize today one baptized yesterday, when you teach today another's crime which yesterday he did not know? Not knowing from what sort of man he had received it, he was not guilty of the baptism he received. Just now he learned this from you: why does he become guilty of the rescinded baptism? For whether he consents to your proofs or does not consent, he who received the baptism of Christ by the ministry of a betrayer, unless you prove that he knew from whom he received it, you cannot rightly baptize even according to yourselves. Now then, turn your mind away from the zeal of parties, and consider how innumerable a multitude of Christians in Africa itself does not know who the betrayers were: how much more in the rest of the world, how great a multitude—unless you convict them of having known when they were baptized—you will either not dare to say must be baptized, or you will dare to judge about the hidden things of the heart? And where is that which pleases you, the divine sentence which was inserted by you: *The things that are manifest are for you; but the things that are hidden, for the Lord your God*[^32]? Believe the Christian world saying to you: I know the baptism of Christ; who the betrayers were in Africa, or anywhere, I do not know. Why do you judge in me hidden human things, so as to rescind in me manifest divine things? Grant that you now prove to me another's crime, which you speak of: when I received baptism, I did not know this: if, on account of those whom you now betray to me, you wish to baptize me, you ought also to baptize those who unknowingly received it from adulterers, whom you have just betrayed. What do you say to these things except: It is not holy, it is not clean, except what I wish, and when I wish?

**3. 33. 37.** "In this matter," you say, "the conscience of almost the whole world is witness." It is answered you: In this matter there is no conscience at all of the whole world. "This," you say, "our forebears received from their parents." It is answered: But, erring, from those erring or slandering. For the forebears of the Jews too received from their forebears that the body of Christ was stolen from the tomb[^33]. "Those have not long ago died," you say, "who knew the crime of this handing over—by whom and in what places it was committed." It is answered: This our people too say on behalf of their party. "There exist also," you say, "books in which the order of the deeds is faithfully and diligently written down: there are acts, there are letters, the manifest confession of many also is held." It is answered you, these too are not lacking to our people on behalf of their party. Either, then, let us believe those who were able to persuade their cause to those Churches which we read written in the divine and canonical books: or, what you said of Optatus, let us neither absolve the doubtful nor condemn them; and let us hold, in fraternal love, the peace of Christ, whose good is not doubtful.

**3. 34. 38.** But you say that "the Easterners," whom you now concede to be ours, "were not ignorant of this crime." And to prove this, you insert "the beginning of the letter of the council of Serdica, where the name of Donatus, your bishop of Carthage, is found written in. Which you think and affirm was done for this reason, namely, because the crime of the betrayers displeased the Easterners, who sent these writings from their council, and they had withdrawn themselves from their communion, and therefore communed with your Donatus." Learn, then, what you do not know: the council of Serdica was of the Arians—which has long been known, and we have it in our hands—gathered especially against Athanasius, the Catholic bishop of Alexandria, who, beyond the rest, sharply rebuked and refuted their error arisen from that very city. It is not, then, to be wondered at if those heretics tried to enlist Donatus to themselves as their own, whom the Catholic Church condemned throughout the whole world. Although we have, without the names of cities, the bishops to whom these letters were addressed. Either, then, there was some Donatus, a bishop not in Africa, to whose name Carthage was added by your people; or, as I said, the Eastern heresy tried to join the African heresy to itself. Which is especially credible from this, that the Eastern Catholic Church would never write to the bishop of Carthage, omitting the Roman: where at least your man ought to have been written to, whom you are accustomed to send to Rome for the few of you from Africa. But thanks be to God, that that conspiracy of the Eastern heretics with the African heretics, if indeed it was begun, did not prevail. You have already placed the Arians, in your letter, among the heretics to be detested by both us and you; whence there is no necessity for me to contend with you about this question too. For as to the question you proposed for yourself, as though objected by us: "If these things are so, how were the Easterners afterward separated from the communion of your people?"—and you answered "that in receiving our people again they could not keep the constancy of the condemned cause"—is it to be wondered at that your bishops, from such far lands, tell you with impunity what they wish? But if it were altogether so, what did so many peoples do, who, since they were ignorant of these things, are nevertheless judged by you to be rebaptized? Or is it not credible that the peoples too could be ignorant of these things, when you, somewhat more zealous about these matters, would not have sought what your Africans did in Africa with the Maximianians, had you not wished to answer my letter?

**3. 35. 39.** But as to what I said: "Nor, if the handing over of some who died in our communion were proved—which is disapproved by us and displeases—would it stain us in any part": how ridiculously "you thought it ridiculous and ill-suited to my prudence." Accordingly I now desire to know how your prudence refuted it. Is it because you say "you do not see how it is disapproved by us, or to what extent it displeases, since, set in their schism, we never condemned, with the error known"? See rather how briefly I answer these things. I am in the Church, of which all those Churches are members which we know, born and established from the labors of the Apostles, together in the canonical writings: their communion, as far as the Lord helps me, whether in Africa or anywhere, I shall not desert. If in this communion there have been betrayers whom I do not know, when you demonstrate them, I shall detest them as dead both in flesh and in heart: yet I shall by no means be alienated from the living who remain in the holy unity of the same Church, on account of the dead. For they themselves did not found this Church; but in it, if good, they were wheat; if bad, chaff. But you, whom the weeds or chaff of so manifest a Church could not stain, what was the cause of your division, except the appetite for sacrilegious schism? "If it displeases you," you say, "disapprove it, flee and leave the Church of the betrayers. Do not follow the footsteps of your erring forebears." To this I answer: If they were not betrayers, they are my forebears: if they were—which I am not—they are not my forebears. I hold the Church full of wheat and chaff. I do not say, if you showed me others bearing their own burden[^34], but if you showed me myself in it to be a betrayer, where it is permitted me to be changed for the better, I have no need to be separated from it. If I come to know any such in the communion of its sacraments, I amend by the word and discipline of the Lord those I can, I tolerate those I cannot amend. I flee the chaff, lest I be it: not the threshing floor, lest I be nothing.

**3. 36. 40.** Accordingly do not be vainly tossed about in this opinion. For rather, how I should do this, the apostolic precept which you yourself set down admonishes me: *Do not commune in the sins of others; keep yourself chaste*[^35]. For to show how anyone should not commune in the sins of others, he added this: *keep yourself chaste.* For he who keeps himself chaste does not commune in the sins of others: although he communes, not in their sins, but in those sacraments of God which they take to themselves unto judgment, with those from whom, by keeping himself chaste, he made himself a stranger. Otherwise Cyprian too—which far be it—communed in the sins of his rapacious and usurious colleagues, with whom nevertheless he remained in the communion of the divine sacraments, of whom he says: "Very many bishops, who ought to have been an encouragement and an example to the rest, neglecting the divine procuration, became procurators of secular affairs, abandoned their chair, deserted their people, wandering through foreign provinces, hunting the markets of profitable trade: while the brethren in the Church went hungry, they wished to have silver in abundance; they seized estates by insidious frauds, they increased their gains by multiplying usuries." Did he commune in the sins of such men? Did he follow their sect? And yet he stood with them in the communion of the same sacraments: because those sacraments those men had not instituted, who did not have them, by holy morals, unto salvation, but, by bad morals, made them avail to their own punishment.

**3. 37. 41.** But what is it that you say, that you had proposed in my person that I never handed over the divine testament? For you add: "But he who handed over created you." Then you weave the things that seemed to you to sound well: "A stream is led down from the fountain, and the members follow the head. With a sound head the whole body is sound, and if there is in this any disease or fault, it weakens all the members. Whatever grows up in the stock looks back to its origin." And after these things, as though at the end, you conclude: "He cannot be innocent who does not follow the sect of an innocent man." In all these your words you made my creator, my head, none but a betrayer—whom indeed you could only accuse, not convict. But I neither make his innocence my creator or fountain or head, but you go back to that in which Petilian erred—lest, when anyone is born in the sanctification of baptism, Christ be the origin and head of the one being born; and you are unwilling to come into the curse of which it is written, *Cursed is everyone who places his hope in man*[^36], while you do not entangle yourself elsewhere, nor, when you have recoiled, fall back into another error.

**3. 38. 42.** But you admonish me also by a testimony from the Scriptures, what I should yet answer you to these things. For you say it was written on this account: *Do not walk in the legal customs of your fathers*[^37]—nor do you notice that it was said to the Jews, that they should not imitate the bad deeds of their fathers, not that they should be cut off from that people of God. If, then, it was permitted to King David, to Samuel, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Zechariah, and the rest of the holy ones and prophets of God to keep the commandments of God among the despisers of the law of God, and to hurl many worthy and true words against those very transgressors of the commandment, and not to imitate, not to follow, but to detest and flee those sins of their fathers too, by which under Moses they so offended God that none of them was worthy to enter the land of promise, and to rebuke in those who committed them the likeness of such fathers—and yet not to set up for themselves another people, as it were purged and refined, by a sacrilegious separation: how is it not permitted to us neither to imitate the deeds of I know not whom, which you rather object than prove, and yet not to be separated from the holy Church which, as the Apostle says, *fruits and grows in the whole world*[^38]? Did the betrayers institute any sacraments in which I might be baptized? Did they compose any books about the doing or imitating of handing over for posterity, and do we hold and follow their doctrine? But if they had done this, and did not allow any to commune with them except those who read and approved those books, they would divide themselves from the unity of the Church; in whose division if you saw me, then you ought to say that I am in the Church of the betrayers. But if they wrote out their most wicked precepts about the doing of handing over, and yet were not gathered outside the Church into their own congregation and communion, they would on that account be counted as weeds, for whose cause the wheat would not be rightly deserted.

**3. 39. 43.** Behold, I say again the things you further forbid me to say: "You arraign before me those whom my knowledge, already long dead, did not judge." You say on the contrary: "It is permitted you even today to judge, and judgment can be held not only of the living, but even of the dead. For although he who sinned be dead, what he committed never dies." What, if while he lived he corrected himself and appeased God, is not what he committed dead and blotted out, as Felicianus and Praetextatus, the ordainers of Maximian, corrected themselves from so great a crime, with a delay given, as you say? But it is marvelous, if a man's own sins do not harm those who have corrected those deeds, and harm others who did not do them at all; and—if it is about us—add, who did not even know the deeds. But you say it is permitted me even today to judge, because judgment can be made not only of the living, but even of the dead. Behold, I wish to judge, but you are unwilling to plead the case itself; nay rather, when we demonstrate it pleaded then, then also without doubt concluded, you are unwilling to acknowledge it. But let us rather make you have those you can teach. Why do you contend that those you have not yet taught must already be rebaptized, since you ought not to baptize even when you have taught them, those baptized by betrayers whom they did not know—just as those who, unknowing, were baptized by adulterers, you do not baptize, even when those men are betrayed and convicted?

**3. 40. 44.** Here perhaps you may say: but about Caecilian it has already been judged. It is answered you: it had already been judged about Primian too, by a hundred of your bishops, to whom Maximian had persuaded that he was most iniquitous, before you held the Bagai council. But in the first judgment he was condemned absent, in the second he was absolved present. If after the first those whom he baptized cannot be rebaptized, how much more after the second. So too Caecilian was first condemned absent in the judgment of Secundus of Tigisis at Carthage, in the second judgment he was absolved present, by Miltiades of Rome. Still you are unwilling that we be certain about him: at least permit us to doubt. For he conquers you not only who knows Caecilian to be innocent, but even he who does not know him to be guilty. But you judge that both must be rebaptized—both those who say "we know," and those who say "we do not know what sort of man Caecilian was." Those whom Primian baptized after the first judgment by which he was condemned absent are not to be baptized; and those whom Caecilian baptized after the second judgment by which he was absolved present are to be baptized. It was not permitted to condemn that condemned man; let it at least be permitted to doubt about this absolved one. Whose crimes, even if they were certain to us, would by no means stain us, set in the Church which the Holy Spirit foretold as a threshing floor with chaff[^39], since the sins of others, not imitated, do not stain. And yet, even if they are said to be uncertain to us, we are not only constituted defendants, but even judged to be rebaptized. Do you act thus? Do you so pervert all things, only so as to reckon that it is truly permitted you, that what you wish be holy, what you wish be unclean? Restrain yourselves, do not advance in evils, lest you perish from the good.

**3. 41. 45.** Now that which I said: "But if you deal with persecutions, I answer quickly: if you suffered anything iniquitously, it does not pertain to those who, although doing such things improperly, nevertheless tolerate them laudably for the peace of unity"—how deceitfully you wished to refute it, not considering at all that your letter would have a reader of sound head. For you answer as though I had said that persecution ought to be done to you for the peace of unity. Which in that place indeed I did not say, but I said: "If you suffered anything iniquitously, it does not pertain to those by whom they are laudably tolerated for the peace of unity, who do such things against you, although improperly." Since this was said most openly, let at least those whom you wished to deceive attend to it explained; for I do not think you did not understand what is so plain, but by the brevity of my sentence you thought it could easily be cast into a fog, where, saying whatever else, you might seem to have answered it. But why I said that the bad of our communion—that is, the chaff of the Lord's threshing floor—when they act iniquitously against you, are laudably tolerated by our good for peace, what need is there for me myself to show, myself to defend, when on my behalf blessed Cyprian says most openly and most candidly that, even when the bad are discerned in the Church, the Church is not to be deserted on their account[^40]—which is what I said, that they are to be tolerated for the peace of unity? For they do not persecute you more than us, who, persecuting you iniquitously, give you a likeness of glory—false, for deceiving the unskilled—but to us a great wound of sadness.

**3. 42. 46.** Then, mentioning also I know not what dead men, whom you say were killed by our people, as though set on the field of your eloquence, you most amply expand the topic, in which you seem to yourselves like the martyrs—when daily we suffer the incredible deeds of your clergy and Circumcellions, far worse than those of any robbers or brigands. For, armed with frightful weapons of every kind, wandering terribly, they disturb not, I say, the ecclesiastical, but the very human quiet and peace; by nocturnal assaults they leave the invaded houses of Catholic clergy bare and empty, and the clergy themselves they cast away half-dead, snatched up and beaten with clubs and gashed with iron. Moreover, by a new and hitherto unheard-of kind of crime, pouring and stuffing into their eyes lime mixed with vinegar, those whom they could have plucked out at a stroke, they choose rather to torment more than to blind more quickly. For at first they used only lime for this deed, but after they learned that those to whom they had done this quickly recovered their health, they added vinegar.

**3. 43. 47.** I pass over how great things they committed before, by which they compelled the same laws to be established against your error—laws tempered more by Christian gentleness than exerted with the force of fitting vigor against such great crimes. A Catholic bishop of Thubursicu, Servus by name, when he was reclaiming a place invaded by your people, and the agents of both parties were awaiting the proconsular examination, suddenly, with your armed men rushing upon him in the said town, scarcely escaped alive. By whom his father, a presbyter grave in age and morals, after a few days departed this life from that slaughter by which he was vehemently afflicted. Maximianus, the Catholic bishop of Bagai, when by a judicial sentence pronounced between the parties he had won the basilica of the Calvianensian estate, which your people had at one time unlawfully usurped—when he held it by clear right, in that very place, under the altar to which he had fled, that same altar being broken over him, and with its wood and other clubs, and cruelly cut even with iron, he covered that whole place with blood. He had received also a great wound in the groin, from which, the blood flowing more freely, he would have at once died, had not their greater cruelty profited him through the hidden mercy of God. For when, with his limbs bared on that side, he was dragged half-dead and face down, the dust secretly stopped his overflowing veins. Thence, when he was being carried by the hands of our people, with them rushing upon him again, he was violently wrenched away and more grievously battered, and cast down by night from a high tower, he lay below softly received on a heap of dung, his senses lost, scarcely holding his last breath. There a certain poor man, passing by, found him, when he had turned aside to that place for the sake of relieving his bowels. He recognized him, when, frightened, he called his wife—whom modesty had kept at a distance—carrying a lamp. Then they both carried him home, either out of pity or hoping also for some little gain, since, whether alive or dead, gathered up, he might be shown to our people. What more? By a marvelous cure he was healed, he lives; more scars are counted on his body than members. Rumor had announced him to the overseas lands as killed by your people, and the monstrousness of that deed, with grave indignation, wherever it could be heard, had moved all things with horrible grief. After he himself followed there, his most recent scars defended that lie of his reputation; for he whom, beholding then, they would scarcely believe alive, did not seem rashly to have been bruited as killed. Here, when he had found there the colleague of Thubursicu, whom I mentioned a little before, and several others who had suffered like or not much lesser things, and when no means of returning to their own places seemed open to them, and because the notable fury of your Circumcellions, furnishing a horrible bodyguard to your clergy, had everywhere become most odiously known, a vast envy blazed up against you, and thence it came about that both all the past laws against you were stirred up, and these new ones were established. The severity of all of which, however, if it be compared with the savagery of your people, ranging disordered and without any law, must be called marvelous gentleness. For by these laws Catholic meekness, having received so great a power, is rather commended than heretical monstrousness is punished; nay rather, against us, by devising, threatening, exercising slaughters, plunderings, burnings, blindings, it rages more boldly and more insanely.

**3. 44. 48.** For I wished to mention these things, through which it came about that in these our times those imperial statutes were passed against you—rather against your error. For what, if you are wise, can be so much on your behalf? But all the savage deeds of your people, which I have either learned from the writings of earlier men or myself come to know, by which from the beginning of your division up to this time they have persecuted the Catholic Church, if I should wish to recount, what tongue, what pen, how much time and leisure would suffice?

**3. 45. 49.** When I was dealing with Optatus, and you wished rather to excuse this than were able to clear it, you said "your people cannot on this account be defendants, because no one has brought a charge to punish them." So many protestations of our people about the most furious violences of your people have filled the public archives sooner than those deeds merited any punishment among you. But perhaps it may here be said that protestations were indeed lodged, but none brought forward to punish those men. Hear, then, certain things which I myself have experienced. When we had learned the case of the Maximianians, received by your people whom they had condemned, and were broadcasting it with fervent insistence wherever we could, those men, not finding what they could answer to matters so recent and so clear by their manifestation, began, with the violences and furious mobs of the Circumcellions more frequent and bolder than usual, to deter us from preaching the Catholic truth and refuting their fallacy. And because many, entangled in the snares of their error, with whom we were dealing that they might be freed from it, answered us that we ought to deal with their bishops, and that they vehemently desired a conference of ours, where they could see by whose assertions truth overcame falsehood, it pleased us in the Carthaginian council of all Africa that, with the testimony of public acts also employed—whereby we could prove to those who demanded it that we were by no means lacking to their desire—your bishops should be peacefully approached, each by that one of us who resided in the same place where he resided, so that, the error being removed through our conference, we might enjoy Christian and fraternal fellowship, unity, love, peace; having regard to this, that if they were willing for this to be done, with the mercy of God helping, whatever was in our cause could most easily be recognized, but if they refused, at least their diffidence would not vainly appear to those who had demanded this of us. It was done, they were approached, they refused; with what words, with what guile, full of cursing and bitterness, it is now long to demonstrate.

**3. 46. 50.** Meanwhile Crispinus of Calama, your bishop, when he was approached before the acts in that same city by my colleague Possidius, first deferred to your council, promising that there, with his colleagues, he would see what he ought to answer. Then, after no small time, the meeting being renewed, he again answered before the acts: *Fear not the words of a sinful man*[^41], and again: *In the ears of a foolish man take care to say nothing, lest, when he has heard, he mock your sensible words*[^42]. Finally I define this answer of mine by a patriarchal saying: let the impious depart from me, I will not know their ways. When the learned and unlearned laughed at this answer of his—a man saying that he does not fear the words of a sinful man, whom he by no means dared to answer; and that he is unwilling to say anything in the ears of a foolish man, as though he were about to entrust rashly some secret to the ears of a fool, when the things he would say many prudent men could hear, for whose sake the Lord Christ too spoke so many things to the Pharisees, foolish though they were; and that he is unwilling to know the ways of the impious, as though those whom he thought impious wished to teach him their ways, and not rather he himself, if he held the ways of God, ought to teach even the impious, as it is written, *I will teach the iniquitous your ways, and the impious will turn to you*[^43]—when, therefore, this answer was understood by many, and was demonstrated to many how empty it was, as far as it pertained to the cause, but how bitter and slanderous, which did not pertain to the cause, and so his most learned old age (as it is reckoned among you) was laughed at, since against a raw recruit standing opposite yesterday it could prove nothing against the truth—suddenly, after a few days, as Possidius was making a journey, another Crispinus, his presbyter and, as is reported, his kinsman, set an ambush of armed men, into which our man had now nearly fallen, had he not, on their being descried and announced, turned aside in flight to a certain estate, where that man would dare nothing, or not prevail, or, if he did anything, could not deny it. On learning of this, he immediately followed, blinded with such great madness that he now reckoned it shameful to lie hidden. Then to hedge with armed men the house in which Possidius had shut himself in with his companions, to pelt it round with stones, to surround it with flames, to attempt entry on every side. But the multitude of inhabitants that was present, mindful of their own danger, if so great a crime were carried out in that place, partly entreated him to spare—a man whom they did not dare to offend by resisting—partly extinguished the fires set beneath. When he, no less fervid and inexorable, pressed his undertaking, the door at length yielded to the blows, they entered, and, the beasts wounded which they had seen in the lower part of the house, brought the bishop down from the upper rooms, afflicting him with blows and insults. There, lest they should rage more grievously, Crispinus himself intervened, as though bent by others entreating; whose entreaty he seemed not so much to heed in his anger, as to fear testimony in the crime.

**3. 47. 51.** After these things were made known in the town of Calama, it was awaited how Crispinus, your bishop, would punish his presbyter. There came also a protestation expressed in the municipal acts, by fear or shame of which he might be compelled to exercise ecclesiastical punishment. When he altogether despised this, and so great a tumult of your people arose that it was thought—nay rather, was now even discerned—that they were about to close the roads to the preaching of the truth, to which they could not answer, the laws which were not lacking, but which, as though lacking, were resting in our hands, were stirred up against Crispinus your bishop, rather that our gentleness might be demonstrated than that their boldness might be punished. For it would not otherwise become known what, with the help of Christ, the Catholic Church could and would not do against its enemies—not, according to heretical presumption, with the Circumcellions raging by private fury, but, according to prophetic truth, with kings subjected to the yoke of the Lord God. Crispinus, therefore, being produced, and—what, when the proconsul asked, he had denied himself to be—most easily convicted a heretic, was nevertheless, by the intervention of Possidius, not compelled to pay the ten pounds of gold which the elder emperor Theodosius had established as a fine on all heretics. Not content with this most mild sentence, by I know not what counsel—which was said to have displeased all of you—he thought he should appeal to the sons of the same Theodosius. It was accepted, it was rescripted—what else but that the party of Donatus might now know that it pertained to that gold penalty along with the other heretics, with whom, on account of the communion of such persecution, let it either reckon itself to have a common righteousness, or, if it does not so reckon, let it not boast itself just on this ground, that it is coerced by that penalty by which it sees coerced also those heresies which it concedes to be unjust, and let it at last understand that it is not the penalty that makes a martyr of Christ, but the cause; but that we are persecutors so savage—let it charge—that not even after the imperial rescript did Crispinus pay out that gold to the treasury, indulgence being obtained for him by Catholic bishops; and now, even among these most recent laws threatening proscription to your bishops, he sits secure in his own property, while Catholic clergy stake, among the hands of your Circumcellions and clergy, their houses, their food, their safety, and the light of their bodies.

**3. 48. 52.** Those who do these things—why should I call them equal to robbers, pirates, to some truculent kind of barbarians, when not even they themselves are to be compared with the devil, the master of all cruelties? He, with all his goods taken away, struck the holy man Job with a most grievous wound from head to foot; and yet there was there a place sound amid the sound, and he did not extinguish the eyes of him whose whole body he had received into his power[^44]. But these things, forsooth, are not carried to your people, since Crispinus preferred to journey to Carthage, to be overcome by his own obstinacy, to refuse the most mild sentence pronounced against him through the intervention of episcopal gentleness, to appeal to the sons of him by whose law he saw himself ensnared, to stir up the whole party of Donatus to what he alone neither wished nor was compelled to suffer, rather than to punish by mere degradation the most bold and most odious deed of his one presbyter.

**3. 48. 53.** There was a certain Restitutus, your presbyter, in the region of Hippo. When he had come over to Catholic peace, before it was commanded by these imperial laws, moved by the reasoning of truth, with manifest willingness, he was snatched from his own house by your clergy and Circumcellions, led openly in daylight to the nearest fort, and, with a multitude watching and daring to resist nothing, beaten with clubs at the will of the raging men, rolled in a muddy pool, dishonored with a covering of rushes; after he had sufficiently tormented the eyes of the grieving and sated those of the mocking, thence led to another place, where none of our people dared to approach, he was scarcely released on the twelfth day. Of this I myself complained to Proculianus, your bishop of Hippo, by municipal acts indeed, lest, if it were necessary to do anything, he should deny that this had been brought to him. What he answered, how he dissembled from inquiring into the cause, and thought our intention should be more guilefully eluded afterward by not answering, the same acts sufficiently testify. But now, what the clergy who have come over to us from you suffer from your people, when can we suffice to relate? Finally, not only do those who come over to us from there abandon the truth under persecution, but many do not come over to us from there, because they fear to suffer persecution from your people for the truth.

**3. 49. 54.** Take away, therefore, from our midst the superfluous envies of the troubles which, for your error, you suffer by royal commands—very small, and almost none, in comparison with the great fury of your people—imputing even to us the things which are done by the earthly powers themselves, in order to drive off the assaults of your people from their own safety, of necessity, not of will. For neither, if those forty who had conspired to kill the apostle Paul[^45] had rushed upon the armed men by whom he was being escorted and had suffered punishment, were these things to be imputed to Paul. I pass over that you transfer even voluntary deaths, which they themselves bring upon themselves, onto us by lying. For about Marculus I have heard that he threw himself down. Which is surely more credible than that any Roman power could have ordered this—a thing too unusual to Roman laws—since this evil, among so many heresies erring under the Christian name, is proper to your heresy. Whence what does it profit that your bishops boast of having forbidden and condemned this in their councils, as you yourself mentioned, when so many cliffs and steep rocks are made deadly daily from that Marculan instruction? I have said, then, what I have heard about Marculus, and whence this can seem more credible; but what is the truth, God knows. But about the other three, whose deaths you likewise objected, what or how it was done, I confess that I did not ask of those whom I reckon to know.

**3. 50. 55.** Yet to no good men in the Catholic Church does it please, if there be raging unto death against anyone, even a heretic. Nor indeed, if the lust of avenging, far from anyone's death, requites evil for evil by troubles, do we approve it, detesting much more, if anyone, on the occasion as it were of striving for unity, should carry off coveted goods of others—not those things which under the name of the Church ought not to be possessed by heretics, but any private goods whatever. All these things displease the good, and they forbid and restrain them as much as they can; but as much as they cannot, they bear them, and, as I said, tolerate them laudably for peace, judging those things not laudable, but damnable, and neither on account of the weeds desert the crop of Christ, nor on account of the chaff the threshing floor of Christ, nor on account of the dishonored vessels the great house of Christ, nor on account of the bad fish the nets of Christ.

**3. 51. 56.** Kings, when they are in error, pass laws against the truth on behalf of that error; when they are in the truth, they likewise decree against error on behalf of the truth: thus the good are tested by bad laws, and the bad are amended by good laws. King Nebuchadnezzar, when perverse, gave a savage law, that an image be adored[^46]; the same man, when corrected, a severe one, that the true God not be blasphemed[^47]. For in this kings serve God, as it is divinely commanded them[^48], insofar as they are kings, if in their kingdom they command good things and prohibit bad—not only those which pertain to human society, but also those which pertain to divine religion.

**3. 51. 57.** In vain you say: "Let me be left to free choice." For why do you not proclaim that you ought to be left to free choice in murders and rapes and any other crimes and shameful deeds? All of which it is nevertheless most useful and most salutary to repress by just laws. God indeed gave man a free will, but he willed neither that the good will be fruitless, nor that the bad be unpunished. "Whoever persecutes a Christian," you say, "is an enemy of Christ." You say truly, but only if he does not persecute in him that which is an enemy to Christ. For neither ought a master in a servant, a father in a son, a husband in a wife, when both are Christians, to fail to persecute the faults contrary to Christian truth. Or, if they do not persecute them, will they not deservedly be held guilty of negligence? But in all things a measure is to be kept fitting to humanity, suited to love, so that neither is the whole of what belongs to one's power exerted, and in that which is exerted love is not lost, while in that which is not exerted gentleness is shown. But where no power is granted by divine or human laws, let nothing be dared improperly and imprudently.

**3. 52. 58.** Hear now about the Maximianians what a little before I deferred, so that you may both know that your bishops lied to you, and may know that not only what pertains to the question of baptism, or to the toleration of others' sins in the Church without one's own defilement, but even what pertains to the envious charge of persecution—in which, indeed, your people against us surpassed all Roman laws and protections—is brought to an end in this one case of the Maximianians. For if it did not weary you to read that memorable sentence of your three hundred and ten bishops of the Bagai council, of which I inserted above what seemed sufficient—since those words most openly show the twelve condemned with Maximian, by whose hands when present he was ordained, but a delay of returning to your communion granted to those who, although they were in the communion of Maximian and had condemned Primian, nevertheless, when Maximian was ordained, were not present (for not all could be present, nor by custom ought to have been)—I greatly marvel how you permitted yourself to be deceived by I know not whom lying, when the very words, which are not obscure and require not an acute wit by which they may be discussed but only a mind by which they may be attended to, you were able to recite, lest you be deceived by him. But because it can happen that you did not read it, and with simple affection easily believed a bishop or bishops insinuating something else to you, receive it and read it, and attend to how truly it is told you by me. For they cannot, for the sake of covering their lie, amend—nay rather, falsify—as they pleased, that which they alleged in public judgment before the proconsul, which so often they took care to insert in the municipal acts, for the necessity of their cause, when they were acting against those men, that they might be expelled from the basilicas.

**3. 53. 59.** Thus, certainly, that sentence begins: "When, by the will of Almighty God and of his Christ, we were holding a council in the church of Bagai—Gamalius, Primian, Pontius, Secundian, Januarian, Saturninus, Felix, Pegasius, Rufinus, Fortunius, Crispinus, Florentius, Optatus, Donatus, Donatian, and the rest, to the number of three hundred and ten—it pleased the Holy Spirit who is in us to confirm a perpetual peace and to cut away the sacrilegious schisms." Then, when it had vomited many horrible things against them, a little after it says: "Maximian, the rival of faith, the adulterer of truth, the enemy of the mother Church, the minister of Dathan, Korah, and Abiram, the thunderbolt of the sentence has struck out from the bosom of peace." You see, I reckon, that Maximian was condemned without any doubt. Then, after a few but plainly most grave things which it pleased to declaim against him, adding his ordainers and condemning them with him without delay: "Nor does just death condemn this man alone," it says, "for the chain of sacrilege drags also very many into the fellowship of the crime." Then, when it had poured out what it thought should be declaimed for exaggerating their crime too, it named them and so concluded them condemned: "The defendants, then, of a notorious crime—Victorianus of Carcabia, Marcianus of Sullectum, Beianus of Beia, Salvius of Ausafa, Theodorus of Usula, Donatus of Sabrata, Miggin of Elephantaria, Praetextatus of Assuras, Salvius of Membressa, Valerius of Melzita, Felicianus of Musti, and Martialis of Pertusa, who by a deadly work glued together a filthy vessel of perdition with collected dregs; and also the clergy at one time of the Church of Carthage, who, while they took part in the crime, lent procuring to an unlawful incest—know that they have been condemned, by the judgment of God presiding, by the truth-speaking mouth of a universal council." What plainer, what more manifest, what more express can be said?

**3. 54. 60.** Hear now to whom a delay of time was granted, that you may see them to be those who were not present when hands were laid on Maximian as he was ordained: "But those," it says, "whom the slips of the sacrilegious shoot did not pollute the plantings—that is, who withdrew their own hands from the head of Maximian out of a modest shame of faith—we have permitted to return to the mother Church." You discern how they do not say that these were condemned, but that they were to be held by that same sentence of condemnation if, after the appointed day had passed, they refused to return; which day they so appoint: "And lest a narrow time take away from those returning the hope of salvation by the pressure of a constricted day, to those acknowledging it, to whom it is permitted, with the preceding statutes remaining, we open the door of acknowledgment to all up to the eighth day before the calends of January next coming, so that, returned, they may have the foundations of whole honor and faith. But if anyone is unable to enter it through lazy sluggishness, let him know that the way to all pardonable approaches has been taken from him by his own will. For the pronounced sentence will remain about them, and for those returning after the appointed day a fixed penance."

**3. 55. 61.** Surely now you perceive, most eloquent man, that you ought rather to retort upon those from whom you heard the lie all those things which you thought should be said against us, as though we had lied about this matter. Surely you perceive that those two of whom we are dealing are of the number of those who were condemned with Maximian, the twelve, without delay; not of those to whom a time of returning was granted. Surely it is clear, it shines, it stands out, it can in no way be confused, obscured, covered over—what he by whom that sentence was pronounced distinguished, illustrated, expressed with so great eloquence. Why are words still being given? Why is there contention against the most lucid truth on behalf of a manifest error? Why do men deceive themselves? If you bind and wrap more tightly the snares of the devil, which you ought to loosen and break, receive still that whence you may more vehemently—and would that with some fruit of correction—blush.

**3. 56. 62.** In the said Bagai council the day and consul were written down, not only of when the decree of the council was framed, but also of the delay itself. Therefore from the eighth day before the calends of May after the consulship of Theodosius Augustus for the third time and of Abundantius—which is the day of the Bagai council—up to the eighth day before the calends of January—which is the day of the delay—about eight months are counted. But there is found a petition before the proconsul Herodes—see how long after the things said against Felicianus and Praetextatus—about expelling them from the places of Musti and Assuras, of which I have subjoined a few things: "After the consulship of our lords Arcadius for the third time and Honorius for the second, Augusti, on the sixth day before the nones of March, at Carthage, in the secretarium of the praetorium, Titianus said: The presbyter Peregrinus and the elders of the Church of Musti and of the region of Assuras pursue such a desire: When the man of venerable memory, Donatus, was asserting the holiness of the Catholic Church from the error of perfidy, the observance of almost the whole world, nourished into his name and worship, grew together. But when the poisons of a certain Maximian were polluting the praiseworthy and admirable purpose of that religion, an assembly of many bishops, gathered into one with God conspiring, condemned the man—or rather the pestilence which displeased the supernal majesty—even by the coercion proper to a pure mind. Those also whom the error of another's presumption had drawn in, it restrained, with an equal admonition of vigor, having first set forth a harbor of repentance, if they wished to return within the time appointed to the path of religion. But iniquity delights in its own institutions, and does not desert itself, when once, headlong, it has fallen. For that same Maximian nourishes the boldness he began, and associates others to himself unto fury. Among whom also a certain Felicianus, who, first following the right, is darkened by the defilement of this depravity—set in the city of Musti—believed that the walls consecrated to Almighty God, and the venerable Church, must be held as by a kind of siege. This man Praetextatus too imitates in the parts of Assuras. But when the fellowship of the priests became known to the power of your equity, you commanded, as the acts testify, that, every effect of contradiction being exploded, the churches vindicated from profane minds ought to be restored to the most sacred priests." You see, surely, that up to the day of this petition, from that of the appointed delay, a time of about three months has elapsed. But this conflict is prolonged, as far as we could trace from the proconsular and municipal acts, up to the proconsul Theodorus—that is, up to the eleventh day before the calends of January of the following year, on which day the clergy and elders acting under the bishop Rogatus, who had been substituted in the place of the condemned Praetextatus of Assuras, alleged the order of the said proconsul, when they were outside your communion, and the enemies of that same communion of yours were being arraigned in public judgments, and were being demanded to be expelled from the places consecrated to the most high God as though sacrilegious.

**3. 57. 63.** Of whatever number, then, they were—although it sufficiently appears of which they were—who within the appointed day of delay did not restore themselves to your communion, how could they, alienated from you all that time, baptize? How did they return, with those whom they baptized outside your communion, not expiated by another baptism? In which deed we do not reprehend you, that you recognized the baptism of Christ, by no means sacrilegious, even in a sacrilegious schism; that, with the perversity of men corrected, you approved that which had been right even in the perverse; that you did not confound human faults with the divine seals; that the gifts of the Church—which, apart from the Church too, could be had and given unto the punishment of those having and receiving them—you judged should be neither condemned in the condemned nor changed in those received.

**3. 58. 64.** Nor do we reprehend this, that those very defendants of a notorious crime, even after the sentence of their condemnation and after the day of delay appointed for others, you nevertheless, on account of the weak peoples who adhered to them—recalling, I believe, the solicitude of the householder, lest together with the weeds the wheat too be rooted up[^49]—believed should be gathered without any degradation, and expiated by the very abundance of love. Nor do we reprehend this, that you persecuted them even through earthly powers, while they were still held by the notorious sacrilege of schism. For this was done, as the outcome itself indicated, out of zeal for correcting, not for harming, so that, agitated by those troubles, they might be compelled to consider their crime, and, the furies of animosity being repressed, to amend it. But because we do not reprehend these things, therefore we rightly blame the crime of your schism, deservedly detest it, by every reasoning convict it, by which you separate yourselves from us—nay rather, from the Catholic communion of the whole world—objecting to us such things as you cannot, I reckon, deny that you yourselves did in the case of the Maximianians. For if there is the baptism of Christ, and therefore in no way to be destroyed, which was given through Felicianus and Praetextatus, when, separated from you, they were joined to Maximian, condemned with him by you for the crime of a nefarious schism, how is it not the baptism of Christ, or how is it to be destroyed, which is given in the Church which, to use Cyprian's words, extends its branches through the whole earth in the abundance of its fertility; which is given, finally, in those churches which you never ceased to recite in the apostolic letters, never condemned by any council as you did Felicianus and Praetextatus? If it pleased to restore the condemned to their whole estate for the patching up of the party of Donatus, what offends you in the unity of Christ diffused through the whole world, which either could not condemn the unheard, or altogether neither condemned nor absolved the unknown, or believed innocent those whom it learned to be absolved? If you expelled from their seats the Maximianians cut off from you, by persecuting them through the commands of the judges, why do you complain that you suffer unworthy things through the emperors, who send those same judges—you who by a nefarious schism cut yourselves off from that Church of which it is written that *all the kings of the earth will adore him, all nations will serve him, and that he will have dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth*[^50]?

**3. 59. 65.** Behold, now I do not say: if it is not lawful to make persecution, Optatus made it, lest you still say that whatever Optatus did with them unknowing does not pertain to your people, when they themselves do not permit lands most remote from Africa to say that whatever Caecilian did with them unknowing does not pertain to them. Who were betrayers in Africa, what evil the African bishops did, we do not know; but plainly I say, if it is not lawful to make persecution, your people made it against the Maximianians in such a way that you cannot deny it. If those who suffer persecution are innocent, the Maximianians suffered it. Will you even now say: "The people, with no one of our people as author, destroyed the basilica or cave of Maximian"? Although, if it be inquired of what communion that crowd was—lest they be believed sent by your people—they themselves will perhaps be found to be yours, or certainly mingled in by helping yours. But what is that to us? For you answer: "We did not do it, we did not send them in, who they were we do not know." Yet that he whom you confess to be unjust suffered persecution from whomsoever—since the matter is clear—the things you suffer cannot demonstrate you to be just. But by whom even the Maximianians suffered persecution, the proconsular acts testify. Advocates were instructed, judgments approached and stirred up as against heretics; those men sought protection for themselves as possessors, your people alleged the Bagai council and demanded that the condemned be expelled from the sacred seats, it was pressed, the case was pleaded, when, with Salvius of Membressa present, you showed them to be heretics, conquered them, expelled them. I see, then, that they suffered persecution, that you made it; I seek the just, you will declare yourselves to be them; I conclude: not, then, is he who suffers it consequently just, nor he who makes it consequently unjust.

**3. 60. 66.** You again will accuse the dialectical arts; yet you will at least silently approve that I say the truth, and you will choose rather to correct your opinion, by which you said that there is no just persecution, than to call the persecutors of the Maximianians unjust, whom you have at last recognized, by the proconsular acts, to be yours. Not indeed without fruit did you persecute the error of your schismatics; for Felicianus and Praetextatus you corrected by that same conflict and those troubles. And Optatus indeed, the Gildonian, threatening graver destructions, the people of Musti and Assuras are said to have feared—as I heard from them too, when present—and to have compelled their bishops to return to the communion of Primian. But since Optatus exacted this by no public acts, what should I do with such men against you, ready to deny whatever you can deny? There exist proconsular and municipal acts, by which, when recited, we showed with how great force the said men were urged by your people to yield their places. We do not blame, we do not envy: you did not labor in vain, you did not terrify to no purpose, you did not act fruitlessly. Their animosity displeased them when afflicted, they were broken, they were amended, they were corrected and received after their condemnation, after the delay of others, after your persecution. But they were received as they had been honored, with no humiliation of graver penance either expiated or degraded—neither they themselves, nor those with whom they were received, whom they had separated with themselves against you, had baptized outside you, whom perhaps they had rebaptized after you.

**3. 61. 67.** You now have nothing whence to thrust dark lies upon men ignorant of matters long past. That your people then accused Caecilian before the emperor Constantine, the public monuments proclaim. That the case was pleaded and brought to its due end, even up to the clearing of Felix of Aptungi, the ordainer of Caecilian, whom in the Carthaginian council they called the fountain of all evils, and whom, as he himself indicates in his writings, they accused before the same Constantine with assiduous interpellations, the proconsular archives testify. You resist, you clamor against, you fight against the most open truth; you say even that the overseas judges were corrupted by Caecilian, that the emperor himself was depraved by I know not what favor; for the conquered accuser is the more shameless the more he becomes also the slanderer of the judge. Yet among all those lies of yours, by which you slander the overseas judges, at least we obtain this—that your first forebears brought this case to the emperor, first accused Caecilian and his ordainer before the emperor, first persecuted Caecilian and his associates before the emperor. Whence, as it seems to you, you stir up most grave envy against us, because, conquered, you suffer what you would surely have done as victors—just as if those wished to slander Daniel who, when he was set free as an innocent man, were themselves consumed by those lions by whom they wished him to be consumed by calumny[^51]. We obtain also this: whatever you may think or feign about the judges by whom Caecilian, present, was absolved in the overseas judgment, whatever about him himself before whom your forebears accused Caecilian, whose judgment finally they chose, preferred to the episcopal judgment—the emperor Constantine, as though corrupted by favor—yet all who then were, whether in near or in remote lands so far and wide diffused, Catholic Christians to whom the report about Caecilian and his colleagues could reach, ought to have believed, not the conquered accusers, but the ecclesiastical judges. For where we cannot all be judges, we better believe those who could be than that, by believing the conquered litigants whose judges we could not be, we should dare to judge about the judges themselves.

**3. 62. 68.** Accordingly, since those by whose accusation Caecilian, present, was absolved, tried with most bold wickedness to bespatter, not only those by whom he was absolved, but even all Catholic Christians established in any nations, who either were altogether ignorant of that dissension of the Africans or in it preferred to believe the judges pronouncing, as it seemed to them, at their own peril, rather than the conquered accusers—to bespatter them with the crimes of Caecilian, whether feigned or certainly unproved, and to deny that all are Christians: the case of the Maximianians was at last sent to you, in which they themselves should condemn, themselves persecute the condemned, themselves receive the persecuted into the same honor, themselves accept the baptism of the condemned, so that those who dared to accuse the innocent should be compelled to absolve the guilty—not those whom they had believed innocent, but those whom, as they say, "by the truth-speaking judgment of God presiding, they had condemned by the mouth of their plenary council." Who ever said to anyone: because you believed the judge absolving rather than me accusing, you are guilty along with those whom I accused? And yet this is said to the Christian world, which, if it were said to one man, would seem, I do not say most iniquitous, but most insane. O thing marvelously unworthy! Africans accused Africans of the crime of handing over, the overseas judges absolved the accused, and the peoples of the nations were made betrayers, because they believed the judges absolving rather than the accusers charging.

**3. 63. 69.** Deservedly, party of Donatus, the case of the Maximianians is offered to you to drink: drink the cup from the hand of the Lord correcting and admonishing. If you understand and acquiesce, it is the mercy of one amending, lest you fall into the judgment of one punishing. To you it is said: O proud and stiff of neck, be reconciled to the Christian peoples everywhere whom you iniquitously accused, being now at least reconciled to your own condemned. Why do you rescind the baptism of Christ in those, or from those churches, which the Apostles founded? You have already received the baptism which your condemned, before they were reconciled to you, gave. Why do you glory in the persecution you suffer? If it is a sign of righteousness, the party of Maximian is more righteous; for it both suffered from you and suffers with you. Hear the divine psalm: *Do not be like the horse and the mule, who have no understanding*[^52]. For you call us your persecutors, when, desiring you to be saved, we medicinally pursue your wounds which we wish to be healed; whence by the clergy and Circumcellions, as by your teeth and heels, we are gravely afflicted while we cure you. Do not be ungrateful for such medicine, which you have imitated; you too corrected Felicianus and Praetextatus by persecutions. And would that you corrected the whole, and, as they returned to you, so they and you returned to the Catholic mother.

**3. 63. 70.** Against which, brother Cresconius, you have been raised up with such great boldness that you tried to refute even those divine testimonies mentioned by me, and to clamor against the words of God. For I set down in that letter against which you speak what is in the holy book about the seed of Abraham, God saying to that father of ours: *In your seed all nations will be blessed*[^53]. Which the Apostle calls a testament, saying: *Brethren, I speak after the manner of men. Yet no one annuls or adds to a man's testament when it has been confirmed. To Abraham the promises were made, and to his seed. He does not say, And to seeds, as of many. But as of one, And to your seed, which is Christ*[^54]. The fertility of this seed was promised so copious that it was said to him: *So will your seed be, as the stars of heaven and as the sand of the sea which cannot be numbered*[^55]. To this multiplication and abundance of the Church, which is spread through the whole world, which is so believed to have been prophesied, is so exhibited to the eyes even of all the unfaithful, that it shuts even the mouths of the pagans, who, very few, have remained against it—you dare to prefer the party of Donatus, saying that you have, even beyond Africa, I know not whom, of whom, however, since they do not appear, you do not deny that you are accustomed to send bishops from Africa, one or two or at most three; and you argue vainly against the most evident truth, that the whole world therefore does not commune with us, because either there are still many barbarian nations which have not yet believed in Christ, or under the name of Christ many heresies alien from the communion of our fellowship.

**3. 64. 71.** Nor do you attend, nor at least ask of those who know, how many of those barbarian nations you mentioned are already given over to the name of Christ, and how the Gospel does not cease, by growing, to bear fruit in the rest, until, when it has been preached among all, the end comes. For the Lord himself says: *And this Gospel will be preached in the whole world for a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come*[^56]. Unless perhaps insane pride hurls you even into such a precipice that you think what was foretold ought to begin from the party of Donatus. Not from those churches which the Apostles planted, with which the party of Donatus does not commune. Or will it try to rebaptize even these, in order to fill the world, when, with the Catholic Church growing through all things, that party is daily diminished even in Africa itself? O insane perversity of men! You believe yourself to be praised for believing in Christ what you do not see, and you do not believe yourself to be condemned for denying about the Church what you see; since that head is in heaven, this body on earth.

**3. 64. 72.** You recognize Christ in what is written, *Be exalted above the heavens, O God*, and you do not recognize the Church in what follows, *and over all the earth your glory*[^57]. You recognize Christ in what is written, *They dug my hands and feet, they numbered all my bones. They themselves considered and looked upon me, they divided my garments among them and over my robe cast lots*[^58], and you do not recognize the Church in what follows a little after, *All the ends of the earth will be reminded and turned to the Lord, and all the families of the nations will adore in his sight, for the kingdom is the Lord's, and he will have dominion over the nations*[^59]. You recognize Christ in what is written, *O God, give your judgment to the king, and your righteousness to the king's son*, and you do not recognize the Church in what the same psalm testifies: *he will have dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth. Before him the Ethiopians will fall down, and his enemies will lick the earth. The kings of Tarshish and the islands will offer gifts, the kings of the Arabs and of Sheba will bring presents. And all the kings of the earth will adore him, all nations will serve him*[^60]. You recognize Christ where it is said to the Jews, *I have no pleasure in you, says the Lord Almighty, nor will I accept sacrifices from your hands*[^61]—because surely the coming of Christ took away all those sacrifices of the Jews—and you do not recognize the Church in what follows: *for from the rising of the sun to its setting my name has been glorified among the nations, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering. For my name is great among the nations, says the Lord Almighty*[^62]. You recognize Christ in what the prophet says, *Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter*[^63], and the rest which are there read of him as in the Gospel, and you do not recognize the Church in what he says a little after: *Rejoice, O barren one who do not bear, break forth and cry out, you who do not travail, for the children of the deserted one are more than of her who has a husband. For the Lord said: Enlarge the place of your tent and fix fast your halls—there is no reason for you to spare—stretch out your cords farther, strengthen your stakes, extend them again and again to the right and to the left. For your seed will inherit the nations, and you will inhabit the cities which were deserted. There is no reason for you to fear—for you will prevail—nor to blush that you were detestable. For you will forget your confusion forever, you will not remember the disgrace of your widowhood. For I am the Lord who made you, the Lord is his name, and he who delivered you, the same God of Israel, will be called of the whole earth*[^64].

**3. 65. 73.** In these holy writings, certainly, Cyprian learned the Church, so that he said: "So the Church of the Lord, suffused with light, stretched out its rays through the whole world, extended its branches through the whole earth in the abundance of its fertility." Against this so great manifestation of the divine words, Cresconius, you slander, by gazing at the remnants of the nations which the Church has not yet occupied; how much it has occupied, whence it is daily diffused to hold the remnants, you do not attend. For how would you not deny the future perfection of these prophecies, you who do not hesitate to deny so great an advance, to which the very perfection is owed—I do not say against the divine voices, but now even against your own? For even from you it was wrung out by the great force of truth itself, that you said, not knowing or not attending to what you said, that "into the Christian name the whole world is daily turned." Why, then, does the party of Donatus not commune with this Church, which by growing is spread through the whole world? Forsooth, lest it be polluted by sinners. And where is it, that Cyprian was unwilling to desert—not this Church of yours, which, remaining in Africa, slanders the world, or sojourns out of Africa in a few hidden ones, but that one which extends its branches through the whole earth in the abundance of its fertility—even if in its communion there be, nay even appear, sinners, saying: "For even if there seem to be weeds in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we discern that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church"?

**3. 66. 74.** Here you too answer, if it pleases you to answer anything true. And how will that be fulfilled which is written, *A bad son calls himself just, but does not wash away his going out*[^65]? Let the bad son be convicted, let him condemn and persecute the Maximianians, let him be reconciled to the condemned and persecuted: let him either be thus convicted, or thus confounded, or thus corrected. "How," you say, "is the whole world full of your communion, where there are so many heresies, of which none communes with you?" Nay rather, not only with heretics, but even with the rest of bad men is the world full, and with the holy and faithful servants of God the world is full; because the sea too is full of bitter waves, and full of sweet fishes.

**3. 66. 75.** "Among the few," you say, "the truth is frequently found, to err belongs to the many"; and lest by your words you should seem to contradict the fertility of that so great barren one, to whom it was said, *The children of the deserted one are more than of her who has a husband*[^66], you brought forward a testimony from the Gospel: *that few are those who are saved*[^67]. Solve, then, the question—how the Lord himself says, *How strait and narrow is the way that leads to life, and few are those who find it*[^68], and the same says elsewhere, *Many will come from the east and the west, and recline with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven*[^69]; how also in the Apocalypse is demonstrated their multitude, which no one can number, from every nation and tribe and tongue, having white robes and bearing palms, who had sustained tribulations for the faith of Christ[^70]—how the same are few who are many. For neither of these is true, the other false, since both were uttered by divine truth, except that these same good and true Christians, who in themselves are many, in comparison with the bad and false are likewise few. So many grains, by which great barns are filled, we call few in comparison with the chaff; so too—to speak of that testament of God which was made to Abraham concerning his seed, which is Christ—the many stars which we cannot number, by which so vast a heaven gleams on every side, we call few in comparison with the sand of the sea. Perhaps the stars signify spiritual Christians, but the sand of the sea the carnal, through whom and from whom even heresies and schisms come about. Yet with both kinds the world is full, because the same Lord says, *the field is this world*[^71], and you yourself said to yourself, the truth wresting it out, "Into the name of Christ the whole world is daily turned." Through the whole field, then—that is, through the whole world—is the wheat, through the whole the weeds, because of both he who cannot be deceived said: *let both grow until the harvest*[^72].

**3. 67. 76.** Let the impious deserters, therefore, restrain themselves from the wheat growing with so great abundance through the whole world, and not dare to glory in the fewness of certain separated weeds. But if they glory, even in this very thing those same Maximianians proceed against them in the divine judgment, set to confound them in all things and, if they are wise, to correct them—whom, far fewer, they persecuted by overconfidence in their own multitude, and by persecuting converted some of them anew to their own communion, and so left the rest, most just, the fewer.

**3. 67. 77.** "The East," you say, "does not commune with Africa, nor Africa with the East." Not indeed in the heretical chaff separated from the Lord's threshing floor; but in the Catholic wheat and the inner chaff, the East altogether communes with Africa and Africa with the East. For some heretics here, others elsewhere and elsewhere, fight with the everywhere-diffused Catholic unity. For it is everywhere, that one from which went out those who could by no means be everywhere, saying according to what was foretold of them, *Behold, here is Christ. Behold, there*[^73]—some in this place, others in that, showing the proper little particles of their presumptions, or rather amputations, and by impious pride denying the root from which they were broken off. To this Church, then, which, with copious successions, while it is spread through the whole world, brings forth from every nation, tribe, and tongue a multitude of those clothed in white and bearing palms, as it is written in the Apocalypse[^74], which no one can number—to this Church, I say, with which it is manifest that the party of Donatus does not commune, your forebears ought to have proved whatever true proofs they had about the betrayers. But if they had done this, they themselves would be in it, but outside it those whom they accused. But now, since we see that the accused remained in it, what good ought we to think of the accusers, whom we see outside it? And through this, in that fourfold distribution of mine, where I said that, when proofs are brought forward on both sides about the betrayers, either both are true, or both false, or ours true and yours false, or ours false and yours true, since you saw that in the first three you were most easily overcome, in vain you chose this last for yourself, as though by it you might escape. For although you may notice how shamelessly it is said, yet those very true human proofs of yours, if there were any, ought to have been proved to that Church which the divine proofs prove.

**3. 68. 78.** Tell me, I beg you—but do not pour mists over the unskilled, so that, because you cannot convict the accused betrayers, you should over and over wish to accuse the dialectical arts—tell me, then, I ask, was this case of yours, with your true proofs, brought to the judgment of the overseas churches founded by apostolic labor, or was it not brought? If it was brought, did you win in that judgment, or were you conquered? If you say you won, why did you not commune with those churches in whose judgment you won? But if—what your communion, alienated from them, sufficiently indicates—you were conquered, why do you litigate with us over a cause lost, whether bad or good, having this as your greatest crime, that with the crime of betrayers, whom in the judgment of the overseas churches, even bringing forward true proofs, you could by no means convict, you bespatter the Christian world, because in that case, in which it could not be present, it chose to believe the judges, not the conquered accusers? Not, then, are you guilty for this reason, that you lost a good cause—to speak according to you—in the overseas judgment, but because you by no means hesitate to object the crime of the defendants—and, to yield too much to you, even of the judges—to so many innocent Christian peoples diffused far and wide through so many nations; to whose communion, by cleaving as to the Lord's wheat, you ought, according to the evangelical writings, even with Cyprian admonishing, to have tolerated as chaff, up to the time of winnowing, both those men, as you say betrayers, and those bad judges, as you reckon, lest by deserting the threshing floor you should perish. But if that case of yours, with its true proofs—which you chose for yourself—was not brought to the judgment of the overseas churches, how could so many bishops, established among their own peoples, be justly condemned by them, the case unknown and not at all referred to them? Or how ought the African Christians—I do not say those who had thought them innocent, but even those, if any, who knew them to be betrayers—on account of the weeds which they discerned to be in the Church, to separate themselves from the so manifest innocence of the wheat growing so far and wide and ignorant of these men, so that, on account of another's crime, by which, tolerated for unity, they would not be stained, they should be condemned by the crime of that very unity violated?

**3. 69. 79.** What, then, does it profit your cause to have chosen this from those four propositions—that your proofs be true, but those which we bring forward against your people be reckoned false? Behold, here too you are conquered, since by your proofs which you think true, the betrayers could not be convicted where they ought to have been—whether those same proofs were concealed by your people, or the betrayers themselves by some cunning hid themselves from the judges, or were hidden by those very depraved judges. Look back to the seed of Abraham, which in the testament of God grows through all nations like the stars of heaven and like the sand of the sea[^75], and dare to say, dare to believe, dare—if there is any fear of God in you—to think, whether on account of some African weeds, concealed for whatever causes, so copious a harvest could perish through the field of the world.

**3. 69. 80.** You exaggerate the persecutions which you say you suffer, by which, while you rebel in so sacrilegious and manifest an obstinacy against the peace of the holy Church, you are spared with marvelous gentleness; and you say in the first parts of your letter that "when the crime of Caecilian became manifest to the emperor Constantine, he too by his own sentence condemned him to live in exile at Brixia." In which who would doubt that you are either deceived or deceiving, when you also mentioned that Felix of Aptungi was convicted, in the judgment of the proconsul, of handing over, by I know not what Ingentius?

**3. 70. 80.** Behold, I insert the sentence of the proconsul Aelianus, by which Felix was cleared and absolved. If you wish to read the whole acts, receive them from the archive of the proconsul. "Aelianus the proconsul said: From the declaration of Caecilian, who says that the acts are false and that very much was added to the letter, it is manifest with what intention Ingentius did these things, and therefore he will be taken back into prison; for he is necessary for a stricter interrogation. But that Felix, the religious bishop, is free from the burning of the deific instruments is manifest, since no one was able to prove anything against him—that he handed over or burned the religious Scriptures. For the interrogation of all the above-written is manifest, that no deific Scriptures were either found or corrupted or burned. This is contained in the acts, that Felix the religious bishop in those times was neither present, nor lent his connivance, nor ordered any such thing to be done."

**3. 70. 81.** I insert also the rescript of the emperor Constantine to Probianus attesting this same thing, and showing how troublesome accusers of innocent men your forebears were before him. "The emperors Caesars Flavius Constantinus and Maximinus and Valerius Licinianus Licinius to Probianus, proconsul of Africa. Aelianus your predecessor deservedly—when the true vicar of the prefects from the rank of the perfectissimi was at that time detained by ill health—having performed the duties of those same parts, believed that among other things this business too, or rather odium, which seems to have been stirred up against the bishop Caecilian and the Catholic Church, should be recalled to his own examination and command. For when he had now caused Superius the centurion, and Caecilianus the magistrate of the Aptungitans, and Saturninus the former curator, and Calibius the younger, curator of the same city, and the sole public slave of the above-written city, to be present, he afforded a competent hearing, so that, when it had been objected to Caecilian that the episcopate seemed to have been conferred on him by Felix, against whom the betrayal and burning of the divine Scriptures seemed to be objected, it was established that Felix was innocent of this. Finally, when Maximus contended that Ingentius, a decurion of the city of the Ziquenses, had falsified a letter of Caecilian from the duumvirs, we saw that same Ingentius, racked upon the acts which were at hand, and therefore by no means tortured, because he asserted that he was a decurion of the city of the Ziquenses. Whence we wish that you send that same Ingentius under suitable escort to the court of me, Constantine Augustus, so that, with those who at present are acting and do not cease to interpellate on the daily days hearing and standing by, it may be able to appear and be made known that they wished in vain to procure odium against the bishop Caecilian and to rise up violently against him. For so it will come about that, such contentions being set aside, as is fitting, the people, without any dissension, may serve their own religion with due veneration."

**3. 71. 82.** I insert still also the words of Constantine from his letter to Eumalius the vicar, where he testifies that he had inquired between the parties and found Caecilian innocent. For when he had related, in those things which he said above, how the parties had been brought to his judgment after the episcopal judgments: "In which I perceived," he says, "that Caecilian was a man endowed with all innocence, and observing the due offices of his religion, and serving it as he ought, nor could any crime be found in him, as had evidently appeared; just as it had been composed against him, absent, by the dissimulation of his adversaries."

**3. 71. 83.** Why did you not insert the sentence of Constantine by which you say he was condemned and set in exile at Brixia, most eloquent man? How much more fittingly you would have inserted this than I know not what of the Serdican council, which is shown to pertain neither to you nor to the cause which is between us and you at all. For why Caecilian was at Brixia—which you most slanderously call exile, when he preferred his presence to be absent from the Church rather than its peace—what need is there for me to say? You, meanwhile, recite no sentence of a condemning emperor, and yet rashly think that Caecilian, condemned by the emperor, should not, I do not say be heard or said, but even be written. Yet according to your words, behold, I see Caecilian in exile, with the emperor condemning him: at least answer by whose accusation, and now ask me what, in your letter, after many empty things, you likewise emptily set down: "Who consents less to the testament when it is brought forward—he who suffers persecution, or he who makes it?" These, certainly, are your words. Look, then, at Caecilian suffering persecution and, as you yourself said, set in exile; look also at your people, as the emperor himself testifies in his own words, "not ceasing on the daily days to interpellate" against Caecilian, and answer me asking in these very words of yours: "Who consents less to the testament when it is brought forward—he who suffers persecution, or he who makes it?" But when you have diligently read everything through, you will find that Caecilian suffered persecution from your people even before the emperor, but you will not find him condemned by the emperor; nay, you will even find him absolved.

**3. 72. 84.** Moreover, if—what you chose for yourself as a great thing, that of the crimes of handing over the proofs you bring forward are true, but those we bring forward are false—even in that the truth of God overcomes you, which, as it foretold, so renders the Church fruiting and growing in the whole world, since the proofs of others' crimes, though true, prejudice it in nothing—proofs which, to the nearer overseas bishops, through whom into more distant parts either the report of such matters passes or does not pass, either were not demonstrated as they ought, or were not believed by those to whom they could be demonstrated, or, believed and concealed, by no means reached others; nor can anyone, not even one man—how much less so great a number of Christians established among so many nations—be guilty of a shared crime of another, if he has either known none of its true proofs, or someone has deceived him by a feigned innocence with false proofs: if, then, as I had begun to say, in this, which you chose for yourself as a great thing—that the testimonies you bring forward about betrayers be true, but those brought forward against you false—you avail nothing against the Catholic Church diffused through the whole world and against the providence of God, by which "into the Christian name," to use your words, "the whole world is daily turned"; how much more do you fail in your accusations, when "this very handing over"—at whose name, as it were shuddering, those who made this schism, separated from the body of Christ, handed over themselves to the devil—much "more probably," as I said in that letter and now say, "we object to you," who only hear betrayers named and the crimes of betrayers spoken by you, but we show both the ecclesiastical acts, by which they themselves confessed about themselves, and the municipal acts, by which they are read to have done this.

**3. 73. 85.** But you, a clever examiner and weigher of words, teach us what the comparative degree means, and exult as though a victor, that I could not say "We object handing over to you more probably," unless I confessed that you object it to us probably. "For if you," you say, "more probably, then we probably"; and teaching this: "that 'probably' and 'more probably' are so related that, if you say 'truly' and 'more truly,' and because that degree augments what was placed before, does not disprove what was said before," you add other words, by which we may understand this more evidently, and say: "So there is 'well' and 'better,' 'badly' and 'worse,' 'horribly' and 'more horribly,'" showing that "probably" and "more probably" are so related. Whence you think it is gathered that, if I object more probably what is false, it remains that you should confirm that you have objected probably what is true. Here do I say that, in an ecclesiastical question or disputation, you try to teach us the technical points of grammar—what the comparative degree means, "that it augments what was placed before, does not disprove what was said before"? For I see how ineptly I would object to you what you were unwilling to see. When you thought you should object to me "counter-accusation" as though from the art of rhetoric—which kind of question, where it is said, "I did not do it, but you did it," I demonstrated even in prophetic authority.

**3. 74. 86.** Yet consider more diligently, lest perhaps you find in the authors of Latin speech, whom the technical points of the grammarians serve, that this degree does not always augment what was placed before, but sometimes is borne over to the contrary. An example of which speech for the moment occurs to me from the Apostle's letter which he wrote to the Hebrews. For when he was speaking of the blessing of the earth, which, having received rain, returns fruit, he then subjoined: *but bringing forth thorns and thistles, it is reprobate and near to a curse, whose end is in burning*[^76]. And lest he should seem to have wished this for those to whom he was speaking, *but we are confident*, he says, *about you, dearest brethren, of better things and things that cling to salvation*[^77]. You attend, surely, how here he said *better things*, not because the things he had said above were good—to bring forth thorns and thistles and to merit burning—but rather because they were bad, so that, those being avoided, they might choose and wish for better, that is, good things contrary to such great evils. But perhaps you think the Apostle should be heard in the way he himself says of himself, as a man "though unskilled in speech, yet not in knowledge"[^78], and therefore you think that not the authority of words in him, but of things and meanings, is to be followed, contending that "good things" ought to have been placed where he himself placed "better things." What, if I too, now accustomed to these letters and having forgotten those I learned as a boy, said thus: "You object handing over. This very thing much more probably we object to you," as though I said "probably," placing the comparative for the positive—not because you probably, but rather because improbably, just as he said "better things," not because those were good, but rather because bad? Why do you so rashly conclude from this that I have confirmed that you object probably, because I said that we object more probably?

**3. 75. 87.** Although neither would you, even according to the grammarians, reprehend me as having erred in a word, if you had at least wished to read attentively, or to recall from memory, the authors of those very words. For there are boyish letters, but not said by an unskilled boy:  
*The gods grant better things to the pious, and that error to the enemies!*  
*They tore the torn limbs with bare teeth*[^79].  
How, then, "better things to the pious," as though those were good things for these men, and not rather great evils, who "tore the torn limbs with bare teeth"? Now you see surely that, just as this poet could wish "better things" for the pious, although those things in comparison with which he wished them were not good, so I too could say: "Handing over we object to you more probably," although you did not object it to us probably—to pass over that even "probable," when it is said, is not consequently true, since those things are said which can and are wont to happen, and are therefore probable—that is, approved and believed—even if they were not done; but what is true, when it is shown, is necessarily probable, and surely more probable than that. Recall the books where you learned words: you will surely find how I do not deceive you.

**3. 76. 87.** Whence, even if I granted that your people objected handing over to ours probably, I would not therefore contend that they objected something true, and I would rightly say that we object it more probably, since from you we are accustomed only to hear betrayers named, but by no acts, whether ecclesiastical or public, recited and demonstrated as having confessed; but by us are brought forward both public acts, where your people handed over, and ecclesiastical ones, where, having confessed, they were left to God the judge.

**3. 77. 88.** But I do not pass over so manifest a help of yours, by which, even unknowing, you greatly favor us, by teaching the rules of the comparative degree, "that it augments what was placed before, does not disprove what was said before." For according to this rule of yours, according to certain words of yours in your letter, without any difficulty we have obtained our cause. For you said in the first parts of your letter, as though charging our obstinacy, that "so many legal proofs cannot persuade us what is better and truer." Where I conclude, according to your technical points, that we now hold the true and good, if what is truer and better cannot be persuaded to us. Since, then, we do not rescind, to speak according to you, the truer and better baptism of yours, why do you rescind the true and good baptism of ours? Likewise, when you had said, "This we too wish, that Christ be the origin and root and head of the Christian," you at once subjoined: "but we ask through whom this is better done." By saying which you surely conceded that it is done well even through a bad minister, but better through a good one. Since, therefore, we do not rescind the baptism which you say is given through your minister as through a good one, why do you rescind the baptism which you contend was given through ours as through a bad one? For you said: "We ask through whom it is better done," and your rule is: "that this degree augments what was placed before." Whence, if through your minister, as you think, it is done better, then through ours too it is done well, and through this, when you rebaptize one baptized through us, you rescind by sacrilegious presumption that which, from this rule of yours, you concede to have been done well.

**3. 78. 89.** I fear lest I press your modesty too much, if I should show how lightly and scurrilously, as though urbane and witty, you thought certain words of mine too which I set down figuratively—"brow" for shame, "mouth" for speech, "trident weapon" for threefold speech, "three-headed beast" for the error raging with three calumnies against the innocence of so many peoples—ought to be harried. Keep boyish things rather for boys. For it does not move me that you say the Neptunian weapon, on account of the trident, does not befit a bishop, since it is also a fisherman's, and through this apostolic, in that the Lord made his apostles fishers of men[^80]. For Scripture says that our God too has both wings and arrows[^81], and yet we do not worship Cupid.

**3. 78. 90.** Moreover you reprehend me for not having kept the gentleness promised in my exordium, in that, mention being made of the Manichees, moved, I said: "Just as Satan cannot, as the Lord says, cast out Satan[^82], so the error of the Donatists cannot overturn the error of the Manichees"—as though I had compared Petilian to Satan, and not the error, from whose snares I desire him to be freed. It is well that the Apostle fortified us against such calumnies in that very place where he admonishes us to be mild, patient, and modest, when we correct one who dissents. For when he had said, *but the servant of the Lord must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all, an apt teacher, patient, correcting with modesty those who think otherwise*, he at once subjoined, *that perhaps God may grant them repentance to know the truth, and they may recover from the snares of the devil, having been captured by him according to his will*[^83]. Behold apostolic gentleness! Those with whom he commanded that one deal calmly, patiently, and modestly, those same he says are captured by the devil, and yet he did not lose the gentleness which he was commending, because he was unwilling to keep silent the truth which he was teaching.

**3. 79. 91.** But see for yourself whose part you play, who, by charging my words as abusive and savage, strive to stir up to litigation those whom you are unwilling to convene for disputation; for I am unwilling to say that it delights you too to litigate. For more sparingly indeed and more modestly, but as though obliquely, you object to me things which do not pertain to me at all. For what should I judge about the most pestilential and—by all Christians to be anathematized—vanity of the Manichees, if I were only accustomed to say it, and did not even testify by many and manifold books written, not even so would there be any place for your calumny against me. For the Catholic Church would not marvel that I am falsely charged by you, by whom the whole Christian world, with so many churches—even those first ones propagated by apostolic labor—is accused with false crimes. But since against the Manichees, who had once deceived me as a youth, my so great writings anyone who wishes can read, no one is so foolish as to believe about me your words rather than his own eyes and senses.

**3. 80. 92.** But "a letter of your prince," you say, "in which he wrote I know not what about you when he did not wish you to be ordained, not a few of our people hold." That letter, indeed, I would least care for—not even if a sentence, not unclear, on our behalf were read of him by whom it is said to have been written, condemning that calumny and falsehood; how much less does the Catholic Church care for it, whose cause we are pleading against you—the Church which, confident in so many divine testimonies, no human testimonies about any man whatever, whether true or false, deprive of the truth in which it rejoices! Cease from such things: I am one man. The cause of the Church is being dealt with between us, not mine—of the Church, I say, which learned from its Redeemer to place its hope in no man. Although neither about myself ought you, who are my enemies, even if you knew my manner of life, rightly to be believed. For the estimation of men, there is a great supply of witnesses who have known me; but for the sight of God, the conscience alone, which, although I bear it undaunted against your accusations, I do not yet dare to justify myself under the eyes of the Almighty, and I rather await from him the overflowing largesse of mercy than the highest examination of judgment, considering what is written: *When the just king sits on the throne, who will boast that he has a chaste heart, or who will boast that he is clean from sin?*[^84]

**3. 81. 93.** But what is it to the matter of which the question turns between us, what sort I myself am, since in the Lord's threshing floor I am stubble if bad, grain if good? Yet you, if you were grain, would not flee the chaff mingled in, before the time of winnowing, as Cyprian himself too admonishes[^85]. Accordingly we, if we have found in you a known bad man, rightly object him to you; for there your whole defense consists, that you separated yourselves on this account, lest you perish by the contagion of others' sins. Whence you boast that you made a new kind of threshing floor, either having only wheat, or in which only wheat appears, to which a winnower is not necessary, but a searcher. For your Parmenian too, comparing your splendid cleanness, as though from the contrary, with our uncleanness, dared to insert a sentence of the prophet Jeremiah, where he says, *What has the chaff to do with the wheat?*[^86] Which he said, as the reading itself indicates, against those who equated their own dreams with the divine oracles. Yet your arrogance and horrible pride is declared by Parmenian's letter, where he proclaimed you, against the divine Scriptures and against the admonitions of Cyprian, before the last winnowing which the world awaits, as wheat purged from the chaff.

**3. 82. 94.** What, then, more worthily against this most deceitful exaltation could be procured for you than the case of the Maximianians? Whatever you are accustomed to say against us as against betrayers, against the Maximianians, whom you received condemned, when they were condemned you said graver things. About the emperors you stir up envy against us as persecutors: you, before the judges whom the emperors themselves sent, made persecution against the Maximianians. You contend that the baptism of the Church cannot be given outside the Church: you, the baptism which the Maximianians gave in the crime of schism, did not rescind in those received to whom they gave it. If these things ought to have been done for the peace of unity, you do not find whence you may accuse us; but if they ought not, do not accuse us, unless you condemn yourselves. I do not want you to spread yourself out through many things; for I too could briefly recall to memory the things that have been said against you. But from this alone consider, set the case of the Maximianians before your eyes. If about this you can answer us, approach the rest; but if you cannot, you would do better to be quiet than to kick against the goad[^87].
