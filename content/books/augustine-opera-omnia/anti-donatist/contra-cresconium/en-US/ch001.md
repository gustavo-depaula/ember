# Book I

**1. 1. 1.** Not knowing, Cresconius, when my writings might be able to reach you, I nonetheless did not at all despair that they would reach you, since yours too were able at some point to reach me, though long after you had written them. You thought you ought to write against those things which I, as best I could, answered briefly in part on behalf of Petilian, the bishop of Cirta among you, who was striving to build up the repetition of baptism and was charging our communion not with the weight of evidence but with the lightness of slander. For his whole letter had not then come into my hands, but only a small earlier part. Why this happened there is no need to ask, since, when the whole of it later reached us, I did not refuse to answer the whole. If, then, I were not to write back to these letters of yours that you sent to me, you would perhaps think it insulting; but in writing back, I again fear you may think it contentious. Yet if you, on finding letters of mine that were not addressed to you, but only because they seemed to refute a bishop of the party of Donatus or the party of Donatus itself, judged that it pertained to your office to take up and set forth a rejoinder—since you are conscious of having some ability, and since you belong to that communion, although bound by no clerical function—how much less was it permitted to me, by the duty of my burden, to keep silent against either Petilian or you yourself, when he was attacking the Church for which I serve as a soldier, while you, in a like work, were even addressing, producing, and composing a discourse to me by name?

**1. 1. 2.** In the first parts of it you labored to make eloquence seem suspect to people. For, as if praising my manner of speaking, and again as if fearing that by this manner I might deceive you or anyone by persuading what is false, you proceeded to an accusation of eloquence itself, even bringing forward a testimony against it from the holy Scriptures, where you think it was said, *In much eloquence you will not escape sin*[^1]—when in fact it was not said *from much eloquence* but *from much talking*. Now much talking is superfluous speech, a fault contracted by the love of speaking. And for the most part those love to talk who do not even know what they are saying, or how to say it, whether with regard to the soundness of their thoughts or to that very wholeness of sound and order of words which is learned through the art of grammar. But eloquence is the ability to speak, fittingly setting forth what we think; and it is to be used when we think rightly. In this way the heretics did not use it. For surely if they had thought rightly, there would have been not only nothing evil but even something good which they could have set forth eloquently. In vain, therefore, by recalling these examples, have you accused eloquence. For a soldier is not to be left unarmed for his homeland because some have taken up arms against their homeland; nor ought good and learned physicians refrain from using medical instruments for health because the unlearned and the very worst misuse them for ruin. For who does not know that, just as a medicine is useful or useless according as the things sought are useful or useless, so eloquence—that is, skill and ability in speaking—is useful or useless according as the things said are useful or useless? And I do not think you are ignorant of this.

**1. 2. 3.** But, I suppose, when you saw that I was thought by some to be eloquent, you judged that eloquence must be accused, in order to deter the reader's or hearer's interest from me, so that whoever was frightened by you, as though I spoke eloquently, would on that very account think me to be avoided and shunned and would no longer attend to what I said. See, then, whether what you have done is not part of "that evil art which," as you recalled concerning Plato, "some have rightly judged ought to be driven out of the state and out of human society." This is not eloquence—which I wish had come to me, as I desired, for setting forth what I think—but a certain sophistic and malicious profession, which sets before itself the aim of speaking, not from conviction but from contentiousness or advantage, for all things and against all things. Of this holy Scripture says, *He who speaks sophistically is hateful*[^2]. From this, it seems to me, the apostle Paul forbids the youth of Timothy, where he says, *Do not wrangle about words; it is useful for nothing, but only ruins the hearers*[^3]. And lest he be thought to have forbidden him the ability of speaking rightly, he at once added, *Be diligent to present yourself to God as an approved workman, unashamed, rightly handling the word of truth*[^4]. Doubtless, then, this disposition of mind crept upon you, that out of zeal for contradiction—not because you thought so, but in order to turn away from us the attention of one wishing to learn—you declared us eloquent and disparaged eloquence. For how am I to believe that you did this from conviction, when I know how you are accustomed to extol the eloquence of Donatus, of Parmenian, and of your others—than which nothing would be more useful, if it flowed in so broad a stream for the peace of Christ, for unity, truth, and love? But why should I speak of others? In your own case, did you not lay open how you were a disparager of eloquence not from conviction but from contentiousness, when you tried both to persuade by eloquence the other things you wrote, and to accuse that very eloquence eloquently?

**1. 3. 4.** For when you say of yourself that you are "both unequal to us in the art of speaking and altogether uninstructed in the examples of the Christian law," to what, I ask, does this pertain? Did I in any way compel you to write back against my writings, so that this is the voice of one refusing and excusing himself? If, then, you are altogether uninstructed, why do you not rather keep silent, or speak in such a way that you desire to be instructed? "I keep insisting"—you say—"and provoking, that your people should debate with me to discern the question of truth; but your people act more prudently and more patiently, who in the Church teach the peoples only what is commanded in the Law, and do not bother to answer us, knowing that if the divine law and so many proofs of the legal Scriptures cannot persuade us what is better, what is truer, no human authority could ever, once our errors are discussed, call us back to the rule of truth." What, then, seemed good to you, that while they keep silent you should speak against us? For if they do well, why do you not imitate them? If badly, why do you praise them?

**1. 3. 5.** You say that "with intolerable arrogance I believe that I alone can settle what seemed to others, as it were, insoluble, and was therefore left to God the judge"—when a little earlier you had said that "I wish to settle, after so many years, after so many judges and arbiters, what could not be settled before the emperors by the bishops of both parties with so many learned men disputing." Surely I alone am striving for this? Surely I alone desire that this question be settled by debate? For I think that if you had wished to blame our people alone for having attempted it, you would not have admitted that your people too were engaged in that attempt. Since, therefore, you cannot now reprehend that attempt, that will and insistence, at least on account of your own people, I am unwilling to be a stranger to so good a work. What do you charge? What do you reprehend? Or do you envy? This is not to be rashly believed of you. It remains, then, that out of zeal for contention you blame in me what you are compelled to praise even in your own people.

**1. 4. 6.** But indeed, you say, since it was not settled among so many and such men, it is intolerable arrogance to presume that it can be settled by one man alone. Do not, I beg, think that this is to be attributed to me alone: we are very many who insist that it be settled—indeed, that it now become known to have been settled. For those declared it not settled who refused to consent to that very settlement and concealed it from you, so that you too, deceived by their authority, would believe it not settled. But our people, from the time it was settled, have at no time ceased to make the settlement itself known, in whatever way they could do so publicly and privately, lest anyone persisting in a most pernicious error should at the last judgment complain of the sluggishness of God's ministers toward him. We do not, then, wish to reopen from the start a cause long ago settled, but to demonstrate how it was settled, chiefly for the sake of those who do not know this—so that when the defenders of error are convicted, either they themselves may be corrected and set free, or at least, with these refuted and remaining in open obstinacy, those who are more desirous of truth than of contention may see what they should follow.

**1. 5. 7.** Nor is this done without fruit, as you suppose. For if you could see how far and wide that error had spread through Africa, and how few of its remnants there are which have not yet crossed over, corrected, into the Catholic peace, you would by no means judge the insistence of those who assert Christian peace and unity to be fruitless and empty—although even where the diligence of this medicine, when applied, does no good, it is enough, for the rendering of an account to God, that it has not ceased to be applied. For just as the malicious tempter to sin, even if he does not persuade, deservedly incurs the punishment of a deceiver, so the faithful preacher of righteousness, even if he is rejected by men, far be it that with God he should be defrauded of the reward of his office. For a sure thing is done with regard to an uncertain one. And I call uncertain, not the reward of the doer, but the mind of the hearer. For it is uncertain to us whether the one to whom truth is preached will assent; but it is certain that truth ought to be preached even to such people, and it is certain that a worthy recompense awaits those who faithfully preach it, whether they are received or scorned, or even on that account suffer any temporal adversities. The Lord says in the Gospel, *When you enter, say: Peace to this house; if those who are there are worthy, your peace will rest upon them; if not, it will return to you*[^5]. Did he make them certain that those to whom they preached peace would receive it? Yet he made them certain that they should preach it without hesitation.

**1. 6. 8.** The apostle Paul also says, *The servant of the Lord must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all, an apt teacher, patient, correcting with modesty those who think otherwise; that perhaps God may grant them repentance to know the truth, and they may recover from the snares of the devil, having been captured by him to do his will*[^6]. Note how the one whom he did not want to quarrel he nevertheless wanted to correct with modesty those who think otherwise, lest the servant of God should suppose that the prohibition of insolence was an occasion for laziness. But because many bear with annoyance and trouble even that correction which is done with modesty—whether favoring their own sins, or not finding what to answer and yet not acquiescing in the truth—they call those who deal earnestly with them, and do not pretend not to see the need to refute their error, quarrelsome and contentious. For falsehood, which fears to be laid bare and refuted, accuses the diligence of truth by the name of those faults which truth condemns. Yet ought one on that account to desist from this insistence? See how the same Apostle binds Timothy, lest, on account of the people to whom the preaching of truth is unwelcome, some sluggishness in preaching should creep upon him. *I charge you*—he says—*before God and Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom: preach the word, be insistent in season and out of season; reprove, exhort, rebuke, with all patience and teaching*[^7]. Who, hearing this, if he serves God faithfully, if he is not a deceitful workman, would rest from this diligence and insistence? Who would dare be sluggish under so great a charge? Let your eloquence, therefore, not clamor against us in this cause: we preach altogether, with the help of the Lord our God, the usefulness, piety, and holiness of Christian unity; we preach to the willing in season, to the resisting out of season; and with all the strength we can muster, we show that this question between us and the party of Donatus was long ago settled, and we do so on behalf of those for whom and against those against whom we can.

**1. 7. 9.** Let them recognize in themselves the name and crime of contentious animosity, who either by stubborn cunning lend patronage to falsehood, or by envious boasting supply heralding to truth. The apostle Paul expressed both kinds of contentious persons: the first in Alexander, of whom he says, *Alexander the coppersmith did me much harm; the Lord will repay him according to his deeds; you too avoid him, for he strongly opposed our words*[^8]; the second in those of whom he says, *Some indeed preach Christ out of envy and contention, not purely, thinking to stir up affliction for my bonds*[^9]. For these without doubt preached the very thing that Paul did; yet not with that mind, not with that will, not out of love, but out of envy, as he said, and out of contention, wishing in their proud sense to excel in that very preaching and to be set above the apostle Paul. He, not bearing this with annoyance—indeed even rejoicing that he saw preached by them what he desired to become more widely known—says, *What then? Provided that in every way, whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is proclaimed*[^10]. For they preached the truth—that is, Christ—not with the truth of their own heart, since it was not with a sincere intention but with rival contention. You, therefore, since you cannot be a judge of the inner parts of our heart, attend only to whether we resist the truth, or wish to refute those who resist the truth. For without doubt, if we persuade truth and refute error, even if we do so not with the truth of our own intention, but seeking the advantage of this world and human glory, the lovers of truth ought to rejoice, since on this occasion too the truth is proclaimed, as the Apostle says, *And in this I will rejoice*[^11]. But if (which is best known to God, and which could have been known even to you yourself, as far as human capacity allows, had you lived with us) we are engaged in the labor of this ministry with the pious solicitude of love, then I think our ministry is by no means justly reprehended, if against any adversaries of the truth we contend with fervent spirit on behalf of the truth.

**1. 8. 10.** For if anyone who has taken care either to bring or to return a verbal dispute to another is held by you to be contentious or quarrelsome and a preparer of brawls, see what you think of the Lord Jesus Christ himself and of his servants the Prophets and Apostles. For surely the Lord himself, the Son of God—did he hold discourse about the truth only with his disciples or with the crowds who believed in him, and not also with enemies who were tempting, disparaging, questioning, resisting, and reviling? Did it weary him to dispute even with one woman about the question of worship against the opinion or heresy of the Samaritans? But, you say, he foreknew that she would believe. How many things did he speak openly to their faces, so many times, against the Jews, the Pharisees, the Sadducees, who were not only by no means going to believe but were going to contradict and persecute him most of all? Did he not, of his own accord, when he willed, ask of them what he willed, in order to convict them by their own answer? Did he not, without any ambiguity, answer those who deceitfully and by tempting asked things by which, refuted, they fell silent? And when he did this, none of these is recorded to have been converted to following him. And he surely knew, since he was foreknowing, that he would profit nothing toward their salvation when he said these things to them, or against them. But perhaps he strengthened us by his example, who are not able to foreknow the future faith or unfaithfulness of men, lest, if at any time we have spoken without the fruit of their salvation to people too hard and too perverse, we should fail and desist from the insistence of preaching, since it had wearied us to labor in vain. What of this—that even the devil himself, whom now not only God but not even men can doubt will in no way be converted to righteousness, the Son of God nevertheless, when he was insidiously tempting and setting the snares of questions from the holy Scriptures, convicted by answering from the holy Scriptures; nor did Christ judge it unworthy to hold a conversation with Satan about the divine words? What did he surely foresee, except that what profited the Jews and the devil nothing would profit the Gentiles who were going to believe?

**1. 8. 11.** We read also that the Prophets were sent to men so disobedient that God himself, who sent the Prophets, foretold concerning those to whom he sent them that they would not obey their words. I pass over the fact that by the prophetic spirit, by which they discerned the future, they too could surely have known this—that those to whom they nevertheless did not keep silent with vehement insistence would despise their words. Most plainly the Lord says to the prophet Ezekiel, *Go and enter into the house of Israel, and speak my words to them; for you are not sent to a people of unknown speech, but to the house of Israel; nor to many peoples speaking diverse or difficult tongues, whose words you could not understand; and if I had sent you to such, perhaps they would have listened to you. But the house of Israel will not listen to you, because they are unwilling to listen to me. For the whole house of Israel is of a restless and hard heart. But I have made your face strong against their face, and I will strengthen your contest against their contest*[^12]. Behold, the servant of God is sent and bidden to speak to those who were not going to hear him, with that very Lord who sent and bade him speak foretelling that they were not going to hear. For what cause, to what good, with what fruit or effect is he sent to the contest of preaching the truth against those who would contest and not obey? Will anyone dare to say that the holy Prophets of God fell into the reproach you cast at me, when you said: "If you know that the matter under discussion cannot be settled by you, why do you take up vain labor? Why do you spend useless effort? Why do you contend superfluously and without fruit? Or is it not a great error to will what you cannot accomplish? Since even the Law admonishes, saying, *Do not seek things too high for you, and do not search out things too strong for you*[^13]; and again, *A spirited man stirs up strifes, and an irascible man heaps up sin*[^14]." You surely would not say these things to Ezekiel, who, when he is sent with the word of God to bring contest to men who were not going to obey, going to think against it, to speak against it, to act against it. For if you said them, he would perhaps answer you what the Apostles answered those same Jews: *Whom ought one to obey? God rather, or men?*[^15] This I too would answer you.

**1. 9. 12.** Here, if you press me to show where God too commanded me to do what you forbid, remember that the apostolic Epistles were written not only to those who at the time they were written heard them, but also to us; for it is for no other reason that they are read aloud in the Church. Attend also to what the Apostle says, *Do you wish to receive proof of him who speaks in me, Christ?*[^16]. And recall now—not what Paul, but what Christ through him, spoke, which I mentioned a little before: *Preach the word, be insistent in season, out of season*[^17], and so on. Attend also to how, in writing to Titus, when he was explaining what sort of man a bishop ought to be, he said that he ought also to be one holding fast to the teaching of the faithful word: *That he may be able*, he says, *both to exhort in sound doctrine and to refute those who contradict. For there are many insubordinate, idle talkers, and seducers of minds, especially those of the circumcision, who must be confuted*[^18]. He does not, then, say that those of the circumcision alone are such, but that they especially are such; yet by an undoubted precept he established that idle talkers and seducers of minds must be refuted and confuted by a bishop in sound doctrine. Hence I recognize that this too is commanded me; this I do with all my strength; on this work, as far as he who commanded it helps, I persevere insistently. Why do you stand in the way? Why do you clamor? Why do you forbid? Why do you reprehend? Ought one to obey you, or God?

**1. 10. 13.** Unless perhaps you think that these proofs which I have brought forward from the holy Scriptures are to be taken in such a way that what you praised your people for habitually doing—namely, that the peoples are taught in church only what is commanded in the Law—is alone permitted. For there, perhaps, you think that those who think otherwise are to be corrected and convicted, so that each teacher should amend the error only of his own people by disputing and preaching; but if anyone undertakes to deal in any such way with those who are outside, then he is to be held spirited, then contentious or quarrelsome—because "even Ezekiel himself"—you say—"and the other Prophets, when they were sent with the words of God to their own people, namely Israelites to Israelites."

**1. 11. 14.** To these things also I answer you. I have already, indeed, mentioned above that the Lord Jesus himself, who offered himself to be imitated by his disciples, did not disdain to assert the truth and to answer concerning the Law not only to the Jews but also to the Pharisees, the Sadducees, the Samaritans, and to the devil himself, the prince of all deceits and errors. But lest you think that this was permitted to the Lord but is not permitted to his servants, receive what is read in the Acts of the Apostles. *Now a certain Jew named Apollos, an Alexandrian by birth, came to Ephesus, powerful in the Scriptures; he had been instructed in the way of the Lord, and fervent in spirit he spoke and taught most truthfully the things concerning Jesus, knowing only the baptism of John. He had also begun to act boldly in the synagogue. When Aquila and Priscilla heard him, they took him aside and expounded to him more accurately the way of the Lord. And when he wished to go into Achaia, the brethren, encouraging him, wrote to the disciples to receive him. When he had come, he contributed much to those who there had believed; for he vigorously refuted the Jews in public, showing through the Scriptures that Jesus was the Christ*[^19]. What do you say of this man? What do you think? Would you not perhaps charge him as a contentious and spirited inciter and preparer of brawls, were you not pressed by so great an authority of the holy book?

**1. 12. 15.** Or is it that, because a Jew had believed in Christ, he therefore had to refute publicly the Jews who resisted the Christian faith and denied that Jesus was the Christ; but we, because we were never of the party of Donatus, therefore ought not to refute the party of Donatus, which resists Christian unity? Was the apostle Paul ever a worshiper of idols, or ever in the heresy of the Epicureans or the Stoics—with whom, nevertheless, he was neither ashamed nor wearied to hold discourse about the question of the living and true God? Receive what is written about this matter in the same book. *Now while Paul was waiting for them at Athens, his spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was given over to idols. He disputed, therefore, with the Jews in the synagogue, and with the Gentiles and the devout, and in the marketplace every day with those who were present. And some of the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers conversed with him; and some said: What does this sower of words wish to say? But others: He seems to be a proclaimer of foreign demons*[^20]. Behold, the apostle Paul did not refuse to confer with him the Stoics and Epicureans—heresies differing not only from him but even from each other, and opposed—disputing with them not only outside the Church but even outside the synagogue; nor, frightened by their insults, did he cease, as though avoiding strifes and contentions, from preaching the Christian truth. For see what holy Scripture next testifies: *And taking hold of him*, it says, *they led him to the Areopagus, saying: May we know what these things are which are spoken by you? For you bring certain strange things to our ears; we wish therefore to know what these things mean. Now the Athenians and the foreign visitors had time for nothing else than to say or hear something new. And Paul, standing in the midst of the Areopagus, said: Men of Athens, in all things I perceive you to be more superstitious; for as I passed by and considered your images, I found also an altar on which was written: To an unknown God. What therefore you worship in ignorance, this I proclaim to you*[^21]—and the rest, which it would be long to recall in full. Yet for the question we are now discussing, it is enough to attend, I beg you, to a Hebrew of Hebrews, an Apostle of Christ, standing and speaking, not in the synagogue of the Jews nor in the church of the Christians, but in the Areopagus of the Athenians—that is, among the most contentious and impious of the Greeks. For there the most loquacious heresies of the philosophers arose, some of which, like the Stoics who are mentioned here, fight more over the adversity of words than of things; which the Apostle forbade Timothy, saying that it is useful for nothing, but only ruins the hearers[^22]. For of these, as you know, Tully says, *The controversy over a word has long tormented those little Greeks, more desirous of contention than of truth*[^23]. Yet our Paul undertook to address and correct these men; nor, frightened by the name of the place itself, which resounds as derived from Mars, whom they call the god of wars, did he there speak undaunted what was peaceful to those who would believe; there, girded with spiritual arms, he stormed pernicious errors; nor did he, most gentle, dread the contentious, nor, most simple, the dialecticians.

**1. 13. 16.** For you know how dialectic flourished most of all among the Stoics—although even the Epicureans themselves, who were not only not ashamed of their ignorance of the liberal disciplines but even delighted in it, boasted that they rather both held and taught certain rules of disputing by which anyone using them would least be deceived. For what else is dialectic than skill in disputing? This I thought ought to be made clear, because you wished to cast this too in my teeth, as if it did not accord with Christian truth, and so your teachers, you say, deservedly judged that I, as a dialectical man, should rather be fled and shunned than refuted and confuted. And since they did not persuade you of this—for it did not weary you to dispute against us even by writing—you nevertheless charged dialectic against me, in order to deceive the unskilled and to praise those who had refused to engage with me in disputation. But you, of course, do not use dialectic when you write against us? Why, then, did you cast yourself into so great a danger of disputing, when you do not know how to dispute? Or, if you do know, why, being a dialectician, do you charge dialectic—being thus either rash or ungrateful, so as either not to restrain the ignorance by which you are conquered, or to accuse the learning by which you are helped? I inspect your discourse, this very one you wrote to me; I see you setting forth some things copiously and ornately—that is, eloquently; and discussing some things subtly and acutely—that is, dialectically; and yet you reprehend eloquence and dialectic. If they are harmful, why do you do these things? If they are not, why do you charge them? But lest the controversy over a word torment us too, since the thing itself is understood, less effort need be spent on what people have pleased to call it. Accordingly, if he is to be called eloquent who speaks not only copiously and ornately but also truthfully; and likewise if he is to be called a dialectician who discusses not only subtly but also truthfully—then you are neither eloquent nor a dialectician: not because your diction is meager and unadorned, nor because your disputation is dull and gross, but because you misuse the very fluency and skill for the defense of falsehood. But if eloquence or dialectic can rightly be named even when something is treated eloquently or vigorously not in truth alone but even with a bad cause, then you are both eloquent and a dialectician, since you both speak empty things fluently and dispute false things acutely. But of you I shall see later.

**1. 14. 17.** The Stoics were certainly dialecticians most of all: why did the apostle Paul not most cautiously avoid their conferring with him? And you praise your bishops for being unwilling to hold discourse with us as with dialecticians? Or if Paul too was a dialectician, and therefore did not fear the Stoics conferring with him, because he disputed not only acutely as they did but also truthfully, which they did not—then beware henceforth of casting dialectic as a crime against anyone, since you confess that the Apostles used it. For when you cast this at me, I do not think you are deceived by ignorance, but that you deceive by cunning. For "dialectic" is a Greek word, which, if usage admitted, would perhaps be called in Latin "disputatory"; just as the most learned in both tongues have called "grammar" in Latin "literature." For just as grammar is named from letters, since in Greek letters are called gr*ammata*, so dialectic took its name from disputation, since in Greek disputation is called di*aloge* or di*alexis*. And just as a grammarian was called in Latin by the ancients a "lettered man" (litterator), so he who in Greek is called a dialectician is much more usually and tolerably called in Latin a "disputant" (disputator). I think now that you do not deny the Apostle was a disputant, even if you deny he was a dialectician. To disapprove, then, in a Greek word what you are compelled to approve in Latin—what else is this than to hold out fallacy to the unlearned and to do injury to the learned? Or if you deny that the Apostle was even a disputant, who disputed so assiduously, so excellently, you know neither Greek nor Latin; or, what is more credible, you deceive both, in the Greek word those who do not know Greek, and in the Latin those who do not even know Latin. For what is, I do not say more ignorant—for you are not ignorant of these things—but altogether more deceitful, than that, when you hear and read so many and manifold discourses of the Apostle asserting truth and convicting falsehood, you should deny that he was accustomed to dispute, when this cannot be done except by disputing?

**1. 14. 18.** But if you confess that this was habitually done by him—because you are compelled to confess it by his writings—yet contend that these are not to be called disputations but discourses or letters, why should I deal with you at such length, as though those who are ignorant of these things might approve which of us they wish and disapprove which they wish? From the divine writings themselves, to which you must yield, I teach this; I bring forward the very words, the very names of the things. You have, in this same testimony which I cited from the Acts of the Apostles, this set down concerning Paul himself: *He disputed, therefore, with the Jews in the synagogue, and with the Gentiles and the devout in the marketplace*[^24]. You have in another place, although he was dealing with a Christian people, the brethren gathered in church, this written: *Now a certain young man named Eutychus, sitting in a window, was overcome by a deep sleep, as Paul disputed*[^25]. You have also in the book of Psalms, *May my disputation be pleasing to him*[^26]. You have also in the prophet Isaiah, *Come, let us dispute, says the Lord*[^27]. And in many other places of the divine Scriptures read where you find this word, and inspect the Greek codices in these same testimonies of the holy Scriptures, and you will see whence dialectic is named—lest what all the just do even with God, to whom it is said, *Come, let us dispute, says the Lord*, you should not imitate with wise piety, but charge as a crime with tasteless rashness.

**1. 15. 19.** For he who disputes discerns by disputing what is true from what is false. Those who cannot do this, and yet wish to seem dialecticians, catch the assents of the incautious by insidious questions, in order to conclude from their answers something whereby they may either laugh at them as deceived in open falsehood, or persuade the deceived of a hidden falsehood, which for the most part they themselves also reckon to be truth. But he who is a true disputant—that is, a discerner of truth from falsehood—first does this within himself, lest, not discerning rightly, he himself be deceived; which he cannot accomplish unless divinely helped. Then, when he brings forth for the teaching of others what he has done within himself, he looks first at what they already know for certain, so that from these things he may lead them to those they did not know or were unwilling to believe, showing these to be consequences of what they already held by knowledge or faith; so that, through those true things about which they perceive themselves to agree, they may be compelled to approve other true things which they had denied; and thus the truth which before was thought false may be discerned from the false, when it is found to be in accord with that truth which was already held before.

**1. 16. 20.** If this true disputant does this broadly and diffusely, he does it eloquently, and is then reckoned and augmented by another name, so that he is called a speaker rather than a disputant; as the Apostle copiously dilates and pours forth that passage: *In all things*, he says, *commending ourselves as ministers of God, in much patience, in tribulations, in distresses, in beatings, in imprisonments, in tumults, in labors, in watchings, in fastings, in chastity, in knowledge, in long-suffering, in kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in love unfeigned, in the word of truth, in the power of God; by the arms of righteousness on the right hand and on the left; through glory and dishonor; through ill repute and good report; as deceivers, and yet truthful; as unknown, and yet known; as dying, and behold we live; as chastened, and yet not killed; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet enriching many; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things*[^28]. For what richer and more ornate—that is, more eloquent—than this apostolic style could you easily find? But if he does it tersely and compactly, they are accustomed to call him a disputant rather than a speaker, as the same Apostle deals with the circumcision and the uncircumcision of father Abraham, or the distinction of law and grace. Not understanding this—indeed, rather slandering—some charged him with saying, *Let us do evil that good may come*[^29]. But whether he be a speaker or a disputant, neither is speaking without disputation—since even in the very breadth of eloquence truth is discerned from falsehood—nor can disputation be without speaking, since the compactness of discourse is surely expressed by words and the tongue itself: whether he uses continuous speech, or by questioning the one with whom he deals compels him to answer what is true, and from this leads him to another truth that was being sought, where dialectic is said to reign most of all.

**1. 17. 21.** For when anyone is convicted by his own answers, both if he has answered badly he has nothing to impute to the disputant, but to himself; and if he has answered well, he is ashamed to resist further—no longer the disputant, but himself. In this kind, when the Lord dealt often against the Jews, and convicted them caught and shut up by their own answers, they had not listened to you, nor learned to revile from you; for they would perhaps more willingly and more enviously have called him a dialectician than a Samaritan. For how do you think they were twisted and confounded, when, wishing to catch him in a word, they first asked whether it was lawful to pay tribute to Caesar, lying in wait, that is, with a two-headed dilemma, so that, choosing whichever he might, he would be caught? If he answered that it was lawful, he would be, as it were, guilty before the people of God; but if he said it was not lawful, he would be punished as an adversary of Caesar. Whereupon he asked that a coin be shown to him, and asked whose image and inscription it bore; and when they had answered, *Caesar's*—for the open truth compelled them to answer this—the Lord at once, dragging them bound and caught by their own answer, said, *Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's*[^30]. I beg you, were those the dialecticians, who, by the snares of the question put forward, sought to overcome by deceiving; or rather was he, who, from the very thing they asked, eliciting their true answer by the prudence of his questioning, compelled them to confess that truth which they thought was being dangerously spoken by him?

**1. 18. 22.** If you say that those were dialecticians, because they wished to catch him in a word by questioning deceitfully, slanderously, maliciously (for you wish even us to seem such), why nevertheless did the Lord answer them? Why did he lead them, by giving a reason, all the way to the confession of truth? Why did he say to them, *Why do you tempt me, hypocrites?*[^31] and not add, "Dialecticians"? Why did he demand that a coin be shown to him, so as to express his truthful judgment even from the mouth of deceivers, and not rather say, "Depart; for one must not speak with you, because you propose captious questions, because you wish to deal with me dialectically"? He said nothing of the kind, nor against captious questioners and cunning catchers of our words did he set us such an example; but rather that we should compel even enemies of the truth, by vigilant questioning and invincible reasoning, to bear witness to the truth. Let your people do this to us, if we are malicious and dialecticians. Or do they indicate that they fear lest we rather do this to them? But if you say that Christ was a dialectician, you will praise dialectic, which you had cast at me as a crime.

**1. 19. 23.** Lest you do this, I see what perhaps you will say—that neither they nor he did anything dialectically in that conversation. If, then, neither those who converse captiously and insidiously, so as to deceive in a word those with whom they deal, nor those who convict such people by their own answer, act dialectically: tell us at last what dialectic is, and teach how much evil it has, how much it harms, how much it is to be fled. Whose name you enviously suggest to the ignorant, show its crime to those who ask. You are unwilling to confess that he acts dialectically who, questioning skillfully and rightly men turned away from the truth, leads them from their answers to the truth—lest you confess that Christ too dealt dialectically with the Jews. Likewise you are unwilling that those act dialectically who, lying in wait with captious questions, strive to deceive the one answering—lest it be shown to you that the Jews dealt thus with Christ, whom nevertheless he did not avoid by keeping silent, but rather overcame by speaking; and so you would be compelled to confess that your bishops do not rightly refuse to hold discourse even with dialecticians—whom you think learned and wise—by which they might teach invincible truth. I see you suffer great heat over how to define a dialectician, so that he is neither a skilled disputant—lest you be compelled to praise what you disparaged—nor an insidious catcher of words—lest it be said to you, "As Christ dealt with such men, so let a Christian deal with this one." Accordingly, if you please to be freed of this care, define a dialectician to be one with whom the experts in the law of the party of Donatus are unwilling to hold conversation. For what else is to be suggested to you, a man casting dialectic at us, and on that account praising your bishops because they are unwilling to hold discourse with us?

**1. 19. 24.** But about the Jews you perhaps find something to say—that, although they cunningly and craftily put forward the deceits of their questions, they were not dialecticians. About the Stoics certainly nothing can be said, who were not only dialecticians but even surpassed the other sects of philosophers in this art or ability. For a Stoic, as you recall with me, was that Chrysippus, of whom the Academic Carneades held this opinion: that whenever he had to dispute with him, he reckoned his heart had to be purged with hellebore, but the rest he could easily overcome even after a meal. If, then, the books of the Stoics taught us to dispute dialectically, let your bishops bring forth the teaching of Paul against us; yet let them suffer us to confer with them, just as he did not repel the Stoics then.

**1. 20. 25.** For this art which they call dialectic, which teaches nothing other than to demonstrate consequences, whether true from the true or false from the false, Christian doctrine never dreads; just as the Apostle did not dread it in the Stoics, whom, when they wished to confer with him, he did not refuse[^32]. For it itself confesses, and it is true, that no one is consequently driven by a disputant to a false conclusion unless he has first consented to the false things by which that conclusion, willy-nilly, is brought about. And through this, whoever takes care that false things he does not want should not, while he speaks, follow upon him, let him willingly take care over the false things that precede. But if he has adhered to true premises, whatever consequences he perceives, which he thought false or about which he doubted, let him, when admonished, embrace them, if he is more a friend of most peaceable truth than of most contentious vanity.

**1. 21. 26.** I would have done too little, unless I showed what I am saying in this very conversation that goes on between us. Behold, in that very question about baptism you proposed, asking of me, where it would be fitting for you to be baptized—whether among us, or in the party of Donatus. And because your aim is that a man ought rather to be baptized in the party of Donatus, you tried to prove this aim from the fact that we too do not deny that there is baptism there. You see surely that you wished to do this: that from what we grant, we might be drawn to what we did not grant; that is, that because we grant there is baptism there, we might be compelled to grant that a man must also be baptized there.

**1. 22. 27.** Consider diligently whether this is a consequence, and answer yourself. For I think that, with this now set before your eyes, you perceive, with the liveliness of your wit, how you gather as consequences things that are not consequent upon the premises. For in truth we say that baptism is there too; but we do not say that it also profits—indeed, rather we say that it even harms. But when it is asked where each one ought to be baptized, I believe it is asked on account of that which the Lord says, *Unless one is born again of water and the Spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of heaven*[^33]. Since, therefore, baptism is to be received for the sake of this usefulness, when it is asked where it is to be received, it is not asked where it is, but where it is useful for attaining the kingdom of heaven. But it would follow that it must be received there too, wherever it is established to be, if everyone who has anything good were taught to have it also to his own good. But since so many have so many goods to their own harm, who does not see that when it is asked where something is to be received, it is not asked where it is, but where it profits? For just as, if you granted me that gold is good, you would also grant that robbers too have gold, you would not, I think, wish me to conclude from these two grants that one who wishes to have gold ought to be in the society of robbers: so, when I too grant that baptism is good, and grant that the Donatists too have baptism, you ought not from these two grants conclude, as though it followed, that one who wishes to have baptism ought to be in the society of the Donatists.

**1. 23. 28.** From this now I do not doubt that many things occur even to yourself, which, although they are good and instituted for some useful purpose, are nevertheless not useful to all who have them, but only to those who use them well. For when both sound and injured eyes are bathed in the same light, to the one it is help, to the other torment. The same food nourishes some constitutions, harms others; the same medicine cures these, weakens those; the same arms protect some, hinder others; the same garment is to some a covering, to others an entanglement. So too baptism avails some for the kingdom, others for judgment.

**1. 24. 29.** Here I see what can move you. For perhaps you say that in all these I mentioned nothing of a sacrament; but baptism is a holy sacrament, and therefore it is not consequent that, if from gold, from light, from foods, arms, garments it could be proved that they are fitting to some who have them, unsuitable to others, although they are good and instituted for some useful purpose, baptism too should at once profit some and harm others who have it. It remains, then, still to inquire whether even those goods which pertain to the law of God do not all profit all who have them. This question being proposed, our aim is that not even all these profit all who have them. See how we prove this aim of ours from your own concessions. For you concede that the apostle Paul is to be believed in all things. I hold one point. You concede also that the same Apostle said, *The law is good, if one uses it lawfully*[^34]: from these two it is established that the law is good, but for those who use it lawfully. If, then, anyone has used it not lawfully, it does not itself become bad, but it will certainly harm the bad.

**1. 25. 30.** Perhaps you will say that no one can be both in the law and use the law badly: for by this very fact, that he lives against the law, he is shown not to be in the law. On the contrary, I say it can happen that anyone be both in the law and not use it lawfully. This too I prove by your concessions. For you concede that the said Apostle set down a testimony from the Psalms against those who boasted in the law and lived against the law. *As it is written*, he says, *that there is none righteous, none who understands, none who seeks God; all have turned aside, together they have become useless; there is none who does good, no, not one. Their throat is an open sepulcher, with their tongues they acted deceitfully, the venom of asps is under their lips; whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness: their feet are swift to shed blood, ruin and misery are in their ways, and the way of peace they have not known; there is no fear of God before their eyes*[^35]. And lest they should think these things said against those who were not in the law, he at once added, *But we know that whatever the law says, it speaks to those who are in the law; that every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world become guilty before God*[^36]. Likewise in another place he says, *What then shall we say? Is the law sin? By no means. But I did not know sin except through the law: for I would not have known concupiscence, unless the law said: You shall not covet. But sin, taking occasion, worked in me through the commandment all manner of concupiscence*[^37]. Likewise a little after, *Sin*, he says, *taking occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me. So the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good. Did then that which is good become death to me? By no means; but sin, that it might appear sin, worked death in me through what is good*[^38]. Do you attend how, with the law praised, he accuses those who are in the law—all who, by using it badly, had evil through what is good? Likewise the Apostle himself says that a certain knowledge from the law, which he said both he and others had, is nevertheless useless and harmful without love. *Concerning sacrifices to idols*, he says, *we know that we all have knowledge. Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up*[^39]. Accordingly this knowledge too, although it pertains to the law of God, if in anyone it is without love, puffs up and harms. What of the very body and blood of the Lord, the one sacrifice for our salvation, since the Lord himself says, *Unless one eats my flesh and drinks my blood, he will not have life in him*[^40]—does not the same Apostle teach that this too becomes pernicious to those who use it badly? For he says, *Whoever eats unworthily, and drinks the cup of the Lord unworthily, will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord*[^41].

**1. 26. 31.** Behold how divine and holy things harm those who use them badly: why not in this way baptism too? Why are good heretics not in good baptism, just as good Jews are not in the good law? Now I have certainly proved it, with you conceding; since you concede that you believe Paul, and that Paul said the testimonies which I set down from the Scriptures; now, then, I have proved by your concessions that certain even good and lawful things nevertheless harm those who have and use them unlawfully: why not so baptism too, which, although good and lawful, nevertheless does not profit all who have it? Why did you conclude, as though most certain and most consequent, that a man must be baptized in the party of Donatus, because we concede that there too there is baptism—and did not attend that we can say that there is indeed there a baptism of Christ that is just, holy, and good, but penal, contrary, pernicious to the enemies of the body of Christ, which is the Church, which according to the divine promises is spread among all nations?

**1. 27. 32.** Will you here find anything to say, except that baptism is not to be counted among those goods pertaining to the law of God which men can both have and not be good, but that the law itself and knowledge and the sacrifice of the body and blood of Christ are such goods that men can both have them and be bad, whereas baptism is such a good that whoever has it is necessarily good? But if you should wish to say this, you will say what is false; and therefore attend to what other false thing follows. This I shall mention, not to lead you from that false statement of yours to other false ones, but so that, when you recognize this consequence to be false, you may, in order to free yourself from it, amend that which precedes. What, then, precedes? On your view, that all who have good baptism are good—which is evidently false. From which it follows that those, of course, were good who made schisms, saying, *I indeed am of Paul, but I of Cephas, but I of Christ*[^42]. Reproving whom the Apostle says, *Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?*[^43] But it is false that those were good, except those who said, *But I of Christ*; and yet they had been baptized with the holy baptism of Christ. Why, then, did this false thing follow? Because a false thing preceded—that all having good baptism are good. Let both, then, be rejected, let both opinions be corrected: so that, since it is manifest that those who made schisms were not good and yet had been baptized with good baptism, this too may be manifest, that not all who have good baptism are good. And through this we are not bound to grant that anyone must be baptized in the party of Donatus, because we grant that the party of Donatus too, which we call bad, has good baptism.

**1. 28. 33.** Again, in order to hold me by that concession to what I do not concede, you set down that it is written, *One God, one faith, one baptism, one undefiled and true Catholic Church*[^44]. All of which I concede, even if they are written somewhat otherwise. But what does this matter, since I concede all these, as I said? Yet what you try to bring about from them is not brought about—namely, that those who are not in the one Church cannot have one baptism: which is altogether most false. And it is better, since you yourself set down whence I can remind you of what I wish. For surely you proposed these things among my concessions, so as from them to lead me to your aim, that there is one God, one faith, one baptism, one undefiled and Catholic Church. Since these are agreed between us, you think that from them that which is not agreed can be shown—that among those who are not in this one Church this one baptism cannot exist. But I say that it can, if it is not changed, if this same thing is observed; nor does it come about that there is not one baptism, because it exists also among those who are not in the one Church. And this I prove from what you set down in the same sentence about one God and one faith. For we find the same God worshiped outside the Church by those who do not know him, nor does it come about that he is therefore not God; and the faith by which it is believed that Christ is the Son of the living God we find even those who do not belong to the members of the Church confessing, nor does it therefore come about that there is not one faith. So too, when we find the same baptism observed by those who are outside the Church in baptizing men, we ought not to reckon that it is therefore not baptism itself.

**1. 29. 34.** Perhaps to this you may say that it cannot happen that even outside the Church the same one God is worshiped, or that the same faith by which we confess Christ to be the Son of God—whence Peter was called blessed[^45]—is found even in those who are not in the Church. This, then, remains for me to prove. You have in this very discourse of blessed Paul, which I mentioned above from the Acts of the Apostles[^46], when he was speaking of God, because he had found inscribed on an altar *To an unknown God*: *What you*, he says, *worship in ignorance, this I proclaim to you*[^47]. Did he say, "Because you worship outside the Church, it is not God himself whom you worship"? But he says, *What you worship in ignorance, this I proclaim to you.* Wishing to confer on them what, except that the same God, whom apart from the Church they worshiped ignorantly and uselessly, they should in the Church worship wisely and salutarily? Thus we too say to you: the baptism which you observe in ignorance, its peace we proclaim to you; not that, when you come to us, you should receive another, but that you should usefully have that which was already among you. Concerning faith too, when the apostle James was speaking against those who reckoned that what they had believed sufficed for them, and were unwilling to work well: *You believe*, he says, *that there is one God; you do well: the demons also believe, and tremble*[^48]. Surely the demons are not in the unity of the Church; nor yet can we say that what they believe is something other, since they even said to the Lord Jesus Christ, *What have we to do with you, Son of God?*[^49] Whence also the apostle Paul says, *If I have all faith, so as to move mountains, but have not love, I am nothing*[^50]. But I do not think anyone is so foolish as to believe that one who does not have love belongs to the unity of the Church. As, therefore, the one God is worshiped ignorantly even outside the Church, nor is he therefore not himself; and the one faith is held without love even outside the Church, nor is it therefore not itself: so too the one baptism is held ignorantly and without love even outside the Church, nor is it therefore not itself. For there is one God, one faith, one baptism, one undefiled Catholic Church; not in which alone the one God is worshiped, but in which alone the one God is worshiped piously; nor in which alone the one faith is held, but in which alone the one faith is held with love; nor in which alone the one baptism is held, but in which alone the one baptism is held salutarily.

**1. 30. 35.** Accordingly, you proposed, with us agreeing, one God, one faith, one baptism, one undefiled and Catholic Church: but not only did you fail to accomplish from these what you wished, but you even helped us much, in that from these we might admonish you of what we wished. See, then, how probable a reason we follow, who correct the things that schismatics or heretics have corrupted when they come to us from there, but acknowledge and approve those things which they have held as they received them—lest, moved by human faults beyond justice, we should do any injury to divine things; since we see even the Apostle confirming rather than denying the name of God found upon an altar of the Gentiles, by whom idols were worshiped[^51]. For the royal mark in a man is not on that account to be changed or disapproved, if he has obtained pardon for his error and the order of service from the king, because a deserter has stamped that same mark by which he gathered to himself accomplices; nor are the marks of sheep on that account to be changed when they are joined to the Lord's flock, because a fugitive slave has impressed the Lord's mark on them.

**1. 31. 36.** But if you dread these as misleading, because they are not ecclesiastical examples (although you should know that similitudes both of sheep and of soldiers have been given in the Scriptures), I wish to say something from the prophetic Scriptures, which are called those of the Old Testament; since in the books of the New Testament an example of this matter is found neither by us nor by you. You do not dare, I think, to deny that circumcision of the foreskin was observed by the ancients as a figure of the future baptism of Christ. Surely a man circumcised among the Samaritans, if he then wished to become a Jew, could not be circumcised again? Would not that man's error be corrected, but the seal of faith, being recognized, be approved? And there are now certain heretics who call themselves Nazarenes, and are called by some Symmachians, and they have the circumcision of the Jews and the baptism of the Christians: and through this, just as if any of them comes to the Jews, he cannot be circumcised again, so when he comes to us, he ought not to be baptized again. To this you will say: the circumcision of the Jews is one thing, the baptism of the Christians another. But since the former was a shadow of this truth, why could that circumcision exist among the heretics of the Jews, but this baptism cannot exist among the heretics of the Christians?

**1. 31. 37.** Bring forward, then, from the canonical Scriptures, whose authority is common to us, someone coming from the heretics who was baptized anew. For the fact that the Apostles ordered certain persons to be baptized in Christ after the baptism of John is a far different case[^52]. For John was not a heretic—that friend of the bridegroom[^53], than whom no one greater has arisen among those born of women[^54]. It is therefore a far different case: otherwise, if Paul baptized after John, although both were in the unity of Christ, how much more ought your bishops, since they say they are in the unity of Christ, to baptize after their colleagues, in which colleagues they rightly reprehend certain morals—when Paul did this, who could reprehend nothing in John? Therefore that is another case, another reason, about which it is now long to discourse, and on which we have already said much in other little works of ours. Prove, then, from the canonical Scriptures one coming from the heretics who was baptized anew. For we too bring forward what was said to Peter: *He who has once been washed has no need to be washed again*[^55]. But you too say: Peter had not been baptized among heretics. Accordingly, since neither can you bring forward from the Scriptures, whose authority is common to us, one coming from the heretics who was baptized anew, nor can we bring forward one so received: as far as this matter is concerned, the case is equal for us.

**1. 32. 38.** But we have shown that many things pertaining even to the law of God exist among those who are not in the Church, which none of you dares to deny: but why you are unwilling that baptism too be something of this kind, I altogether do not see, nor do I trust that you can demonstrate it. We, indeed, in this matter follow the most certain authority of the canonical Scriptures. For it is to be held of no small moment that, when among the bishops of an earlier age than that in which the party of Donatus began this question fluctuated, and the colleagues held among themselves, with unity preserved, various opinions, it was resolved that throughout the universal Catholic Church, which is spread through the whole world, what we hold should be observed. For you too bring forward the council of Cyprian, which either was not held, or was deservedly overcome by the other members of unity, from whom he was not divided. For we are not on that account better than the bishop Cyprian (although he judged that heretics should be baptized anew), because we rightly do not do this: just as we are not better than the apostle Peter, because we do not compel the Gentiles to Judaize, which he is shown to have done, with the apostle Paul attesting and correcting it[^56]—since likewise among the Apostles the question of circumcision, as afterward of baptism among the bishops, wavered with no small difficulty.

**1. 33. 39.** Accordingly, although no certain example of this matter is brought forward from the canonical Scriptures, yet the truth of those same Scriptures is held by us even in this matter, when we do what has already pleased the universal Church, which the authority of those very Scriptures commends: so that, since holy Scripture cannot deceive, whoever fears to be deceived by the obscurity of this question may consult that same Church concerning it, which holy Scripture demonstrates without any ambiguity. But if you doubt that holy Scripture commends the Church which is spread through all nations, most copious in multitude (for if you did not doubt, you would not still be in the party of Donatus), I will load you with many and most manifest testimonies brought forward from the same authority, so that from your own concessions, if you are not unwilling to be too stubborn, you may be led even to this—when I have first shown that to my letter too, which you wished to answer to the contrary, you were able to answer nothing pertaining to the truth.

**1. 34. 40.** Let this for the moment be enough—what, on account of the excessive obstinacy of men, I judged ought to be said against those who, having a bad principal cause, wish by a technicality to turn the judges away from discussing it, when they say that there must be no speaking with us at all. For I have proved, both from the holy Scriptures and by as clear a reasoning as I could, that neither eloquence, however great, nor dialectic, of whatever sort, is to be feared by the asserters of truth, so as to keep them from convicting the asserters of falsehood by disputing with them and refuting them. And I have also demonstrated that point by which you said you were moved, apart from my letter—how it is not consequent that, if we concede there is baptism in the party of Donatus, we should at the same time also concede that anyone ought to be baptized in that same society: because, just as the reprobate people of the Jews could have a good law, so a reprobate society of heretics can have a good sacrament. But what is properly given in the Church, which apart from it is not given at all, will be shown in its place without difficulty[^57]. For we would not deal rightly with the heretics, whom we confess to have baptism, urging them by every means to come to the Catholic Church, unless by coming they received something which they cannot receive elsewhere—and which, unless they receive it, they have in vain and perniciously whatever other things, however good and pertaining to the law of God, they were able to receive anywhere. For whatever this is, which according to the Scriptures and most certain reason could be found, can be given or received only in the holy Church, this will pertain to the *sealed fountain, the well of living water, the paradise with the fruit of orchards*[^58], of which you made mention as best you could, but you showed that you did not understand what it was; since you reckon this was said of visible baptism—which, although it is holy and by no means to be passed over, since it excels by a most sacred signification, yet very many receive it, not only the good who according to his purpose are called to be conformed to the image of the Son of God[^59], but also those who will not possess the kingdom of God, among whom, as the Apostle says, both drunkards and the avaricious are numbered[^60]; I think that, if you lay aside obstinacy and consider that I speak truly, you will easily answer yourself, so as not to seek the sealed fountain and the well of living water except where those who displease God are divinely not permitted to approach.
