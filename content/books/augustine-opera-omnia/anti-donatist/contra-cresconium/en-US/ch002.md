# Book II

**2. 1. 1.** By the long discourse of the preceding volume I think that at last we have persuaded that your bishops are not to be praised in this, nor approved, in that, concerning the cause of the dissension which severs our communion, they are unwilling to hold conversation with us. For by this technicality, as it were, they think themselves most safe in a most evil cause—which, unless I am mistaken, I have thoroughly cut away both by true and certain reasonings and especially by examples of the divine Scriptures, by which I have most clearly taught that discourse was held by the holy preachers and asserters of truth, even against its present adversaries, and not only against those who were of the same people as they, but even against foreigners and strangers; and—whence above all you cast vain fears upon the unskilled—against those who professed dialectic most of all: lest the most diligent preacher be held most contentious, and lest the tireless disputant be thought a quarreler, being insistent, according to the precept of the Apostle, in season and out of season[^1], so that in sound doctrine those who contradict may be refuted, idle talkers confuted, the restless corrected, the faint-hearted consoled, the weak supported[^2], while against all who resist the word of evangelical salvation is defended with patience and preached without diffidence. I have also shown how you ought not to think that a man must be baptized among you because we too consent that baptism can be both had and given by you—since we say also that it is had perniciously and given perniciously; for those holy things which even the bad can use, the holier they are, the more uselessly and penally are they handled by them. Whence, when they come to the holy Church, they themselves are to be corrected: those things are not to be violated by the good, which were not even changed by the bad.

**2. 1. 2.** Hear, then, Cresconius, while I briefly demonstrate this too, that you said nothing through your whole letter whereby you might refute mine—unless perhaps that you taught me to derive or decline names, so that I should say "Donatians" from Donatus rather than "Donatists"—though you concede this to be at least a Greek declension, namely that "Donatists" are named from Donatus as "Evangelists" are from "Evangel," in which you say you delight, that for those of you who preach the Gospel the declension of the word should be borrowed from a similar one. See, then, lest perhaps your forebears themselves wished to be so called, because they have Donatus for a Gospel: for these are from Donatus just as all the saints are unwilling to depart from the fellowship of the Gospel; and therefore they delight to be called "Donatists" as "evangelists": and you rather do them injury, when you write, "in the Latin tongue," approving only the Latin rule: that they should be better called "Donatians" from Donatus, as "Arians" and "Novatians" from Arius and Novatus. For when I was writing, this name already sounded to me as propagated by I know not whom, nor did I take care to change it, since I reckoned that this too sufficed enough for the distinction I wished. For if Demosthenes, the most renowned of orators, who had such care for words as our authors had for things, when nevertheless Aeschines had cast at him a certain unusualness of speech, denied that the fortunes of Greece were placed in whether he had used this word or that, or had stretched out his hand this way or that[^3]: how much less ought we to labor over the rules of deriving names, when, whether we say this or that, what we say is understood without ambiguity—we whose greater intention is not in the polishing of speech, but in the demonstration of truth? But if any of our people first inflected this name, it seems to me in no way that he looked to that likeness by which "Evangelists" are named from "Evangel": but because, through Donatus—not only the one of Carthage, who is reported to have especially strengthened this heresy, but also the elder Donatus of Casae Nigrae, who first erected an altar against an altar in the same city—a great scandal was made; thus perhaps he wished to call them "Donatists" from Donatus, as "Scandalists" from scandal.

**2. 2. 3.** But I show myself most accommodating in a matter in which nothing of our cause is diminished, and when I deal with you, I now call you "Donatians"; but when I deal with others, I rather follow custom, which rightly rules over these sounds: only remember that I, to whom you attributed such great eloquence, do not yet know how to decline names, and announce security to your people, that they may no longer fear me as a dialectician, for whom you see a grammarian is still necessary. But if the discipline of disputing—whether you wish to call it dialectic or anything else—nevertheless teaches soberly enough that, when the thing is established, one ought not to labor over the name: just as I do not care whether that very thing be called dialectic, yet I care, as much as I am able, to know and be able to dispute—that is, to discern truth from falsehood in speaking; because unless I take care of this, I shall err most perniciously: so I do not care whether you be more expertly and learnedly declined "Donatists" or "Donatians"; whether, finally, from Donatus—either the one who first sacrificed outside the Church, or the one who especially strengthened this dissension—or from Maiorinus, who was the first bishop of your party ordained against Caecilian, the word ought to be applied to you, when we speak, for the sake of distinction. Yet that you are heretics, and therefore, lest you deceive, most cautiously to be avoided, unless I take diligent care to demonstrate, I shall incur no small blame of negligence for the burden of my office.

**2. 3. 4.** Although you judge that what has happened between us ought to be called a schism rather than a heresy, and—what dialecticians rarely dare—you distinguish these things even by definitions: where I shall not be able to demonstrate sufficiently how much you help us, unless I insert your words from your letter. "What do you mean," you say, "by what you say of the sacrilegious error of heretics? For heresies are accustomed to arise only among those following different things, nor is anyone a heretic except a worshiper of a contrary or otherwise interpreted religion, as are the Manichees, Arians, Marcionites, Novatians, and the rest, whose opinion stands among themselves at variance against the Christian faith. Among us, for whom the same Christ was born, died, and rose again, one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance, a schism is said to have been made, not a heresy. Since indeed heresy is a sect of those following different things; but schism, a separation of those following the same things. Wherefore in this too, by your zeal for accusing, you see how great an error you have fallen into, when you call a heresy what is a schism." These are indeed your words, which I have set down from your letter.

**2. 4. 5.** Now attend, if you are not stubborn, by what an easy shortcut you yourself will settle what was being dealt with between us. For if to both us and you the same Christ was born, died, and rose again, one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance, is it not perverse to rebaptize? For you set down three things, of which, had you set down one, it would have been more than enough. But, as if you were dealing faithfully against the Donatians, lest anyone, whether too acute, should try to interpret otherwise what you had once and briefly said, you took care to plunge and drive your sentence even into dull ears and hearts: "One religion," you say, "the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance." And do we still labor against one another? Now at last restrain dissension, mend the rupture, end the strife, love peace. What do you reject? What do you blow upon with contempt? Why do you rebaptize? "One religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance." For if to you and to us there is not one baptism, how is there one religion? But you said, "One religion": therefore also one baptism. If to us and to you there is not the same baptism, how are there the same sacraments? But you said, "The same sacraments": therefore the same baptism too. Likewise, if to us and to you baptism is different, how is nothing different in Christian observance? But you said, "Nothing different in Christian observance"; therefore baptism too is not different. Since this is so, we rightly neither disapprove, nor blow upon with contempt, nor repeat what is one and the same and not different; but we acknowledge, take up, accept it. But you impiously refuse to acknowledge what is one and the same and not different, refuse to take it up, are unwilling to accept it; but you choose to disapprove, dare to blow upon it with contempt, do not fear to repeat it. And while in this very thing—that those things which between us are not changed, we take up, you repel; we, if they were given by you, judge them given; you, if they were given by us, demand them again as though not given: while you follow things so different, you disdain to be called heretics.

**2. 4. 6.** Attend diligently to what you say, what I say. You certainly defined and said: "Heresy is a sect of those following different things; but schism, a separation of those following the same things." Likewise you said: "To us and to you there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance." If there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance, why then do you rebaptize a Christian? But if, in the very fact that you rebaptize a Christian, I do not rebaptize? We surely follow different things. Why are you unwilling to be called a heretic? I think that by no small sign we recognize heretics, who, while they confess that to themselves and to us there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance, are unwilling to acknowledge us as baptized. Or do you contend with such obstinacy, resist the truth with such dissension, that you separate baptism from religion, from the sacraments, from Christian observance? But if you do this, in that very thing you are heretics, in that you are unwilling that baptism pertain to religion, to the sacraments, to Christian observance. But if you do not do this, in that very thing you are heretics, in that those who have with you one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance—since you confess that baptism too pertains to religion, to the sacraments, to Christian observance—you nevertheless rebaptize. For attend diligently to your definition, in which you said: "Heresy is a sect of those following different things"; and see whether you do not follow different things—either by separating baptism from the religious observance of the Christian sacraments, with which we join it among great things; or, those with whom in one religious observance of the Christian sacraments there is also one baptism, by nevertheless rebaptizing, which we detest.

**2. 5. 7.** How I wish, if I could, when your people seize someone deceived from among our faithful, to be destroyed by their wicked snares—when they say that one already baptized among us has not even begun to be a Christian, when they blow upon him as upon a pagan, when they make him a catechumen, in order then to prepare him to be re-dyed, or rather extinguished—suddenly to appear from somewhere, and, holding out this letter of yours, and this very passage of it, by reciting it in the midst of their daring, to cry out: What are you doing? Behold, hear, see, read; there is to us and to you one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance: first ask in whose name this man was baptized, and then, if in your baptism you name another, a better one, give it. Then perhaps, if they did not tremble at the very evidence of the facts, they would at once produce their counsel, great of course and acute, and say: Who is this man to us, whose letter you carry? He is a layman of ours: he may win for us, he is conquered for himself. Then I, if I were present, turning to you, would say: At least, I beg you, tell us, what are these men doing? Behold, they arrange to rebaptize one baptized among us. "Surely, then, to us and to you there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance"? Or would you answer: But the baptism of Christ is not religion, is not a sacrament, is not Christian observance? May God turn away this madness from your mind. What, then, would you answer me pressing and saying: To us and to you there is one religion; but those to whom there is not one baptism, to them there is not one religion: therefore to us and to you there is one baptism. To us and to you there are the same sacraments; but those to whom baptism is not the same, to them the sacraments are not the same: therefore to us and to you baptism is the same. There is nothing to us and to you different in Christian observance; but those to whom baptism is different, surely there is something different in Christian observance: therefore to us and to you baptism is not different. Why is what is one disapproved? Why is what is the same blown upon with contempt? Why is what is not different repeated?

**2. 6. 8.** When I dealt thus in their presence, when I thus pressed, to what evasions would you flee? Of course, in your letter they would despise the grammarians, and you in mine would accuse the dialecticians: but the truth would on both sides overcome the heretics, showing this alone in them to be different from us, which is agreed to be perverse: because we acknowledge our own sacraments and amend another's error; but you confess the same sacraments, which, as though they were nothing, you repeat, with great difference disapproving what you concede to be not different.

**2. 7. 9.** Accordingly, although between schism and heresy I rather approve that distinction by which a schism is said to be a recent dissension of a congregation arising from some difference of opinions (for a schism too cannot come about unless those who make it follow something different); but a heresy, a schism grown old: yet what need is there for me to labor over this, since your definitions help me so much that, if it were granted me by your other people too, I would more willingly call you schismatics than heretics. For if those make a schism who have with those from whom they divide themselves one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance: hence your rebaptizing is the more damnable; because in one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance, there cannot be another and different baptism. But since what you follow as different is neither nothing nor anything small—seeing that, separated from the bond of unity, you also dissent from us about the repetition of baptism—it comes about that, according to that very definition of yours by which you said, "But heresy is a sect of those following different things," you are both heretics and shown to be conquered: heretics indeed, in that, not only divided, you follow something different in rebaptizing; but conquered, because the baptism given through us, as though it were not itself or as though it were nothing, you repeat—which you confess to be one and the same and not different. For these are your words, that "to us and to you there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance."

**2. 8. 10.** Wherefore, if the party of Donatus subscribed to your letter, and then considered, without mad obstinacy or shamelessness, the things said by you and by me, it would think and say nothing further against us. But since it is you to whom I am answering, I now think that you yourself also see how it was not from zeal for accusing, but in order to refute a pernicious fallacy, that I said: "the sacrilegious error of the Donatist heretics." Of these four words or names—since this pleases either you or the art of grammar—the first that was set down, "Donatist," I correct and change, and say "Donatian"; but the other three, since you now perceive, as I think, that they were said most truly, you yourselves correct, you yourselves change. Change, I say, and correct, the "Donatians'"—or whatever else you are to be called—nevertheless "sacrilegious error of heretics"; for you are heretics, either because you have remained in an inveterate schism, or, from your own definition, because concerning the Church, which is the body of Christ, or concerning the repetition of Christian baptism, you follow something different. And it is a sacrilegious error, not only to be separated from Christian unity, but also to violate and rescind the sacraments, which according to your own confession are one and the same. But if you correct and change these things, how do we "receive you such as you were"? Whence in vain have you spoken so many things, and, although you are of so acute a wit, you are dulled by the habit of hearing vanity, so that it seems to you that, when they come over to us from you, we receive them such as they were: because in them we approve the tradition of the Christians, which neither the alienated had alienated nor the perverse had perverted; which sacraments even you, although not such as we are, could not nevertheless confess to be anything but such—and not such as though they were other, similar ones, but altogether the same.

**2. 9. 11.** I beg you, tell me, how is he such as he was, who venerates the Church which he blasphemed, who holds the unity which he did not hold, who has the love which he did not have, who receives the peace which he rejected, who approves the sacrament which he blew upon with contempt? Or are all things so inverted, true things put after false, that those are not said to be changed in whom the things that were different are corrected by truth, and those are said to be changed in whom the things that were one and the same and similar are repeated by vanity? Do not, then, any further in this matter be wise, not only carnally, but even childishly, so as to reckon that your people are received by us such as they were—who, by the conversion of their will from error to truth, from division to unity, from dissension to peace, from enmities to love, from human presumption to the authority of the divine Scriptures, do not begin to be ours before they have ceased to be yours. This conversion of will suddenly changed not only a sinner at the tax booth[^4] but even a robber on the cross. Unless you think that Christ would have wished a bloody and wicked man to be with him in paradise, if that conversion of heart had not at once made him innocent, so that on that day, from that place, from that wood, he passed over to the immortal reward of faith, on which he had received the punishment of death for his iniquity[^5]. For whether to evil or to good the mind is changed in a brief moment, yet what it merits is not therefore small. In bodily and temporal goods and benefits themselves, a single blow destroys an age of whatever length, however long nourished; and the Lord, as soon as he deigned to command it, healed a sickness of thirty-eight years[^6]. Believe in sure things, not in empty words. Your people, changed, come over to us; far be it that they should be those who were. And would that you too did this, and believed for yourself how true this is in you.

**2. 10. 12.** You seemed to yourself to say something great by naming "Candidus of Villa Regia and Donatus of Macomades, who from being your bishops were bishops even among us," and by an approved life attained the more honorable merit of old age: as though the sacraments and the invocation of the name of God which take place among you were themselves an enemy to us; whereas even in those who are outside the Church there is nothing at all but what is of the Church. In which question, if I labored with my own words, I would be helped by yours. For if you perceived that nothing ecclesiastical could be outside the Church, you would not yourself have said: "to us and to you there is one religion, the same sacraments, nothing different in Christian observance." To which words of yours I do not wholly consent. For you lack the Christian Church, you do not have Christian love. But I do indeed recognize the Christian sacraments in you, and in these I disapprove and reject that one difference, that while you have them the same even in schism, you blow upon them with contempt the same in the Catholics. Wholly does the Church recognize in you all the things that are hers: nor are they therefore not hers, because they are found also among you. For among you they are alien: but when she whose they are receives you corrected, the things you held perniciously as alien become salutarily yours too. Discord possesses you under the title of peace. Let discord, then, be driven out, let peace be brought in. What reason is there for the title to be laid down? "He is a bishop," you say, "you receive a bishop; a presbyter, a presbyter." You could also say this to me: He is a man, you receive a man. For I recognize the Christian sacraments in him as much as the human members; nor do I care through whom they were sown, but by whom they were created. If anyone wishes to use them badly, by that very fact he becomes bad, in that he offends the Creator by his goods: but if he begins to use them well, he will correct himself, not change them.

**2. 11. 13.** And concerning the receiving of bishops or clergy, indeed, it is another question. For although, when they are ordained among you, the name of Donatus is not invoked over them, but of God, yet they are received in such a way as seems to suit the peace and usefulness of the Church. For we are not bishops for our own sake, but for the sake of those to whom we minister the word and the sacrament of the Lord: and through this, as the necessity of governing them without scandal demands, so we ought either to be, or not to be, what we are not for ourselves but for others. Indeed, certain men endowed with holy humility, on account of certain stumbling blocks in themselves by which they were piously and religiously moved, laid down the office of the episcopate, not only without fault but even with praise. Would they lay down the Christian name and faith likewise, with praise, and not rather with damnation? Just as in receiving these offices there can be just causes why anyone should excuse himself from becoming a bishop, yet there can in no way be any just cause why anyone should excuse himself from becoming a Christian. Why so, except because without the episcopate or clerical office we can be saved, but without the Christian religion we cannot?

**2. 12. 14.** Accordingly your bishops, or any clergy, as far as the ecclesiastical offices themselves are concerned, are received into Catholic unity in the way that seemed expedient for those to whose salvation it was provided by the exercising or the omitting of their office. Yet even concerning those who administered the same honors among us too, can you, just as you could say, "He is a bishop, you receive a bishop," likewise say: He is a heretic, you receive a heretic; or: He is a schismatic, you receive a schismatic; or: He is a Donatian, you receive a Donatian? For by these names not a grade of honor is distinguished from plebeian rank, but the crime of error from Catholic truth. Accordingly those, as it were ecclesiastical offices, found even among aliens, which those who become ours by leaving you and coming over to us, are for the usefulness of the peoples whom by this dispensation we serve, either received or not received; but those faulty things, properly your own, are healed, corrected, changed; provided nevertheless that those sacraments without which a man cannot become a Christian are so handled even among the heretics that, when they come to the Church, what was lacking is added, what is recognized is approved—lest, while we are too much offended by those evils which they brought forth against the Church, we persecute also the goods which they drew from the Church in departing. For even a broken branch, if it is again, as the Apostle says, to be grafted in[^7], the root is restored to it, the form is not changed.

**2. 12. 15.** "But indeed, since you call our people," you say, "heretics and sacrilegious, which is a nefarious and inexpiable crime, ought or can such men be pardoned without some expiation? Why then," you say, "do you not cleanse the one who comes? Why do you not first wash and purify him, and so let him commune with you?" What of the fact that from these words of yours another might much more fittingly have said that such men neither ought nor can now be pardoned: and might have shown that you spoke contrary to yourself, in that you said such men must be pardoned with some expiation, because what is charged is inexpiable? How then is what is inexpiable expiated? How shall I hope that you will hear what I say, who so near at hand do not yourself hear what you say, when you immediately contradict yourself, judging that what you said was inexpiable must be expiated? But we so call your error heretical and sacrilegious that we nevertheless do not call it inexpiable: otherwise it was in vain that we judged we should deal with you in whatever ways we can, so that, leaving it, you might come over corrected to the Catholic Church. Nor should you reckon that I "use your word," as you write, as though "we say this evil to be without pardon and without a physician"; which we by no means say; because both those who repent of this evil merit pardon, and the physician is almighty who says through the prophet, *If you turn and groan, then you will be saved*[^8]. Wherefore, if you perhaps fell upon someone either less instructed in these matters, or less attentive to what he was saying—although he seemed to be of the Catholic communion—who said this to you, let him rather merit pardon for this inconsiderate word: just as you, who, although you are so liberally educated and not moderately learned in the art of words, yet attending less to what you say, judge that what you said was inexpiable must be expiated; and, what is more monstrous, must be expiated for this reason, because it is inexpiable. But it is not Catholic to exhort to be saved by correcting their error those whom we would assert to err inexpiably and incurably. But you think they are therefore not cleansed when they come over to us from you, because they are not baptized anew: as though by baptism alone, which ought not to be repeated, since it is one and the same, men are cleansed from error. They are cleansed also by the word of truth by him who says, *Now you are clean because of the word which I have spoken to you*[^9]. They are cleansed also by the sacrifice of a contrite heart by him of whom it is said, *A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God does not despise*[^10]. They are cleansed also by almsgiving by him who says, *Give alms, and behold all things are clean for you*[^11]. They are cleansed by that very love which surpasses all things, by him who said through the apostle Peter, *Love covers a multitude of sins*[^12]; which one thing if it is present, all those things are rightly done; but if that is lacking, all are done in vain. But whence this comes, hear the Apostle saying, *The love of God*, he says, *has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us*[^13]. Whence it is deservedly believed that those who have received the baptism of the Church outside the Church do not have the Holy Spirit, except when they themselves adhere to the Church in the bond of peace through the connection of love.

**2. 13. 16.** For now there is occasion to show what we deferred in the first book—what can properly be received in the Church, which is the holy body of Christ, that cannot be received apart from it. For concerning those who made schisms, the same Apostle says, *But the animal man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God*[^14]. Read the First Epistle to the Corinthians, and you will find it. Baptism, therefore, is the sacrament of the new life and of eternal salvation, which many have, not unto eternal life, but unto eternal punishment, not using so great a good well; but holy love, *which is the bond of perfection*[^15], no one who is not good can have; no one who has it can be either a schismatic or a heretic. Since, therefore, anyone coming to the unity of the Church, when he is truly joined to its members, receives the Holy Spirit, through whom love is poured out in our hearts[^16], and that same love covers a multitude of sins[^17], so that the baptism which he first had unto judgment he now merits to have unto reward; how do you deny that he is cleansed, except that you are altogether ignorant of what spiritual cleansing is? Not, then, as you revile, do we "receive your guilty as into the asylum of Romulus," whom the city of God, by receiving, makes innocent, when they have come over to it with a truthful heart, of which its founder says, *A city set upon a mountain cannot be hidden*[^18]. For he did not found it who, angered, proudly slew his brother, but he who, slain, humbly redeemed his brethren. This city he gladdens by the cleansing Holy Spirit, of which he cried out, saying, *If anyone thirsts, let him come and drink*[^19]; not commending the visible water which is given in the sacrament of baptism, which both the good and the bad can have, although without it the good cannot be saved. Which water, although it is the Church's, nevertheless flows even outside, when it is found also among those who went out from us, but were not of us[^20]: just as water in any one of those memorable four rivers cannot be denied to be water of paradise[^21], although it is not in paradise alone, because it flowed thence even outside.

**2. 14. 17.** Not this water, then, but by the name of water he commended the invisible gift of God, the Holy Spirit, saying, *If anyone thirsts, let him come and drink*[^22]; as the Evangelist plainly testifies, adding, *Now this he said of the Spirit which those who believed in him were to receive. For the Spirit had not yet been given, because Jesus had not yet been glorified*[^23]. And surely, as far as the sacrament of the visible washing is concerned, before he was glorified by rising again, he had already baptized more than John, as the Gospel itself says[^24]. Whence he says to his disciples, *John indeed baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit, whom you are also to receive not many days hence*—up to Pentecost[^25]. This Holy Spirit, coming upon them, gave first such a sign that those who had received him spoke in the tongues of all nations[^26]; because it portended that the Church would be among all nations, and that no one would receive the Holy Spirit except one joined to its unity. With the broad and invisible river of this fountain God gladdens his city, of which the Prophet foretold, *The rush of a river gladdens the city of God*[^27]. For to this fountain no stranger comes, because no one comes except one worthy of eternal life. This is proper to the Church of Christ, to which it was prophesied so long before, *Let the fountain of your water be your own, and let no stranger share it with you*[^28]. For of this Church and of this fountain it is said also in the Song of Songs, *An enclosed garden, a sealed fountain, a well of living water*[^29].

**2. 15. 18.** Your people, taking these things of the visible sacrament of baptism, so err that they are compelled unwillingly to confess most absurd things: namely, that to that fountain which is proper to the one dove, of which it was said, *Let no stranger share it with you*[^30]—to the enclosed garden and the sealed well—Simon Magus could come, whom we read was baptized by Philip[^31]; that so many feigned persons could come, of whom Cyprian, groaning, speaks: "renouncing the world in words only and not in deeds"; so many avaricious bishops, of whom the same man testifies: "seizing estates by insidious frauds, increasing their gains by multiplying usuries." For these are indeed found baptized and baptizing with the visible baptism. Yet to that proper fountain, with which no stranger communes; to that sealed fountain—that is, to the gift of the Holy Spirit, by which the love of God is poured out in our hearts[^32]—none of these comes except changed, so altogether to be changed that he is not a stranger, but is a partaker of heavenly peace, an associate of holy unity, full of inseparable love, a citizen of the angelic city. To this city, therefore, whoever, having laid aside the error of heresy or schism, with morals corrected, with a pious mind, is converted, if he already bore the sacraments, which could flow outside even to the unworthy, these are honored in him, because even in aliens they are not seen to be alien; but he is now cleansed by that proper fountain with which no stranger communes, by that sealed fountain of the Holy Spirit, from which among you whoever is praiseworthy in his other morals is separated by the one crime of schism or heresy.

**2. 16. 19.** When, therefore, your people come to us, ceasing to be yours, beginning to be ours, they receive what they did not have, so that they may begin salutarily to have what they had so much the more perniciously the more unworthily they had it. For they first receive the Church itself, and in it peace, love, unity, through its proper and invisible fountain, the Holy Spirit, without which surely, no one doubting, they would have perished, whatever else of the Church's, which could be drawn outside, they had had: but with easier pardon they receive what they had not yet had, than if they had had it and already deserted it. And this is distinguished among us, that those who left the Catholic Church are received in one way, those who come to it for the first time in another. For the former are burdened more by the crime of desertion: but the latter the bond of unity, not broken away from them but recognized and held, relieves. Whence it can happen that those who rebaptized the seduced, may themselves pray to the Lord for those, when penitent, if the latter were first reconciled, rather than those reconciled, to the Church. Just as it can happen that even worshipers of idols, if they have perhaps made apostates of some Christians seduced to idols, if the seducers are first made Christians and have attained some great merit in the Church, those whom they had deceived may return through them, may through them be commended and reconciled to the Lord, through whom they had forsaken the Lord. For what avails to cleanse the sacrileges of the Gentiles—the rightly received sacrament of baptism—this avails to cleanse the sacrileges of schismatics and heretics—the truly grasped love of unity. Wherefore, just as seducers of faithful Christians, coming to Christ, are set before the seduced who return, whence the latter can even become bishops, the former cannot: so those deceived by heretics ought not to wonder, when they return to the Catholic Church, that their own deceivers are set before them when they come to the Catholic Church. For the latter more excusably seek what was lacking to them; the former more humbly seek again what they were: the latter we summon more honorably to where they had not yet stood; the former we recall more suspiciously from whence they had fallen.

**2. 16. 20.** Accordingly you now perceive, I suppose, that it was not in vain that I said "the sacrilegious error of the Donatist"—or, as you prefer, "Donatian—heretics," if you both dissent from the Catholic Church and rescind the sacraments which you confess to be one and the same: nor yet are you without pardon or beyond cure by the mercy of God; because, when the divisive error has been laid aside, and you are converted to Catholic truth and peace, through his proper gift—that is, his Holy Spirit, through whom love is poured out in our hearts[^33]—you will be able to be cleansed and healed: not so that the Church's sacraments be destroyed in you, which you held alien and perniciously outside, but so that you may now salutarily have those very things within, now your own.

**2. 17. 21.** Now let us see how you show that Petilian, or anyone else, said rightly: "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the one who receives." To which I said: "What if the conscience of the giver is hidden, and perhaps is stained? How will it be able to cleanse the conscience of the receiver?" You, on the contrary, spoke at length, not what is said by you, an acute man, but what is said by your people, which can all be briefly stated thus: "That the conscience of the giver is attended to, not according to its sincerity, which cannot be seen in it, but according to the reputation about it, which is either true or false": because, namely, it is that of a man, who, even if he is a hidden criminal, it suffices for the receiver that he is of good estimation, not yet known, not yet judged, not yet separated from the Church. See, I beg you, into what a precipice the narrow strait of finding no way out has driven men. So, can the stained conscience of the giver cleanse the conscience of the receiver, if he has a good reputation, and will it have power to cleanse just so far as a good one, if he contracted a good reputation by deceiving? Do you attend to what is said, and do you wish that we now pass over this point, or shall I still turn this same thing over, so that you may be compelled to attend more diligently? Petilian said: "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the one who receives." I said: "What if the conscience of the giver is hidden, and perhaps is stained? How will it be able to cleanse the conscience of the receiver?" You, or rather your people, said (for when would you, such a man, have said these things?): "Although he has a stained conscience, yet to me, who am baptized by him, since it is hidden and I do not know it, it suffices that I receive from one whose conscience I think innocent, because he is in the Church. For therefore," you say, "I attend to the conscience of the giver, not so that I may judge of hidden things, which cannot be done, but so that, if there is anything about him in the public conscience, I may not be ignorant. For on this account it was said by Almighty God: *The things that are known are for you; the things that are hidden, for me*[^34]. Always, then, I attend to the conscience of the giver; and because I do not see it itself, I ask what about it is known in public. Nor does it matter to the case, if one thing is in the secret conscience, another in the public reputation. For it suffices to have known that the conscience of him from whom I receive is not yet condemned."

**2. 18. 22.** Behold, I have set down your very words, by which I might show you saying what I briefly and plainly comprehended in my words—that the conscience of the one who cleanses is attended to for this, that what reputation there is about it may be known. Not, then, is it itself attended to, good man, not itself which cannot be seen: but the reputation is attended to, which can even be false, which you yourself confess and concede. For you too saw that a stained conscience does not avail to cleanse. Not, then, is the conscience of the one who gives holily attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver; but the reputation by which one is thought to give holily, even one who does not give holily, and one is thought to cleanse who does not cleanse. The good reputation of a bad man, then, cleanses the receiver, not the polluted conscience of the giver himself. Why, then, was it said: "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver," except that it does not cleanse that of the receiver, if there is not the conscience of one giving holily? If it is stained and unclean, what then is attended to? It itself, you say, is attended to, when its reputation is attended to; and if the reputation is good, it makes no difference for cleansing the receiver, even when the conscience is bad, but his good reputation cleanses. Tell me, I ask you, when the conscience is bad, is its good reputation true or false? Without doubt, surely, false. Accordingly, when the conscience of the giver is not good and is hidden, however you may seem to yourself to attend to it, according to this opinion it does not cleanse the receiver, except by the giver's either false reputation or polluted conscience. Both are insane: if such things delight you, you choose which is more insane. Moreover, since truth does not admit that either a false reputation of the giver or a polluted conscience cleanses the conscience of the receiver, it remains that we also require of you what we asked there too (since, as Petilian or anyone else says, with you agreeing, when there is the conscience of one giving holily—that is, when there is a good and clean conscience—it cleanses the conscience of the receiver), whence the receiver is cleansed when the stained conscience of the giver is hidden. For I think you will not now repeat and say that his false good reputation fills the place of a good conscience in cleansing: it suffices that you asserted that your people, not you, say this. Let you be ashamed of them, not also of yourself. It remains, then, that either God or some holy angel cleanses. But if you say this, that horrible absurdity will follow, which, mentioned in that letter of mine, I do not say you were unwilling to see, because you altogether did not touch it; but you saw it the more attentively and acutely the more you feared to touch it. For if you say this—that when a holy man baptizes, his holy conscience washes the conscience of the receiver, but when the conscience of the giver is secretly unclean, then God or an angel cleanses—beware lest those who believe you saying these things should choose to find hidden bad men by whom they may be baptized, so that they may be much more holily cleansed by God himself or his angel. This absurdity, to be either derided or detested, when you perceived it follow consequently upon the preceding words of Petilian, and mentioned by me too in my letter, you cautiously, indeed, as though I had said none of these things, judged so great a matter must be passed over in silence; but you fled to something more absurd, I know not what—that when the stained conscience of the giver is hidden, and therefore cannot cleanse the receiver's, then the receiver is cleansed by his false reputation, and in him falsehood works truth.

**2. 18. 23.** Now go and slanderously accuse the dialecticians, because "by a cunning of speech to be fled they bring it about that, if a thing is false, it is true; if it is true, it is false." Behold, you yourself inject something of the kind into the sacraments of Christian regeneration—indeed, something worse and more to be fled. For they, not in their own fraud nor in the truth of things, but in the perplexity of human speech, say certain words to seem true when they are false, false when they are true; which, when they fall into disputation, are discerned by the mind, even if they cannot be resolved by speech. But you say that not any word whatever, nor any thing whatever, but that very cleansing of conscience by which we are born again into eternal life, can become true in a man through the false reputation of another's conscience. And lest this be attributed to you, who learned dialectic, you say that this is the opinion of your people, with whom you have agreed not as a dialectician, but plainly as a heretic. Through you, then, or through your people, this is the magnificent doctrine invented or demonstrated: When there is the good conscience of one giving holily, then one who is baptized becomes good according to it, then a good tree bears good fruit[^35]: but when the conscience of the giver is bad and hidden, then his good reputation, which is false, is consulted; so that then a man receives true baptism from a deceiver, when he has believed what is false about him: and thus, lest there be lacking a tree to bear the fruit of heretical error, falsehood becomes the mother of truth. All this, therefore, so execrable, so wonderfully perverse and insane, is to keep what is God's from being said to be God's; so that what is taken from God may be attributed to men; lest he who said, "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver," seem to have erred.

**2. 19. 24.** "Our people," you say, "prove this from the Scriptures, since Judas the betrayer, before he was condemned, did all things as an apostle." What is this to Petilian's fixed and defined opinion: "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver"—except that this Judas too is too much against you, when you try to lend patronage to another's words inconsiderately brought forward? For when Judas baptized as an apostle, although he was bad, since he was a thief, and carried off the things that were put into the purses entrusted to him[^36]; surely his conscience was not attended to, but God and Christ in whom belief was placed. For neither did the false good reputation of that bad man cleanse the believers who received, nor did the falsehood of human opinion generate in a man the grace of divine truth.

**2. 19. 25.** And that testimony which you set down from the Scriptures, *The things that are manifest are for you; but the things that are hidden, for the Lord your God*[^37]—it refutes and convicts these words. For if the things that are hidden are to be left to the Lord our God, how is the conscience of the giver—not only bad, but even good—attended to when it is hidden, that it may cleanse the receiver's? Or if it itself, when it is hidden, is not attended to, what then do you bid the receiver attend to, whence his conscience may be cleansed?

**2. 20. 25.** Wake up at last, now at least say: Let him attend to God. Why do you fear to be humbled, if you glory not in man but in the Lord[^38]? There is something, you say, that I fear: for if, when the conscience of the giver is hidden, I say that the receiver should attend to God, and confess that then his conscience is cleansed by him, that horrible absurdity will follow upon me—that men are more holily cleansed when they have hidden bad baptizers than when they have manifest good ones, if a man cleanses when the conscience of the giver is good and manifest, but God when it is bad and hidden. Say, then, what we say; because this is true, this is sound, this is Catholic: that Christ cleanses the consciences of the receivers, whether through good ministers of his baptism, or through bad, since of him it is written, *Christ loved the Church, and gave himself for it, that he might sanctify it, cleansing it by the washing of water in the word*[^39].

**2. 20. 26.** "You," you say, "answer how those baptize whom the Church has condemned." Now, then, one withdraws from Petilian's words; since, when he says, "The conscience of the one who gives holily is attended to, that it may cleanse the conscience of the receiver," when I asked who cleanses the conscience of the receiver when the polluted conscience of the giver is hidden, I could not be answered. For it is as much against the truth to say that his false reputation can cleanse, as if it were said that the polluted conscience itself could.

**2. 21. 26.** But if you ask, "How do those baptize whom the Church has condemned?" I answer that they baptize in the same way as those baptize whom God has condemned, before the Church judged anything about them. For he who with a perverse mind seems to be within, when he is without, has already been judged by Christ himself. For he says, *He who does not believe has already been judged*[^40]. And the apostle Paul says, *The Church is subject to Christ*[^41]. The Church, therefore, ought not to set itself before Christ, so as to think that it can baptize those judged by him, but cannot baptize those judged by itself; since he always judges truthfully, but ecclesiastical judges, like men, are for the most part deceived. They baptize, therefore, as far as the visible ministry is concerned, both the good and the bad: but invisibly through them he baptizes whose are both the visible baptism and the invisible grace. Both good and bad, then, can dip; but cleanse the conscience none can except he who is always good. And through this, even when the Church does not know it, those condemned by Christ on account of a bad and polluted conscience are no longer in the body of Christ, which is the Church, since Christ cannot have condemned members. Accordingly, these too baptize outside the Church. For far be it altogether that all these monsters be counted among the members of that one dove: far be it that they be able to enter the bounds of the enclosed garden[^42], whose guardian is he who cannot be deceived. Yet if they confess and are corrected, then they enter, then they are cleansed, then they are numbered among the trees of the enclosed garden, then among the members of the one dove, nor yet are they baptized anew. So too, when they come from the heretics with that same baptism, which they had outside too, but not with that same cleansing which they receive within; so that what was lacking is supplied, and what was not changed is approved.

**2. 22. 27.** "Your conscience," you say, "was condemned both by your forebears for the crime of handing over and of incense-offering, and by you for the crime of persecution." Concerning those who handed over the Scriptures and offered incense, indeed—who they were who committed this wickedness—you believed not the holy Scriptures, but the reputation of men. Which, if it can be falsely good about the bad, why can it not also be falsely bad about the good? But concerning persecution I would answer you this briefly and again, which, when I had answered Petilian, you were by no means able to refute. For in holy Scripture, which deceives no one, the Church of God is called a threshing floor; and the Lord himself is said to be going to come with a winnowing fork and to cleanse his threshing floor, so as to store the grain in the barn, but to burn the chaff with unquenchable fire[^43]. Either, then, you rightly suffered persecution; or, if the Christian measure was exceeded, our chaff did it, on account of which the Lord's threshing floor ought not to have been deserted, lest whoever fled the chaff from the threshing floor before the time of winnowing should, separated from the grain, become chaff. But you, when you tried to refute this testimony—not mine, but of holy Scripture—said that no persecution can be just: where you must be pardoned, not knowing the Scriptures, whence it could come into your mind how just is the voice in which it was said, *Him who secretly detracted from his neighbor, I persecuted*[^44]. And by the Lord Jesus Christ himself in a most excellent prophecy: *I will persecute my enemies, and overtake them, and I will not turn back until they fail*[^45]. And many other divine testimonies which it would be long to pursue: provided you do not slander me on account of this word, so that, because I said it would be long to pursue, you charge me with being a persecutor of the divine testimonies.

**2. 23. 28.** You oppose to me words of the Scriptures, so often shown how little they help you: *Let not the oil of the sinner make fat my head*[^46]—when you cannot deny that there are among you at least hidden sinners, who nevertheless baptize, and who are not excepted by these words. For it does not say: The oil of the manifest sinner; but absolutely: *The oil of the sinner.* And: *They have become to me like deceitful water having no faithfulness*[^47]. Here I marvel at your prudence, how the deceitful water of the hidden dissembler does not seem to you to be such—whose false reputation too you believed to profit something for cleansing another's conscience: except that you reckoned that what is not dialectical but plainly sophistical, which you vainly reprehended in me as in a dialectician, comes to your aid: *If you lie, you speak the truth.* For what else do you try to assert, when, attributing to a man the baptism which you are unwilling to acknowledge to be God's, you therefore say that an adulterer can give true baptism, because by hiding himself he lies that he is chaste? And so he then speaks the truth in baptism, when he lies in his crime; and his water is not deceitful, which you wish to be in the Church spread, according to the promise of so many prophecies, through the whole world: although Jeremiah called not baptism, but lying men, deceitful water, according to the understanding which is manifest in the Apocalypse, where, when John asked what those waters shown in the vision were, it was answered that they were peoples[^48].

**2. 24. 29.** But concerning that testimony which is written, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit?*[^49] attend a little to how you did not understand what I set down in that letter, and how much you helped me with your own words. For when you had thought that by "a dead man" in this place I understood a worshiper of idols, as though I had excepted those alone who cannot baptize, you did as much as you could—repeating that testimony about oil—to show that he who said, *The oil of the sinner shall not make fat my head*[^50], willed that no sinner should baptize, excepting no sinner; whence you are the more pressed, as I showed a little before. For if no sinner is excepted, accordingly rebaptize anew those baptized by hidden bad men, when they have been convicted. Here you will now try to except the hidden sinner, whom holy Scripture did not except. This whole understanding of yours, then, is convicted of being false by the truth, since it is shown likewise to be repugnant to your own aim. For neither was this in truth set down concerning baptism in the Psalm, rather than concerning the flattery of a fawner; which the preceding words sufficiently indicate; for the whole sentence is connected thus: *The righteous man will amend me in mercy, and reprove me; but the oil of the sinner shall not make fat my head*[^51]. He preferred, then, to have his head bruised by one truthfully reproving, than to be anointed by one falsely flattering—the words being transferred, oil and anointing signifying the smoothness of the flatterer.

**2. 25. 30.** But what I thought in that letter about this testimony, where it was said, *He who is baptized by a dead man*[^52], I shall sufficiently show, as I think, by the very words repeated from it. For when I was discussing what a Catholic Christian ought to answer to these things: "When he hears," I said, "*He who is baptized by a dead man, his washing does not profit him*, he will answer: *Christ lives, and now dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him*[^53]; of whom it was said, *It is he who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^54]. But those are baptized by dead men who are baptized in the temples of idols. For they themselves do not reckon that they receive from their priests the sanctification which they suppose, but from their gods. Who, since they were men, and so died that they live neither upon the earth nor in the rest of the saints, are truly themselves baptized by dead men." Thus far I have transcribed the very words from my letter: in which you now perceive, as I suppose, if now at least you attend diligently, that I did not call the worshipers themselves dead—although in another way they too are dead—but the false gods whom they worship, because they were men, and as men departed from the body, and did not rise again, nor had any merit of the life which is promised after this life. By these gods, then, those who are baptized, as I said, are truly baptized by dead men—that is, those who are baptized in their name; because they themselves too think they are sanctified not in the name of their priests, but of the gods about whom they suppose vain things. But Christ rose again, and lives: accordingly he who is baptized by him, not only through a good but even through a bad minister dead by ruined morals, is not baptized by a dead man. For he is baptized by him who lives forever, and of whom it was said in the Gospel, which I also mentioned there: *It is he who baptizes in the Holy Spirit*[^55].

**2. 26. 31.** This, in my letter, as far as your words indicate, you did not understand; for I am unwilling to say: Understanding it too little, you wished to defraud. But I marvel that you did not notice the words there following mine, or thought they should be dissembled. For a little after I added and said: "For if in this place I understand by 'a dead man' a sinner baptizer, that same absurdity will follow—that whoever has been baptized even by a hidden impious man, as though baptized by a dead man, has been washed in vain. For it does not say: He who is baptized by a manifest dead man; but said absolutely: 'by a dead man.'" This manifestation of my words there—whom would it not rouse from sleep, or rather from death itself? And yet it did not rouse you: and moreover what I had said against Petilian you, as though speaking against me, even more vehemently confirmed; just as men are accustomed, in not knowing how to extract a shot arrow, to fix it even more deeply and inwardly. For you asserted that "a dead man" is to be understood as nothing but a sinner baptizer, and that no sinner is to be excepted: and therefore what I was saying is concluded against you—that the hidden one cannot be excepted, where no one is excepted. Rebaptize, then, those whom it is established to have been baptized by hidden sinners, who can, while living and knowing, be helped; so that it harms those alone now who either do not know, or have died before, so that, their bad baptizers being afterward made manifest, they can no longer be baptized. Baptize, I say, with the adulterer betrayed and condemned, those whom it is established to have been baptized by him while he was hidden: for they were baptized by a dead man, since you said that every sinner without any exception is to be understood, who cannot baptize, by adding the testimony about the oil of the sinner. You said it, you wrote it: hear yourself, read yourself. If every sinner, as a dead man, cannot baptize, neither can the hidden one. For he is not alive because he is hidden; since he is even more deeply consumed by the lie of dissimulation. For he would be called less dead, if he at least confessed. But there happens in him what is written in another place: *From a dead man, as from one who is not, confession perishes*[^56]. From this dead man sunk in such depth of death, those whom it has become clear to have been baptized, you do not rebaptize: and those baptized by men who in the farthest lands have heard the name neither of Caecilian, nor of Maiorinus, nor of Donatus, you do not at all doubt to rebaptize, if you can, objecting to them: *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^57]—calling those dead to whom not even the breath of African corpses, whosesoever they were, could reach; and not thinking him a dead man who can hide his own crime; while Scripture cries out: *From a dead man, as from one who is not, confession perishes.* So, because he is feigned, is he not dead? When rather, on account of that very feigning, by lacking the spirit of life he has utterly expired, Scripture again saying, *For the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee the feigned*[^58]. Still defend these dead men, and say that they live, so that you too may die the worse by that very false defense.

**2. 26. 32.** They are dead, you say: but what should one do who, to be baptized, approached them in ignorance? Let him now, then, do it, when, that man being betrayed, he has recognized himself baptized by a dead man. For if his conscience could not be harmed for this reason, that he did not know; now it begins to be harmed, because he knew: just as if one were unknowingly clothed in a tunic from a robbery, from then it becomes a garment of iniquity, from when he has known it, and he himself is unjust unless he casts it off: and one who unknowingly married another's wife, from then becomes an adulterer, from when he has learned it, unless he departs. Let this man too, then, cast off the baptism which he has learned he received from a dead man. For he has what he may yet do: he can be baptized again. Finally, whether he knew or did not know: *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^59] "It is a strict sentence," as you yourself too cry out, "it excepted no one: 'by a dead man,'" I say, "he was baptized, his washing profits him nothing." Cleanse the man by baptizing, you who live, or rather cleanse yourselves from this error, lest by holding such opinions you perish. For you contend, as though against me, so as to say that no one is excepted in what is written: *The oil of the sinner*[^60]; and: *He who is baptized by a dead man*[^61]; nor do you perceive that you are tightening, by struggling, the knot by which you are bound. For this is what I am doing, this I press, hence I urgently drive you toward changing your vain and perverse opinion: that in the oil of the sinner and the baptism of the dead man, no dead man, no sinner is excepted: just as you, speaking for me as though against me, say. For thus neither is the hidden one excepted: by which all that you say is overturned; and so those who taught you such things are compelled to rebaptize all whom they can find in this life baptized by hidden bad men.

**2. 27. 33.** What are you doing? Where do you turn? Your own words are recited to you. For not only did you fail to refute mine when I said them, but, not knowing that you had read mine, in other words you yourself said this same thing as though it were your own; and, so that it could be more diligently heard and considered, you wrote it, so that it may be recited to you as often as you please. Hear, then, your own words; for these are they: "But if," you say, "you please to except only the worshiper of an idol, what of that one of whom it is said, *I will not have the oil of the sinner anoint my head*[^62]—is this sinner here that one only who worships an idol, or whoever commits what is not lawful? But if you think the sinner too to be only the worshiper of an idol, then will a Christian, if he has done anything against the law, not be said to have sinned? But if nothing more foolish or more absurd can be said, it has been openly foretold that not only the worshiper of an idol, but whoever among all men is a sinner, should not usurp to himself the right of baptism." These, certainly, which I have inserted, are your words. I did not except the worshiper of an idol, but I said that their gods themselves are dead, by whom to be baptized profits nothing: for they think themselves baptized by those gods in whose name they reckon they are cleansed: but you excepted no sinner. But if in this place a sinner and a mortal man who baptizes is to be understood, excepting no sinner, without doubt you have not excepted the hidden one either. "It has been openly foretold," you say, "that not only the worshiper of an idol, but whoever among all men is a sinner, should not usurp to himself the right of baptism." Hear, then, yourself: "Whoever is a sinner," you said; you excepted neither the manifest nor the hidden. Accordingly, with what face do they compel baptism after the manifest one, who are unwilling to do it after the hidden one, where they assert that no one is excepted? Flee, then, brother, from the insane understanding to the sound one, so that in this testimony about the oil of the sinner, as the preceding words of the same psalm prescribe, you may understand the smooth softness of a now deceitful flatterer. For thus both no one will be excepted, and the hidden bad baptizer will not run into insuperable straits. So too in the other testimony, where it was said, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^63] either inspect diligently the ancient codices, and especially the Greek, lest perhaps the very words written otherwise intimate, from the preceding and following context of speech, another sense: or certainly let us understand "dead men," as I said, those in whose name the worshipers of idols are baptized, so that you understand each one to be baptized by him in whose name he believes himself to be cleansed. For thus there too no one is excepted, since no dead god of the pagans can cleanse those believing in him. But if in this place you understand every sinful man to be a dead man, so many things which you do not wish follow upon you, that you cannot find how you yourself live—John saying, *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us*[^64]—so that you altogether cannot find a man by whom you may be baptized, if you wish to avoid every sinner.

**2. 28. 34.** Although, if you altogether understood "a dead man" to be nothing but a heretic or schismatic, so that whoever is baptized among them is baptized by a dead man, of whom it was said, *He who is baptized by a dead man, what does his washing profit him?*[^65]—which you see with how headlong a presumption it is so taken, as if it were said: He who is baptized by a heretic or schismatic—not even thus would it be against us, who confess that the baptism of Christ profits a man nothing, if he is baptized among heretics or schismatics, attributing baptism to those very ones by whom he is baptized; but that it begins to profit him then, when he passes over to the body of Christ, which is the Church of the living God. For then, under that same baptism which was Christ's outside too, but, placed outside, profited nothing, the cleansing will profit—not from him by whose hands he was dipped, but from him into whose members he was inserted.

**2. 28. 35.** Nor shall I dread that too severe sentence of yours, in which you said: "Whoever among all men is a sinner, let him not usurp to himself the right of baptism," except that you do not find anyone who truthfully says in the Lord's Prayer, *Forgive us our debts, as we too forgive our debtors*[^66], unless he confesses that he is a sinner. I would like to ask one by one those who baptize among you whether they are not sinners at all. For any of them can answer me: I am not a betrayer of the Scriptures, not an incense-offerer, not an adulterer, not a murderer, not a worshiper of idols, not, finally, a heretic, not a schismatic. But that I am not a sinner—I do not know whether anyone can be found, even of heretical pride, who would dare to say it, dare to think it: I do not know whether anyone is so blinded by so great a swelling of arrogance that he would not, I do not say profess with his voice, but even silently within himself reckon, that the prayer is not necessary to him in which we say to God, *Forgive us our debts.* For we do not pray for the forgiving of those debts which we trust were once forgiven in baptism; but surely for those without which human frailty does not exist, however much we watch over keeping the Lord's precepts. Finally, let whoever wishes take up the brazenness of this shamelessness, and say: I am not a sinner; since all things were forgiven me in baptism, no sin can be found in me. I rather believe John, and much more confidently answer: *You deceive yourself, and the truth is not in you*[^67]. Nor does this headlong and deceitful profession bring it about that sins are not found in you, but that those that are found are not forgiven. If, then, you are already baptized, I would like to know whom you found who would say against the apostle John: I have no sin. For if you could find any such man, how were you baptized by him who deceived himself, and in whom the truth was not? But if, not unmindful of some humility, he said that he was a sinner, how, according to your opinion, did he usurp to himself the right of baptism? For you said it, you were by no means afraid even to write it: "That whoever among all men is a sinner should not usurp to himself the right of baptism." But if you are not yet baptized, either correct this most vain sentence, or seek angels by whom you may be baptized.

**2. 28. 36.** But let us reckon that you, convicted, have corrected it: "Whoever among all men is a sinner by that crime which our people charge, let him not usurp to himself the right of baptism." Neither is this against us; because if even such a man usurped and gave it, I say that he ought not to have usurped it for himself, but I do not say that he did not give it. But he who received it, if a good man from a bad man, if a faithful man from a faithless one, if a pious man from an impious one—it will be pernicious to the giver, not to the receiver. For that holy thing judges the one using it badly, sanctifies the one using it well. But if he too who received it received it wrongly; not even so is the sacrament rescinded, but, recognized, what harmed the perverse man will profit the corrected one.

**2. 29. 37.** For thus I shall suppose you to have said, no longer: "Whoever is a sinner," because, unless I am mistaken, you now perceive how rashly it was said; but: "Whoever among all men is a sinner of such a kind as our people describe, let him not usurp to himself the right of baptism"; just as it is written, *But to the sinner God says: Why do you declare my justifications, and take up my testament through your mouth?*[^68] And to show to what sort of sinner he said this, lest all men should abstain from preaching his word, who dare neither to think nor to say that they are not sinners, there follows, and he describes him: *But you have hated discipline, and cast my words behind you. Your mouth abounded in malice, and your tongue embraced deceit. If you saw a thief, you ran with him; and with adulterers you placed your portion. Sitting, you detracted against your brother, and against your mother's son you placed a stumbling block*[^69]. To such a sinner, then, he says: *Why do you declare my justifications, and take up my testament through your mouth?* As though he said: In vain do you do this; as far as you are concerned, it does not profit you; this will avail you for the judgment of damnation, not for the merit of salvation. Yet if, even with such a sinner declaring the justifications of God and taking up his testament through his mouth, those who hear from his mouth believe, do, advance; will they not, when he is reprobate, be praised; when he is blamed, be justified; when he is condemned, be crowned; since they have taken care to obey the Lord saying, *What they say, do; but what they do, do not do: for they say, and do not do*[^70]? As, therefore, this sinner, if he usurps to himself the right of preaching the divine testament, profits himself nothing, but to those who hear and do, not he, but what he preaches, profits: so too he who ought not to have usurped to himself the right of baptism harms himself, who usurped a good thing badly, not him who received a good thing well.

**2. 30. 38.** You see that not only could you not refute what I said against Petilian, but with how great a light of truth what you yourself said against me is in every way refuted. And still you proceed and say: "That we make our cause not reputable, and in a certain way confess ourselves to be sinners, since, when it is objected to us by what license we claim for ourselves the right of baptism; we treat not of the merit of deeds, not of innocence of life, but say that it is lawful for anyone." Attend, certainly, from what has been said, that we do not say it is lawful for anyone; but that there is punishment for him who handles a holy thing unlawfully; and that he is to be corrected, not the holy thing which he handles unlawfully to be unlawfully rescinded: just as we wish men using the law not lawfully to be corrected, we do not hold the law itself void; just as we reprehend one taking up God's testament unlawfully through his mouth, we do not deny or rend the testament itself: nor do we therefore confess that we have sinned, because even in a sinner we disapprove what is his own, but honor what is God's: because we are unwilling that one believing in God should depend on hidden human things, but we admonish him to glory in the Lord, about whom he is certain. For neither did the Apostle distrust his own bad conscience, but he was unwilling that the hope of the believer be placed in man, and stably placed it in God, when he said, *Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters; but God who gives the increase*[^71]. Not, therefore, do we, saying what is written, *Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory*[^72], accuse our conscience; but you, wishing the hope of those who are baptized to be placed in the morals of men, do not acknowledge your own pride.

**2. 31. 39.** What of the fact that you even dare to make mention of blessed Cyprian, as though he were the author of your division—so great a defender of Catholic unity and peace? First be in the Church, which it is established that Cyprian held and preached; and then dare to name Cyprian as though the author of your opinion: first imitate the piety and humility of Cyprian, and then bring forward the council of Cyprian. For we do Cyprian no injury, when we distinguish whatever letters of his from the canonical authority of the divine Scriptures. For not without cause was the ecclesiastical canon established with such salutary vigilance, to which certain books of the Prophets and Apostles belong, which we may not at all dare to judge, and according to which we may freely judge of the other writings of the faithful or the unfaithful. Accordingly, since the Apostle—whose Epistles flourish in canonical authority—says, *As many of us, then, as are perfect, let us be of this mind; and if you are of any other mind, this too God will reveal to you*[^73]; as long as Cyprian was of another mind, if it is established that those are his writings which you think should be brought forward on your behalf; as long, then, as he was of another mind about this matter than the truth more diligently considered made plain, until God should reveal this too to him, he did not, with ineffable praise of love, leave the unity and Catholic peace even with his colleagues, from whom he was of another mind.

**2. 32. 40.** You inserted his words from the letter to Jubaianus into your letter, by which you might show that it pleased him that those who had been baptized in heresy or schism should be baptized in the Catholic Church. I am not bound by the authority of this letter; because I do not hold the letters of Cyprian as canonical, but I consider them by the canonical ones, and what in them agrees with the authority of the divine Scriptures I receive with praise of him; but what does not agree I reject with peace toward him. And through this, if those things which you mentioned, written by him to Jubaianus, you recited from some canonical book of the Apostles or Prophets, I would have nothing to contradict at all. But now, since what you recite is not canonical, by that liberty to which the Lord called us[^74], that man whose praise I am not able to attain, with whose many writings I do not compare my own, whose genius I love, with whose mouth I am delighted, whose love I marvel at, whose martyrdom I venerate—this, in which he was of another mind, I do not accept. I do not accept, I say, what blessed Cyprian thought about the baptizing of heretics and schismatics; because the Church does not accept this—the Church for which blessed Cyprian shed his blood. But because you say that he confirmed legal proofs for this opinion: although he could not confirm legal proofs, but rather himself confirmed by them whatever he rightly thought: leave aside, then, the writings of Cyprian, and mention those very legal proofs which you say he used. If I do not demonstrate that they help your cause in nothing, you have won. Wherefore I so do not accept this of Cyprian's, although incomparably inferior to Cyprian; just as that of the apostle Peter, that he compelled the Gentiles to Judaize[^75], I neither accept nor do, although incomparably inferior to Peter. But you, who oppose to us the writings of Cyprian as though they were supports of canonical authority, whatever we can bring forward of Cyprian against you, you must necessarily yield to, and it is just that, conquered, you be silent, and at some time convert yourselves from the error of a most pernicious dissension to Catholic unity.

**2. 33. 41.** Whence, not to be long, from this very letter to Jubaianus, attend to what I bring forward, which overturns and consumes your error. For when either holy Cyprian, or whoever wrote that letter, was trying to persuade that heretics coming to the Church ought to be baptized—because, namely, no baptism was to be reckoned which they had received outside among the heretics—he posed a question to himself, and said: "But someone will say: What then will become of those who in the past, coming from heresy to the Church, were admitted without baptism? The Lord can by his mercy grant indulgence, and not separate from the gifts of his Church those who, simply admitted to the Church, fell asleep in the Church." This simplicity suffices for us, to which Cyprian himself bears witness, understanding so great a good to be the unity of the body of Christ that he piously presumed, in pious reflection, that those simply admitted into it—even those whom he reckoned to be without baptism—merit indulgence of the divine mercy, and are not separated from the gifts of the Church. This was the custom of the Church before the council of Cyprian; this could not be overcome and taken away even by the council of Cyprian; that those coming from heresy, not indeed without baptism, as he says, because the same baptism was in them outside too, but did not profit, yet, simply, as he likewise says, being admitted, should merit indulgence of God's mercy, and not be separated from the gifts of the Church. This simplicity, rather than duplicity, pleased the universal Church spread through the whole world.

**2. 33. 42.** What testimony Cyprian himself bears to it, hear from the letter which he wrote on Unity. "Pluck away," he says, "a ray of the sun from the body, the unity of light does not admit division. Break a branch from a tree, the broken one will not be able to bud. Cut off a stream from the fountain, the cut-off one dries up." In these words of Cyprian we find and understand that light does not admit division except in the saints predestined to the kingdom of God, who can in no way be divided from the Church; and that the broken branch does not bud, we take of the bud of eternal salvation; but the dryness of the stream cut off from the fountain, we recognize in this—that those who are separated from unity are emptied of the Holy Spirit; not in the sacrament of baptism, which both good and bad can have, as openly outside as hiddenly within, when separated from the holiness of the Church. But—a thing no one doubts—what Cyprian thought of the fruitfulness of the Church itself spread through the whole world, attend to in what he adds: "So," he says, "the Church too, suffused with the Lord's light, stretches out its rays through the whole world; yet there is one light which is diffused everywhere, nor is the unity of the body separated. It extends its branches over the whole earth in the abundance of its fertility, it spreads more widely its richly flowing streams; yet there is one head, and one origin, and one mother, copious in the successions of fruitfulness." This Church Cyprian loved, held, commended—promised in the holy Scriptures, rendered in the whole world: which the lost schismatics or heretics, wishing as it were to discern and separate themselves from the bad, abandoned by impious seditions. And lest they should try to wash away their impious departures with vain excuses, holy Scripture foretold, *A bad son calls himself just, but does not wash away his going out*[^76]. Because not on account of the bad who seem to be within are the good to be deserted who truly are within.

**2. 34. 43.** Hence receive what blessed Cyprian thought, from the letter which he wrote to the presbyter Maximus and the others, whom he congratulates on returning to the Church from schismatic and heretical error. "For even if there seem," he says, "to be weeds in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we discern that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church. We have only to labor that we may be able to be wheat; that when the wheat begins to be stored in the Lord's barns, we may receive fruit for our work and labor. The Apostle in his Epistle says, *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and clay; and some indeed honored, some dishonored*[^77]. Let us give effort, and labor as much as we can, that we may be a vessel of gold and silver. But to break the clay vessels is granted to the Lord alone, to whom a rod of iron has been given[^78]. A servant cannot be greater than his lord[^79]; nor can anyone claim for himself what the Father attributed to the Son alone: so as to think that he can either bear the fork or the winnowing fan now for winnowing and cleansing the threshing floor, or separate all the weeds from the wheat by human judgment. This is proud obstinacy, and sacrilegious presumption, which a depraved fury takes up for itself: and while certain men always take up for themselves more than gentle justice demands, they perish from the Church; and while they insolently extol themselves, blinded by their own swelling, they lose the light of truth." You see, brother, that according to the divine Scriptures Cyprian commanded this, even on account of the bad—who, although they are spiritually separated from the good in life and morals, yet bodily seem to be mingled with them in the Church until the day of judgment, on which they will also be bodily separated to their due punishments—that the Church is not to be deserted, the wheat on account of the chaff or the weeds, the great house on account of the dishonored vessels. You see, you hear, you perceive, you grasp, you understand with how great a crime, on account of those who displease you whether rightly or when you are deceived, you separate yourselves from the Church which is spread through the whole world, to which, according to the divine Scriptures, Cyprian bears so great, so firm, so clear and luminous a testimony.

**2. 35. 44.** Wherefore attend diligently to my brief reasoning about this whole question. If those coming from the heretics are rightly received into the Church, so that their own error is corrected in them, but the divine sacrament is not rescinded; we congratulate those living well there as the Lord's wheat. But if, as you suppose—and you boast that Cyprian too favors you in this opinion—they do not have baptism; certainly, being simply admitted to the Church, according to the same Cyprian, on account of the merit of unity itself they merit the indulgence of God, and are not separated from the gifts of the Church. And whoever, according to the earlier custom about which Cyprian did not keep silent, simply admit them, and live rightly and peacefully, are counted among the same wheat destined for the barn. But those who, either knowingly fight contentiously against the truth in receiving them, or live by depraved and perverse morals, are tolerated among the weeds and the chaff due to the flames: yet on account of them the Church, which is spread through the whole world with copious successions—that is, the Lord's wheat, which grows together up to the harvest or is threshed together up to the winnowing—is not to be deserted, God commands, Cyprian is witness. And through this, if in the communion of the sacraments the bad stain the good, when in the times of Cyprian or before Cyprian heretics were received without baptism, as you suppose, say that the Church perished, and show whence you yourselves were born. But if, as the truth teaches even through Cyprian, when, for the peace of the Church, even known weeds are tolerated, they do not stain the wheat: *A bad son calls himself just, but does not wash away his going out*[^80]; because he ought not to have gone out from the Church on account of the bad.

**2. 36. 45.** Again I say—behold, I do not allow this most invincible reasoning to be dissembled: If those not communicating in the sins of the bad, on account of that communion of the sacraments alone, lose the good, when in the past those coming from heresy to the Church were admitted without baptism, they surely lost the good by their contagion. Then, therefore, there was already not a Church which Cyprian could hold, preach, nor whence Donatus afterward came out. But if that contagion did not lose the good, neither could the contagion of those whom you accuse lose the Christian world: do not, being separated, slander, and return corrected to the Church. Caecilian and his associates, against whom Secundus of Tigisis then drew together and founded a council, it is necessary for you to accuse, it is not necessary for me to defend. Accuse them with all the strength you can: if they were innocent, your windiness will harm them nothing, as wheat; if they were guilty, the wheat ought not to have been deserted on account of those weeds, whom they harmed nothing. Accuse as much as you can; I win if you do not prove it, I win if you prove it: I win, I say, if you do not prove it, with you yourself as judge; I win if you prove it, with Cyprian as witness. What do you want them to have been? If innocent, why do you slander the Lord's wheat, when you are weeds? If guilty, why are you separated from the Lord's wheat on account of the weeds? The Church stands clear and conspicuous to all; indeed, a city which cannot be hidden, set upon a mountain[^81], through which Christ has dominion from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth[^82], like the seed of Abraham multiplied as the stars of heaven and as the sand of the sea, in which all nations are blessed[^83]. This Church too blessed Cyprian so commends that he says it is suffused with the Lord's light, stretches its rays through the whole world, extends its branches over the whole earth in the abundance of its fertility. This Church either would not be accused in its wheat, or would not be deserted on account of the weeds: one of these answer yourselves even for yourselves, the other learn from Cyprian's admonitions. For these are his words, attesting and saying: "For even if there seem to be weeds in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we discern that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church."

**2. 37. 46.** You say that the Church perished from the whole world by the contagion of the bad Africans, and that in the party of Donatus its remnants have remained, as in wheat separated from the weeds and the chaff; thinking most openly against Cyprian, who says that neither do the good perish in the Church by the admixture of the bad, nor can the same bad be separated from the admixture of the good before the time of the divine judgment. You, then, according to your error, or rather fury, are compelled to accuse not only Caecilian and his ordainers, but even those Churches which we read alike in the apostolic and canonical Scriptures—not only of the Romans, whither you are accustomed to ordain a bishop for the few of you from Africa, but also of the Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Thessalonians, Colossians, Philippians, to whom the apostle Paul most openly writes; the Church of Jerusalem, which the first apostle James ruled by his episcopate; the Church of Antioch, where the disciples were first called Christians[^84]; the Churches of Smyrna, Thyatira, Sardis, Pergamum, Philadelphia, Laodicea, to which is the Apocalypse of the apostle John[^85]. So many other Churches of Pontus, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia, to which the apostle Peter writes[^86], and whatever else Paul testifies that he filled with the Gospel from Jerusalem all the way to Illyricum[^87]: to say nothing of other so broad and immense parts of the lands, into which, from these apostolic labors and plantings, the extended Church grew and grows. These Churches, certainly, which I have named from the divine and canonical writings, set so far from Africa, you are compelled to accuse as though they had perished from the sins of the Africans, rather than correct the error which drives you by a nefarious dissension to so great a crime.

**2. 37. 47.** But we, that we may more easily convict this error of yours, are not compelled to defend even the Africans themselves, whose false crimes you dare to pour out upon other nations also; not even them, I say, are we compelled to defend. For they have fellowship in the kingdom with those overseas Churches, if they were innocent: but if guilty, like weeds among wheat, they could not even in Africa harm those who were unwilling, on account of them even when known, to separate themselves from the unity of the Church. For, to pass over how many believed them innocent, to whom their crime, even if there was any, could nevertheless not be demonstrated—and surely not even you can say that these could be stained by the unknown sins of others: but to pass over these, I say—those very men who either knew or thought them guilty, although established in the African Churches, when they saw that they could not be convicted before the overseas Churches, nor their crimes demonstrated to the far and wide diffused members of the Church, if they wished, on account of those whom they knew to be bad, to separate themselves, as by fear of a pestiferous contagion, from the communion of so many nations, to whom they were unable to show them—Cyprian himself, whom you dared to name, not I, not you, not Donatus, not Caecilian, would hold them; and would say those words which he wrote to Maximus:

**2. 38. 48.** "If there seem to be weeds in the Church, yet neither our faith nor our love ought to be hindered, so that, because we discern that there are weeds in the Church, we ourselves should withdraw from the Church. We have only to labor that we may be able to be wheat; that when the wheat begins to be stored in the Lord's barns, we may receive fruit for our work and labor. The Apostle in his Epistle says, *But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and clay; and some indeed honored, some dishonored*[^88]. Let us give effort, and labor as much as we can, that we may be a vessel of gold and silver. But to break the clay vessels is granted to the Lord alone, to whom also a rod of iron has been given[^89]. A servant cannot be greater than his lord[^90], nor can anyone claim for himself what the Father attributed to the Son alone: so as to think that he can either bear the fork or the winnowing fan now for winnowing and cleansing the threshing floor, or separate all the weeds from the wheat by human judgment. This is proud obstinacy, and sacrilegious presumption, which a depraved fury takes up for itself: and while certain men always take up for themselves more than gentle justice demands, they perish from the Church; and while they insolently extol themselves, blinded by their own swelling, they lose the light of truth." By these words of Cyprian, those fearing God would be held in the Church who wished to separate themselves from it on account of known bad men; by which words you are condemned, who, being separated, accuse even the good. By these words Cyprian holds us too in the house of God, whose beauty he loved[^91]; even if—what you could never do—we could know betrayers accused and demonstrated by you, and any other bad men whatever; lest, on account of vessels made for dishonor, we desert that house. By these words may the Peacemaker bring you too, corrected, into Catholic peace, lest, offended by any sins of others, whether true or false, you cast such great evils against the Church of Christ fruiting and growing in the whole world through the Scriptures—lest on account of the weeds you accuse the wheat, lest on account of the chaff you desert the grain, lest on account of the dishonored vessels you remain outside the great house.

**2. 38. 49.** Behold how much blessed Cyprian, mentioned by you, has helped us—to whom, if he thought anything otherwise about the repeating of baptism, the Lord without doubt revealed that it should be corrected, on account of such great merits of his most flaming love: because he remained in that vine as a branch so opulent with the fruit of peace and love, that even if anything were found in him to be purged, if by no other thing, certainly by the pruning hook of martyrdom it would be purged. Although, therefore, for convicting your error, and, if you are willing, correcting it, these things could suffice more than enough: yet lest anyone reckon that you had set down anything in your letter which I was not able to refute, or in which I could not demonstrate that you answered nothing suitable to our writings against Petilian, let us see the rest in the following volume.
