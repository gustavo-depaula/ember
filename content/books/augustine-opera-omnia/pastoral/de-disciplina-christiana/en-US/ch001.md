# On Christian Discipline

**1. 1.** God's word has spoken to us and has been brought forth to encourage us, as Scripture says: *Receive discipline in the house of discipline* [^1]. Discipline takes its name from learning; the house of discipline is Christ's Church. What, then, is learned here, and why is it learned? Who learns, and from whom? We learn to live well. We learn to live well so that we may come to live forever. Christians learn; Christ teaches. First, then, what it means to live well; next, what the reward for a good life is; third, who true Christians are; and fourth, who the true teacher is: please listen as we say a few words about these things, as the Lord grants us. We are all in the house of discipline, but many do not want discipline, and, what is more perverse, they do not want discipline even in the house of discipline. They ought to receive discipline in the house of discipline so that they may also keep it in their own homes. Instead, they want not only to have disorder in their own homes but also to bring it with them into the house of discipline. Therefore, those for whom God's word is not fruitless, who join their hearts to their ears, who are not the path where birds pick up the seed that falls, who are not the rocky places where the seed cannot take deep root but sprouts for a moment and withers in the heat, who are not the thorny field where the seed, when it sprouts and begins to rise into the air, is choked by the thick thorns, but who are good soil, ready to receive the seed and yield a hundredfold, sixtyfold, or thirtyfold [^2]—you who enter the school of discipline with a purpose remember that I have recalled these comparisons from the Gospel—those, then, who are like this should receive what the Lord deigns to say through me. Since he is the sower, what am I? Barely the sower's basket. He deigns to put in me what he will scatter among you. So pay attention not to the basket's cheapness but to the seed's preciousness and the sower's power.

**2. 2.** What, then, does it mean to live well? What do we learn here? There are many commandments in the law that contain, command, and teach the good life itself. There are very many commandments, more than one can count. One can scarcely count the pages containing the commandments, much less the commandments themselves. Yet, for the sake of those who might excuse themselves because they have no time to read, or cannot read, or cannot readily understand, God willed that no one should have an excuse on the day of judgment. He willed, as it is written, to bring his word to completion and make it brief upon the earth, as the prophet had foretold of him: *For the Lord will make his word complete and brief upon the earth* [^3]. God also willed that this complete and brief word should not be obscure. It is brief so that there should be time to read it; it is clear so that no one may say, “I could not understand.” The divine Scriptures, then, are a great treasure containing many wonderful commandments, like many gems, precious necklaces, and large vessels of costly metal. But who can explore this treasure, use it, and reach everything it contains? When the Lord gave this comparison in his Gospel and said, *The kingdom of heaven is like a treasure found in a field* [^4], lest someone say that he was not capable of searching through the treasure, he immediately gave another comparison: *The kingdom of heaven is like* *a merchant seeking fine pearls, who found one precious pearl, sold everything he had, and bought it* [^5]. Thus, if you were too lazy to search through the treasure, do not be too lazy to carry one pearl under your tongue, and walk safely wherever you wish.

**3. 3.** What, then, is the word that brings everything to completion and makes it brief? *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind; and you shall love your neighbor as* *yourself. On these two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets* [^6]. This is what we learn in the house of discipline: to love God and to love our neighbor; God as God, our neighbor as ourselves. You cannot find God's equal, so that you could be told, “Love God as you love that person.” A rule has been found for you concerning your neighbor, because you yourself have been found to be your neighbor's equal. Do you ask how to love your neighbor? Look at yourself, and love your neighbor as you love yourself. There is no room for error. So I now want to entrust your neighbor to you on this basis, that you should love him as yourself. I want to, but I am still afraid. I want to tell you, “Love your neighbor as you love yourself,” and I am afraid, because I still want to examine how you love yourself. So do not take offense. You, to whom your neighbor is to be entrusted, must not be let off lightly; we must not deal with you in passing. You are one person; your neighbors are many. To begin with, you must not understand “neighbor” simply as a brother, a relative, or a relation by marriage. Every human being is every human being's neighbor. Father and son, father-in-law and son-in-law, are called close to one another. Nothing is so close as one human being to another. But if we think that only those born of the same parents are neighbors, let us look at Adam and Eve: we are all brothers. And if we are brothers as human beings, how much more as Christians? As a human being, you had one father, Adam, and one mother, Eve; as a Christian, you have one Father, God, and one mother, the Church.

**4. 4.** See, then, how many neighbors one person has. All the people he meets and can associate with are his neighbors. How carefully, then, must we examine whether he loves himself, when so many neighbors are to be entrusted to him to love as himself? Let no one be angry, then, if I examine how he loves himself. Certainly, I am conducting the examination; let him discover himself. Why am I examining him? Is it because I will discover him? I examine him so that he may question himself, become visible to himself, not remain unknown to himself, not hide himself from himself, and place himself before his own eyes, not behind his back. Let him do this while I speak; let him do it without my knowing. How do you love yourself? Whoever you are who hear me—or rather, who hear God through me—in this house of discipline, look at yourself and see how you love yourself. For surely, if I ask whether you love yourself, you answer that you do. Who hates himself? That is what you will say: “Who hates himself?” Then you do not love wickedness if you love yourself. For if you love wickedness, listen to the psalm, not to me: *Whoever loves wickedness hates his own soul* [^7]. So if you love wickedness, hear the truth: the truth that does not flatter you but tells you plainly, “You hate yourself.” The more you say that you love yourself, the more you hate yourself; for *whoever* *loves wickedness hates his own soul*. What shall I say of the flesh, the less valuable part of a human being? If he hates his soul, how does he love his flesh? In fact, those who love wickedness and hate their souls practice every kind of shameful behavior with their flesh. So you who love wickedness, how did you want your neighbor entrusted to you, to love him as yourself—you who destroy yourself? For if you love yourself in such a way as to destroy yourself, you will certainly also destroy the person you love as yourself. So I do not want you to love anyone: at least perish alone. Either correct your love or give up companionship.

**5. 5.** You will tell me, “I love my neighbor as myself.” I hear you, certainly, I hear you. You want to get drunk with the person you love as yourself. “Let us enjoy ourselves today; let us drink as much as we can.” See: this is how you love yourself, and you draw him to you and invite him to what you love. You inevitably draw the person you love as yourself toward what you yourself love. A human being—or rather, a beast, loving the sort of things beasts love! God made beasts with their faces bent down, seeking food from the earth; but he raised you from the earth onto two feet. He willed your face to look upward. Do not let your heart disagree with your face. Do not have your face above and your heart below. Rather, hear the truth and act on it. Lift up your heart; do not lie in the house of discipline. For when you hear this, respond, but let your response be true. Love yourself in this way, and you will love your neighbor as yourself. What does it mean to have your heart lifted up, except what was said first: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind* [^8]? Since there are two commandments, then, would it not have been enough if he had stated one? Even one is enough, if it is understood. For sometimes Scripture speaks this way, as does the Apostle Paul: *You shall not commit adultery; you shall not kill; you shall not covet. And any other commandment is summed up in this saying: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Love of neighbor does no evil. Charity, then, is the fulfillment of the law* [^9]. What is charity? Love. He seems to have said nothing about love of God but to have said that love of neighbor alone is enough to fulfill the law. Whatever other commandment there is, it is summed up in this saying, fulfilled in this saying. In what saying? *You shall love your neighbor as yourself* [^10]. Here is one commandment. Yet certainly there are two commandments on which all the Law and the Prophets hang.

**6. 5.** See how it has been shortened still further, and we are still lazy. See: what were two commandments have become one. Simply love your neighbor, and that is enough. But love him as you love yourself, not as you have hated yourself. Love your neighbor as yourself, but first you must love yourself.

**6. 6.** You must ask how to love yourself, and you must hear: *You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your mind, and with all your* *soul* [^11]. For just as a human being could not make himself, so he cannot make himself happy. Something other than the human being himself made him human; something other than the human being himself will make him happy. Indeed, even in his error he sees that he cannot be happy through himself, and he loves something else through which he may be happy. He loves whatever he thinks will make him happy. What, do we suppose, does he love, thinking it will make him happy? Money, gold, silver, property—in brief, wealth. For everything people possess on earth, everything they own, is called wealth. Whether it is a slave, a vessel, a field, a tree, or an animal, any of these is called wealth. And where did the word for wealth, “pecunia,” first come from? It is called “pecunia” because the ancients held all their possessions in livestock, “pecora.” “Pecunia” takes its name from livestock. We read that the patriarchs of old were wealthy shepherds. So you love wealth, O man; what you think will make you happy is wealth, and you love it very much. You wanted to love your neighbor as yourself: divide your wealth with him. That is what I was examining. You have been discovered; you have become visible to yourself; you have seen yourself and considered yourself. You are not prepared to divide your wealth with your neighbor. But what answer does benevolent greed give me? What does it answer? “If I divide it with him, there will be less for both me and him. What I love is diminished; neither he nor I will have the whole. But because I love him as myself, I wish him to have as much, so that mine is not diminished and he becomes my equal.”

**7. 7.** “I wish him well”—in a way that costs you nothing. If only you truly said this, or wished it! For I am afraid that you may be envious. How can your happiness be shared when another's happiness torments it? When your neighbor begins to grow rich and, as it were, to rise and follow you, do you not fear that he may catch up with you, fear that he may surpass you? Yet surely you love your neighbor as yourself! But I am not speaking of the envious. May God keep this plague far from everyone's soul, let alone Christians' souls: it is the devil's vice, the vice of which the devil alone is guilty, and guilty beyond pardon. For the devil is not told, when he is condemned, “You committed adultery; you stole; you seized another's estate,” but, “Having fallen, you envied the human being who stood.” Envy is the devil's vice, but it has a mother. Pride is the name of envy’s mother. Pride makes people envious. Choke the mother, and you will not belong to the daughter. That is why Christ taught humility. So I am not speaking to the envious; I am speaking to those who wish others well. I am speaking to those who wish their friends well, that they may have as much as they themselves have. They wish the needy well, that they too may have as much as themselves, but they do not want to give them anything from what they have. Is this what you boast about, Christian, that you wish others well? Better than you is the beggar who wishes you still more and has nothing. You want to wish well to someone who receives nothing from you: give something to someone who wishes you well. If wishing well is good, give it a reward. The poor man wishes you well: why are you hesitant? I will add something: you are in the house of discipline. I will add something to what I said, “Give to the one who wishes you well”: he is Christ. The very one who gave to you asks something of you. Be ashamed. Rich as he was, he chose to be poor so that you would have a poor person to give to. Give something to your brother, something to your neighbor, something to your fellow traveler. You are rich; he is poor. This life is a road; you are walking together.

**8. 8.** But perhaps you say, “I am rich; he is poor.” You are walking together, are you not? What does your saying, “I am rich; he is poor,” mean except, “I am burdened; he travels light”? “I am rich; he is poor.” You are describing your baggage, praising your load. And what is worse, you have bound your baggage to yourself; that is why you cannot stretch out your hand. Burdened and bound, what are you boasting of? What are you praising yourself for? Undo your bonds; lessen your baggage. Give to your fellow traveler: you both help him and relieve yourself. Amid these words of yours praising your baggage, Christ still asks and receives nothing. And you dishonor the name of family devotion with cruel words, saying, “Then what am I to save for my children?” I put Christ before him; he puts his children back before me. What splendid justice indeed: that your son should have the means to live luxuriously while your Lord goes without! *For when you did it to one of the least of mine, you did it to me*. Have you not read this, not paid attention? *When you did not do it to one of the least of mine, you did not do it to me* [^12]. Have you not read this, not been afraid? See who is in need, and you count your children? Well, count your children, and add him as one among them: your Lord. You have one: let him be the second. You have two: let him be the third. You have three: let him be the fourth. You want none of this. See how you love your neighbor: you would make him your companion in this destruction.

**8. 9.** What shall I say to you? Do you love your neighbor? What will you whisper in his ears, greedy man, except, “My son,” or “My brother,” or “My father, it is good for us to enjoy ourselves while we live here. What you have is what you are worth [^13]. Break the moon if you must, but make your fortune”? These are the things you will whisper to your neighbor, things you did not learn in the house of discipline; you did not hear this here.

**9. 9.** I do not want you to love your neighbor in this way. If only I could keep you from associating with anyone! For *bad conversations* *corrupt good morals* [^14]. But I cannot, I cannot prevent you from associating with someone into whose ear you may whisper these evils that you refuse to unlearn; not only do you refuse to be taught to give them up, but you even strive to teach them. I do not want to—rather, I do want to, but I cannot—cut you off from others' ears. Let me warn those others whose ears you seek, whose ears you try to penetrate, whose hearts you plan to enter through their ears. You who receive sound teaching in the house of discipline: *Hedge your ears with thorns* [^15]. *Bad conversations corrupt good morals. Hedge your ears with thorns*. Hedge them, and hedge them with thorns, so that whoever dares to intrude uninvited may not only be driven back but also pricked. Drive him away from you; say, “You are a Christian; I am a Christian. This is not what we received in the house of discipline, not what we learned in that school we entered without payment, not what we learned under that Teacher whose chair is in heaven. Do not say these things to me, or do not come near me.” This is what it means to *hedge* *your ears with thorns*.

**9. 10.** Let me turn back to that man. You are greedy; you love money. Do you want to be happy? Love your God. Money does not make you happy: you make it ornate; it does not make you happy. But since you love money so much, and I see that you go where desire commands, go, lazy man, where love commands. Look and see how great the difference is between your money and your God. This sun is more beautiful than your money, and yet this sun is not your God. Moreover, if this light is more beautiful than your money, how much more beautiful is the one who made this light? Or perhaps you want to compare your money with the light? Look: the sun has set; show me your money. It glitters, and at night I take away the lamp. Surely you are rich: show me your riches. Once you are deprived of light, once you have nothing by which to see what you have, where are your riches?

**10. 10.** Yet the dreadful depth of greed is not open to the eyes, while it wells up in souls. We have seen greedy people who are blind too. Tell me why blind people, who cannot see, are greedy. What he has, he does not even have; yet the blind man is greedy. Why? He is greedy because he believes he has it. Faith makes him rich: he is rich by believing, not by seeing. How much better to turn his faith toward God! You do not see what you possess, and that is how I preach God to you. You do not yet see him: love, and you will see. Blind man, you love money that you will never see. Blind, you possess it; blind, you will die, leaving here what you possess. You did not grasp it even while you lived, because you did not see what you had.

**10. 11.** What are you told about God? See, Wisdom herself says this: love her *like money* [^16]. It is unworthy and insulting to compare Wisdom with money, but it is love that is being compared with love. For I see that here you love money so much that, at its command, you undertake hardships, endure fasting, cross the sea, and entrust yourselves to winds and waves. I can choose something else for you to love, but I have nothing to add to the love with which you love. “Love me like that; I do not ask to be loved more,” says God. I am speaking to the wicked; I am speaking to the greedy. “You love money: love me just as much. Certainly I am incomparably better, but I do not ask greater love of you. Love me as much as you love money.” Let us at least be ashamed, confess, and beat our breasts—but not to pack a pavement down over our sins. For someone who beats his breast and does not amend his ways makes his sins firm; he does not remove them. Let us beat our breasts, punish ourselves, and correct ourselves, so that he who is the teacher may not punish us later. We have said what is learned here; now, why it is learned.

**11. 12.** Why did you go to school and receive beatings? Why were you led there by your parents, sought out when you ran away, dragged back when you were found, and stretched out for a beating when you were brought in? Why were you beaten? Why did you endure so many hardships in childhood? To learn. To learn what? Letters. Why? To possess money, or to obtain honor and hold an exalted position. See: mortal as you are, for the sake of something perishable, you learned something perishable at the cost of such effort and such punishment. And the person who dragged you to punishment loved you: the very person who loved you dragged you to punishment. He had you beaten out of love, so that you would learn—learn what? Letters. Are letters a good thing? I know what you will tell me: “Why, did you bishops not study letters too? Do you not use that learning to discuss the divine Scriptures?” Certainly, but that is not why we learned letters. For when our parents sent us to school, they did not tell us, “Learn letters so that you will be able to read the Lord's books.” Even Christian parents do not say this to their children. “Learn letters.” Why? “So that you may be a man.” What, then? Am I a beast now? “What do I mean by ‘so that you may be a man’? So that you may stand out among men.” Hence that proverb too: “What you have is what you are worth” [^17]. So that you may have as much as others, or as much as only a few; or more than others, or more than the few, and derive honor and rank from it. And where will all these things be when death comes? How this fear stings, how it interrupts us! How my mere mention of that name has struck everyone's heart! How your groans have borne witness to your fear! I heard, I heard: you groaned; you fear death. If you fear it, why do you not take precautions? You fear death: what do you fear? It will come. Whether I fear it or not, it must come; sooner or later it will come. Your fear will not prevent what you fear from happening.

**12. 13.** Fear instead what will not happen if you refuse it. What is that? Sinning. Fear sinning, because if you love sins, you will rush into another death, which you could avoid if you did not love sins. But now, in your perversity, you love death more than life. “Certainly not!” you say. “What person loves death more than life?” Perhaps I can prove that you love death more than life. Here is how I prove it. You love your tunic and want it to be good; you love your estate and want it to be good; you love your son and want him to be good; you love your friend and want him to be good; you love your house and want it to be good. Why is it that you also want your death to be good? For every day, since death is going to come, you pray, saying, “May God give me a good death; may God keep a bad death far from me.” So you love your death more than your life. You fear dying badly; you do not fear living badly. Correct your bad life; fear a bad death. But do not be afraid: someone who has lived well cannot die badly. I firmly affirm it; I dare to say it: *I believed, and therefore I spoke* [^18]. Someone who has lived well cannot die badly. Now you say to yourself, “Have not many righteous people perished in shipwrecks?” Certainly, someone who has lived well cannot die badly. “Has not an enemy's sword killed many righteous people?” Certainly, someone who has lived well cannot die badly. “Have not robbers killed many righteous people? Have not wild beasts torn many righteous people apart?” Certainly, someone who has lived well cannot die badly. And I answer: do you think these things are a bad death? Do you think perishing in a shipwreck, being struck by a sword, or being torn apart by beasts is a bad death? Did not the martyrs whose birthdays we celebrate undergo these deaths? What kind of death did they not undergo? Yet if we are Christians, if we remember that we are in the house of discipline, whether we are here or listening here, if we do not forget when we leave but remember what we hear here, do we not call the martyrs blessed? Examine the martyrs' deaths. Ask the eyes of flesh: they died badly. Ask the eyes of faith: *Precious in the Lord's sight is the death of his saints* [^19]. Whatever, then, it is that you dread in death, do not dread it at all if you imitate them. Strive to have a good life. Whatever the occasion of your departure from this body, you depart for rest, you depart for happiness that has neither fear nor end. For the rich man's death in purple and fine linen was a good death—but the death of one thirsting in torment and longing for a drop of water was a bad death. The death of the poor man lying before the rich man's door, among the dogs' tongues, hungry and thirsty, longing for crumbs from the table, seemed a bad death: a bad death, a death to shrink from. Look at the outcome. You are a Christian: direct the eye of faith toward it. *That poor man died and was carried* *by angels into Abraham's bosom* [^20]. What use was a marble tomb to the rich man thirsting in the underworld? What harm did his rags and the pus of his sores do to the poor man resting in Abraham's bosom? From far away the rich man saw at rest the one he had despised as he lay there. Now choose a death: tell me who died well and who badly. I think the poor man died better than the rich man. Or do you want to be buried with spices and thirst in the underworld? You answer, “Far be it from me!” I think that is what you will choose. You will learn, then, to die well if you learn to live well. For the reward of a good life is eternal.

**13. 14.** Those who learn are Christians. As for those who hear and do not learn, what is that to the sower? The path does not frighten the sower's hand; neither stones nor thorns frighten it. He scatters what is his. Someone who fears that it may fall on bad soil will never reach the good soil. We too speak, cast seed, and scatter seed. Some despise it; some criticize it; some laugh at it. If we fear them, we shall have nothing to sow, and at harvest we shall go hungry. So the seed reaches the good soil. I know that whoever hears, and hears well, diminishes and grows: he diminishes in wickedness and grows in truth; he diminishes for the world and grows for God.

**14. 15.** Who, then, is the teacher who teaches? Not just any man, but the Apostle. Certainly the Apostle—and yet not the Apostle. *Do you wish*, he says, *to receive proof of Christ who speaks in me?* [^21] Christ is the one who teaches; as I said a little earlier, his chair is in heaven. His school is on earth, and his school is his body. The head teaches its members; the tongue speaks to its feet. Christ is the one who teaches: we hear him; let us fear and act. And do not despise Christ himself because for your sake he was born in the flesh, wrapped in the rags of mortality; for your sake he hungered and thirsted; for your sake he sat weary at the well; for your sake he slept exhausted in the boat; for your sake he heard insults he did not deserve; for your sake he did not turn men's spit away from his face; for your sake he received slaps in the face; for your sake he hung on the wood; for your sake he poured out his life; for your sake he was laid in the tomb. Do you perhaps despise all this in Christ? Do you want to know who he is? Recall the Gospel you heard: *I and the Father are one* [^22].

**14. 16.** Turning to the Lord, let us entreat him for ourselves and for all his people standing with us in the courts of his house, that he may deign to guard and protect them, through Jesus Christ his Son, our Lord, who lives and reigns with him forever and ever. Amen.
