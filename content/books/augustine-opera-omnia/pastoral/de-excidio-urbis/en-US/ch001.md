# On the Sack of the City of Rome

**1. 1.** Let us consider the first reading, from the holy prophet Daniel, in which we heard him praying and were amazed that he confessed not only the people’s sins but also his own. For after that prayer—his words showed him to be not only a suppliant but also one confessing—after that prayer, then, he says, *while I was praying*, *and confessing my sins and the sins of my people to the Lord my God*[^1]. Who, then, professes himself without sin, when Daniel confesses his own sins? For through Ezekiel it was said to a certain proud man, *are you wiser than Daniel*?[^2] Again, among three holy men through whom God signifies three kinds of people whom he will deliver when great tribulation comes upon the human race, he included this Daniel, saying that no one would be delivered from it except Noah, Daniel, and Job[^3]. It is clear that by these three names God signifies, as I said, three kinds of people. For those three men have already fallen asleep, their spirits are with God, and their bodies have decayed in the earth. They have been placed at God’s right hand and fear no tribulation in this world from which they might desire deliverance. How, then, will Noah, Daniel, and Job be delivered from that tribulation? When Ezekiel said this, perhaps Daniel alone was still in the body. Noah and Job had long since fallen asleep and had been joined to their fathers in the sleep of death. How, then, could they be delivered from an approaching tribulation, having long since been delivered from the flesh? But Noah signifies good superiors who rule and govern the Church, as Noah steered the ark in the flood. Daniel signifies all holy people who practice continence; Job signifies all who are married and live well. For God delivers these three kinds of people from that tribulation. How highly Daniel is commended is evident from his deserving to be named as one of those three; yet he confesses his sins. With Daniel confessing his sins, whose pride should not tremble, whose arrogance should not subside, whose swelling self-exaltation should not be restrained? *Who will boast that he has a chaste heart, or who will boast that he is clean from sin*?[^4]

**2. 1.** And people are astonished—and would that they were merely astonished and did not also blaspheme—when God corrects the human race and stirs it with the lashes of loving chastisement, exercising discipline before judgment, and often making no distinction in whom he chastises because he does not wish to find anyone to condemn. For he chastises the just and the unjust alike[^5]; although who is just, if Daniel confesses his own sins?

**2. 2.** A few days ago a reading was read from the book of *Genesis*[^6] which, unless I am mistaken, made us very attentive. Abraham asks the Lord whether, if he finds fifty just people in the city, he will spare the city for their sake or destroy the city together with them. The Lord answered that if he found fifty just people in the city, he would spare it. Then Abraham continued questioning and asked whether, if there were five fewer and forty-five remained, he would likewise spare it. God answered that he would spare it for the sake of forty-five. Why say more? Gradually questioning and reducing the number, he came down to ten and asked the Lord whether, if he found ten just people in the city, he would destroy them with the countless others who were evil, or would rather spare the city for the sake of the ten just people. God answered that even for ten just people he would not destroy the city. What, then, shall we say, brothers? For a pressing and powerful question confronts us, especially from people who attack our Scriptures impiously rather than seek them devoutly. Referring especially to the recent sack of so great a city, they say: Were there not fifty just people in Rome? Among so many believers, so many consecrated women and people practicing continence, so many servants and handmaids of God, could fifty just people not be found, or forty, or thirty, or twenty, or ten? But if this is incredible, why did God not spare that city for the sake of fifty or even those ten? Scripture does not deceive, if a person does not deceive himself. When God’s justice is questioned and God answers concerning justice, he seeks the just according to the divine standard, not the human. So I answer promptly: Either he found that many just people there and spared the city, or, if he did not spare it, he did not find the just. But I am told that it is evident that God did not spare the city. I answer: On the contrary, that is not evident to me. For the city was not destroyed as Sodom was[^7]. It was Sodom that Abraham was asking God about. God said, *I will not destroy the city*; he did not say, I will not chastise the city. He did not spare Sodom; he destroyed it. He consumed Sodom entirely by fire, not postponing its punishment until judgment but executing in it what he reserved for other evil people at judgment. Absolutely no one remained in Sodom. No livestock, no human being, no house was left; fire swallowed everything altogether. That is how God destroyed a city. But how many left the city of Rome and will return! How many remained and escaped! How many could not even be touched in the holy places! But, they say, many were led away captive. So was Daniel, not for his punishment but for the consolation of others. But, they say, many were killed. So were many just prophets, *from the blood of righteous Abel* *to the blood of Zechariah*[^8]; so were many apostles; so was Jesus himself, the Lord of prophets and apostles. But, they say, many were tortured with various torments. Do we suppose that anyone suffered as much as Job?

**2. 3.** Horrible things have been reported to us: slaughter, fires, plunder, killings, and the torture of human beings. It is true: we have heard much, groaned over it all, often wept, and scarcely found consolation. I do not deny or dispute that we have heard many things, that many things have been done in that city.

**3. 3.** Nevertheless, my brothers, attend, beloved, to what I say. When we heard the book of holy Job, we heard that, after losing his possessions and losing his children, he could not even keep unharmed his own flesh, which alone remained to him. Struck with a grievous sore from head to foot, he sat on a dung heap, rotting with ulcers, running with pus, crawling with worms, racked by the bitterest agonies of pain. If we were told that an entire city was suffering this, with no one there healthy, everyone grievously afflicted and rotting in this way, living people putrefying with worms as the dead do, which would be worse: this, or that war? I think the sword would rage more mildly against human flesh than worms would; blood bursting from wounds would be more bearable than pus dripping from decay. You look at a decomposing corpse and shudder. But its suffering is less—indeed, there is no suffering—because the soul is absent. In Job, however, the soul was present to feel, bound so that it could not flee, subjected to pain, goaded to blaspheme. Yet Job endured tribulation, and it was reckoned to him as great righteousness. Let no one, then, consider what he suffers, but what he does. Human being, what you suffer is not in your power; in what you do, your will is either guilty or innocent. Job was suffering; his wife stood by, the only one left to him, not for consolation but for temptation, not to bring medicine but to dictate blasphemy: *Say something* *against God, and die* [^9]. See how death would have been a benefit to him, yet no one granted him that benefit. But amid all that this holy soul endured, patience was exercised, faith tested, his wife put to shame, and the devil defeated. A great spectacle: amid that foul decay, the radiant beauty of virtue! The enemy lays waste in secret; the woman who has become an enemy openly urges evil, helping the devil, not her husband. She is a new Eve, but he is not the old Adam. *Say*, she says, *something against God, and die*. Extort by blaspheming what you cannot obtain by praying. *You have spoken*, he says, *like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the Lord’s hand, why should we not endure evil things?* [^10] Attend to the words of this steadfast believer; attend to the words of a man rotting outwardly but sound within: *You have spoken* *like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the Lord’s hand, why should we not endure evil things?* He is a father. Is he to be loved when he caresses us and rejected when he corrects us? Is he not the same father when he promises life and when he imposes discipline? Have you forgotten: *My son, when you approach the service of God, stand in righteousness and fear, and prepare your soul for temptation. Accept whatever is brought upon you; endure in sorrow and be patient in your humiliation, because gold and silver are tested in fire, but acceptable people in the furnace of humiliation* [^11]? Have you forgotten: *For the Lord corrects the one he loves, and scourges every son he receives* [^12]?

**4. 4.** Think of any torments you please; stretch your mind to every kind of human punishment. Compare it with Gehenna, and everything you suffer is light. Here both the tormentor and the tormented are temporary; there both are everlasting. Are those who suffered when Rome was laid waste still suffering? But that rich man [^13] is still suffering in the underworld. He burned, he burns, he will burn; he will come to judgment and receive his flesh again, not for benefit but for punishment. Let us fear those punishments, if we fear God. Whatever a person suffers here is correction if he is reformed; if even this does not reform him, it is a double condemnation. For here he will pay temporary penalties, and there he will experience everlasting ones. I tell you, beloved brothers: we certainly praise, glorify, and admire the holy martyrs; we celebrate their feast days with devout solemnity, honor their merits, and imitate them if we can. The martyrs’ glory is surely great; yet I do not know whether holy Job’s glory was any less. He was not told, however: Put incense before idols, sacrifice to foreign gods, or deny Christ. Yet he was told: Blaspheme God. Nor was this said with the meaning: If you blaspheme, all the decay will leave you and health will return. Rather, his foolish, senseless wife said: If you blaspheme, you will die, and by dying you will escape your torments. As though everlasting pain did not follow the death of a blasphemer! The foolish woman recoiled from the distress of his present decay and gave no thought at all to the everlasting flame. He bore present punishments to avoid falling into future ones. He restrained his heart from evil thoughts and his tongue from cursing; he preserved his soul’s integrity amid his body’s decay. He saw what he would escape in the future and therefore bore what he was suffering. So, whenever any Christian suffers some bodily affliction, let him think of Gehenna, and he sees how light his suffering is. Let him not murmur against God or say: God, what have I done to you? Why am I suffering this? Rather, let him say what Job himself said, holy though he was: *You have searched out all my sins and sealed them as though in a bag* [^14]. He who suffered not to be punished but to be tested did not dare to say that he was without sin. Let everyone say this when he suffers.

**5. 5.** There were fifty righteous people in Rome; indeed, if you consider the human measure, there were thousands of righteous people. If you demand the standard of perfection, let no righteous person be found in Rome. Whoever dares to call himself righteous will hear from the Truth: *Are you wiser than Daniel?* [^15] Listen, then, to Daniel confessing his sins [^16]. Or was he perhaps lying when he confessed? Then he had a sin for that very reason: he was lying to God about his sins. Yet people sometimes argue and say: Even a righteous person ought to say to God, I am a sinner; although he knows he has no sin, he should still say to God, I have sin. I wonder whether this should be called sound advice. Who made you free from sin? If you have no sin at all, was it not God who healed your soul—if, indeed, you have no sin? For examine yourself, and you will find sins rather than merely a sin. Yet if you have absolutely no sin, is that not the gift of him to whom you said: *I said: Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against you* [^17]? If, then, your soul is without sin, your soul is entirely healed. If your soul is entirely healed, why are you ungrateful to the physician, saying that a wound still remains where he has already made everything sound? If you showed a physician your sick or wounded body and asked him to give it careful treatment, and he made you healthy and restored you unharmed, yet you still said you were not healthy, would you not be ungrateful and insulting to the physician? In the same way, God has healed you, and do you still dare to say: I have a wound? Do you not fear that he may answer: Then I have accomplished nothing, or all my work has been wasted. Am I to receive no payment? Do I deserve no praise? May God keep this madness and this empty argument away from us. Let a person say, I am a sinner, because he is a sinner; let him say, I have sin, because he has sin. For even if he has none, he is not wiser than Daniel. [The negative follows the supplied source.] So, my brothers, let me at last bring that question to an end. If people are to be called righteous in the qualified human sense in which they are now called righteous, according to a certain manner of life by which they live blamelessly among others, there were many such people in Rome. On their account God spared it: many escaped. But God also spared those who died. For those who died after a good life, in true righteousness and faith—did they not escape the misfortunes of human suffering and reach divine refreshment? They died after their tribulations, like that poor man before the rich man’s gate. But did they suffer hunger? So did he. Did they suffer wounds? So did he; perhaps dogs licked them less. Did they die? So did he. But listen to the outcome: *It happened that the poor man died and was carried* *by angels into Abraham’s bosom* [^18].

**6. 6.** If only we could see the souls of the saints who died in that war! Then you would see how God spared the city. For thousands of saints are at rest, rejoicing and saying to God: We thank you, Lord, because you have rescued us from the troubles of the flesh and its grievous torments. We thank you because we no longer fear either barbarians or the devil. We do not fear famine on earth; we do not fear an enemy, a persecutor, or an oppressor. We have died on earth, but with you, God, we shall not die, through your gift, not our merit. What a city of the humble it is that says these things! Or do you think, brothers, that a city is to be reckoned by its walls rather than by its citizens? After all, if God had said to the people of Sodom: Flee, because I am going to burn this place, would we not perhaps say that they had deserved great favor if they had fled and the flame descending from heaven had destroyed only the buildings and walls? Would God not have spared the city, since the city had moved away and escaped the destruction of that fire?

**6. 7.** Was it not a few years ago, under the emperor Arcadius, at Constantinople—perhaps some listening to me know this, and there are people in this congregation who were present there—that God, wishing to terrify the city and by terrifying it to reform, convert, cleanse, and change it, came in a revelation to a faithful servant of his, a soldier, so it is said? He told him that this city would perish by fire coming from heaven and instructed him to tell the bishop. The message was delivered; the bishop did not dismiss it but addressed the people. The city turned to the mourning of repentance, as ancient Nineveh once had [^19]. Yet, lest people think that the man who had spoken was either deceived by something false or had deceitfully misled others, the day threatened by God arrived. While everyone watched intently and awaited the outcome in great fear, at nightfall, when the world was already growing dark, a fiery cloud appeared in the east. It was small at first, then gradually grew as it approached over the city, until a vast terror hung threateningly over the whole city. A dreadful flame seemed to hang from heaven, and the smell of sulfur was also present. Everyone fled to the church; the place could not hold the crowd. Each person urgently sought baptism from anyone able to give it. Not only in the church but also in houses, lanes, and squares, people demanded the salvation of the sacrament, to escape the wrath—not, of course, the present wrath, but the wrath to come. Yet after that great tribulation, once God had attested his servant’s trustworthiness and his servant’s revelation, the cloud began to diminish just as it had grown, and little by little it vanished. The people, now somewhat reassured, heard again that they absolutely must move away, because the city would perish the following Saturday. The entire city departed with the emperor; no one remained at home, and no one shut his house. Withdrawing far from the walls and looking back at their beloved homes, they bade a pitiable farewell to the cherished dwellings they had left behind. That vast multitude advanced several miles, but gathered in one place to pour out prayers to the Lord. Suddenly they saw a great cloud of smoke and raised a loud cry to the Lord. At last, seeing that all was calm, they sent people to bring back news. Once the anxious hour that had been foretold had passed, and the messengers reported that all the walls and houses stood unharmed, everyone returned with immense rejoicing. No one had lost anything from his house; everyone found it open just as he had left it.

**7. 8.** What shall we say? Was this God’s wrath, or rather his mercy? Who could doubt that the most merciful Father wished to correct them by terrifying them, rather than punish them, when that immense, imminent calamity harmed no person, no house, and no wall? It happened to that city exactly as when a hand is raised to strike and then, once the person about to be struck is terrified, withdrawn in pity. Yet if, when it was abandoned and all the people had departed, devastation had fallen upon the place and destroyed the whole city like Sodom, leaving not even ruins, who could doubt even then that God had spared that city? Forewarned and terrified, the city would have departed and moved away while the place itself was consumed. In the same way, we must have no doubt that God also spared the city of Rome, a large part of which, in its many inhabitants, had moved away before the enemy’s fire. Those who fled had moved away; those who left the body sooner had moved away. Many who remained managed to hide; many were preserved alive and unharmed in the holy places. That city, then, was corrected by the reforming hand of God rather than destroyed, like the servant who knows his master’s will and does things deserving blows: he will be beaten with many lashes [^20].

**8. 9.** May this serve as an example that inspires fear! When the Lord shows how unstable and fleeting are all the world’s vanities and lying follies, may evil desire, thirsting for the world and eager to enjoy its most ruinous pleasures, be restrained, instead of our murmuring against the Lord under the scourges we so richly deserve. Yet the threshing floor feels one threshing sledge, so that the straw may be broken up and the grain cleansed; the goldsmith’s furnace undergoes one fire, so that the chaff may turn to ash and the gold be freed from impurities. So too Rome endured one tribulation, in which the devout person was either delivered or corrected, while the ungodly person was condemned—condemned, I say, whether he was snatched from this life to a place where he would instead pay the most just penalties, or remained here to blaspheme still more damnably; unless, indeed, God in his ineffable mercy preserved for repentance those whom he knows are to be saved. Let us not, then, be shaken by the suffering of the devout: it is their training. Or do we perhaps shudder when we see a righteous person endure undeserved and grievous things on this earth, and forget what was endured by the righteous one of the righteous and the holy one of the holy? What that entire city suffered, one man suffered. But see who that one man was: *King of kings and Lord of lords* [^21], arrested, bound, scourged, subjected to every insult, hung on a tree and crucified, killed. Weigh Rome against Christ; weigh the whole earth against Christ; weigh heaven and earth against Christ. Nothing created can be weighed against the Creator; no work can be compared with its maker. *All things were made through him, and without him nothing was made* [^22]; and yet he was handed over by his persecutors. Let us therefore bear whatever God wills us to bear. He who sent his Son to treat and heal us also knows, like a physician, what pain is beneficial. Surely it is written: *Let patience have its perfect work* [^23]. But what will patience’s work be if we suffer nothing adverse? Why, then, do we refuse to endure temporal evils? Are we perhaps afraid of being perfected? Yet let us indeed pray and groan to the Lord that what the Apostle says may be fulfilled for us: *God is faithful: may he not allow you to be tempted beyond what you can bear, but with the temptation also provide a way out, so that you can endure* [^24].
