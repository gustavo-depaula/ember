# Book One: Is it permitted for a spouse to depart from a spouse?

## First question to Pollentius: whether it is permitted to depart from a spouse and for what cause; whether, if a spouse has departed, a new marriage is permitted or is not permitted (1, 1–12, 13)

### The words of the Apostle concerning those who are in marriage.

**1. 1.** The first question, most beloved brother Pollentius, of those which you took up in writing to me, as though by way of consultation, concerns what the Apostle says: *To those who are in marriage I give charge, not I but the Lord, that the wife should not depart from her husband; but if she does depart, let her remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband; and the husband should not divorce his wife*[^1],

whether it is to be taken in such a way that he is understood to have forbidden marriage to the woman who has departed from her husband without the cause of fornication—for this is your view—or, as I held in those books which I wrote many years ago on the Gospel sermon that the Savior delivered on the mountain according to Matthew, that he commanded those women to remain unmarried who had withdrawn from their husbands for that one cause which alone is permitted, namely fornication. For it seems to you that a woman departing from her husband at that point ought not to marry, if she departed without being compelled by any fornication of her husband. Yet you do not consider that, if her husband had no cause of fornication, she ought not to depart and remain unmarried, but ought not to depart at all. For surely she to whom it is commanded that, if she has departed from her husband, she remain unmarried, is not deprived of the license to depart, but of the license to marry.

But if this is so, then license is granted to women who wish to be continent to await no consent of their husbands, so that what was said—*that the wife should not depart from her husband*[^2]—would seem to have been a command given to those who could choose not continence but such a divorce as would allow them to enter into marriages with others. And so women who have come to desire no intercourse and to bear no marriage will be permitted to leave their husbands even without any cause of fornication and to remain unmarried according to the Apostle. And husbands likewise (since the rule is the same for both), if they wish to be continent, will desert their wives even without their consent and will remain without any marriage. For then, as you suppose, it would be permitted them to seek other marriages, if the divorce arose from a cause of fornication. But when this cause is absent, it remains, according to what you think, either that a spouse not depart from a spouse, or, if a spouse has departed, that she remain without marriage, or that she return to her former marriage.

Therefore, when no cause of fornication exists, any spouse will be permitted to choose one of three things: either not to depart from the spouse, or, if she has departed, to remain so, or, if she has not remained so, not to seek another but to render herself back to the first.

### What Pollentius supposes is refuted.

**2. 2.** And where, then, is it that the same Apostle was unwilling that spouses defraud one another of the carnal debt, even for a time so as to be free for prayer, except by mutual consent?

How will what he says be sound: *But because of fornications, let each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband. Let the husband render to the wife her due, and likewise also the wife to the husband. The wife does not have power over her own body, but the husband; likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^3]?

How will this be true, except that, when the spouse is unwilling, it is not permitted to the spouse to practice continence? For if it is permitted to a woman to dismiss her husband in such a way that she remains unmarried, then it is not the husband who has power over her body, but she herself; and the same is understood concerning the husband. Then, when it was said, *Whoever dismisses his wife, except for the cause of fornication, makes her commit adultery*[^4], how are we to understand what was said, except that it is forbidden for a man to dismiss his wife if no cause of fornication has arisen?

And the reason was stated, namely, lest he make her commit adultery; and this assuredly because, even if she does not herself dismiss him but is dismissed, she will be an adulteress if she marries.

**2. 3.** Therefore, on account of so great an evil, it is not permitted for a man to dismiss his wife except for the cause of fornication. For then he does not himself make an adulteress by dismissing her, but he dismisses an adulteress.

What then? If someone should say, "I do indeed dismiss my wife without any cause of fornication, but I shall remain continent," shall we for that reason say that he has done with impunity what he did? Who will dare to say this, who understands the will of the Lord when he says these things? For he who excepted the sole cause of fornication did not will that a spouse be dismissed even for the sake of continence.

### A wife may depart from her husband for the sole cause of fornication, but let her remain unmarried.

**3. 3.** Let us return, then, to the very words of the Apostle, who says: *To those who are in marriage I give charge, not I but the Lord, that the wife should not depart from her husband; but if she does depart, let her remain unmarried*[^5].

And let us, as it were, question him and consult him in some way as though present: Why did you say, Apostle, *but if she does depart, let her remain unmarried*? Is it permitted to depart, or is it not permitted? If it is not permitted, why do you charge the one departing to remain unmarried? But if it is permitted, then surely there is some cause for which it is permitted.

And when this is sought out, no cause is found except the one which the Savior alone excepted, that is, the cause of fornication. And on this account the Apostle does not charge a woman, if she has departed, to remain unmarried, except the one who departs from her husband for that cause for which alone it is permitted her to depart from her husband.

For where it is said, *I charge that she not depart; but if she does depart, let her remain unmarried*, far be it that the woman who departs in such a way that she remains unmarried should act against this charge.

Unless, therefore, she be understood to be the one to whom it is permitted to depart (and it is not permitted except when her husband is committing fornication), how is she commanded to remain unmarried if she has departed?

Who is there who would say, "If a woman departs from a husband who is not committing fornication, let her remain unmarried," when it is not permitted her at all to depart except from a husband who is committing fornication? And so this view of yours, how greatly it stands opposed to the marriage bond—where the Lord did not will that even continence be undertaken except by equal accord and consent—I think you now understand.

### Let not a woman depart from a husband who is not committing fornication even for the sake of a desire for continence.

**4. 4.** But let us set out the matter itself a little more plainly and, as it were, place it before our eyes. Behold, continence has pleased a woman; it does not please her husband. The woman has departed from him and has begun to live continently—she herself, indeed, about to remain chaste, but about to make her husband, which the Lord does not will, an adulterer; for he, since he will not contain himself, will seek another.

What are we to say to the woman, except what the sound doctrine of the Church says? Render the debt to your husband, lest, while you seek something by which you may be more highly honored, he find something by which he may be condemned. For we would say this also to him, if, while you were unwilling to be continent, he had wished to be. For you do not have power over your own body, but he does; just as neither does he have power over his own body, but you do. Do not defraud one another except by consent.

When we have said these things and more of this kind that pertain to this matter, does it please you that the woman should answer us out of this reasoning of yours: "I hear the Apostle saying, *I charge that the wife not depart from her husband; but if she does depart, let her remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband*[^6]?

Behold, I have departed; I do not wish to be reconciled to my husband, but I remain unmarried. For he did not say, 'If she departs, let her remain unmarried until she is reconciled to her husband,' but, *let her remain*, he says, *unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband.* 'Let her do this,' he says, 'or that.' He permitted one of two things to be chosen; he did not compel her into one of them. I choose to remain unmarried, and thus I fulfill the charge. Correct me, reprove me, rebuke me, use what severity you will—if I marry."

### A woman who departs from a husband not committing fornication does not excuse herself by not marrying.

**5. 5.** What shall I say against this woman except: You do not understand the Apostle well? For he would not have charged that, if she had departed from her husband, the woman remain unmarried, except the one to whom it had been permitted to depart—for that one cause, namely, which is there left unspoken because it is most well known, that is, fornication.

For God the master excepted this cause alone, when he was speaking of the wife to be dismissed, and he gave us to understand that such a rule was to be observed by the husband as well, since not only does the wife *not have power over her own body, but the husband; but likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^7].

Since, therefore, you cannot charge your husband with fornication, how do you think you excuse yourself, by not marrying, for departing from him from whom it is not permitted you to depart at all?

When the woman has heard these things from us, I think you would not wish her to answer in such a way as to say that she remains unmarried for this reason, that she departed without any fornication of her husband; for if he had committed fornication, it would have been permitted her not only to depart but also to marry.

### Even the woman who has dismissed a husband committing fornication is not permitted to marry another.

**6. 6.** By no means would she say this, since you yourself were ashamed to grant this license to women. For you said: *"*If a husband dismisses an adulterous wife and takes another, the woman alone will bear the reproach. But if a woman dismisses her husband for the cause stated above and takes another, not the man alone but also the woman will bear the reproach.*"*

Giving the reason for this opinion of yours, you say: *"*For they will say that she departed for this purpose, to join another man to herself, even though he should perhaps be such a one as the one from whom she departed; for it is exceedingly easy for men to rush into the fault of this disease. But if she has both dismissed him and married another, they will say all the more that she sought a multiplicity of men.*"*

Having given this reason, you conclude and say: *"These things, therefore, having been treated and indeed discussed, the woman ought either to bear with her husband or to remain unmarried."*

You have plainly given good counsel to women: that, although they know it is permitted them, if they have dismissed adulterous husbands, to be joined to others, they should nevertheless not do so on account of the reproach, but should rather bear with even adulterous husbands, lest they seem to wish on this occasion to mingle with many—since it is difficult for a woman not to find one to marry such as the one she dismissed, because men are exceedingly prone to this disease.

Since, therefore, we say that even the woman who has dismissed a husband committing fornication is not permitted to marry another, while you say that it is indeed permitted but not expedient, both of us without doubt say that she who dismisses a husband committing fornication ought not to marry.

But there is this difference: that we, when both spouses are Christians, say to the woman that, if she has departed from a husband committing fornication, it is not permitted her to marry another, but that from a husband not committing fornication it is not permitted her to depart at all; whereas you say that, if a woman has departed from a husband not committing fornication, it is not permitted her to marry another on account of the precept, but if she has departed from one committing fornication, it is not expedient for her to marry on account of the reproach.

And so a woman who will not marry is permitted to depart from her husband, whether he is committing fornication or is not committing fornication.

### A woman departs blamably from a husband who is not committing fornication.

**7. 7.** Furthermore, since the blessed Apostle—or rather the Lord through the Apostle—does not permit a woman to depart from a husband who is not committing fornication, it remains that he forbids her, if she has departed, to marry—the woman whom he permits to depart from one committing fornication.

For concerning her of whom it is said, *If she has departed from her husband, let her not marry*, she is permitted to depart on this condition, that she not marry.

If, then, she has chosen not to marry, there is no reason why she should be forbidden to depart, just as that woman of whom it is said, *If she does not contain herself, let her marry*[^8], is permitted on this condition not to contain herself, namely, that she marry. If, then, she has chosen to marry, she cannot be compelled to be continent.

As, therefore, this incontinent woman is compelled to marry, so that what she does not contain may not be damnable, so that woman departing from her husband is compelled to remain unmarried, so that her departing may not be blameworthy. But she departs blamably from a husband not committing fornication, even if she has remained unmarried.

Therefore she is charged to remain unmarried, if she has departed, who departs from one committing fornication. Since these things are so, if we should understand the Apostle in such a way as to say to women, "Do not depart from your husbands, even chaste ones, in such a way that, if you wish to depart, you remain unmarried," then all to whom continence has been pleasing will suppose that it is permitted them to depart even without the consent of their husbands.

And since without doubt we ought not to permit this, it remains that we ought to teach that what was said—*if she has departed, let her remain unmarried*[^9]—was said of her to whom we have learned it is permitted to depart, namely, from one committing fornication,

lest, if we teach otherwise, we throw Christian marriages into confusion under the pretext of continence and, against the most merciful precept of the Lord, drive into adulteries the incontinent husbands dismissed by continent wives, or the incontinent wives dismissed by continent husbands.

### In marriage let the rule for husband and wife be equal.

**8. 8.** That, then, which the Lord said—not indeed in the very sermon which was being expounded by us, but nonetheless elsewhere—*Whoever dismisses his wife, except for the cause of fornication, and marries another, commits adultery*[^10],

if this is to be understood in such a way that whoever dismisses his wife for the cause of fornication and marries another does not commit adultery, then the rule does not seem to be equal for husband and wife in this case,

since a woman, even if she has departed from her husband for the cause of fornication and has married another, commits adultery, whereas a husband, if he has dismissed his wife for the same cause and has married another, does not commit adultery.

But if the rule is equal in both, both commit adultery if they join themselves to another, even when they have separated themselves from one committing fornication.

That the rule for husband and wife is equal in this case the Apostle shows (which must often be recalled) where, when he had said, *The wife does not have power over her own body, but the husband*, he added and said, *likewise also the husband does not have power over his own body, but the wife*[^11]*.*

### Why did the Lord interpose the cause of fornication?

**9. 9.** *"*Why, then," you say, "did the Lord interpose the cause of fornication and not rather say generally, 'Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery,' if he too is an adulterer who, having dismissed a wife committing fornication, marries another?*"*

I believe it is because the Lord wished to recall what is the greater. For who denies that it is a greater adultery, when a wife not committing fornication has been dismissed, to marry another, than if someone has dismissed a wife committing fornication and then married another? Not because the latter is not adultery, but because it is less, where, the wife committing fornication having been dismissed, another is married.

For the Apostle James too, using a similar manner of speech, says, *To one who knows the good to do and does not do it, to him it is sin*[^12]*.*

Is it then for that reason not sin also for the one who does not know the good to do and therefore does not do it? Surely it is sin; but this is more grievous, if one also knows and does not do it—nor is the former for that reason nothing, because it is less.

So that we may state both in the same way: just as whoever dismisses his wife, except for the cause of fornication, and marries another, commits adultery, so whoever knows the good to do and does not do it, sins. But just as here it cannot rightly be said, "Therefore if he does not know, he does not sin"—

for there are also sins of those who are ignorant, though lesser than those of those who know—so neither there can it rightly be said, "Therefore if he has dismissed her for the cause of fornication and married another, he does not commit adultery"; for there is adultery also in those who marry others, having left their former wives on account of fornication, but assuredly a lesser one than in those who dismiss not on account of fornication and marry others.

For it can, just as it was said, *To one who knows the good to do and does not do it, to him it is sin*[^13], in the same way also be said: "To one who dismisses his wife without the cause of fornication and marries another, to him it is adultery." Just as, therefore, if we should say, "Whoever marries a woman dismissed by her husband apart from the cause of fornication commits adultery," we without doubt say what is true, yet do not for that reason absolve from this crime the man who has married a woman dismissed on account of the cause of fornication, but we by no means doubt that both are adulterers:

so we pronounce an adulterer the man who has dismissed his wife apart from the cause of fornication and married another; nor do we for that reason defend from the stain of this sin the man who has dismissed her on account of the cause of fornication and married another.

For we recognize that both are adulterers, although the one more grievously than the other. For no one is so absurd as to deny that he is an adulterer who has married the woman whom her husband cast off on account of the cause of fornication, while he calls an adulterer the man who has married the woman who was cast off apart from the cause of fornication. Thus, therefore, these two are both adulterers. Hence, when we say, "Whoever marries a woman dismissed by her husband apart from the cause of fornication commits adultery," we speak indeed of one of them, yet do not for that reason deny that he commits adultery who has married the woman whom her husband dismissed on account of the cause of fornication:

so, since both are adulterers—both, namely, the man who has dismissed his wife apart from the cause of fornication and married another, and the man who, his wife having been dismissed on account of the cause of fornication, has coupled himself to another—surely, when we read of one of them, we ought not so to understand it as though, by the fact that the one is expressed, the other is denied to be an adulterer.

### The words of the Evangelists concerning marriage are compared.

**9. 10.** But if the evangelist Matthew, because, one case being expressed, he passed over the other in silence, makes it difficult to understand, did not the others perhaps embrace this same thing generally in such a way that it can be understood of both?

For according to Mark it is written thus: *Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery against her; and if a wife dismisses her husband and marries another, she commits adultery*[^14].

According to Luke thus: *Everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery; and he who marries one dismissed by her husband commits adultery*[^15].

Who, then, are we, to say, "There is one who commits adultery in marrying another after dismissing his wife, and there is one who, doing this, does not commit adultery," when the Gospel says that everyone who does this commits adultery? Accordingly, if whoever does this—that is, everyone who does this, namely, that, his wife having been dismissed, he marries another—commits adultery, then without doubt both are included there: both he who dismisses his wife apart from the cause of fornication and he who dismisses her on account of the cause of fornication. For this is the meaning of *whoever dismisses*; this is the meaning of *everyone who dismisses.*

### Some copies of Matthew express the same sense concerning the cause of fornication in different words.

**10. 11.** But it is not the case (as for some reason seemed to you) that, when I was citing the words of the Gospel according to Matthew, I omitted what is written, *and marries another*, and so said *commits adultery*; rather, I set down those words which are read in that lengthy sermon which the Lord delivered on the mountain. For it was this sermon I had undertaken to treat, and the words there are read just as I set them down, that is: *Whoever dismisses his wife, except for the cause of fornication, makes her commit adultery; and he who marries a woman loosed from her husband commits adultery*[^16].

And although some copies render the same sense in different words, they nonetheless do not differ from what is understood. For some have, *whoever dismisses*; others, *everyone who dismisses*; and likewise some, *except for the cause of fornication*; others, *apart from the cause of fornication*; others, *unless on account of the cause of fornication*; likewise some, *he who marries a woman loosed from her husband commits adultery*; others, *he who marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery.*

Here, I think, you see that it makes no difference to one and the same meaning. Although that last clause, that is, *he who marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery*, some manuscripts both Greek and Latin do not have in that sermon which the Lord delivered on the mountain. I believe this is because there too the sense could be thought already to have been unfolded in what was said above, *makes her commit adultery.* For how does a dismissed woman become an adulteress, unless he who marries her becomes an adulterer?

### What is less clearly understood in Matthew is clear in the others.

**11. 12.** But the words which you yourself set down, from which it seemed to you that he does not commit adultery who has dismissed his wife on account of the cause of fornication and married another, are indeed set down obscurely. Hence I do not wonder that the reader labors in understanding them; but they are not in that sermon of the Lord which was then being treated by me, when I wrote those things which moved you as you read them.

For elsewhere the same Matthew related that the Lord said these things, not when he was delivering that lengthy sermon on the mountain, but when he had been asked by the Pharisees whether it was permitted to dismiss a wife for any cause whatever. But what is less clearly understood in Matthew can be understood in the other evangelists. Therefore, when we read in the Gospel according to Matthew, *Whoever dismisses his wife, unless on account of fornication*—or, as is more often read in the Greek, *apart from the cause of fornication, and marries another, commits adultery*[^17]—we ought not at once to think that he does not commit adultery who has dismissed his wife on account of the cause of fornication and married another, but still to remain in doubt until we consult the Gospel according to the other evangelists, by whom this was related.

What then? For what if, according to Matthew, not indeed the whole of what pertains to this matter was stated, but a part was so stated that the whole was to be understood from the part, which Mark and Luke, as it were explaining, preferred to state in full, so that the complete meaning might be clear?

And so, when, first not doubting that what is read in Matthew is true—*Whoever dismisses his wife apart from the cause of fornication and marries another commits adultery*—we have asked whether only this man commits adultery by marrying another wife, who has dismissed his former wife apart from the cause of fornication, or everyone who, his wife having been dismissed, marries another, so that there too is included the man who has dismissed a wife committing fornication,

will it not be answered to us according to Mark: "Why do you ask whether the one is an adulterer and the other is not? *Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery*[^18]"?

Will it not also be said to us according to Luke: "Why do you doubt whether the man who has dismissed his wife on account of the cause of fornication and married another does not commit adultery? *Everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery."*

And on this account, since it is not lawful for us to say that the evangelists, though speaking of one matter in different words, dissent from one sense and the same meaning, it remains that we understand Matthew to have wished to signify the whole by a part, yet to have held the same meaning, so that it is not the case that a certain man who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery—that is, the one who has dismissed her apart from fornication—while a certain man does not commit adultery—that is, the one who has dismissed her on account of fornication—but that it be in no way doubted that everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery.

### A woman, even one dismissed for the cause of fornication, does not cease to be a wife.

**12. 13.** For that too which follows according to Luke—*He who marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery*[^19]—how is it true?

How does he commit adultery, except that she whom he has married is still, while he by whom she was dismissed is living, another man's wife? For if he is now united to his own wife, not to another man's, then surely he does not commit adultery;

but he does commit adultery. She, therefore, to whom he is united is another man's. Now if she is another man's, that is, his by whom she was dismissed, then even if she was dismissed on account of the cause of fornication, she has not yet ceased to be the wife of the one who dismisses her.

But if she has ceased to be his, then she is now this man's, whom she has married as another; and if she is his, he is to be judged not an adulterer, but a husband.

But because Scripture calls him not a husband but an adulterer, she still belongs to the one by whom she was cast off, even for the cause of fornication. And therefore whatever woman, once she has been dismissed, this man too marries, because she lies with another man's husband, she is an adulteress. And how can it come about that he too is not an adulterer, since it is established that she whom he has married commits adultery?

## Second question: concerning unequal marriages, whether it is the Lord or the Apostle who forbids leaving an unbelieving spouse (13, 14–25, 32)

### The words of the Apostle concerning unequal marriages.

**13. 14.** Let us now consider that which the Apostle says: *But to the rest I say, not the Lord*[^20], intending, namely, to speak of unequal marriages, that is, where both were not Christians.

And it seems to me that he said this by way of admonishing. For because a believing spouse could lawfully leave an unbelieving spouse, therefore it is not the Lord but the Apostle who forbids this to be done. For what the Lord forbids, it is not at all permitted to do.

The Apostle therefore admonishes, because it could be an occasion for gaining many, that believing spouses not use the permitted license in leaving unbelievers. But it seems to you that it is also not permitted for unbelievers to be dismissed by believers, because the Apostle forbids this, whereas I say that it is permitted, because the Lord does not forbid it, yet that it is not expedient, because the Apostle admonishes that it not be done—he who also gives a reason why it is not expedient to do it, although it is permitted.

*For how do you know, wife*, he says, *whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife?*[^21]*.* Since he had also said above, *For the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving woman is sanctified in the brother*—that is, in the Christian—*otherwise your children*, he says, *would be unclean; but as it is, they are holy*[^22]*.*

Thus, toward gaining spouses and children for Christ, he seems to have exhorted them even by examples that had already come about. Why, then, it is not expedient that unbelieving spouses too be dismissed by believers, the cause has been clearly expressed. For it is not in order that the marriage bond with such persons be preserved, but in order that they may be won over to Christ, that the Apostle forbids departing from unbelieving spouses.

### Many things are to be done not by the law's command, but by free love.

**14. 15.** Now there are many things to be done not by the command of the Law, but by free love; and those are the more pleasing among our offices which, although it was permitted us not to expend them, we nevertheless expend for the sake of love.

Hence the Lord himself first, when he had shown that he did not owe the tribute, nevertheless paid it, lest he give offense to those whom, bearing manhood, he was caring for unto eternal salvation[^23]. And how the Apostle commends these things, his words bear witness, where he says: *For although I am free from all, I have made myself the servant of all, that I might gain the more*[^24]*.*

When he had said a little before: *Do we not have the power to eat and to drink? Do we not have the right to take about with us a sister as wife, as do the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas? Or do I alone and Barnabas not have the power to do this? Who ever serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who tends a flock and does not partake of the milk of the flock?*[^25]*.* And a little after: *If others*, he says, *share in this power over you, do not we still more? But we have not used this power, but we endure all things, lest we put any hindrance in the way of the Gospel of Christ*[^26]*.*

Then after a few words: *What then*, he says, *will be my reward? That in preaching I may set forth the Gospel without cost, so that I may not make full use of my power in the Gospel*[^27]*.* And he immediately subjoins what I recalled a little earlier: *For although I am free from all, I have made myself the servant of all, that I might gain the more*[^28]*.*

Likewise in another place, concerning certain things that pertain to food: *All things*, he says, *are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient; all things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of anything. Food is for the belly and the belly for food, but God will destroy both this and these*[^29]*.*

Likewise elsewhere on this same matter: *All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient; all things are lawful, but not all things build up. Let no one seek what is his own, but that which is another's*[^30]*.* And, to show whence he was speaking: *Eat*, he says, *whatever comes in the market, asking no question on the ground of conscience*[^31]*.* And yet elsewhere he says: *I will not eat meat forever, that I may not give offense to my brother*[^32]*.*

And likewise elsewhere: *All things indeed are clean, but it is evil for the man who eats with offense*[^33]*.* What is *all things are lawful* is the same as *all things indeed are clean*; and what is *but not all things are expedient* is the same as *but it is evil for the man who eats with offense.* Thus he shows that those things which are lawful—that is, are forbidden by no precept of the Lord—are rather to be handled as is expedient, not by the prescription of the Law, but by the counsel of love.

These are the things that are paid out in addition for the wounded man, who, to be cared for, was brought to the inn through the compassion of that Samaritan[^34]. And therefore they are said not to be commanded by the Lord, although it is by the Lord that they are admonished to be offered, so that they may be understood to be the more pleasing the more they are shown to be undue.

### Something can be permitted and yet not be expedient.

**15. 16.** But concerning those things which are such that, although they are permitted, they are not expedient, it cannot be said of them, "This is good, but that is better," as was said: *He who gives in marriage does well; and he who does not give in marriage does better*[^35]*.* For there both are permitted, and sometimes this, sometimes that, is expedient.

For to those women who do not contain themselves, it is surely expedient to marry, and what is permitted is expedient; but to those who have vowed continence, it is neither permitted nor expedient.

Further, to depart from an unbelieving spouse is permitted, but it is not expedient; whereas to remain with that spouse, if the spouse consents to cohabit, is both permitted and expedient; because, if it were not permitted, it could not be expedient.

Therefore something can be permitted and not be expedient; but what is not permitted cannot be expedient. And on this account, not all things that are lawful are expedient; but all things unlawful are not expedient. For just as everyone who has been redeemed by the blood of Christ is a man, yet not everyone who is a man has also been redeemed by the blood of Christ, so everything that is not permitted is not expedient, yet not everything that is not expedient is also not permitted. For there are lawful things which are not expedient, as we have learned on the Apostle's testimony.

### But this is difficult to define by any universal rule.

**16. 17.** But what the difference is between that which is unlawful and therefore not expedient, and that which is lawful and yet not expedient, is difficult to define by any universal rule.

For someone would more readily say: "Everything that is not expedient to do is sin; but every sin is unlawful. Therefore everything that is not expedient is unlawful." And where then will be those things which the Apostle said are lawful but not expedient, if everything that is not expedient is not lawful?

Wherefore, since we cannot doubt that the Apostle spoke the truth, and we do not dare to say that some sins are lawful, it remains for us to say that something is done which is not expedient, and yet, if it is lawful, is not a sin—although, since it is not expedient, it is surely not to be done.

But if it seems absurd that something be done which is not expedient and that the one who has done it be said to have sinned, it must be understood that this is absurd from the customary use of speech, which extends so widely that we say even beasts of burden, although they are devoid of reason, ought nonetheless for the most part to be beaten when they "sin"; whereas to sin, properly speaking, belongs only to one who uses the rational choice of the will, which among all mortal living things has been divinely assigned to none but man. But it is one thing when we speak properly, another when we borrow words by transferring them from other things or by using them loosely.

### What is permitted and not expedient, and what is neither permitted nor expedient.

**17. 18.** In order, then, if we can, to strive to distinguish by some fixed boundary between that which is permitted and not expedient, and that which is not permitted and therefore not expedient: those things seem to me to be permitted and not expedient which are indeed allowed by the justice that is in the sight of God, but, on account of giving offense to people—lest they be thereby hindered from salvation—are to be avoided; whereas those things are not permitted and therefore not expedient which are so forbidden by justice itself that they are not to be done, even if they should be praised by those to whom they have come to notice. And if this is so, then only unlawful things are forbidden by the Lord, so that those things which are lawful and not expedient may be guarded against not by the bond of the Law, but by the free kindness of love.

### To dismiss an unbelieving spouse is permitted, but it is not expedient.

**17. 19.** Wherefore, if it were not permitted to dismiss an unbelieving spouse, the Lord would forbid this to be done, and the Apostle, in forbidding it, would not say, *I say, not the Lord*[^36]. For if, on account of fornication of the flesh, a person is allowed to be separated from a spouse, how much more is the fornication of the mind to be detested in a spouse—that is, the fornication of unbelief, of which it is written: *For behold, those who put themselves far from you shall perish; you have destroyed everyone who commits fornication against you*[^37]!

**18. 19.** But because it is lawful in such a way that it is not expedient—lest, on account of separations of spouses, people, taking offense, shudder at the very doctrine of salvation by which unlawful things are forbidden, and thus, worse and bound to perish, remain in the same unbelief—the Apostle intervenes and by admonition forbids what is lawful in such a way that it is not expedient.

For believing men or women are so not forbidden by the Lord to withdraw from unbelieving wives or husbands that neither are they commanded to. For if they were commanded to dismiss such spouses, there would be no place for the counsel of the admonishing Apostle that this not be done. For in no way would a good servant forbid to be done what the Lord commands.

### Concerning unbelieving spouses, the New Law is other than the old.

**18. 20.** For at one time the Lord commanded this through the prophet Ezra, and it was done; the Israelites dismissed their foreign wives, all who were then able to have them[^38], through whom it was coming about that they too were led astray to foreign gods, rather than that those wives were won over to the true God through their husbands.

For the grace of the Savior had not yet shone forth so greatly, and the multitude of that people still gaped after the temporal promises of the Old Testament.

And therefore, since they saw earthly goods, which they sought from the Lord as something great, abound also among those who worshiped many false gods, they were first afraid, through the blandishments of their wives, to offend those gods, and then were led on to worship them as well.

Hence the Lord had commanded through holy Moses that no one take a foreign wife[^39].

Rightly, then, the wives they had taken when the Lord forbade it, they dismissed when the Lord commanded it.

But when the Gospel had begun to be preached to the gentiles, it found gentiles already joined to gentiles as spouses:

and of these, if both did not believe, but only the one or the other, and the unbeliever consented to dwell with the believer, the believer ought neither to be forbidden by the Lord to dismiss the unbeliever nor to be commanded to:

not forbidden, namely, because justice allows departing from one committing fornication, and the fornication of an unbelieving person is greater, being in the heart,

nor can his chastity with his spouse be called true, because *everything that is not of faith is sin*[^40]:

although the believer has true chastity even with an unbelieving spouse, who does not have the true.

And believers ought not to be commanded to be separated from unbelievers, because it was not against the command of the Lord that the two gentiles were joined.

### That a believer should not depart, the Apostle says, not the Lord.

**18. 21.** Since, therefore, the Lord neither forbids nor commands a believer to depart from an unbeliever, therefore it is the Apostle who says that one should not depart, not the Lord—the Apostle having, to be sure, the Holy Spirit, in whom he could give useful and faithful counsel.

Hence, when he had said concerning a woman whose husband has died, *But she will be more blessed if she remains so, according to my counsel*[^41], lest anyone think this counsel was to be despised as human and not divine, he added: *but I think that I too have the Spirit of God*[^42]*.* Accordingly it must be understood that even those very things which are not commanded by the Lord, but are usefully urged by his holy servant, are urged by the inspiration of the same Lord.

For far be it that any Catholic should say that, when the Holy Spirit urges, it is not the Lord who urges, since the Spirit is himself the Lord and the works of the Trinity are inseparable.

Yet he says: *But concerning virgins I have no command of the Lord; but I give my counsel*[^43]*.* Not that we should reckon this counsel foreign to the Lord, since it immediately follows that he says: *as one who by the mercy of God has been made faithful*[^44]*.* Therefore, in accord with God, he gives faithful counsel in that Spirit of whom he says: *but I think that I too have the Spirit of God*[^45]*.*

### The command of the Lord who orders is one thing; the faithful counsel of a fellow servant is another.

**18. 22.** Nevertheless the command of the Lord who orders is one thing; the faithful counsel of a fellow servant, according to the mercy of love which has been inspired and given to him by the Lord, is another.

There it is not permitted to do otherwise; here, however, it is permitted, in such a way, to be sure, that the permitted thing itself is in part expedient and in part not expedient.

It is expedient then, when it is allowed not only by the justice that is in the sight of the Lord, but also no hindrance to salvation is thereby brought upon people: as when the Apostle gives counsel to virgins not to marry—whence he testifies that he does not have a command of the Lord—it is permitted to do otherwise, that is, to marry and to hold the good of marriage, which is less than that of continence, yet a good.

And the permitted thing itself is also expedient, since it so receives, by the honorableness of marrying, the weakness of the flesh that would otherwise rush into forbidden and unlawful things, that it hinders no one from salvation—although it would be more expedient and more honorable if the virgin should seize the counsel into which the precept does not compel her.

But that which is lawful is not expedient at the point when it is indeed permitted, but the use of that very power brings upon others a hindrance to salvation.

Such is the case, of which we have now long been speaking: the departure of a believing spouse from an unbeliever, which the Lord does not forbid by a precept of the Law, because it is not unjust in his sight,

but which the Apostle forbids by the counsel of love, because it brings upon unbelievers a hindrance to salvation: not only because, taking offense, they are scandalized most perniciously, but also because, when they have fallen into other marriages while those still live by whom they are dismissed, they are bound by adulterous ties most difficult to be loosed.

### It is better not to dismiss an unbelieving spouse, although it is permitted.

**19. 23.** Therefore here, where what is permitted is not expedient, it cannot be said, "If he dismisses the unbeliever, he does well; if he does not dismiss her, he does better,"

as was said: *He who gives in marriage does well; and he who does not give in marriage does better*[^46];

since in that case not only is each alike permitted—whence by a precept of the Lord no one is compelled to either of them—but each is also expedient, the one less, the other more, whence whoever can lay hold of it is, by the counsel of the Apostle, summoned to that which is more expedient.

But in this case, where the question is of dismissing or not dismissing an unbelieving spouse, each is indeed alike lawful by the justice that is in the sight of the Lord, and therefore the Lord forbids neither of them; but not each is expedient, on account of the weaknesses of people, and therefore the Apostle forbids what is not expedient, the Lord giving him free room to forbid, since neither does the Lord forbid what the Apostle admonishes, nor does the Lord command what the Apostle forbids.

And unless this were so, the Apostle would neither admonish anything against the Lord's prohibition, nor forbid anything against his command. Accordingly, in these two cases—one concerning marrying or not marrying, the other concerning dismissing or not dismissing an unbelieving spouse—there is something alike in the words of the Apostle, and something unlike.

Alike indeed in this, that there too he says, *I have no command of the Lord, but I give my counsel*[^47]; and here he says, *I say, not the Lord*[^48]*.* For what *I have no command of the Lord* is, that *the Lord does not say* is; and what *I give counsel* is, that *I say* is.

But this is unlike, that concerning marrying and not marrying it can be said that this is done well, that better, since each is expedient, the one less, the other more; whereas concerning dismissing or not dismissing an unbelieving spouse, since one of these is not expedient and the other is expedient, it ought not to be said, "He who dismisses does well, and he who does not dismiss does better," but it ought to be said, "Let him not dismiss, because, even though it is permitted, it is not expedient."

Thus, then, we can say that it is better not to dismiss an unbelieving spouse, although it is also permitted to dismiss her, just as we rightly say that what is both permitted and expedient is better than what is permitted but not expedient. Dismissing an unbelieving spouse is not to be done, on account of free goodwill.

**20. 24.** For these reasons it came about that, in expounding the sermon of the Lord which he delivered at length on the mountain, when I came to the question of dismissing or not dismissing spouses, I said, adducing apostolic testimonies as well, that it was a counsel of the Apostle, not a precept of the Lord, where he says, *But to the rest I say, not the Lord*[^49], admonishing those who had unbelieving spouses that, when they consented to dwell with them, they should not dismiss them.

And this assuredly had to be admonished, not commanded, because people are not to be forbidden with so great a weight to do lawful things, although they are not expedient, as with the weight by which unlawful things are forbidden.

But if somewhere the Apostle deigned to admonish even those things which are to be commanded, he did this by sparing weakness, not by prejudging the command. Hence if he said, *I write these things not to confound you, but I admonish you as my dearest children*[^50], what difficulty is there where he says, *I say, not the Lord*? Likewise, where he says, *Behold, I, Paul, say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing*[^51], did he there too say, *I say, not the Lord*?

And so these cases are not alike, because even those things which the Lord commands, it is neither unworthy nor contrary if the Apostle admonishes the same. For we admonish those whom we hold dear to do the precepts, or commands, of the Lord. But when he says, *I say, not the Lord*, he shows clearly enough that the Lord does not forbid what he himself was forbidding[^52]. But the Lord would have forbidden it, if it were unlawful. Therefore, according to those things which we have said above at length and turned over much, it was lawful by justice, but, though lawful, was not to be done, on account of free goodwill.

### The opinion of Pollentius errs . . .

**21. 25.** But you, to whom it is pleasing that what the Lord does not forbid, but the Apostle does, be not permitted just as what the Lord forbids is not permitted, when you wished to expound what he meant by saying *I say, not the Lord*[^53], since he was addressing believers who had unbelieving spouses, you said: *"*Because the Lord commanded that marriages of different religion not be joined to one another,*"*

and you adduced this very testimony of the Lord, who says: *You shall not take a wife for your son from the daughters of foreigners, lest she lead him astray after their gods and his soul perish*[^54]*.* You also added the words of the Apostle, where he said, *A woman is bound as long as her husband lives. But if her husband dies, she is freed; let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord*[^55], which you expounded by adding, *"*that is, a Christian.*"*

Then you went on and said: *"This, therefore, is the Lord's precept both in the Old and in the New Testament, that only marriages of one religion and faith remain joined to one another."*

If, then, this is the Lord's precept, both in the Old Testament and in the New, and the Lord commands this, the Apostle teaches this, that only marriages of one religion and faith remain joined together, why, against this command of the Lord, against his own teaching, against the precept of the Old and New Testament, does the Apostle command that marriages of different faith remain joined together?

*"*Because Paul," you say, "the preacher and Apostle of the gentiles, not only admonishes but also commands those already established in marriage that, if one or the other of the spouses has believed, he not dismiss the other, who does not believe but nevertheless consents to dwell with him.*"*

By these words of yours you show clearly enough that this is one thing and that another.

For the one concerns those marriages which are first joined to one another, that a woman not marry a man not of her religion, or a man take such a woman to wife. *"*For this, as you say, God commands, the Apostle teaches, both Testaments enjoin.*"*

But who would deny that this other is different, where the question is not of those to be joined, but of those already joined? For both were of one and the same unbelief when they were joined; but when the Gospel had come, the one without the other, or the other without the one, believed.

If, then, this is a different matter, which appears without the least scruple of doubt, why does not the Lord, like the Apostle, also command that a believer remain in marriage with an unbeliever? Unless perhaps in this place there is no force to what he himself says so confidently: *Do you wish to receive proof of the Christ who speaks in me?*[^56] And surely Christ is the Lord. Do you understand what I am saying? Or shall I dwell somewhat more carefully on explaining this?

### . . . and so is refuted.

**21. 26.** Attend, that we may set the matter itself in plainer speech, to be considered as it were in plain view. Behold, two spouses of one unbelief; so they were when they were joined. There is no question about these that pertains to that command of the Lord and the apostolic teaching and the precept of the Old and New Testament by which a believer is forbidden to join a marriage with an unbeliever.

They are now spouses, and both are still unbelievers; they are still such as they were before they were joined, and such as they were when joined. The preacher of the Gospel comes; the one or the other of them believes—but in such a way that the unbeliever consents to dwell with the believer. Does the Lord command the believer not to dismiss the unbeliever, or does he not command it? If you say, "He commands it," the Apostle cries out against you: *I say, not the Lord*[^57]; if you say, "He does not command it," I ask the reason.

Nor are you going to give me that reason which you put in your letter: *"*Because the Lord forbids believers to be joined to unbelievers.*"* For here that reason in no way applies; we are speaking of those already joined, not of those to be joined.

If, then, you have not found the reason why the Lord does not forbid what the Apostle forbids (for you now see, as I suppose, that it is not the reason you had thought it was), consider whether perhaps it is that which seemed to me, both to be brought forward then and to be defended now,

namely, that we understand the Lord to say that which has, in his sight, a justice that may in no way be transgressed—that is, which he so commands or forbids that to do otherwise is not at all permitted;

but that which he so leaves to the power of the one willing that it is neither done nor omitted unlawfully, he himself gives room to the counsel of his servants, so that they may rather urge what they have seen to be expedient.

### That unlawful things not be committed.

**21. 27.** Let this, then, be held first and above all: that unlawful things not be committed. But where something is permitted in such a way that to do otherwise is not unlawful, let what is expedient, or what is more expedient, be done.

Those things, therefore, which the Lord says as the Lord—that is, not by the counsel of one admonishing, but by the command of one who rules—it is not permitted not to do, and therefore neither is it expedient.

The Lord, then, gave charge *that the wife not depart from her husband; but if she does depart* (for that cause, namely, for which it is lawful to depart)*, let her remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband*[^58]*.*

For *a woman is bound by law to her husband, while her husband lives*; and *while her husband lives, she will be called an adulteress if she is with another man*[^59]; since *a woman is bound as long as her husband lives*[^60]*.*

Hence: *If a wife dismisses her husband and marries another, she commits adultery*[^61]; and *He who marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery*[^62]*.*

And therefore from the same precept of the Lord: *and let the husband not dismiss his wife*[^63]; since *he who dismisses his wife apart from the cause of fornication makes her commit adultery*[^64]*.*

But if he has dismissed her on account of this cause, let him too remain so: for *everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery*[^65]*.*

### The Lord's ordinances are to be kept without any reconsideration.

**22. 27.** These ordinances of the Lord are to be kept without any reconsideration. For they have that justice which is in his sight, whether people approve or disapprove. And therefore it ought not to be said, on account of giving offense to people, or lest people be hindered from that salvation which is in Christ, that they are not to be kept. For what Christian would dare to say, "Lest I offend people, or that I may win people for Christ, I will make my wife commit adultery, so that I myself may become an adulterer"?

**22. 28.** For it can happen that, when some Christian has dismissed an adulterous wife, he is so tempted that some woman who has not yet believed, desiring to enter into marriage with him, promises that she will become a Christian—not deceitfully, but altogether intending to do this if she marries him.

And so, to this man refusing this marriage, that tempter will be able to suggest: "The Lord said, *Whoever dismisses his wife apart from the cause of fornication and marries another commits adultery*[^66]; but you, who dismissed your wife on account of the cause of fornication, if you marry another, will not commit adultery."

To this man suggesting such things let him answer with an instructed heart that, indeed, he commits adultery more grievously who, his wife having been dismissed apart from the cause of fornication, has married another;

but that he too, who, his wife committing fornication having been dismissed, has coupled another to himself, does not for that reason not commit adultery, because he leaves behind one committing adultery:

just as he commits adultery who marries the woman who is dismissed apart from the cause of fornication, neither for that reason does he not commit adultery who marries the woman whom he has found dismissed apart from the cause of fornication.

And therefore what is set down somewhat obscurely in Matthew, since the whole was signified by a part, has been expounded in the others, who expressed the whole generally, as is read in Mark: *Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery*[^67]; and in Luke: *Everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery*[^68]*.*

For they did not say that some commit adultery and others do not commit adultery who, having dismissed their wives, marry others; but, *whoever dismisses*—they said that absolutely everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery, without any exception."

### A Christian who commits unlawful things cannot escape damnation.

**23. 29.** But if that Christian should answer the tempter in this way, understanding that it was indeed permitted him to dismiss an adulteress, but that it is not permitted to marry another, what then? If that tempter should say, "Commit this sin, so that you may win a soul for Christ—the soul of a woman set in the death of unbelief, who is ready, if she marries you, to become a Christian"?

what else ought the Christian to answer to these things and to say, except that he cannot, if he does this, escape the judgment which the Apostle recalled, where he says, *And as some report that we say, "Let us do evil that good may come," whose condemnation is just*[^69]? And how could she be wholesomely a Christian who will be an adulteress with the man by whom she is married?

### What people have vowed without any condition is to be broken on no condition.

**24. 30.** But not only must adultery not be committed—which is committed not by a certain man but by everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another, even if he married for the purpose of making her a Christian—

but also whoever, not being bound to a wife, has vowed continence to God ought in no way to sin by this trade-off, so as to believe that he ought to take a wife because the woman who seeks marriage with him has promised that she will become a Christian.

For what was permitted to anyone before he had vowed, will not be permitted once he has vowed that he will never do it;

provided, however, that he has vowed what ought to be vowed—such as perpetual virginity, or continence after marriages have been experienced, by those loosed from the marriage bond, or by believing and chaste spouses who vow by consent and release to one another the carnal debts—which it is not lawful for the one to vow without the other.

These things, then, and any others that are most rightly vowed, when people have vowed them, are to be broken on no condition, which they vowed without any condition. For this too is to be understood as commanded by the Lord, where it is read, *Make vows and render them to the Lord your God*[^70]. Hence the Apostle says of certain women who vow continence and afterward wish to marry, which before they had vowed was surely permitted them: *having*, he says, *condemnation, because they have made their first faith void*[^71].

**24. 31.** Nothing, therefore, that is unlawful is expedient, and nothing that the Lord forbids is lawful.

### In those things which the Lord has not commanded, let the Apostle be heard.

**25. 31.** But in those things which, with no precept of the Lord constraining, are left in our power, let the Apostle be heard, admonishing and counseling in the Holy Spirit, that either better things be laid hold of, or those which are not expedient be guarded against.

There let him be heard saying, *I have no command of the Lord, but I give my counsel*[^72]; and, *I say, not the Lord*[^73]. There, if he who hears chooses the better things—*Are you loosed from a wife? Do not seek a wife*, because *even if you take a wife, you do not sin*[^74]—there let the virgin not marry. For *he who does not give in marriage does better; and he who gives in marriage does well*[^75].

There let the woman be more blessed by remaining so, who, her husband having died, has it in her power to marry *whom she wishes, only in the Lord*[^76]. This can be taken in two ways: either remaining a Christian, or marrying a Christian. For I do not recall that, in the time of the revealed New Testament, it was declared without ambiguity, in the Gospel or in any apostolic writings, whether the Lord forbade believers to be joined to unbelievers, although the most blessed Cyprian does not doubt of this, nor does he reckon among light sins *to join the bond of matrimony with unbelievers*, and he says that this is *to prostitute to the gentiles the members of Christ*[^77].

But because concerning those who are already joined it is another question, here too let the Apostle be heard, saying: *If any brother has an unbelieving wife and she consents to dwell with him, let him not dismiss her; and if any woman has an unbelieving husband and he consents to dwell with her, let her not dismiss her husband*[^78]*.*

And let him be so heard that, although it is lawful to do it, because the Lord does not say this, it nevertheless not be done, because it is not expedient. For the Apostle teaches most plainly that not all things which are lawful are expedient[^79], as we have already shown above.

On account of any kind of fornication whatever, however—whether of the flesh or of the spirit, where unbelief too is understood—both, when a husband has been dismissed, it is not permitted to marry another, and, when a wife has been dismissed, it is not permitted to marry another;

since the Lord says, with no exception made: *If a wife dismisses her husband and marries another, she commits adultery*[^80]; and, *Everyone who dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery*[^81]*.*

### The question concerning marriages is most obscure and most involved.

**25. 32.** Having thus treated and discussed these things to the measure of my ability, I am nonetheless not unaware that the question concerning marriages is most obscure and most involved. Nor do I dare to profess that I have yet unfolded all its recesses, either in this work or in another, or that I could now unfold them, if I were pressed. But as for that on which you think I should be consulted on another sheet, I too would take care to resolve it separately, if it seemed otherwise to me than it seemed to you; but since our opinion is the same, there is no need to dispute about it any longer.

## Third question: If catechumens, lying in despair of life, cannot ask baptism for themselves, are they to be baptized or not (26, 33–28, 35)

### Catechumens in despair of life, even if they cannot ask for baptism, are to be baptized.

**26. 33.** For catechumens, then, set in the last extremity of this life, if they are so overcome by illness or by some accident that, although they still live, they nevertheless cannot ask baptism for themselves or answer the questions, let it profit them that their will is already known to be in the Christian faith, so that they may be baptized in the same way as infants are baptized, whose will has not yet appeared at all[^82].

Yet we ought not on that account to condemn those who act more timidly than it seems to us ought to be done, lest we be judged to have wished to judge more rashly than cautiously concerning the money entrusted to a fellow servant. For in such matters that word of the Apostle must be sufficiently regarded, where he says: *Each one of us will render an account of himself to God*[^83]*.*

Let us, then, no longer judge one another. For there are those who think that, whether in these matters or even in others, attention must be paid to what we read the Lord said: *Do not give what is holy to the dogs, nor cast your pearls before swine*[^84]*.* And, revering these words of the Savior, they do not dare to baptize those who have been unable to answer for themselves, lest perhaps they bear a choice of will to the contrary.

This cannot be said of little ones, in whom there is as yet no use of reason. But not only is it incredible that a catechumen does not wish to be baptized even at the end of this life;

but also, if his will is uncertain, it is far better to give to one unwilling than to deny to one willing,

where it does not appear whether he wishes it or not, yet in such a way that it is more credible that he, if he could, would rather have said that he wished to receive those sacraments without which he has already believed that he ought not to depart from the body.

### The words of Matthew do not mean to signify the contrary.

**27. 34.** But if the Lord, where he says, *Do not give what is holy to the dogs*[^85], wished that to be understood which these men think must be guarded against, he would not himself have given to his own betrayer that which he, unworthy, received together with the worthy, to his own ruin, without fault on the part of the giver.

Hence, when the Lord said this, it must be believed that he wished to signify that unclean hearts do not bear the light of spiritual understanding. And if a teacher should press things to be borne upon those who do not rightly receive them, because they do not grasp them, they either tear them apart with the bites of reproach or trample them down by contempt. For if the blessed Apostle says that to those who were already reborn in Christ, yet still little ones, he gave milk, not solid food—*For you were not yet able*, he says, *nor indeed are you even now able*[^86]—if, finally, the Lord himself said to the chosen apostles, *I have yet many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now*[^87], how much less can the unclean minds of the ungodly bear whatever is said about the incorporeal light?

### Even adulterous catechumens, if they have lain in despair, are to be baptized.

**28. 35.** But, so that our discourse may rather be closed at the point from which it took its beginning: I think that not only other catechumens but also those very persons who, joined to the spouses of living people, retain adulterous unions—although, while they are sound in body, we do not admit them to baptism—are nonetheless to be baptized if they have lain in despair and have been unable to answer for themselves, so that this sin too, with the rest, may be washed away by the laver of regeneration.

For who knows whether perhaps they had resolved to be held back by the allurement of adulterous flesh only until baptism?

But if, restored from that despair, they should be able to live, they will either do what they had resolved, or, being instructed, will comply, or there will become of them as despisers what ought to become even of such baptized persons.

But the cause of reconciliation is the same as that of baptism, if perhaps the peril of ending life has come upon a penitent before its time. For mother Church ought not to wish even these to depart from this life without the pledge of her peace.
