# Fifth Sermon for the First Sunday in November: On the Way in Which the Seraphim Cover the Face and Feet of God, and on the Manner of Their Flying

“ Upon it stood the Seraphim. The one had six wings and the other had six wings. With two they covered His face and with two they covered His feet and with two they flew.” — LSet 2.

The Holy Scriptures, my brethren, represent to us Christ the Lord as being of the Father, and in the Father, and with the Father, and from the Father, and for the Father, and even as being under the Father. When He is said to be of the Father, there is allusion to His ineffable divine nativity ; when He is said to be im the Father, the reference is to Their consubstantial unity ; when. He is said to bewith the Father, we have insinuated to us the equality of Their majesty. These three attributes, namely, to be of, and in, and with the Father, are eternal in the Son. But if He is born of the Father, in what sense is He declared to be in the Father and with the Father? Perhaps it would not be absurd to say that He reclines in the bosom of the Father and sits with Him upon His throne? And listen to the reason of this sitting with the Father and of this reclining. Just as the Son’s mere sitting is expressive of majesty, so does His sitting with the Father indicate an equality of majesty, especially since we are told that He is seated at the right hand of the Father, not at His feet nor at His back. Now sitting is already repose, but reclining is much more so. Which of these two shall we consider to be the source of the greater delight and sweetness to the Son: that He reclines in the bosom of the Father, or that with the Father He governs the universe? In which of the two shall we suppose to consist that sovereign ‘‘ peace of God which surpasseth all understanding ” (Phil. iv. 7)? To which had we better assign that singular repose of the Lord ? And if such a question cannot be worthily expressed in human language, perhaps it may be piously conceived in the mind: perhaps it may seem possible, without prejudice in any respect to the absolute simplicity of the Divine Essence, to draw a mental distinction between the equality of majesty and the identity of Substance corresponding to that which we recognise between the sitting of one person with another and the reclining of one person on another ?

Thus, the Spouse in the Canticle is not satisfied with beholding her Bridegroom seated: she asks to see Him reclining. ‘‘ Shew me,” she exclaims, “ O Thou Whom my soul loveth, where Thou feedest, where Thou liest in the mid-day ” (Cant. i. 6). Indeed it may be said with truth that every soul of healthy palate finds a sweeter relish in the words of St. Paul, “ He who is joined to the Lord is one Spirit with Him ” (1 Cor. vi. 17), than in the promise given to the Apostles, “ When the Son of man shall sit on the seat of His majesty, you also shall sit on twelve seats judging the twelve tribes of Israel ’’ (Matt. xix. 28). But before proceeding further with this investigation, it is necessary to explain what those wings are wherewith the Seraphim are said to fly : “ And with two they flew.” Perhaps we shall not be far astray if we understand them as symbolising nature and grace, especially since I have already expounded in this sense the height and the elevation of the throne. For by the natural vivacity of their intelligence and by the supernatural, grace-given fervour of their will, they are always tending towards and intent upon Him Who sits above them ; and (as I have said) they are represented as standing because of their ministry, and as flying on account of their ardent affection. It is true, they cover the face of the Lord, and cover also His feet : still it is not from themselves that they cover them, but from the eye of the evil one. They themselves are for ever winging their way up and down from the feet to the face of the Lord, diligently searching out the deep things of His wisdom and the high things of His power. Therefore, according to this interpretation by the face or head of God we must understand His majesty and His sovereign authority, “ His eternal power also and Divinity” (Rom. i. 20). The Psalmist is alluding to the head of God where he sings, “ Thy justice is as the mountains of God” (Ps. xxxv. 7). What follows in the same Psalm, viz., “Thy judgments are a great deep ” (ibid.), may be referred, as I think, and not unreasonably, to the Lord’s feet. For what are the feet of the Lord but His “ unsearchable ways ” (Rom. xi. 33), and His inscrutable judgments, and the profundity of His wisdom, and the irreprehensible or rather incomprehensible dispositions of His will? To the same feet belong also and especially, as we know, the mystery of the Lord’s incarnation and the whole economy of redemption.

It is necessary for us, brethren, to consider the loftiness of that divine justice which the Psalmist contemplated with astonishment and compared to “ the mountains of God.” Our own human justice—if existent at all—isa diminutive justice, so to speak, and though perhaps true, is never entirely pure. This we must acknowledge unless we would claim to be better than our fathers, who confessed with as much truth as humility, “ all our justices are as a filthy rag ” (Is. lxiv. 6). For how can justice be pure there where as yet sin cannot be absent ? (rt John i. 8). But the justice of men can nevertheless be true, even in the present life, provided only that they do not consent to sin, so that sin may not “ reign in their mortal body ” (Rom. vi. 12). The justice of the first man was, at least in the beginning, not only true, but pure also, and such it remained so long as he was able by the grace of God to withhold his consent to sin. But even this justice, not being firmly established, quickly lost its purity, and proved unable to preserve even its truth. Amongst the holy angels we find a justice that is true and pure and steadfast ; it is lofty too, but falls infinitely short of the altitude of God’s justice. For their justice is not innate in the angels, but bestowed upon them by God, so that of itself the angelic nature is capable not only of justice but of injustice as well. Is not this capacity for injustice the wickedness which, according to Job (Job iv. 18), He who is essential Justice found in His angels ? “In Thy sight no living creature shall be justified (non justificabitur omnis vivens) ”’ (Ps. cxlii. 2): these, my brethren, are the words of one who was by no means ignorant of the justice of God. Observe how he does not say, ‘““ No man,” but “ No living creature,” lest we should suppose that he is excepting even the very angelic spirits. For the angels are included amongst living things, and they live more truly than men for that they stand closer to Him “ with Whom is the fountain of life” (Ps. xxxv. 10). Nevertheless they also are just only by His grace, not in His sight; through His bounty, not in comparison with His justice. He is justice to Himself, Whose will is not so much righteous as righteousness, and both, viz., the will and its righteousness, are in nothing distinct from His Substance. Very properly, therefore, may we compare to a mountain that justice which is true and pure and stable and, if I may so speak, substantial. Oh, what unsuspected sublimities lie hidden in this head of God! How great a glory, how dizzy an altitude do we not discover in this shadowy and cloud-capped mountain !
