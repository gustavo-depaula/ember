Be vigilant in mind, brethren, so that you may not fail to profit by the mysteries of the passing time. Very abundant are the graces now being offered. Come with clean vessels to receive them: present devout minds, watchful senses, chaste affections, and pure consciences, for so great a largess of the divine bounty. Such special vigilance at this holy season is required of you, not only by the particular manner of life which you have embraced, but also by the general practice of the Church whose children you are. For during the present hallowed week all Christians, with more than their ordinary fervour, practise piety, give example of modesty, cultivate humility, put on gravity, in order to show in some way how they compassionate the sufferings of Christ. Who indeed can be so irreligious as not to be softened to sorrow during these blessed days? Who so proud as not to feel humble ? Who so vindictive as not to forgive? Who so self-indulgent as not to deprive himself? Who so sensual as not to practise self-restraint ? Who so hardened in evil as not to repent? The liability to death, my brethren, is undoubtedly a great weakness of our nature ; but to die in this manner is a proof of almighty power. We have here in truth, a threefold cord, and “ a threefold cord is not easily broken ” (Eccles. iv. 12). Therefore many draw this triple cord of vanity, or perhaps I should have said that many are drawn by it. But— as indeed it is only natural to anticipate—the previously mentioned triple cord of virtues prevails in the elect. But besides this triple concupiscence there are three kinds of sin, and I have been thinking of explaining to you how they also are effaced by the power of the cross. I think you will derive greater profit from such a discussion. First, there is original sin; secondly, what I would call personal sin ; and thirdly the singular sin of Deicide. By original sin is meant that most grievous sin which has been transmitted to us from the first Adam, “in whom we have all sinned ” (Rom. v. 12), and because of whom we are all made liable to death: a most grievous sin, undoubtedly, since it has so completely corrupted, not alone the entire human race, but even every individual member of the human race, so that there has been none left untouched by it, no, not so much as one.* From the first of men to the last it extends its malign influence ; and in each individual human person its poison is diffused “ from the sole of the foot unto the top of the head ” (Is. i. 6). In another sense also it is universal, in that, namely, it occupies every stage of our human existence, from the day when our mothers conceived us until the day of our restoration to our common mother, the earth. How otherwise can it be true what is written, “ A heavy yoke is upon the children of Adam from the day of their coming out of their mother’s womb until the day of their burial into the mother of all” (Ecclus. xl. 1)? We are conceived in shame, fostered in darkness, brought forth in sorrow. Before birth we burden our unhappy mothers, and we rend them like vipers in the very act of being born: the wonder is that we are not rent ourselves. Our first utterance is a wail of woe, which is nothing surprising, since we have entered now the valley of tears. Thus, the words of holy Job are perfectly applicable to us, ‘‘ Man, born of a woman, living for a short time, is filled with many miseries ’’ (Job xiv. 1). How true this is we have been taught not by words alone but also by bitter experience. “Man. born of a woman,” says the Patriarch. What could be more humiliating ? And lest we should perhaps feel inclined to console ourselves with the thought of the bodily pleasures to be found in sensible things, here, at our very entrance into the world, we receive a terrible admonition of our exit therefrom, when it is added, “ living for a short time.” Furthermore, lest we should suppose that even the short space intervening between our entrance and our exit shall be free for our enjoyment, we hear the words, “ filled with many miseries.” Yes, indeed, ‘‘ man is filled with many miseries,” many and multiform, miseries of body and miseries of mind, miseries in his sleep and miseries in his wakefulness, miseries whithersoever he turns. For even Christ Himself, born of a Virgin, yea, “ made of a woman ” (Gal. iv. 4), but of one “ blessed among

Have you any doubts, my brethren, as to the sufficiency of Christ’s obedience to atone for all the guilt of the primeval transgression? Indeed, it is more than sufficient, because “ not as the offence, so also is the gift. For judgment indeed was by one unto condemnation: but grace is of many offences unto justification ’’ (Rom. v. 16). Great assuredly was the malice of that first sin, which had power to corrupt not only the person who committed it, but even the very nature to which he belonged. Nevertheless, for each individual more grievous than the guilt he inherits from Adam is that which he owes to himself, when he now recklessly “‘ yields his members as instruments of iniquity unto sin ” (Rom. vi. 13), no longer held captive by the sin of another, but by his own personal fault. But gravest of all is the third kind of sin, committed against the Person of the Lord of Majesty, when wicked men wickedly slew the Just One (Acts vii. 52), when the most cruel of homicides, nay, rather of deicides—if it is allowable to use the word—laid sacrilegious hands on the Son of God Himself. What comparison can there be between either of the other kinds of sin and this, which appalled and convulsed the whole universe and” well nigh threw the world back again into the chaos whence it was drawn? Suppose a certain nobleman has invaded with a hostile army and laid waste the territory of his king ; and suppose another who, after being admitted to the table and to the council of his royal master, with a traitor’s hand has strangled the monarch’s only son : does not the former appear almost innocent and undeserving of punishment as compared with the latter? So, my brethren, is every other sin in comparison with the sin of deicide. And nevertheless even this sin He took upon Himself Who made Himself ‘‘sin’’ for our sakes, He Who knew no sin (2 Cor. v. 21), in order that “ of sin He might condemn sin in the flesh ”’ (Rom. viii. 3). For by means of this sin both original and actual sin have been blotted out : yea, this sin of deicide has been the means of destroying itself.*

Now if we are able to walk under such a burden, what. do you suppose we shall do when we are relieved of that burden? Shall we not run? Shall we not fly ? Yea, and “ upon the wings of the winds ” (Ps. xvii. 11). My brethren, the Lord Jesus has embraced us by taking upon Himself our labours and sorrows ; let us in return “embrace Him with the two arms, so to speak, of patience and purity of intention, by acting and suffering for justice, sake, by directing our actions according to the rule of justice, and by bearing our sorrows for the love of justice. Let us also say with the Spouse in the Canticle, “ I held Him and I will not let Him go” (Cant. iii. 4). And let us say to Him with the Patriarch, “ I will not let Thee go except Thou bless me” (Gen. xxxii. 26). For what more remains now but the blessing? What but the kiss remains after the embrace ? Oh, if I cleave thus closely to my God, surely I may be permitted to cry out with the Spouse, “Let Him kiss me with the kiss of His mouth” (Cant. i. 1). Meantime, “feed us, O Lord, with the bread of tears and give us for our drink tears in measure ” (Ps. lxxix. 6), until Thou hast brought us to that “ good measure and pressed down, and shaken together and running over which Thou wilt give into our bosoms” (Luke vi. 38), Thou Who art Thyself “in the bosom of the Father ” (John i. 18) and over all things God blessed for ever. Amen.
